## How Medicare Passed in a Single Session

Some ideas fail in Washington for so long that their defeat becomes part of the furniture. For twenty years, national health insurance was one of those ideas. Presidents proposed it, legislators reintroduced it, committees studied it, and the same House committee buried it again and again. Medicare changed that in 1965. In the space of a single congressional session, health insurance for Americans aged 65 and older went from a perennial casualty to enacted law, signed by President Lyndon Johnson on July 30, 1965, in Independence, Missouri, with former President Harry Truman looking on.

The question is why 1965 was different, and the answer has two parts. First, the 1964 election redrew the arithmetic of the House Ways and Means Committee, the gate through which every health insurance bill had to pass. After the Democratic landslide of that November, which Republicans privately called an electoral catastrophe, the committee's Democratic majority could report a bill that its chairman, Wilbur Mills, had refused to report before. Second, Mills himself supplied the decisive move. On March 2, 1965, in executive session, he combined three rival proposals into one package: the administration's compulsory hospital insurance for the elderly, which became Medicare Part A; the voluntary physicians' coverage proposed by the committee's ranking Republican, John Byrnes, which became Medicare Part B; and an expanded Kerr-Mills program of aid to the medically needy, which became Medicaid. The opposition's own alternative became the program's second half, leaving the bill's critics with almost nothing left to fight over. The committee approved the package on March 24 by a straight party vote of 17 to 8 and reported H.R. 6675 to the House on March 29.

![Medicare passage history](/assets/images/blog/blog-05.webp)

This article traces the long defeat that preceded the quick victory. It opens with Truman's 1945 proposal and the reasons it failed, follows the Forand bills that kept the idea alive through the 1950s, examines the Kerr-Mills experiment and the King-Anderson bills that defined the rival approaches, and then turns to the 1964 election, the committee battle of early 1965, the floor fight, and the signing in Independence. The aim is to show how two decades of failure made the 1965 breakthrough possible: the defeats narrowed the proposal, identified the population it would serve, and clarified the coalition that could carry it. The later sections examine the legislative mechanics in detail, and close with the terms of [the enacted statute](/2010/11/01/social-security-amendments-1965-medicare/) itself.

To grasp how unlikely the 1965 result looked even a year earlier, consider the record. The original Wagner-Murray-Dingell bill of 1943 never reached a vote. Truman's 1945 message produced hearings and then silence. A narrower bill limited to the elderly, introduced in 1952, went nowhere. Representative Aime Forand's hospital insurance bills of 1957 and 1959 were voted down twice in the Ways and Means Committee in 1960, and a Senate amendment in the same spirit was rejected that August. Each defeat taught the program's supporters the same lesson: the chairman of Ways and Means and the committee's conservative majority were the wall, and no bill could pass until the wall moved.

Two features of the long defeat shaped the eventual victory. The first was fear. Opponents, led by the American Medical Association, branded national health insurance as socialized medicine, a charge that carried force in the early Cold War years. The second was narrowing. Supporters learned that a universal program united every interest against it, while a program limited to older Americans invited a different response. Framing hospital coverage as an earned benefit under Social Security, financed by payroll contributions and free of any means test, let supporters argue for dignity rather than charity. By the time the Forand bills of the late 1950s carried that framing, the movement had its population and its mechanism. What it still lacked was a majority in the House Ways and Means Committee, and that is what the election of 1964 supplied.

## Truman's 1945 Proposal and the First Defeat

On November 19, 1945, barely three months after the end of the Second World War, President Harry Truman sent Congress a special message recommending a comprehensive national health program. The message laid out five proposals: federal grants to help build hospitals and related facilities across the country; federal grants to expand public health services and maternal and child health care; federal grants for medical education and research; a nationwide system of health insurance; and compensation for wages lost to non-industrial disability. Truman argued that the question was not whether government should protect the health of the people, since federal, state, and local spending on public health and medical services already approached a billion dollars a year, but how much further that responsibility should extend. It was the first time a sitting president had proposed national health insurance, and Truman presented it as the centerpiece of a larger program to repair a system in which some forty percent of the nation's counties had no hospital meeting even minimum professional standards.

The most contested of the five points was the insurance proposal. On the same day the message arrived, a revised Wagner-Murray-Dingell bill was introduced in Congress as S. 1606 and H.R. 4730. The original version had been introduced in 1943 by Democratic Senators Robert Wagner of New York and James Murray of Montana, along with Representative John Dingell Sr. of Michigan, and Congress had taken no action on it. The revised bill would have created a national health insurance fund administered by the federal government and open to all Americans on an optional basis, with participants paying monthly fees and the government paying the cost of services provided by doctors who chose to join. It also provided a cash benefit to replace wages lost through sickness or long-term disability. Truman went to considerable lengths to deny that the plan amounted to socialized medicine, insisting that patients would continue to receive care through their own voluntary choices and that doctors and hospitals would retain the same professional freedom they had always enjoyed.

The response from organized medicine was swift and disciplined. The American Medical Association created a National Physicians' Committee for the Extension of Medical Service to fight the Wagner bill, and its campaign attached the label of socialized medicine to any compulsory plan. In 1946 the House held hearings on the Wagner bill from April 29 to July 13, and the proposal died in committee without ever reaching a vote. An alternative, the Taft-Smith-Ball bill, which would have authorized grants to states for medical care for the poor, was introduced as a substitute and received no action either. The one piece of Truman's program that survived was the least controversial: the Hill-Burton Hospital Survey and Construction Act of 1946, which funded hospital construction, the same kind of bricks-and-mortar aid his message had recommended. Building hospitals commanded broad agreement; insuring the people who used them did not.

Three forces combined to kill the insurance proposal. The first was the AMA, which mobilized doctors as a national political lobby and made the phrase socialized medicine into a weapon. The second was Congress itself, where southern Democrats and Republicans on the key committees had little appetite for extending federal power over a profession as widely respected as medicine, and where committee chairmen could bury a bill simply by refusing to schedule it. The third was the political climate of the early Cold War, in which any proposal involving government administration of personal services was easily painted as alien to American institutions. Truman's assurances that participation was voluntary and that the plan was insurance rather than state medicine could not overcome that combination, and the administration never found the votes to force the issue.

The defeat was complete, and for several years national health insurance barely stirred in Congress. But the 1945 message had fixed the terms of the debate that would run for the next two decades. Supporters would keep returning to the model of a national insurance fund tied to Social Security, while opponents would keep returning to the charge of socialized medicine. The open question was whether the proposal could be narrowed enough to split that opposition, and the next serious attempt would begin more than a decade later, aimed at one population only.

## The Forand Bills Keep the Idea Alive

The narrowing began inside the Social Security Administration in the early 1950s, where officials started suggesting that government health insurance benefits be limited to aged persons. A bill embodying that approach was introduced in 1952, and it went nowhere, but the concept survived: instead of insuring the entire nation, insure the population least able to protect itself. The vehicle for that concept became Representative Aime J. Forand of Rhode Island, a member of the Ways and Means Committee who took up hospital insurance for Social Security beneficiaries as his cause.

Forand introduced his original bill, H.R. 9467, on August 27, 1957, just before Congress adjourned. The bill would have provided health insurance for Social Security beneficiaries, covering hospitalization, nursing home care, and surgical benefits, financed through the Social Security system rather than through general revenues or state-administered welfare. The strategy was deliberate. By attaching health coverage to the contributory machinery of old-age, survivors, and disability insurance, Forand's supporters could argue that beneficiaries had earned the coverage through payroll taxes and should not be forced to submit to the humiliation of a means test. The AMA saw the danger at once, and that December its House of Delegates resolved to defeat the bill.

The Ways and Means Committee held hearings on the health issue in June 1958 and, rather than voting, asked the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare to study alternative ways of providing insurance against hospital and nursing home costs for Social Security beneficiaries. Forand reintroduced his bill on February 18, 1959, as H.R. 4700, and the committee held five days of hearings that July. The 1959 version attracted serious attention and a growing roster of supporters, including organized labor, which had committed itself to government health insurance in 1957, but Chairman Mills still would not report it. The bill's friends had made their case; the committee's arithmetic had not changed.

The decisive year was 1960. On March 31, the Ways and Means Committee voted on the Forand bill in executive session and defeated it 17 to 8. On June 3, after weeks of further deliberation, the committee voted a second time and defeated it again by the same 17 to 8 margin; a stripped-down version providing hospital benefits only was defeated 16 to 9. Ten days later, on June 13, the committee reported a different bill, H.R. 12580, which included a new program of federal grants to the states for medical services to the medically indigent elderly, the program that became known as Kerr-Mills. The House passed that bill on June 23 by 381 to 23. In the Senate, an amendment by Kennedy and Anderson embodying the health insurance approach was rejected on August 23, along with a Republican alternative, and the Senate approved the Kerr-Mills version. The President signed it into law on September 13, 1960.

The Forand bills stalled for the same reasons Truman's proposal had failed, sharpened by a hostile administration. HEW Secretary Arthur Flemming told the Ways and Means Committee in March 1960 that the Eisenhower administration opposed any program of compulsory health insurance. The AMA campaigned against the bills as it had against Wagner-Murray-Dingell. And Mills, who controlled the committee's agenda, preferred the Kerr-Mills approach of federal grants to states with means tests, the welfare model rather than the insurance model. Yet the Forand campaign accomplished something the defeats of the 1940s had not. It kept the contributory social-insurance design alive, it attached the cause to the sympathetic case of the elderly, and it made health insurance for the aged a national political issue in the 1960 presidential campaign. The bills lost every vote that mattered, but they defined exactly what the winning bill would look like five years later.

### Why did Truman's 1945 health proposal fail?

It failed because the American Medical Association campaigned against it, Congress would not move the revised Wagner-Murray-Dingell bill out of committee, and the early Cold War climate rewarded warnings about government control of medicine. Hospital construction aid passed instead, while national insurance died in committee.

## Kerr-Mills and the Limits of the States-First Approach

The Kerr-Mills Act of 1960, signed on September 13, 1960, as Public Law 86-778, was Congress's first answer to the problem of medical care for the aged. It did not create a federal health insurance program. Instead, it offered federal matching funds to states that set up their own programs of "medical assistance for the aged," a category of aid aimed at elderly persons whose incomes were too high to qualify for old-age assistance but too low to absorb large medical bills. The concept had a name borrowed from the drafters: medical indigency, the condition of being driven into need by medical costs rather than by general poverty. States retained the power to set eligibility standards, define the benefit package, and decide how much of their own money to commit. Washington would share the cost generously, in some formulas up to 80 percent, and pay half the administrative expense.

The theory was that the states, given federal money and flexibility, would move swiftly to cover their elderly poor. The theory failed. Implementation was slow, uneven, and heavily concentrated in a handful of wealthy states that already had the tax base and administrative machinery to act. By 1965, only 40 states had implemented Kerr-Mills at all, and three more had merely authorized it without putting programs into operation. Senator Robert Kerr, one of the act's sponsors, had estimated that as many as 10 million people might be covered. A more restrained early estimate put the realistic figure at 2 million. Neither came close. In 1965 Kerr-Mills covered 264,687 people nationwide, less than 2 percent of the elderly population, according to the Social Security Bulletin of that year.

The geographic distribution told the story more bluntly than any aggregate. At the end of the program's first year, 60 percent of the enrollees and almost 90 percent of the expenditures were concentrated in three states: New York, Massachusetts, and California, as reported by the Senate Special Committee on Aging in 1962. The imbalance softened somewhat but never disappeared. Even in 1965, New York, California, and Massachusetts accounted for 45 percent of the recipients, and the top five states, adding Pennsylvania and Michigan, accounted for 62 percent of the total. The pattern followed money: states that could afford generous programs drew the great bulk of federal matching dollars, while poorer states, where need was often greatest, put up little and received little in return.

Why so many states stayed on the sidelines had several interlocking causes. Kerr-Mills arrived as a brand-new title in the Social Security Act, and state legislatures had to enact enabling laws, appropriate state matching funds, and build administrative systems before a single dollar could flow. Many legislatures met only biennially and moved at the pace of their appropriations cycles. Others resisted on principle, suspicious of federal involvement in medical care or unwilling to spend state revenue on a new welfare category. Means testing itself created friction: the program required intrusive determinations of need, and the stigma of public assistance carried over from the older old-age assistance programs. Doctors and hospitals in some states were slow to participate, uncertain about fee schedules and state vendor payment rules.

The fiscal design also worked against broad adoption. Federal matching was open-ended in principle, but it required states to spend their own funds first, and the formulas tilted toward states that spent more per recipient. A state with a large low-income population and a narrow tax base faced the worst arithmetic: the highest need, the least capacity, and a federal formula that rewarded spending rather than need. The result was the opposite of a national safety net. Kerr-Mills became a map of fiscal capacity, rich states offering substantial medical assistance to their aged poor while poor states offered next to nothing.

Supporters of the states-first approach had argued that Kerr-Mills would answer the demand for government action and make a federal program unnecessary. Its defenders in Congress, especially the coalition of Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats on the House Ways and Means Committee, pointed to it whenever hospital insurance bills came up: give the program time, they said, let the states build their systems, and the problem would be managed without extending the Social Security system into health care. For a few years this argument had force. It bought time and gave opponents of Medicare a plausible alternative to endorse.

But the numbers, once they accumulated, undercut the argument and handed advocates of a federal program their strongest evidence. By the early 1960s, anyone who studied the figures could see that Kerr-Mills was reaching a small fraction of the elderly, that its benefits varied wildly from state to state, and that its growth depended on state political will and state budgets rather than on national standards of need. The program's weakness made the case for hospital insurance under Social Security more vivid than any speech could. A compulsory federal system would cover everyone eligible for Social Security or railroad retirement benefits regardless of where they lived, without means tests, without state opt-outs, and without the indignities that came with proving one's poverty to a county welfare worker.

The lesson drawn by the Kennedy administration and by Medicare's congressional sponsors was straightforward. Kerr-Mills had tested the proposition that the states, subsidized by Washington, could solve the problem of medical costs for the aged. The test had produced an answer: they could not, or at least they would not, in anything resembling a uniform or adequate fashion. When the King-Anderson hospital insurance bill was reintroduced in 1963, its supporters cited the Kerr-Mills record as proof that the voluntary, state-administered, means-tested model had reached its limits. The states-first approach had been given its chance, and its thin coverage, its geographic concentration, and its dependence on state fiscal capacity became exhibit A for the proposition that only a federal program could do the job.

## King-Anderson and the Committee Bottleneck

While Kerr-Mills moved slowly through the state legislatures, a competing idea was taking shape in Washington. On February 10, 1961, President John F. Kennedy sent Congress a special message on health, and three days later Representative Cecil King of California and Senator Clinton Anderson of New Mexico introduced the administration's hospital insurance bill, H.R. 4222 and S. 909. The King-Anderson bill proposed to finance hospital care for persons aged 65 and older through the Social Security system, with payroll contributions paying for a defined package of inpatient benefits. It carried no means test. Eligibility followed Social Security entitlement, which meant coverage would be national, uniform, and attached to a program Americans already understood and trusted.

The bill's sponsors framed it as insurance rather than welfare. Workers and employers would contribute during working years; the Hospital Insurance Trust Fund would pay benefits in old age. The design sidestepped the objections that had sunk earlier, more ambitious health proposals, such as President Truman's comprehensive national health insurance plan, by limiting coverage to the aged and anchoring it in the existing Social Security structure. The American Medical Association opposed it fiercely, calling it socialized medicine and warning that government payment for hospital services was the entering wedge of federal control over medical practice. But the bill's logic was plain: the elderly faced catastrophic hospital costs, private insurance largely ignored them, and the Social Security mechanism was the proven vehicle for reaching them.

The obstacle was not the bill's logic but its path. All tax and Social Security legislation in the House passed through the Committee on Ways and Means, and Ways and Means was controlled by Chairman Wilbur Mills of Arkansas. Mills was one of the most powerful members of Congress, a master of tax policy and legislative procedure, and in the early 1960s he was not convinced that hospital insurance belonged in the Social Security Act. The committee itself was dominated by a conservative coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats who shared the AMA's suspicion of the proposal or who, like Mills, believed the Kerr-Mills program deserved more time to prove itself. Whatever their individual motives, they formed a durable majority against reporting the bill.

The bottleneck operated quietly, through procedure rather than debate. Ways and Means held hearings on the King-Anderson bill in July and August of 1961, giving the appearance of serious consideration, and then took no further action. The bill died without a vote. This was the classic committee burial: no public roll call for supporters to cite, no floor debate for the press to cover, just the chairman's refusal to schedule a markup and the committee's unwillingness to force one. Supporters of the bill counted votes inside the committee and believed they were close. One account from the period, drawing on the recollections of the bill's advocates, described the committee as within one vote of a pro-hospital-insurance majority, with Mills himself declining to cast the vote that would have broken the deadlock. Mills's advice to the bill's supporters was revealing: take the bill to the Senate, he told them, where the terrain was friendlier. A revenue measure raising payroll taxes had to originate in the House under the Constitution, so the suggestion was as much a dismissal as a strategy.

The Senate proved to be the friendlier terrain Mills had described, though not friendly enough. In 1962, with the bill still bottled up in Ways and Means, its supporters attempted an end run. On May 20, 1962, President Kennedy took the case to the public in a nationally televised address from Madison Square Garden, and two days later the AMA's Dr. Edward Annis answered with a televised rebuttal of his own. The public campaign set the stage for a parliamentary gambit. Senators Anderson and Jacob Javits of New York offered a compromise Medicare measure, the Anderson-Javits amendment, as an amendment to a welfare bill then moving through the Senate, H.R. 10606. The amendment carried the main features of King-Anderson along with provisions allowing commercial insurance companies to participate, a concession meant to attract moderate votes.

It nearly worked. On July 17, 1962, the Senate voted on whether to table the Anderson-Javits amendment, and the motion to table carried by 52 to 48, a margin of four votes. The amendment's supporters had come within reach of the first Senate floor victory for hospital insurance, and the closeness of the vote shocked the bill's opponents. Even if the amendment had survived the tabling motion, its prospects in the House were dim, since Ways and Means stood in the way and the House leadership had shown no appetite for overriding the committee. The 87th Congress took no further action on hospital insurance proposals, and the vote stood as the high-water mark of the early campaign: a moral victory that produced no law.

The bill came back in 1963. On February 21, 1963, President Kennedy sent another special message to Congress on the problems of the aged, and a slightly revised King-Anderson bill was reintroduced the same day as H.R. 3920 and S. 880. Ways and Means held hearings that fall, but the proceedings were interrupted by the assassination of President Kennedy in November, and the hearings were not completed until January 1964. By then Lyndon Johnson was president, and on February 10, 1964, he sent Congress his own special message on the health of the nation, pressing the Medicare cause.

Mills and the committee held their ground. On June 24, 1964, Ways and Means met in executive session and voted to postpone action on Medicare. Two weeks later, on July 7, the committee reported the Social Security Amendments of 1964, H.R. 11865, a bill that included a cash benefit increase but contained no health care proposal at all. The House passed H.R. 11865 on July 24. When the bill reached the Senate, the Finance Committee reported it on August 20 without adding a health insurance provision, though the full Senate later inserted the King-Anderson hospital insurance program into its version of the bill. The two chambers could not reconcile their differences, and the conference committee adjourned on October 3, 1964, without making any recommendations. The 1964 election, which gave Johnson a landslide and his party large congressional majorities, would change the arithmetic. But through the end of 1964, the story of hospital insurance remained the story of a bill with growing public and Senate support and a chairman in the House who would not let it reach the floor.

### How did Ways and Means bottle up hospital insurance?

Chairman Wilbur Mills and a conservative coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats used committee procedure to smother the King-Anderson bill: hearings were held in 1961 and 1963 but no markup was ever scheduled and the bill was never reported, so the full House could not vote. The 1962 Anderson-Javits end run in the Senate was tabled 52 to 48.

## The 1964 Election Changes the Arithmetic

In November 1964, Lyndon B. Johnson buried Barry Goldwater in one of the most lopsided presidential elections the country had seen, and the congressional returns behind him changed every calculation surrounding the administration's hospital insurance plan. Democrats picked up 36 seats in the House, swelling their majority to 295 against 140 Republicans, a better than two-to-one advantage not seen since the New Deal era. In the Senate the party reached 68 seats against 32, a net gain of two. Johnson had campaigned openly on the promise to make hospital insurance for the aged the centerpiece of his domestic program, and his margin carried the flavor of a mandate for it.

The raw totals mattered less than where the new votes sat. Forty-two new Northern Democrats entered the House, and nearly all of them were supporters of the hospital insurance proposal. Until that election, the proposal had a record of near misses. It had passed the Senate once, in 1964, by a narrow margin, but it had never been approved by the House Committee on Ways and Means, so it had never reached the House floor at all. Chairman Wilbur Mills of Arkansas had simply refused to let the committee report it, and the committee's old balance of power protected his refusal.

The election dissolved that protection. Three vacancies had opened on Ways and Means, and the party ratios on committees were reset to mirror the new House. The committee would seat 17 Democrats and 8 Republicans in the 89th Congress. Just days after the election, House Majority Leader Carl Albert was already telling the president that this arithmetic, 17 to 8, would give the administration control of the Medicare question on the committee. The Speaker, meanwhile, had for years made each new Democratic appointee to Ways and Means pledge support for the hospital insurance bill as the price of the seat, so the fresh Democratic members were not merely Democrats, they were counted votes. Mills had long insisted that he would move only when he had the substantial majority he considered necessary for legislation of this weight. The 1964 returns handed him that majority, and the old blocking coalition of conservative southern Democrats and Republicans, which had kept the bill bottled up for years, could no longer command a majority inside the committee room.

Mills moved quickly once the numbers were settled. The committee began holding executive sessions on the administration's bill on January 27, 1965, and on March 29 it reported H.R. 6675 to the full House on a straight party vote, all 17 Democrats in favor and all 8 Republicans opposed. For the first time, the hospital insurance proposal would face the House with a favorable committee report behind it, which was the procedural threshold that had eluded it for nearly a decade.

## What the 1964 Campaign Decided

The election that changed Medicare's arithmetic was fought, in part, as a referendum on the program itself. Johnson made health insurance for the aged a centerpiece of his domestic platform, promising voters that the hospital insurance bill the old Congress had refused to report would become law under the new one. Goldwater and the Republicans opposed it, and the American Medical Association campaigned against it with the same machinery it had used against King-Anderson, including the Reagan recording and the Operation Coffeecup letter drives. Voters in November 1964 therefore had a clear choice on the question, and they chose by one of the widest margins in American history.

The result broke more than the presidential race. The 36-seat Democratic gain in the House replaced dozens of members who had voted with the conservative coalition, or who had sat on the fence, with Northern Democrats elected on Great Society platforms. The Speaker's practice of extracting pledges of support for hospital insurance from each new Democratic appointee to Ways and Means meant that the committee's new members arrived as counted votes rather than as persuadable ones. The 17-to-8 ratio was not an accident of seniority; it was the product of an election in which the winning side had campaigned on the issue and then staffed the decisive committee with its own supporters.

Johnson treated the mandate as personal and immediate. He had watched Kennedy's 1962 campaign for the same program fail, and he understood that a landslide's capital depreciates quickly. Within weeks of the election he was pressing the legislative leadership to move, and his recorded telephone conversations with Mills show a president who treated delay as the enemy. Letting the bill sit idle, he warned, would give the doctors time to organize, which was exactly what the AMA's machinery was built to do. The campaign had decided the question in principle. Johnson's job, as he saw it, was to make sure the principle became a statute before the opposition could regroup.

The campaign also decided something subtler: the terms on which Republicans would fight. The scale of the defeat convinced many Republican leaders that open identification with the AMA's resistance had been a political liability, and they had no appetite for repeating it. The Byrnes alternative gave them a different posture, support for health insurance for the aged on voluntary rather than compulsory terms. That posture shaped everything that followed, from the motion to recommit on the House floor to the nearly even Republican split on the conference report. The 1964 campaign did not convert Republicans to Medicare. It convinced enough of them that opposition was costlier than participation, and participation, once the three-layer cake was on the table, meant voting for a bill that contained their own alternative.

## Three Bills on the Table

By early 1965, three distinct proposals for health coverage of the aged were before the committee, and they were presented as mutually exclusive alternatives.

The first was the administration's own plan, the hospital insurance program that had grown out of the King-Anderson approach and that was carried in the new Congress as H.R. 6675. It was compulsory insurance for the aged, tied to the Social Security system and financed through payroll taxes on workers and employers. Its benefits covered hospitalization and related nursing home care, but it did not cover physicians' services. Its constituency was the administration itself, organized labor, and the elderly voters Johnson had promised to protect, all of whom treated it as the unfinished business of Social Security.

The second was the Republican alternative offered by Representative John Byrnes of Wisconsin, the ranking Republican on Ways and Means, who introduced it as H.R. 7057. Byrnes proposed a voluntary insurance program covering physicians' bills and prescription drugs, modeled on the existing health plan for active and retired federal employees. Its financing split costs equally between the government, out of general revenues, and the insured, who would pay premiums. Enrollment would be voluntary, a deliberate contrast with the compulsory character of the administration plan. Byrnes's constituency was the House Republican minority, physicians and their allies who preferred voluntarism to compulsion, and elderly voters who wanted the doctor coverage the administration bill omitted. Byrnes himself took to calling his plan Bettercare, a name that carried an unmistakable jab at the administration's Medicare label.

The third was the American Medical Association's proposal, known as Eldercare. Introduced in Congress by Representative Thomas Curtis of Missouri and Representative Sydney Herlong of Florida with the AMA's backing, Eldercare was framed as an expansion of the Kerr-Mills program, the federal-state initiative enacted in 1960 for the medically indigent elderly. It offered voluntary medical insurance for the elderly in participating states, distributed on the basis of need, with federal matching grants helping the states pay the costs. Its constituency was the AMA, which denounced compulsory hospital insurance as government interference in the doctor-patient relationship, along with conservatives who preferred a welfare model of federal grants to the states over a social insurance model. Most observers understood that the AMA pressed Eldercare mainly as a means of defeating the administration plan, and of dramatizing how expensive federal health insurance would be.

Each proposal attacked the others' weak point. The administration plan was condemned as compulsory and as inadequate, since it covered only hospital costs. Bettercare was voluntary but offered no solution for the elderly poor who could not afford premiums. Eldercare was voluntary and means-tested but perpetuated the patchwork that had left most aged Americans without coverage. In committee, the three camps stared at one another across that stalemate for weeks.

## The Mills Merge

The breakthrough came in an executive session of Ways and Means on March 2, 1965. After weeks of testimony on the three rival proposals, Mills leaned back in his chair, turned to Wilbur Cohen, the Health, Education, and Welfare official who had shepherded the administration's case, and asked whether the three could be combined into a single bill. He told Cohen to work the idea out overnight and see what it might come to. One participant later said the moment had the quality of a light bulb switching on.

The label that stuck to the maneuver was "three-layer cake," though it was not Mills's coinage. Cohen had used the phrase earlier for a different and much slower proposal of his own, hospital insurance first, private coverage for physicians' bills second, and an expanded Kerr-Mills program underneath. Participants in the March 2 session, and chroniclers after them, borrowed Cohen's phrase for Mills's combination, which stacked the proposals at once rather than in sequence. The top layer was the Byrnes payment plan for physicians' services, including the copayment idea Mills had praised during the hearings. The middle layer was hospital care financed by payroll taxes, the core of the administration bill. The bottom layer was an expanded Kerr-Mills program for the low-income, reaching beyond the elderly poor.

Mills then presided over the markup with the practiced control of a chairman who knew his vote count. From March 3 to March 23, the committee worked through the combination in executive session, Mills questioning witnesses persistently, soliciting comments from the floor, and giving particular room to Byrnes, whose cooperation was the political point of the exercise. Some pieces fell away under scrutiny, including payment for prescription drugs used outside hospitals and nursing homes, which the committee rejected as too unpredictable and potentially costly. On financing for the separate physicians' insurance, the committee kept Byrnes's own suggestion of individual premium payments rather than social insurance taxation, preserving the voluntary character of his plan inside the larger bill. The result was a package that disarmed every objection at once, as shown by [how a chairman controls a bill](/2022/03/01/committee-system-and-markups/).

When the committee reported H.R. 6675 on March 29, the three rivals had become three titles. The administration's hospital insurance survived at the core and became Part A, while the Byrnes alternative, enacted with its voluntary enrollment and its premium-plus-general-revenue financing intact, became Medicare's Part B; for how that division functions in the enacted law, see [how Parts A and B work in the statute](/2010/12/01/medicare-parts-a-b-statute-explained/). The bottom layer did not merely expand Kerr-Mills but replaced it outright with a broader federal-state medical assistance program open to the poor of all ages, enacted as Title XIX of the Social Security Act and known as Medicaid; its structure is traced in [the Medicaid statute](/2011/02/01/medicaid-statute-structure-explained/). Mills had kept the basic elements of the administration program financed through Social Security, added a supplemental benefit the aged would willingly purchase, and absorbed the AMA's own approach as the welfare layer, leaving Republicans, in Cohen's delighted phrase, with nowhere serious to attack.

### How did Mills turn three rival bills into one?

Mills kept the administration's hospital insurance core, then grafted the Byrnes voluntary physician plan and an expanded Kerr-Mills program onto it, so each rival constituency found its own proposal inside the reported bill. Adopted in executive session on March 2, 1965, the combination made floor opposition politically costly and passed the committee 17 to 8.

## The House Acts: H.R. 6675 as Reported by Ways and Means

No health insurance bill for the aged had ever cleared the House Ways and Means Committee, and that single fact had defined Medicare's politics for a decade. Presidents had proposed such coverage, the American Medical Association had campaigned against it with open ferocity, and the Senate had even passed a version in 1964, though only by a narrow margin, only to watch it die for want of a House willing to act. The committee remained the graveyard where every plan was buried. The 1964 elections broke the deadlock. Forty-two new Northern Democrats, almost all of them Medicare supporters, enlarged an already commanding Democratic majority, and Wilbur Mills of Arkansas, the chairman who had long stood athwart compulsory health insurance, returned to Washington with a different calculation. What he did with that calculation startled even his allies.

The road to that moment had been long and littered with failures. In the spring of 1962, President John F. Kennedy had launched a national campaign for health care for seniors, after a study showed that 56 percent of Americans over 65 lacked health insurance, and had carried the case to a nationally televised address from Madison Square Garden. The legislation failed. The medical association's opposition had only hardened in the years since, and its warnings about government intrusion into medicine echoed through every debate. Johnson, who had watched Kennedy's effort collapse, understood that the 1964 landslide had given him a majority large enough to outvote the opposition rather than persuade it.

In the opening months of 1965, Mills executed one of the most consequential reversals in modern legislative history. Rather than merely permitting a Medicare bill to advance, he set out to make it larger than anything the administration had dared to propose. Observers speculated endlessly about his motives, whether principle, ambition, or a hard reading of the new political map, but the result was beyond dispute. The committee began executive sessions on H.R. 1, the administration's hospital insurance bill, on January 27. The bill would have established a social insurance program for hospital and related care for the aged. Before the members lay three competing approaches. The administration wanted compulsory hospital insurance financed through payroll taxes. John Byrnes of Wisconsin, the ranking Republican, offered a voluntary plan covering physicians' services and drugs, financed from general revenues and scaled to each participant's Social Security benefits. The American Medical Association pressed its Eldercare alternative, built on the Kerr-Mills framework of federal aid to the states. Mills took all three proposals and fused them into a single structure, though Byrnes's preference for lower taxes had to give way once the program's predicted costs made higher taxes unavoidable, and the AMA's Eldercare approach was absorbed as the federal-state third layer rather than left standing as an alternative.

Inside the committee, the deliberations were brisk and unsentimental. When the sessions began in 1965, the suggestions pressed by the medical association's allies were rejected outright, and the members worked in executive session, away from the floor's glare. Mills drove the process personally, and the chairman who emerged from those closed rooms was unrecognizable from the obstructionist of earlier sessions. He became Medicare's champion, sponsoring a bill that broadened medical coverage beyond anything Johnson and the liberal Democrats had believed politically possible in January. The expansion was deliberate. By folding in the voluntary physicians' insurance that Republicans favored, Mills gave the bill a breadth that made it harder to attack and harder to stop. The tally sheet from the March 24 executive session recorded the decision as a straight party vote, 17 to 8. The package the committee approved had three distinct components: a mandated hospital insurance program for the elderly financed by an increase in payroll taxes, a voluntary supplemental plan for other health costs financed through general revenues, and a new title of the Social Security Act, Title XIX, offering federal matching funds to states covering the indigent aged and other medically needy groups.

The bill that emerged, H.R. 6675, carried three parts. The first was a compulsory hospital insurance program, Part A, financed by a separate payroll tax and serving Social Security and railroad retirement beneficiaries aged 65 or older. The second was a voluntary supplementary medical insurance plan, Part B, to help pay physicians' bills and related charges, financed through monthly premiums matched by federal payments from general revenues. The third added a new title to the Social Security Act, Title XIX, a liberalized federal-state medical assistance program for the poor that became Medicaid. To the health provisions the committee attached a 7 percent across-the-board increase in cash Social Security benefits, extended compulsory self-employment coverage to doctors, carried child's benefits through age 21 for full-time students, and raised both the payroll tax rate and the taxable wage base to pay for the new commitments. The amendments reached beyond health care. They cut the quarters of work needed for insured status for persons 72 or older, let widows draw actuarially reduced benefits at 60 rather than 62, provided benefits to divorced wives and widows under certain conditions, raised the earnings test to $1,500 with $1 withheld for every $2 earned up to $2,700, and rewrote the disability test so that a worker qualified when a disability was expected to end in death or to last twelve consecutive months. The report's own statement of purpose was plainer: to provide a hospital insurance program for the aged under the Social Security Act, with a supplementary health benefits program and an expanded program of medical assistance.

In late March the committee approved the package on a straight party-line vote, 17 Democrats in favor and 8 Republicans opposed, and on March 29 it reported the bill to the full House as House Report No. 213, recommending that it pass without amendment. President Johnson, who had issued a statement backing the bill after the committee's favorable vote, watched the chairman who had once been the program's chief obstacle become its most effective advocate, a signal that the White House would spend its full prestige on the measure. The report carried the full apparatus of committee justification, and the bill itself now stretched to 296 pages. The report was committed to the Committee of the Whole House and ordered printed the same day.

The House took up the bill on April 7, and when the members gathered to vote the next day they gave Mills a standing ovation. His explanation of the measure was exhaustive, described by one account as a masterly explanation of a complicated measure, and it needed to be: the rules gave the opposition almost no room. The bill reached the floor under a procedure permitting no amendments, so the Republican alternative could be offered only through a motion to recommit with instructions, the minority's customary last weapon. Byrnes rose to move that the bill be recommitted with instructions to substitute the text of H.R. 7057, a bill he had introduced a week earlier. His voluntary plan covered hospitalization, nursing home, medical, and surgical care, with costs split between the patient and general revenues rather than payroll taxes. The substitute could not be offered as an amendment, because the rule did not permit it, so the motion to recommit was the minority's only vehicle. It drew 128 of the 138 Republicans, a measure of how completely the minority preferred the voluntary approach. The motion failed, 191 to 236. The breakdown exposed the fault lines: 128 Republicans and 63 Democrats backed the substitute, while only 10 Republicans joined the 226 Democrats who voted with the majority. Once it was clear the bill would pass, party lines re-formed.

Floor debate centered on the Medicare proposal itself. Supporters called it long overdue, a promise deferred since the architects of Social Security had set medical coverage aside in 1935 for fear of sinking the whole program. Critics answered that compulsion was the wrong principle, that a payroll tax was regressive, and that the new spending would endanger the cash benefits retirees already received. Republican spokesmen countered with their own vision: a voluntary health plan, underpinned by a Medicaid-like program of medical assistance for the needy aged. The chamber heard both cases fully, and then voted its arithmetic.

Final passage came the same day. The House approved H.R. 6675 by 313 to 115, with 5 members not voting. The party split measured the contours of the session's politics: Democrats voted 248 to 42 in favor, Republicans divided 65 to 73. Critics had denounced the measure as compulsory, financed by a regressive payroll tax, and a threat to the cash benefit program, while Republican spokesmen had pressed for a voluntary health plan with a Medicaid-like program underneath it for the needy aged. The margin owed more to the size of the Democratic majority than to any bipartisan compact, and the 42 Democratic no votes, drawn heavily from the South, showed where the party's internal resistance lived. The Congressional Record logged the two April 8 roll calls as numbers 70 and 71, each with five members not voting. The margin was overwhelming by any standard of contested legislation.

The contrast with [the contrasting 1964 floor fight](/2010/01/15/civil-rights-act-1964-passage-history/) sharpens the point. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 had reached the statute books only after months of floor combat, including a Senate filibuster that ground the chamber to a halt. Medicare compressed its decisive House battle into two days of debate and two roll calls. The difference was partly subject matter and partly procedure: civil rights had to break a filibuster before it could advance, while Medicare's supporters controlled the committee, the rule, and the calendar, and they pressed each advantage without apology. No filibuster materialized against Medicare; its Senate fight would be waged through amendments, not delay.

### How did the House vote on Medicare in April 1965?

On April 8, 1965, the House passed H.R. 6675 by a vote of 313 to 115. Democrats voted 248 to 42 in favor, while Republicans split 65 to 73. Five members did not vote. The tally followed the defeat of a Republican substitute, 236 to 191, and sent the bill to the Senate.

## The Twenty-One-Day Rule

One procedural change, little noticed outside the House, helped guarantee that the bill Mills reported would reach the floor. The 89th Congress reinstated the twenty-one-day rule, a device that limited the power of the Rules Committee to bury legislation it disliked. Under the rule, if the Rules Committee held a bill for more than twenty-one days without acting, the Speaker could bring it to the floor through other procedures. The rule had been adopted in 1965 at the start of the session, over the resistance of the committee's chairman, who had used his control of the floor schedule to block civil rights and other liberal legislation for years.

For Medicare, the rule mattered as insurance rather than as a weapon. The Rules Committee in 1965 was friendlier to the administration than it had been in earlier Congresses, and the bill moved through it without a confrontation. But the rule's existence changed the bargaining in advance: opponents on the Rules Committee knew that indefinite delay was no longer available, and supporters knew that a chairman's refusal to schedule a vote could be circumvented. The closed rule the committee granted the bill, permitting no floor amendments, was itself a product of that confidence. The leadership could afford to give the minority only the motion to recommit, because no one doubted that the bill would reach the floor one way or another.

The episode illustrates how thoroughly the 1964 election had rearranged the House's procedural landscape. The committee ratio gave Medicare its substance. The twenty-one-day rule gave it a guaranteed path to the floor. Between the two, the bill's opponents were left with only the weapons of debate and the record vote, and on the record vote they lost.

## The Senate and the Conference

The Senate offered no closed rule, and that single procedural difference guaranteed a longer and messier fight. The Finance Committee reported its version of H.R. 6675 on June 30, approving the bill 12 to 5, with 10 Democrats and 2 Republicans in favor and 4 Republicans and 1 Democrat opposed. Only a single Democrat opposed the bill in committee, a measure of how thoroughly the party had committed itself. The committee's titular chairman, Harry Flood Byrd of Virginia, opposed the bill and was in failing health, so the floor management fell to Russell Long of Louisiana, the acting chairman, who carried the measure through the chamber. Long had already left his mark on the bill with an amendment distinguishing between those who could and could not meet their own medical needs, and he proved a tenacious manager. The Senate, unlike the House, does not vote on social security bills under a closed rule, so Long knew the floor fight would be fought amendment by amendment, with himself as the bill's manager.

Debate opened in early July, and the Senate at once began rewriting the House bill. About 100 amendments recommended by the Finance Committee were adopted, along with some 30 floor amendments, and the chamber's appetite for expansion ran ahead of the House version. Robert Byrd of West Virginia won adoption of an amendment lowering to 60, rather than 62, the age at which workers could receive reduced retirement benefits. Hartke of Indiana carried amendments broadening benefits for the blind, one of them by a vote of 78 to 11, liberalizing the definition of blindness and letting blind workers with at least 6 quarters of coverage receive benefits regardless of other earnings, and on July 9 he won a voice vote eliminating the time limit on hospital care. The Senate had already accepted the committee's recommendation of unlimited hospital care with $10 coinsurance payments after 60 days. Smathers of Florida secured a voice-vote increase in payroll taxes to pay for the new benefits. Kennedy of New York carried an amendment barring federal payments to hospitals that failed to meet state or local standards. Newspaper estimates put the Senate's additions at roughly $900 million above the House bill's cost. The additions guaranteed that the conference committee would have real money to argue over.

The opposition had its moments, though none of them carried. Ribicoff of Connecticut moved to remove all time limits on hospital stays and lost, 39 to 43. Miller of Iowa proposed an automatic 3 percent increase in pensions tied to the retail price index and lost, 21 to 64. Prouty of Vermont offered benefit increases scaled by income bracket and lost, 12 to 79. Curtis of Nebraska, the bill's most persistent Senate critic, offered an amendment to strike Medicare, Parts A and B, from the bill and lost 26 to 64, with 18 Republicans and 8 Democrats on his side. He tried again with an amendment requiring patients to pay a deductible scaled to their ability to pay, and lost 41 to 51. He then moved to recommit the bill with instructions to substitute a plan patterned on the health insurance program for retired federal employees, financed from current premiums. He argued that recommittal would give the aged better protection at half the cost under private enterprise. Majority Leader Mike Mansfield of Montana urged the chamber to recognize the motion for the delaying tactic it was, and it was defeated, 26 to 63. When the chamber finally voted on passage on July 9, the result was 68 to 21, with 11 senators not voting. The party split ran 55 Democrats and 13 Republicans in favor against 7 Democrats and 14 Republicans opposed.

The Senate's freehand rewriting meant the two chambers had passed different bills, and the Constitution's demand for identical text sent H.R. 6675 to a conference committee. The conferees faced more than 500 differences between the House and Senate versions, most of them financial and administrative, and they worked through the standard machinery of compromise, splitting differences and trading concessions. The most publicized decision was the rejection of a plan to change how certain specialists were paid under the hospital insurance program. The bulk of the decisions were quieter, compromises between divergent benefit levels reached by accepting one chamber's version or combining the two. The committee finished its work on July 26, having worked out the financial and administrative differences through compromise.

The conference report then required fresh votes in each chamber, and both majorities held. On July 27 the House adopted the report by 307 to 116, with 237 Democrats and 70 Republicans voting yes and 48 Democrats and 68 Republicans voting no. The following day the Senate adopted it by 70 to 24, with 57 Democrats and 13 Republicans in favor and 7 Democrats and 17 Republicans opposed. The margins were slightly narrower than the original passage votes, as conference compromises usually cost some support on both flanks, but neither chamber was ever in serious doubt. In the House, 8 Democrats and 2 Republicans did not vote; in the Senate, 4 Democrats and 2 Republicans sat out.

On July 30, President Johnson traveled to Independence, Missouri, to sign the bill at the Harry S. Truman Library, with the former president, then 81, present for the ceremony. Truman sat beside Johnson as he signed, with Bess Truman and Vice President Hubert Humphrey behind them and Lady Bird Johnson behind her husband. The choice of venue was deliberate and personal. Truman had been the first president to propose national health insurance, in 1945, having made the program his goal during his Senate years, and Johnson, who called him the "real daddy of Medicare," presented the Trumans with the first two Medicare cards, Harry's bearing number one and Bess's number two. Johnson said the act lifted the burden of crushing medical expenses from the shoulders of some 18 million Americans over the age of 65, many with low incomes and threatened by unaffordable medical expenses. The signing made the Social Security Amendments of 1965, Public Law 89-97, the law of the land. Medicare's hospital and medical insurance took effect the following year, and some 19 million people enrolled in the first year, while Medicaid began its long transformation of health coverage for the poor. Medicaid itself, the federal-state program for the poor, became law the same day as Title XIX of the same act.

From the Ways and Means report on March 29 to the signing on July 30, the bill had traveled from committee to statute in four months, passing each chamber twice. The final tallies, 307 to 116 and 70 to 24, were narrower than the first passage votes but never in doubt. A program that had died in committee for a decade was now the law, carried by commanding Democratic majorities and a president willing to spend them.

### Why did the bill need a conference committee?

The Senate rewrote the House bill, adopting about one hundred committee amendments and thirty floor amendments, which created more than five hundred differences between the two versions. The Constitution requires both chambers to pass identical text, so a conference committee met to reconcile them, finishing July 26. The compromise then required fresh votes in each chamber.

## The Cost Debate: Who Paid and Who Objected

Beneath the ideological combat over socialized medicine ran a harder and more technical argument about money: how much the program would cost, who would pay for it, and whether the payroll tax could bear the weight. Wilbur Mills cared about this argument more than any other. He had blocked hospital insurance for years not from hostility to the aged but from a conviction that the financing was unsound, and that expanded benefits would force payroll taxes beyond reasonable levels. When he finally moved the bill, he moved it on terms that answered his own objection.

The committee's markup showed the fiscal discipline at work. Some pieces of the emerging package fell away under scrutiny, including payment for prescription drugs used outside hospitals and nursing homes, which the committee rejected as too unpredictable and potentially costly. The members were willing to build a large program, but they drew a line at benefits whose costs could not be estimated with the actuarial tools of the time. On financing for the separate physicians' insurance, the committee kept Byrnes's own suggestion of individual premium payments rather than social insurance taxation, a choice that held the payroll tax increase to the hospital program alone and kept the voluntary half of the program off the wage base.

The payroll tax itself was the most contested fiscal question. Byrnes and the House Republicans argued that it was regressive, falling hardest on wage earners, and that it risked crowding out the cash benefits Social Security had been created to pay. Their alternative financed health benefits from general revenues, which they called fairer and more honest about the true cost, since the burden would fall on the progressive income tax rather than on a flat levy on wages. Floor critics in April made the same point, warning that the new spending would endanger the cash benefit program. The argument had force, and it drew the 42 Southern Democratic no votes and most of the 73 Republican no votes on final passage.

The Senate made the fiscal picture worse before it made it better. The chamber's freehand rewriting added roughly $900 million above the House bill's cost, by contemporary newspaper estimates, through amendments that broadened benefits, liberalized eligibility, and raised the payroll tax to pay for the additions. Robert Byrd's amendment lowering the retirement age for reduced benefits to 60, Hartke's amendments for the blind, the voice-vote elimination of the time limit on hospital care, Smathers's payroll tax increase to fund the new benefits: each had a constituency and a cost. The conference committee's most important work was fiscal, reconciling more than 500 differences between the chambers, most of them financial, and splitting the difference on benefit levels and tax rates until both chambers could vote again.

Mills's answer to the cost objection was structural. By combining the three proposals, he spread the financing across three different revenue sources: payroll taxes for hospital insurance, premiums plus general revenues for physicians' insurance, and federal matching grants for the federal-state medical assistance program. No single tax bore the whole program, and each layer's supporters had a fiscal stake in defending their own. The payroll tax increase was real, from 3.625 percent to 3.85 percent on employers and employees, with the hospital insurance tax added on top, but it was smaller than it would have been if the entire program had been financed through Social Security. Wilbur Cohen's delighted observation that the combination left Republicans with nowhere serious to attack applied to the fiscal argument as well: the party that had denounced the payroll tax as regressive found its own preferred financing, premiums and general revenues, enacted as Part B.

The cost debate never fully ended; it moved into the program's administration, where actuaries and Congress would revisit the rates for decades. But in 1965 the fiscal argument was settled the same way the substantive argument was settled, by a committee chairman who treated cost as a design problem rather than a reason to do nothing. The bill that emerged was expensive, and its critics said so on the record. It was also paid for, and that was Mills's doing.

The episode left a lasting imprint on how Congress writes health legislation. The 1965 committee worked from formal cost estimates, assumed a fixed taxable base, and rejected benefits it could not price, a discipline that later Congresses would formalize into the modern scoring process. Mills's insistence that every benefit carry its own revenue source became the template for the program's finances: separate trust funds, separate taxes, and a visible connection between what beneficiaries paid and what they received. Critics of the payroll tax had warned that compulsion plus regressivity was a dangerous combination. The committee's answer was not to abandon compulsion but to confine it to the hospital program, where the insurance logic was strongest, and to let voluntarism govern the rest.

## How the Law Paid for Itself

The politics of Medicare get the attention, but the financing design of Public Law 89-97 was the quietest of the three-layer cake's layers and the one that kept the program solvent in its early years. Each part of the program was given its own revenue stream, its own trust fund, and its own political logic, so that the compulsory and voluntary halves of the law stood on separate fiscal foundations.

Part A, hospital insurance, was financed by a separate payroll tax dedicated to a new Hospital Insurance Trust Fund. For 1966, the tax was 0.35 percent on the employer and 0.35 percent on the employee, applied to the same earnings base as Social Security's cash benefits. The schedule written into the law then stepped upward: 0.50 percent for 1967 through 1972, 0.55 percent for 1973 through 1975, 0.60 percent for 1976 through 1979, 0.70 percent for 1980 through 1986, and 0.80 percent for 1987 and thereafter. The same rates applied equally to employers, employees, and self-employed persons. The cost estimates behind the schedule assumed that the earnings base would not be raised above $6,600, the level the law set beginning in 1966. That base itself was a jump: the amendments raised the taxable maximum from $4,800 to $6,600, the first increase since the base had been set at $4,800. In practical terms, a worker earning at or above the base paid $23.10 in hospital insurance tax in 1966, with the employer matching it, a small levy that bought the promise of hospital coverage in old age.

The cash side of Social Security carried its own increases. The OASDI tax rate on employers and employees rose from 3.625 percent to 3.85 percent beginning in 1966, and from 5.4 percent to 5.8 percent for the self-employed. The combined OASDHI rate for 1966 was therefore 4.2 percent on employers and employees each, and 6.15 percent on the self-employed, with an ultimate scheduled rate of 5.65 percent on employers and employees each, and 7.8 percent on the self-employed, by 1987. Those numbers were the price of the package the committee had assembled: the 7 percent cash benefit increase, the new health benefits, and the expanded disability and survivors provisions all had to be paid for, and Mills, the tax craftsman, insisted that the arithmetic balance.

Part B, the voluntary supplementary medical insurance, was financed on an entirely different principle, the one John Byrnes had insisted on. Enrollees paid a monthly premium, set at $3 for the program's first year, and the federal government matched it with $3 a month from general revenues. The plan covered physicians' and surgeons' services whether furnished in a hospital, clinic, office, home, or elsewhere, plus home health services and other medical and health services, paying 80 percent of the patient's bill above an annual deductible of $50. Reasonable charges were determined by the carriers that administered the program, which looked to the physician's customary charges and the prevailing charges in the locality for similar services. States were permitted to buy the medical insurance protection for their public assistance recipients receiving cash assistance, a provision that tied the voluntary federal program to the welfare population at state option. Persons who dropped out of the plan had only one chance to reenroll, and reenrollment had to occur within three years of termination, a rule designed to keep healthy beneficiaries from gaming the risk pool by waiting until they were sick to join.

The original enrollment window showed how seriously the designers took adverse selection. The initial general enrollment period was scheduled to end on March 31, 1966, for people who would be 65 before January 1, 1966. Anyone who missed the deadline would not have another opportunity to enroll until two years later, and would then pay higher monthly premiums. The penalty structure worked: by the time benefits began, the great majority of the eligible population had enrolled.

The financing carried a political message as well as an actuarial one. Part A's payroll tax made hospital insurance feel earned, a benefit workers had paid for during their working years, just like the retirement checks that arrived in the same mail. Part B's premium and general revenue split made the voluntary half feel purchased, a bargain the beneficiary had chosen and partly funded. The two halves were kept in separate trust funds precisely so that neither could be said to subsidize the other. Mills had built a tax bill as carefully as he had built a coalition, and the financing survived essentially intact into the program's second decade.

## The Non-Health Provisions of H.R. 6675

The bill remembered as the Medicare law was, in its full title, the Social Security Amendments of 1965, and roughly half its pages dealt with cash benefits rather than health care. The health provisions dominated the debate, but the cash provisions dominated the statute's page count, and they changed the lives of millions of beneficiaries who never set foot in a hospital.

The most visible was the 7 percent across-the-board increase in old-age, survivors, and disability benefits, made effective in January 1965, before the bill had even passed. It was the largest percentage increase Congress had granted since the early 1950s, and it reached every beneficiary on the rolls. The earnings test was liberalized alongside it: recipients could earn up to $1,500 a year and keep all their benefits, and only earnings above that line reduced checks, at $1 withheld for every $2 of earnings up to $2,700. For retired workers who supplemented their pensions with part-time work, the change was immediate money in the pocket.

Disability insurance was rewritten in two directions. The definition of disability was liberalized so that a worker qualified if the disability could be expected to result in death or to last for a continuous period of not less than twelve months, replacing the old requirement that it be of long-continued and indefinite duration. At the same time, a new offset provision reduced disability benefits to workers and their families when those benefits combined with workmen's compensation exceeded 80 percent of the worker's previous earnings, a guard against duplicate income replacement.

Survivors and dependents gained in several ways. Widows could elect actuarially reduced benefits at age 60 rather than waiting until 62. Children's benefits were extended through age 21 for full-time students, where prior law had cut them off at 18. Divorced wives and widows became eligible for benefits if they had been dependent on the wage-earner's support and their marriage had lasted twenty consecutive years or more. And for the oldest generation, the quarters of work needed for insured status were cut from six to three for persons aged 72 or older, with parallel reductions for their wives, so that very elderly workers who had spent their careers outside covered employment could still qualify.

One provision carried a quiet irony given the bill's history. Compulsory self-employment coverage was extended to doctors, the very profession whose national association had spent two decades warning that federal health programs were the entering wedge of government control of medicine. The doctors would now pay the same payroll taxes as everyone else.

These provisions matter to the legislative history because they show what the 1965 vehicle made possible. A bill that began as hospital insurance for the aged became, in conference and committee, the occasion for the broadest overhaul of cash Social Security benefits in a decade. The health program needed the payroll tax increase; the payroll tax increase made room for the benefit package; and the benefit package bought votes from members whose constituents cared more about their monthly checks than about hospital insurance. Medicare's passage is usually told as a health policy story. The statute itself is also a Social Security story, and the two were inseparable from the moment the committee decided to move them together.

## The Opposition's Case, in Its Own Words

The American Medical Association had been arguing against compulsory health insurance long before the King-Anderson bill existed. In 1939, the association's executive director declared that "all forms of security, compulsory security, even against old age and unemployment ... represent a taking away of individual responsibility, a weakening of national caliber, a definite step toward either communism or totalitarianism." That was the frame the AMA carried into the 1960s: government health insurance was not a humanitarian project but an ideological threat, dressed up to look like one. Everything the association did against Medicare flowed from that premise.

The campaign machinery was formidable. In the late 1950s the AMA launched Operation Coffeecup, an effort aimed not at doctors but at their wives. The AMA Woman's Auxiliary organized local members to host informal coffee parties, at which the hostess would play a recording, lead a discussion, and then, as the campaign literature instructed, urge each woman to write her letters to Congress while still in the house and in the mood. Each hostess filled out a report form recording the number of attendees, the number of times the record was played, and the number of letters written. The recording was the 1961 LP "Ronald Reagan Speaks Out Against Socialized Medicine," pressed in an initial run of 3,000 copies and distributed to auxiliary members nationwide. The letters that resulted, the AMA boasted, were "legion." Each LP arrived packaged with instructions on how to write an effective letter to Congress, turning neighborly persuasion into a coordinated pressure campaign. The campaign was kept deliberately quiet: organizers warned that the record was to be used only under the controlled conditions of the informal coffees, and under no circumstances was it to be given a commercial broadcast.

The Reagan recording itself is the clearest surviving statement of the AMA's case. Reagan, then a private citizen and the former president of the Screen Actors Guild, was speaking against the King-Anderson bill backed by Representative Cecil King of California. He quoted the socialist presidential candidate Norman Thomas, who had said in 1927 that the American people would never vote for socialism but "under the name of liberalism the American people will adopt every fragment of the socialist program." Reagan then argued that medicine was the classic vehicle: "One of the traditional methods of imposing statism or socialism on a people has been by way of medicine. It's very easy to disguise a medical program as a humanitarian project." His conclusion was blunt: "we do not want socialized medicine." The argument was not about benefit levels or actuarial tables. It was that a compulsory program, however modest its first step, would bring the federal government into the doctor-patient relationship and, from there, into medicine itself.

The AMA did not only say no. Through 1962, AMA president-elect Dr. Edward Annis carried the argument on television, including a widely watched Madison Square Garden speech against King-Anderson. The association's substantive position was that the Kerr-Mills program, enacted in 1960 to aid the medically indigent elderly through federal-state matching funds, was the right instrument and should simply be enlarged. That became Eldercare, the bill introduced by Representative Thomas Curtis of Missouri and Representative Sydney Herlong of Florida: federal matching grants to help states pay for physicians' services, surgical charges, drugs, nursing home costs, and X-ray and laboratory services for the aged poor. In the AMA's telling, a need-based program preserved free choice of physician and kept federal supervision out of medical practice, while King-Anderson would furnish care to many who did not need it, furnish inadequate care to many who did, and exclude the poorest elderly who were not eligible for Social Security. Policy analysts of the period contended that the AMA backed Eldercare largely as a way to defeat Medicare outright, by making any federal expansion look unaffordable, but on its own terms the association's position was consistent: help only the neediest among the aged, and leave everyone else alone.

The Republican alternative worked the same ground with different tools. Representative John Byrnes of Wisconsin, the ranking minority member on Ways and Means, offered a plan he called Bettercare. Its core principle was voluntarism: the aged would be free to join or not, as they chose. Benefits would resemble the federal employees' health plan, modeled on an Aetna policy, and would cover the major risks King-Anderson left out, particularly doctors' services and prescription drugs. The participant's share of the cost would be scaled to the amount of the participant's Social Security cash benefits, so that poorer beneficiaries paid less, while the government's share would come from general revenues rather than from a payroll tax. Byrnes's logic was fiscal as well as philosophical. Payroll taxes fell hardest on wage earners and risked crowding out the cash benefits Social Security was created to pay; financing health benefits from general revenues was, in his view, fairer and more honest about the true cost. He pitched the plan as the responsible way to cover the aged: generous benefits, honestly priced, paid from the same revenues that funded the rest of government, without a new tax on wages. House floor critics made the same point during debate, calling the payroll tax regressive and warning that it endangered the cash benefit program.

Byrnes forced a direct choice. On April 8, 1965, after the Ways and Means bill reached the House floor, Republicans moved to recommit H.R. 6675 with instructions to substitute the text of H.R. 7057, Byrnes's bill as introduced the week before. The motion failed, 236 to 191, with 128 of 138 Republicans voting for the substitute. That defeat ended the alternative as a bill. It did not end it as a design. The voluntary principle Byrnes had championed became Part B of the enacted statute, supplementary medical insurance for physicians' services, financed by premiums and general revenues rather than payroll taxes. A proposal introduced to stop Medicare became one of its two permanent pillars, and the voluntary half of the program became the part beneficiaries experience as a personal choice.

## The Bipartisan Margins

The story that Medicare passed on a party-line vote is wrong, and the numbers show it. The House passed H.R. 6675 on April 8, 1965, by 313 to 115. Democrats supplied 248 of the yes votes, but 65 Republicans voted yes as well, against 73 who voted no. In the Senate, the bill passed on July 9 by 68 to 21. On the conference report, the final text, the House voted 307 to 116, with Republicans splitting almost evenly, 70 in favor and 68 against. The Senate adopted the conference report 70 to 24, with 13 Republicans voting yes and 17 voting no. Nearly half of all voting House Republicans, and more than two-fifths of voting Senate Republicans, put their names on the final passage of a Democratic administration's signature program.

The pattern of the votes tells the rest of the story. On the motion to recommit, 128 of 138 House Republicans had backed the Byrnes substitute; only 10 Republicans voted with the Democratic majority to kill it. But once the substitute failed and the question became the bill itself, the Republican bloc broke in half. In the Senate, an amendment to strip Part A, the compulsory hospital insurance core, from the program also failed. The losing side of that vote drew 18 Republicans and 8 Southern Democrats, which means a majority of each party's senators voted to keep the compulsory core intact. Opposition to Medicare was real but it was never a united Republican front, and support for it was never confined to Democrats.

There was a reason Republicans were unwilling to stand shoulder to shoulder with the AMA. Some Republican leaders believed that identification with diehard AMA opposition had contributed heavily to their party's 1964 electoral disaster, and they had no interest in repeating the posture. The Byrnes alternative had given Republicans a way to be for health insurance for the aged without being for King-Anderson. When the substitute failed, many of them followed the logic of their own position and voted for the bill rather than against the only health program on offer. The final statute, in fact, drew more Republican votes than the Republican substitute had drawn Democratic ones. Medicare was enacted with substantial Republican support because the debate had never been simply government medicine versus no medicine. It had been one design of government medicine against another, and a large share of Republicans preferred the enacted design to nothing at all.

## The Landslide Question

The case for the landslide begins with numbers that need no interpretation. Lyndon Johnson won 61.1 percent of the popular vote and 486 electoral votes to Barry Goldwater's 52. Goldwater carried only Arizona and five Deep South states. In the House, Democrats gained a net 36 seats and held 295 to the Republicans' 140, the first two-thirds Democratic majority since 1936. In the Senate, Democrats held 68 seats to 32. No Medicare bill had ever reached a House floor vote before 1965; the 1964 Senate had passed hospital insurance only as a floor amendment, 49 to 44, and the conference had deadlocked. After November 1964, the votes for Medicare in the full House were no longer in doubt; contemporary participants treated passage as a foregone conclusion. Johnson had campaigned on the promise of health insurance for the aged, and he treated the result as a mandate. He pressed the legislation personally, including a recorded telephone call in which he warned Chairman Wilbur Mills that letting the bill sit idle would give the doctors time to organize, and told him, "Don't let dead cats stay on your porch." The presidency supplied the pressure, the election supplied the margin, and the margin supplied the votes. On this account, Medicare passed because the voters of 1964 demanded it and Johnson would not let Congress forget.

The case for committee arithmetic starts from the same election and reaches a different conclusion about what did the work. The election changed the composition of the House Ways and Means Committee, shifting the traditional three-to-two party ratio to the two-to-one ratio of the full House, from 15 Democrats and 10 Republicans to 17 Democrats and 8 Republicans. That change was decisive because of who the Democrats had been. Six of the fifteen committee Democrats, including Chairman Mills himself, had opposed the Forand bill and King-Anderson, leaving supporters four votes short of a majority. Mills had blocked the program for three years, not out of hostility to the aged but from a conviction that the financing was unsound and that expanded benefits would force payroll taxes beyond reasonable levels. The twenty-one-day rule was also reinstated, limiting the ability of the Rules Committee to bury the bill. When Ways and Means reported H.R. 6675 on March 29, the committee vote was a straight party-line 17 to 8. Every Democrat voted yes; every Republican voted no. The new ratio had done exactly what its designers intended. The landslide made that arithmetic possible. But arithmetic alone would have produced a narrower bill, a hospital-only King-Anderson reported by a dutiful committee.

What produced this statute instead was a choice Mills made on March 2, 1965. Rather than pose a choice between the administration's bill and Byrnes's alternative, Mills proposed combining them, and adding a third element besides: an expanded Kerr-Mills program, the very federal-state approach the AMA favored. He turned to Wilbur Cohen of the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare and asked whether such a combination was possible. Cohen, who had spent weeks preparing for a straight fight between the two bills, was stunned and at first suspected a plot to kill the administration's proposal. The result was the three-layer cake: Part A, compulsory hospital insurance financed by payroll taxes, from King-Anderson; Part B, voluntary physicians' insurance financed by premiums and general revenues, from Byrnes; and Title XIX, Medicaid, an expanded federal-state program for the medically needy, built from the AMA's own preferred mechanism. As the historian Julian Zelizer observed, the tide had turned since November, and the issue was no longer whether there would be a health-care program but which one there would be. Cohen's own verdict on Mills's move was that it was the most brilliant legislative maneuver he had seen in thirty years, because the doctors could not complain about a program that included their alternative, and the Republicans could not complain about a program built partly from their own bill.

Both accounts are true, and neither is sufficient alone. The landslide supplied what the old committee could never have given: a pro-Medicare majority on Ways and Means and a House in which the bill's passage was a certainty rather than a hope. But majorities do not write statutes. Committees do, and chairmen shape what committees report. Without the 17-to-8 ratio, Mills's merge would have died in his own committee, as King-Anderson had died before it. Without the merge, the 89th Congress would likely have enacted a narrower hospital-insurance bill, closer to what the administration asked for in January 1965. The margin was necessary. It was not sufficient to produce this statute, with its voluntary half, its federal-state third layer, and its political durability. The landslide opened the door. Mills decided what walked through it.

### Did Lyndon Johnson's landslide alone pass Medicare?

No. The 1964 landslide supplied the votes, but the decisive step was committee action: the new 17-to-8 Ways and Means ratio and Wilbur Mills's March 1965 decision to merge the Democratic hospital bill, the Republican voluntary plan, and the Kerr-Mills expansion into one statute.

## The Signing in Independence: Why Truman

Presidents usually sign landmark bills in the White House, before cameras and crowds of legislators. Johnson chose to sign the Social Security Amendments of 1965 at the Harry S. Truman Library in Independence, Missouri, with the 81-year-old former president beside him. The choice was deliberate, and Johnson explained it in the tribute he paid at the ceremony: Truman had been the first president to propose national health insurance, and Johnson, who called him the real daddy of Medicare, wanted the man who had started the fight to be present at its conclusion.

The symbolism ran deeper than courtesy. Truman had sent his health message to Congress in November 1945, almost exactly twenty years before the 1965 signing. In the intervening two decades the proposal had been defeated, narrowed, reintroduced, bottled up in committee, and finally merged with its rivals. Presenting the first two Medicare cards to Harry and Bess Truman, numbered one and two, closed that twenty-year arc in a single gesture. Johnson said the act lifted the burden of crushing medical expenses from the shoulders of some 18 million Americans over the age of 65, many with low incomes and threatened by unaffordable medical expenses. The number gave the ceremony its scale; the cards gave it its story.

The setting also served a political purpose. Signing in Independence, far from Washington, framed Medicare as a national achievement rather than a congressional one, and it associated the program with a former president whose plain-spoken Midwestern image softened the ideological edges of the debate. The AMA had spent years warning that government health insurance was the entering wedge of statism. It was harder to make that charge stick against a program whose first beneficiary was Harry Truman, receiving his card in his hometown library. Johnson understood the theater of legislation as well as its mechanics, and the signing was staged to make the program's legitimacy visible.

Truman's presence also marked a transfer of authorship. The program bore Johnson's signature and Mills's design, but its lineage ran through Truman's 1945 message, the Wagner-Murray-Dingell bills, Forand's narrowing to the aged, and Kennedy's hospital insurance campaigns. Every strand of that history was represented in the room: the president who had proposed it, the president who had passed it, and the statute that combined them all. The ceremony lasted minutes. The history it summarized had taken twenty years.

## Eleven Months to Launch: Building the Program by July 1966

The signing ceremony was the easy part. Congress had given the Social Security Administration eleven months, from the end of July 1965 to July 1, 1966, to enroll the nation's elderly, sign up its hospitals and doctors, write its regulations, and build the administrative machinery for the largest new federal program since Social Security itself. Benefits under both parts of Medicare, and under Medicaid, were to begin on July 1, 1966. No part of the program could pay a claim until the people it covered knew they were covered.

The enrollment campaign was the most visible piece. Hospital insurance was automatic for people aged 65 and over who were entitled to monthly Social Security or railroad retirement benefits, but people in that age group who were not on the benefit rolls had to apply to establish entitlement under special transitional provisions of the law, and everyone who wanted the voluntary medical insurance had to enroll and pay premiums. The administration mounted a public information campaign followed by direct contact, and where necessary recontact, with virtually all of the 19 million people aged 65 or over in the country. Several mailings and personal visits told the aged of the benefits available under the two-part program. A handbook for physicians, describing the hospital and medical programs and explaining the parts of particular interest to the practicing doctor, was distributed to more than 250,000 physicians. A White House conference on the implementation of Medicare brought the message to organized medicine, followed by meetings across the country with local medical societies and smaller groups of physicians, where doctors could get answers to their questions about how the program would work.

The operational apparatus grew just as fast. Working relationships had to be established among the Social Security Administration and the other participating federal and state agencies, the providers of health care, the carriers and intermediaries that would process claims, and the insured elderly. Involved initially were the staffs of some 6,500 participating hospitals, the personnel of some 1,200 participating home health agencies, 74 Blue Cross organizations, 33 Blue Shield plans, 15 insurance companies, 1 independent health insurer, and more than 100 group practice prepayment plans. On the government side, Medicare drew in the Public Health Service, the Welfare Administration, and state health and welfare agencies. Policy and procedures had to be established, regulations developed and issued, and an operating manual written giving detailed information on procedures and policy.

By July 1, 1966, the results were in. About 18.9 million persons aged 65 or over were entitled to hospital insurance benefits under Part A, and 17.6 million had voluntarily enrolled for medical insurance under Part B at the $3 monthly premium, matched by $3 from general revenues. One year later, the numbers had risen to 19.4 million for hospital insurance and 17.9 million for medical insurance. The medical insurance enrollees amounted to 93 percent of those entitled to hospital insurance, a take-up rate that vindicated the designers' faith in voluntarism backed by a hard enrollment deadline. The rate varied by state, from 91 percent in the East South Central states to 96 percent in New England, with Alaska at 77 percent and Mississippi at 82 percent at the low end and Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, New Jersey, and Vermont at about 96 percent at the high end. For a program assembled in less than a year, the geographic spread was remarkably even.

## The Civil Rights Consequence: Desegregating Hospitals

One of Medicare's most consequential effects was never debated on the House or Senate floor. In addition to meeting the quality standards established under the health insurance legislation, hospitals, extended care facilities, and home health agencies wishing to participate in Medicare had to comply with Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The rule was simple and absolute: institutions participating in the program had to provide access to their services and facilities without regard to race, color, or national origin, make ancillary services and facilities equally available to all people, and recruit and employ staff in a nondiscriminatory manner. No discrimination, separation, or other distinction on the basis of race, color, or national origin could influence the admission, care, or treatment of patients. Any hospital that wanted Medicare money had to desegregate first.

The enforcement apparatus was built in the spring of 1966. In March, the Office of Equal Health Opportunity opened in the Surgeon General's office within the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, under Robert Nash and the direction of William Stewart. Nash hired 100 employees to staff the office and 300 people as Medicare certification officers, who went into the field to inspect hospitals. Almost 8,000 facilities had to be certified as compliant with the nondiscrimination provisions in order to be eligible for Medicare reimbursement beginning in July. The staff was small, the deadline was fixed, and the department's leadership decided not to let the hospitals off the hook the way public schools had been. There would be no "go slow" plans, no phased timetables of the sort the courts had permitted in school desegregation. Hospitals had to comply immediately in order to be accredited to participate in Medicare.

The decision was, in retrospect, one of the most important policy choices in the program's history. Medicare's money was so large, and so many hospitals depended on it, that the compliance requirement moved institutions that a decade of moral argument had not moved. In the South, where segregated wards, separate entrances, and divided facilities were still common in 1965, hospitals desegregated in the months before July 1, 1966, because the alternative was exclusion from the largest new revenue source in American health care. The historian David B. Smith documented the episode in a study of the federal government's use of Title VI and Medicare to racially integrate hospitals from 1963 through 1967, published in the American Journal of Public Health, concluding that Medicare's certification drive accomplished in months what other civil rights enforcement had taken years to attempt.

The episode belongs in the legislative history because it shows how the three-layer cake kept generating consequences its bakers had not foreseen. The hospital insurance layer was designed to pay hospital bills for the aged. Because the bills were paid with federal money, the hospitals that received them fell under federal civil rights law. A program conceived as a solution to the medical costs of old age became, almost incidentally, the most effective hospital desegregation program the country had ever seen. Wilbur Mills had not intended it. Lyndon Johnson had not campaigned on it. It happened because the statute's reach, once enacted, extended further than its authors had measured.

## The Structure Endures: What the 1965 Design Became

The three-layer cake was a legislative improvisation, assembled in a single committee markup to solve a political problem. What is remarkable, in retrospect, is how little of its structure changed afterward. The program Congress enacted in July 1965 kept its shape for decades, and the financing Mills designed proved adaptable enough to carry it.

The hospital insurance tax schedule written into the law ran only through 1987, reaching 0.80 percent on employers and employees each. Congress raised it further in later years, and the rate reached 1.45 percent in 1986, where the hospital insurance tax stood for the rest of the century. The $6,600 taxable base, which the 1965 cost estimates assumed would never rise, rose repeatedly as well: Congress lifted the hospital insurance base to $125,000 in 1991 and eliminated it entirely in 1994, so that all earnings became subject to the hospital tax. The separate trust fund Mills had insisted on survived every one of those changes. Part A remained a payroll-tax-financed, compulsory program, exactly as the 1965 committee had designed it.

Part B kept its voluntary character and its premium-plus-general-revenue financing, though the $3 monthly premium of 1966 was only a starting point. Congress extended the program's reach in 1972 to Americans under 65 with certain disabilities and to people of all ages with permanent kidney disease requiring dialysis or transplant, the first expansion of the beneficiary population beyond the aged. The deductible and coinsurance structure, 80 percent of the patient's bill above the annual deductible, carried forward as the template for the program's cost sharing.

Medicaid, the third layer, grew the most. What began as an expanded Kerr-Mills program for the medically needy became, over the following decades, one of the largest health programs in American government, covering far more people than Medicare itself. The federal-state structure Mills had borrowed from the AMA's own preferred mechanism proved extraordinarily elastic: states could expand eligibility, add benefits, and draw federal matching funds, and most of them eventually did.

The endurance of the structure is the strongest evidence for the verdict that follows. A bill produced by a landslide majority could have been repealed or restructured by the next majority. What survived was not the margin but the design: three programs, three financing streams, three constituencies, each with defenders who had a stake in its continuation. Mills had not only passed a bill. He had built a coalition into the statute itself, and the coalition held long after the 89th Congress had adjourned.

## Verdict: The Merge as the Decisive Act

Read the enacted statute as a record of the procedural moment and the shape tells the story. A purely Democratic bill would have been hospital insurance and nothing else. A purely Republican bill would have been voluntary insurance and nothing else. The AMA's preference would have been a state-administered program for the needy and nothing else. What Congress actually passed was all three at once, stitched into a single law: compulsory hospital coverage, voluntary physicians' coverage, and a federal-state program for the medically needy. That structure is the fingerprint of one committee markup in the spring of 1965, and of one man's decision to refuse the choice his committee was expected to make.

The namable claim is this: the most consequential structural decision in American health policy was a chairman's choice to combine his opponents' bills with his own. Wilbur Mills had spent years as Medicare's most effective obstacle. In March 1965 he became its most effective architect, and he did it by making the bill larger than the administration had dared to propose. It is worth noting what he did not do: he did not split the difference between the rival bills, shaving each down until both sides could tolerate the result. He added them together, making the program bigger and, paradoxically, harder to kill. He took the Byrnes benefit plan, extracted it from Byrnes's financing, and made it Part B. He took the Kerr-Mills approach the AMA had championed as a substitute for Medicare and made it Medicaid, an addition instead. Wilbur Cohen's judgment stands as the contemporary verdict: the doctors could not complain, because the bill answered their complaints about compulsion and about missing physicians' benefits, and the Republicans could not complain, because part of the bill was theirs. Mills had disarmed both oppositions by enacting pieces of their programs.

The ironies outlived the moment. The AMA's campaign of coffee parties and letter-writing, its Reagan recording warning of statism through medicine, failed to stop the law, yet the federal-state mechanism the association preferred became Medicaid, which grew into one of the largest programs in American government. The Republican alternative failed on the House floor by 45 votes, yet its voluntary principle became Part B, the part of Medicare beneficiaries choose to join. Opposition does not always lose when it loses the vote. Sometimes it gets written into the statute.

That is why the merge, more than the margin, deserves the title of decisive act. Landslides are spent quickly; the 1964 majority began eroding in the very next election. Committee decisions, once codified, structure politics for generations. Anyone who wants to understand how Medicare became the particular program it is, rather than the narrower one its supporters first proposed, should start not with the returns of November 1964 but with the executive session of March 1965, and with the chairman who looked at three rival bills and chose all of them. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Medicare pass Congress in 1965?

Medicare moved through Congress in 1965 as H.R. 6675, the Social Security Amendments of 1965. The Ways and Means Committee approved the bill on March 24 and reported it on March 29, after Chairman Wilbur Mills fused three rival proposals into one package. The House passed it on April 8 by a vote of 313 to 115. The Senate Finance Committee reported its own version on June 30. The Senate passed the bill on July 9 by 68 to 21, after rejecting several amendments that would have expanded benefits further. Because the two chambers had passed different versions, a conference committee reconciled them and completed its work on July 26. The House adopted the conference report on July 27 by 307 to 116, and the Senate adopted it on July 28 by 70 to 24. President Lyndon Johnson signed the bill into law on July 30 at the Truman Library in Independence, Missouri.

### Q: Who was Wilbur Mills and what did he do for Medicare?

Wilbur Mills was the Democratic representative from Arkansas who chaired the House Ways and Means Committee, the panel through which all tax and Social Security legislation had to pass. For years he used that position to block hospital insurance bills, keeping them bottled up in committee. After the 1964 elections enlarged the Democratic majority, Mills reversed course and decided to lead the legislation instead of stopping it. In early 1965 he worked with the Johnson administration to combine three competing proposals into a single bill: the administration's hospital insurance plan, a voluntary physician coverage plan, and an expanded state medical assistance program. He steered the resulting H.R. 6675 through committee approval, explained the nearly 300 page bill on the House floor, and dominated the conference committee that produced the final version. His conversion from obstructionist to architect was the decisive procedural turn in Medicare's passage.

### Q: What was the three-layer cake in the Medicare bill?

The three-layer cake was the label that stuck to the package the Ways and Means Committee assembled in March 1965. The first layer was Part A of the new Title XVIII: compulsory hospital insurance for Americans 65 and older, financed by an increase in the Social Security payroll tax. This was the administration's original Medicare proposal. The second layer was Part B: voluntary supplementary insurance covering physicians' services, financed half by enrollee premiums and half by general federal revenues, an idea borrowed from the Republican alternative. The third layer was a new Title XIX, Medicaid: a liberalized version of the Kerr-Mills program giving federal matching funds to states for medical care for the poor. By stacking all three into H.R. 6675, Mills gave each faction in the debate something to support and converted three competing bills into a single measure that could command a majority in both chambers.

### Q: Why did the American Medical Association oppose Medicare?

The AMA opposed Medicare because it viewed compulsory federal health insurance as a threat to the independence of medical practice. Its leaders argued that a payroll tax financed program would put the government between doctors and patients, lead to federal control over fees and standards, and open the door to what they called socialized medicine. The association also disputed the need, claiming that private insurance and the voluntary Kerr-Mills program could care for the elderly without federal compulsion. Institutionally, the AMA ran one of the largest lobbying campaigns of the era against the King-Anderson bill, financed the Eldercare alternative, and mobilized physicians through efforts such as Operation Coffeecup. Its opposition helped stall the bill from 1961 through 1964. After passage, physicians participated in the program in large numbers, and the predicted collapse of medical practice never occurred.

### Q: What was Kerr-Mills and how did it lead to Medicare?

The Kerr-Mills Act of 1960 created Medical Assistance for the Aged, a federal-state program that offered matching funds to states providing medical care to elderly people whose incomes were too high for welfare but too low to pay their medical bills. States designed their own programs and set their own eligibility rules. The results were meager and uneven: by 1965 only 40 states had programs, enrollment stood at 264,687 people, less than 2 percent of the elderly population, and a handful of states accounted for most of the spending. Medicare supporters used these figures to argue that a voluntary, means-tested, state-run approach could never solve the problem. In 1965 Mills took the Kerr-Mills framework, liberalized it, and made it the third layer of his bill as Title XIX, Medicaid, extending federal matching aid for medical care for the poor.

### Q: How long did Congress debate Medicare before 1965?

Roughly two decades. President Truman proposed national health insurance in 1945 as part of his Fair Deal and campaigned on it again in 1948, but Congress never acted. The fight then narrowed to coverage for the elderly. Through the late 1950s, bills such as the Forand bill sought hospital insurance under Social Security without success. President Kennedy made the King-Anderson bill a priority in 1961, but the Ways and Means Committee held hearings and took no action, a Senate test vote failed 52 to 48 in 1962, and Kennedy's assassination interrupted the 1963 hearings. In 1964 the Senate attached a Medicare provision to a Social Security bill by 49 to 44, but the conference committee deadlocked and the provision died. Only in 1965, after the elections changed the arithmetic in Congress, did the bill move from committee to the President's desk.

### Q: What role did the 1964 election play in Medicare's passage?

The 1964 election supplied the votes. Johnson won 61 percent of the popular vote and 486 electoral votes, and Democrats made large gains in Congress, including 42 new Northern Democrats in the House who were nearly all Medicare supporters. That influx broke the conservative coalition that had allowed committee chairmen to bury the bill. Mills, who had blocked hospital insurance for years, read the new arithmetic and chose to take charge of the legislation rather than be overrun by it. Senate Finance Chairman Harry Byrd, another longtime opponent, faced a chamber where delay was no longer viable. The results also gave Johnson a mandate claim and the political capital to make Medicare a first-order priority for 1965. Without those added seats, the committee blockades that had held since 1961 would likely have continued.

### Q: Did Republicans support the Medicare bill in 1965?

Yes, in significant numbers, although most Republicans voted against it. On the decisive House vote of April 8, 1965, 65 Republicans joined 248 Democrats to pass H.R. 6675 by 313 to 115, while 73 Republicans voted no. In the Senate's July 9 vote, 13 Republicans joined 57 Democrats in the 68 to 21 majority. Republican support was also substantive rather than merely numerical: Representative John Byrnes of Wisconsin, the ranking Republican on Ways and Means, authored the voluntary physician insurance proposal that became Part B, and 128 of the 138 House Republicans backed his substitute on the motion to recommit, which failed 236 to 191. On final passage of the conference report, the Senate voted 70 to 24. Medicare therefore passed with bipartisan votes, even though the Republican leadership and the AMA-backed alternatives stood in opposition.

### Q: What was Operation Coffeecup?

Operation Coffeecup was a 1961 AMA campaign run through the organization's Woman's Auxiliary to generate opposition to the King-Anderson bill. Doctors' wives hosted informal coffee parties, played a long-playing record titled Ronald Reagan Speaks Out Against Socialized Medicine, and then had their guests write letters to Congress against the legislation. The AMA pressed thousands of copies of the record and distributed them through state auxiliaries along with instructions and report forms that tallied attendees, record plays, and letters written. Reagan, then the former president of the Screen Actors Guild, warned that the bill was a step toward government control of medicine. Organizers kept the campaign quiet to preserve the appearance of spontaneous public protest, and the AMA later boasted that the resulting letters were legion. The drive helped stall the bill in the early 1960s but could not stop it after the 1964 elections.

### Q: What alternative did John Byrnes propose?

Representative John W. Byrnes of Wisconsin, the ranking Republican on the Ways and Means Committee, offered a voluntary insurance plan for physicians' services as the Republican alternative to the administration's compulsory hospital insurance. Under his proposal, elderly people could buy coverage for doctors' bills with costs shared between enrollees and general federal revenues, modeled on the health plan for federal employees. His substitute, introduced as H.R. 7057, would have covered hospitalization, nursing home, medical, and surgical care through this voluntary system rather than through a payroll tax. Mills rejected the substitute in committee but adopted its core idea as Part B of the three-layer package. On April 8, 1965, Byrnes moved to recommit H.R. 6675 with instructions to substitute his text, and the motion failed 236 to 191, with 128 of 138 Republicans and 63 Democrats in favor.

### Q: What was the Eldercare proposal?

Eldercare was the AMA-backed alternative to Medicare, introduced by Representative Thomas Curtis of Missouri and Representative A. Sydney Herlong of Florida. It proposed a voluntary, state-administered program that extended the Kerr-Mills approach: federal matching grants would help states pay for physicians' services, surgery, prescription drugs, nursing home care, and X-ray and laboratory services for elderly people who could not afford them, with benefits scaled to financial need rather than granted universally. The AMA promoted the plan in 1964 and 1965, arguing that it offered more generous benefits than the administration's hospital-only bill at lower cost to taxpayers. Critics answered that the AMA had earlier insisted no federal action was needed at all, and that Eldercare's state-by-state design repeated the uneven record of Kerr-Mills. The proposal died in committee when Mills folded its constituency into the Medicaid layer instead.

### Q: Who introduced H.R. 6675?

Chairman Wilbur Mills. The administration's hospital insurance bill had been reintroduced in January 1965 as H.R. 1 in the House and S. 1 in the Senate, but Mills set that draft aside while his committee worked in executive session. At the March 2 meeting he proposed his own three-part draft, the committee approved it on March 24, and the finished product was reported to the House as H.R. 6675 on March 29. The bill number therefore dates the moment Mills replaced the administration's narrower draft with his expanded package. President Johnson issued a statement supporting the bill the day the committee approved it. On the floor the measure was universally called the Mills bill, and Mills personally explained its nearly 300 pages to the House before the April 8 vote.

### Q: What did Ways and Means change during its markup?

In executive sessions that began on January 27, 1965, the Ways and Means Committee transformed the administration's hospital-only draft into the three-layer bill. At Mills's urging, the committee added a voluntary supplementary medical insurance program based on Byrnes's proposal, creating what became Part B, and attached a liberalized Kerr-Mills program as a new Title XIX, Medicaid. It fixed the financing for each layer: Part A through an increase in the Social Security payroll tax, and Part B through enrollee premiums matched dollar for dollar by general revenues. The committee also folded in increases to Social Security cash benefits and other amendments to the Social Security Act. On March 24 the committee approved Mills's draft on a straight party vote of 17 to 8, and on March 29 it reported H.R. 6675 to the House with House Report 213. The markup more than doubled the bill's scope and cost, which Mills judged necessary to build a winning coalition.

### Q: What amendments did the Senate add in 1965?

The Senate Finance Committee reported its version of H.R. 6675 on June 30 by a vote of 12 to 5. The Senate version was more generous, costing roughly $900 million more than the House bill. On the floor, liberal Democrats tried to expand benefits still further, and each attempt failed: Senator Ribicoff's amendment to remove all limits on the length of hospital stays was rejected 39 to 43, Senator Miller's automatic cost-of-living increase in benefits was rejected 21 to 64, Senator Prouty's benefit increase formula was rejected 12 to 79, and Senator Curtis's income-scaled deductible was also rejected. The Senate then passed H.R. 6675 on July 9 by 68 to 21. Because the Senate version differed from the House bill in cost and detail, the measure went to a conference committee instead of going directly to the President.

### Q: What did the conference committee decide?

The conference committee reconciled the House and Senate versions of H.R. 6675 and completed its work on July 26, working through more than 500 differences between the two texts, most of them financial. Mills played a central role in the proceedings, and the conference report kept the three-part design intact: compulsory hospital insurance financed by the payroll tax, voluntary supplementary medical insurance financed by premiums and general revenues, and the new Medicaid title for the poor. Only modest Senate changes survived. The House adopted the conference report on July 27 by 307 to 116, and the Senate adopted it on July 28 by 70 to 24, sending the bill to the President. The conference thus served as the final editorial pass on the coalition Mills had assembled in the Ways and Means Committee.

### Q: Why was the bill signed at the Truman Library?

Johnson chose the Harry S. Truman Presidential Library in Independence, Missouri, to honor the former President who had proposed national health insurance in 1945 and championed it through his presidency. At the July 30, 1965 ceremony, Johnson called Truman the real daddy of Medicare. Truman, then 81 years old, was enrolled as Medicare's first beneficiary and received Medicare card number one, while Bess Truman received card number two. The setting framed Medicare as the fulfillment of a twenty-year Democratic effort rather than a new Johnson invention, and it linked the Great Society programs to Truman's Fair Deal.

### Q: What was in the Ways and Means committee report?

House Report 213, filed on March 29, 1965, accompanied H.R. 6675 to the House floor. The report explained each part of the nearly 300 page bill: hospital insurance under Social Security, the new voluntary supplementary medical insurance program, the expanded medical assistance program for the poor, and the accompanying increases in Social Security cash benefits. It set out cost estimates, described the financing mechanics for each layer, and gave the committee's rationale for combining the rival proposals into one package. Mills used the report as the script for his floor explanation on April 7 and 8, walking the House through the complicated measure provision by provision until members gave him a standing ovation. The report also recorded the straight party-line committee vote of 17 to 8, a signal that Republicans intended to fight the bill on the floor.

### Q: What happened in the 1962 Senate test vote?

On July 17, 1962, Medicare supporters tried to bypass the Ways and Means blockade by attaching a compromise measure to a welfare bill, H.R. 10606, on the Senate floor. The Anderson-Javits amendment combined the main features of the King-Anderson hospital insurance bill with a role for commercial insurance companies. The vote followed months of public campaigning, including President Kennedy's televised address on Medicare from Madison Square Garden in May and the AMA's televised reply. After intense lobbying on both sides, the Senate tabled the amendment by 52 to 48. The narrow margin encouraged supporters, since the chamber had never before come that close to passing the bill. But the House remained closed to the measure, and the defeat confirmed that without a change in the makeup of Congress, the committee blockade would hold.

### Q: How did the Senate vote on the final bill in 1965?

On July 9, 1965, the Senate passed H.R. 6675 by 68 to 21, with 11 senators not voting. The majority consisted of 55 Democrats and 13 Republicans, while the opposition consisted of 7 Democrats and 14 Republicans. The vote followed three days of floor debate in which amendments to expand the bill's benefits were rejected by both parties. After the conference committee reconciled the Senate version with the House bill, the Senate adopted the conference report on July 28 by 70 to 24. The final tallies showed a comfortable but not overwhelming bipartisan majority: most Republicans voted no, yet enough joined the Democrats to defeat every attempt to weaken the bill or to expand it beyond the committee's design.

### Q: When did Medicare coverage actually begin?

July 1, 1966. Although Johnson signed the law on July 30, 1965, Congress gave the new bureaucracy eleven months to build the program: enrolling beneficiaries, certifying hospitals and other providers, contracting with insurance companies to process claims, and setting up the premium collection system for Part B. Hospital insurance under Part A took effect for services furnished on and after July 1, 1966, and enrollment for the voluntary Part B program opened in the months before that date. About 19 million Americans 65 and older signed up in the first year. The gap between signing and start-up was a deliberate administrative runway, which is why the program recorded enrollment but paid no benefits before mid-1966.
