The plan for how to stop Rafael Leao begins with a number, and the number most defenders have been given is wrong for this opponent. Stand two metres off a carrier and jockey: that is the rule taught on coaching courses, printed in defending manuals, and drilled into full-backs from the age of ten. It is a sound rule derived from an average, and the average it is derived from does not describe the player your full-back is about to face. Against a carrier whose stride covers substantially more ground per contact than the average, two metres is not a safe distance. It is an invitation.

![How to stop Rafael Leao, the stride-adjusted jockeying distance and the touch-count window - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-96.webp)

What the plan accepts giving up is the ball. You are not going to win it cleanly in the wide areas and you should stop trying, because every attempt to win it is a commitment and every commitment is what the carrier is waiting for. What the plan refuses to give up is the acceleration lane: the strip of ground, roughly ten to fifteen metres long and angled infield, in which a long-striding carrier converts a standing duel into a running one. Once he is travelling in that lane at full stride, no defender in the world recovers the situation, and everything that happens afterwards is your goalkeeper's problem.

## The touch-count window

The framework this article builds is called the touch-count window and it is worth stating precisely before any of the geometry, because it reorganizes how a defender should think about a wide duel.

The number of times a carrier touches the ball across a given stretch of ground is the number of chances a defender gets. That is the whole idea. A defender cannot engage a carrier at an arbitrary moment; he can only engage in the fraction of a second after a touch, when the ball is away from the carrier's feet and before it returns under control. Between touches, the ball is either under close control or travelling, and in both states an engagement attempt is a lunge.

An average carrier moving ten metres with the ball takes roughly four to six touches. That gives the defender four to six engagement windows in the space of a few seconds, which is why the standard jockeying instruction works: a defender who holds his distance and stays patient will eventually get a window in which the touch is heavy, the carrier's weight is on the wrong foot, and the tackle is available.

A long-striding carrier covers the same ten metres in noticeably fewer touches, because his stride length allows a larger push per contact while still reaching the ball comfortably. Reduce the touch count and you reduce the window count proportionally. The defender who waits patiently for the moment his coaching told him to wait for will find that the moment arrives half as often, and that between those moments the carrier has covered more ground than the defender's positioning assumed.

That is why the defensive plan against this profile cannot be built on waiting. It has to be built on manufacturing extra touches, which is an active job rather than a patient one, and which is the single most important adjustment in this article.

### Why does the standard jockeying rule fail against a long-strider?

Because it was derived from an average stride and an average touch count. A carrier who covers more ground per contact reaches the defender's committed position sooner and can push the ball further past him while still reaching it first, so the defender is beaten at a distance that would have been safe against most opponents.

## The arithmetic behind the corrected distance

The two-metre rule is not arbitrary and it is worth understanding where it comes from, because understanding it is what lets a coach adjust it rather than replace it with a different arbitrary number.

The rule solves a specific problem. A defender standing too close can be knocked past with a single push, because the carrier only needs to move the ball beyond the defender's reach and accelerate. A defender standing too far off gives the carrier free ground and a clear sight of the pass or the shot. Two metres is roughly the distance at which a defender can still reach a ball pushed past him with one stride, while being far enough back that a single touch cannot beat him.

Everything in that calculation depends on how far one touch moves the ball. The rule assumes a touch that pushes the ball about two to three metres, which is what an average carrier does when accelerating, and it assumes the defender can cover roughly that distance in one recovery stride.

Now change the input. A carrier with a longer stride pushes the ball further per touch, because he can afford to, since he will reach it in the same number of strides that a shorter-striding player needs for a shorter push. Call the push four metres rather than two and a half. The defender standing two metres off is now facing a ball that arrives four metres beyond him, which he cannot reach in one stride, while the carrier is running onto it at full extension.

The defender has not made a mistake. He has executed the instruction correctly against an opponent the instruction does not fit.

The corrected distance follows directly. If the touch is longer, the defender has to be further back, so that the ball pushed past him lands at a distance he can still contest. The practical adjustment is roughly one additional metre, taking the standing distance to around three metres rather than two, and it should be understood as an adjustment to a range rather than a new fixed number, since the right distance also depends on the defender's own recovery speed and on how much ground lies behind him.

There is a limit, and it matters. Standing further back concedes the shot and the pass, and against a carrier who can strike from distance that concession is expensive. Beyond roughly three to three and a half metres a full-back has stopped defending the duel and started defending nothing at all. The corrected distance is a metre, not five.

## The correction that matters more than the distance

Coaches will take the extra metre from the section above and stop there, and the extra metre on its own will not save them, because distance is the smaller half of the correction.

The larger half is timing, and it splits into two instructions that pull in opposite directions: set earlier and retreat later.

Setting earlier means the defender reaches his defensive position, feet balanced, weight low, body angled, before the carrier has begun to travel. Against an average carrier a full-back can arrive at his position roughly as the ball does, because the first touch will not immediately produce ground-covering speed. Against a long-strider the first touch can produce ground-covering speed, which means a defender still arriving at his position when the carrier's first touch lands is a defender who will be moving backward off balance from the beginning of the duel, and a defender moving backward off balance has already lost.

The practical version is that the full-back starts his retreat before the pass arrives, not after. He is already going backward, already at speed, already at the corrected distance, when the ball reaches the carrier's feet. That looks passive and it is the opposite of passive; it is the only way to be in position for a duel that begins at full speed.

Retreating later means something more specific and more counterintuitive. Once the duel is running, the defender's instinct is to keep retreating for as long as he feels pressure, backing off toward his own goal, waiting for a moment that the reduced touch count will not deliver often. Retreating past the point where cover has arrived surrenders ground for nothing, and ground surrendered in the wide areas becomes ground gained in the acceleration lane.

The instruction is therefore to hold the retreat and change to a lateral shuffle at the moment the covering defender is set. From that moment the full-back stops trying to stay in front of the carrier and starts trying to stay on one side of him, which is a different job with a different footwork pattern, and which forces the carrier either to take an extra touch or to attack a defender who is now supported.

Both instructions are hard to coach because they contradict what the defender feels. Setting earlier feels like retreating too soon. Holding the retreat feels like stepping into danger. The way to install them is with a covering player present in every repetition, so that the defender learns that the moment the cover is set is the moment his job changes, and learns it as a physical cue rather than a rule.

## Manufacturing extra touches

If the touch count is the window count, and the profile produces fewer touches, then the plan's central job is to raise the touch count. This is the active half of the plan and it is where most defensive coaching against ball carriers is silent.

There are four mechanisms and all four are available at any level.

The first is the angle of the retreat. A defender retreating on a straight line lets the carrier run a straight line, and straight lines take the fewest touches. A defender retreating on a diagonal that pushes the carrier toward a curve forces a curved run, and a curved run takes more touches per metre of ground covered, because each contact has to redirect the ball as well as advance it. That is a free extra window, purchased with a body angle, and it is the single cheapest thing in this article.

The second is the second body. A covering defender positioned in the carrier's line does not need to make a tackle to be useful; his presence forces a touch to move the ball around him. Every defender placed in the path of the intended run buys a touch, and the touches accumulate.

The third is surface. This sounds trivial and it is not: a carrier taking long touches is relying on the ball behaving predictably over ground, and pitches that are heavy, uneven, or long in the grass reduce the length of the touch he can safely take. A home side facing this profile has a legitimate and entirely ordinary preparation decision available to it in how the pitch is presented, and it is worth more against a long-touch carrier than against almost any other type.

The fourth is contact. A carrier who is running unimpeded takes long touches because he can. A carrier who is being physically engaged, shoulder to shoulder, has to take shorter ones to keep the ball protected. This is why the plan tolerates and encourages fair contact in the wide areas and why a full-back who defends this profile at arm's length will find the touch count stays low all afternoon.

Those four together can raise the touch count across a ten-metre carry from perhaps three to perhaps five, and that difference is the whole duel. Five windows instead of three is close to double the number of chances the defender gets, and it converts a duel he loses most of the time into one he loses slightly less often than he wins.

### How do you create more chances to engage a carrier who takes very few touches?

Force a curved run rather than a straight one, place a covering defender in the intended line so the ball has to be moved around him, and engage with fair shoulder contact so the carrier has to shorten his touches to protect the ball. Each of the three buys an extra engagement window.

Defenders who stand off a back-to-goal reception out of respect for a carrier's speed have declined the only free window they were going to be given.

## The shape and the line height

The plan runs from a mid-block with a back four, with two specific modifications that distinguish it from the standard wide-defending arrangement.

The first modification is the covering depth. Against most wide attackers the covering centre-back holds a position five to eight metres behind and inside the duel, close enough to be an immediate second challenge. Against this profile that depth is wrong, because a covering defender that close is inside the acceleration lane and will be run past along with the first defender, which turns one beaten defender into two. The correct depth is eight to twelve metres, angled so that the covering player is defending the lane rather than the ball, and arriving as a second duel rather than as an immediate support.

That is a real concession and it should be named. A deeper covering defender means the carrier gets to run at the first defender with less immediate help, which raises the chance of the first duel being lost. The trade is that losing the first duel no longer means conceding a shot, because there is a genuine second duel to come. Sides that keep the covering defender tight will win more first duels and concede more goals, which is the kind of trade that looks wrong in the moment and right on the video.

The second modification is line height. The instinct against a fast carrier is to drop, and here the instinct is more defensible than it was in the transition case earlier in this cluster, because the threat is a carry rather than a run onto a pass. Dropping deep does shorten the acceleration lane, which is the thing you most want to shorten.

The cost is the familiar one. A deeper block invites him to receive facing your goal with the whole pitch in front of him, which is his preferred starting condition, and it puts the duel closer to your own penalty area where a beaten defender is fatal rather than inconvenient. The recommendation is a medium block with a compact vertical distance, sitting the defensive line around the edge of your defensive third, which shortens the lane without surrendering the middle.

There is one shape that should be avoided against this profile, and it is the high line with a slow covering centre-back. The combination produces the exact situation the plan exists to prevent: a carrier receiving with fifty metres of open ground and a covering defender who cannot defend the lane. If your centre-backs are not quick in recovery, drop the line, and if you cannot drop the line because you need territory, change the covering assignment so that the recovering player is a midfielder with pace rather than a centre-back without it.

## The defender profile that copes

The selection question here has a clearer answer than in most articles in this cluster, because the duel imposes a specific physical demand that not many defenders meet.

The first requirement is recovery speed over fifteen to twenty-five metres, which is different from acceleration over five. A full-back defending this profile will be beaten sometimes, and the question that decides the sequence is whether he is still in it three seconds later. Explosive defenders who are quick over the first two steps and ordinary thereafter are less useful here than defenders who are unremarkable in the first two steps and genuinely fast at full extension, which is close to the opposite of the profile most sides select for in a full-back.

The second is height and reach, and it matters for a reason that is not the obvious one. It is not about aerial duels. A taller defender with a longer stride can retreat at the corrected distance while covering ground with fewer steps, which means his feet are on the floor and balanced for a larger proportion of the duel, and a defender whose feet are set is a defender who can change direction. A short-striding defender retreating at three metres against a long-striding carrier is taking many small steps and is airborne between them more often than he thinks, and the moment of the change of direction always seems to arrive while he is mid-step.

The third is contact tolerance. The plan requires shoulder-to-shoulder engagement in the wide areas as a mechanism for shortening the carrier's touches, and a defender who avoids contact will not produce it. This is a fair-contact instruction and it needs to be given as one, with the explicit boundary that the challenge from behind into a sprinting player's legs is dangerous, is not part of any plan in this series, and should not be coached.

The fourth is the willingness to be beaten. A defender who cannot accept losing a duel will lunge, and a lunge against this profile is the goal. The mental requirement is a defender who can be run past, stay calm, recover, and defend the second duel, and that quality is more valuable here than any individual duel-winning ability.

Height and pace are the visible qualities and discipline is the decisive one, which is the same finding as everywhere else in this cluster and worth restating because coaches keep selecting for the visible ones.

## The plan in sequence

Here is the plan as it unfolds, in order.

The sequence begins before the ball reaches him, in the phase where his side is circulating. The near-side full-back's job in this phase is positioning rather than pressing: he holds a starting position that is deeper than his instinct wants, roughly level with the near-side centre-back rather than ahead of him, because the setting-earlier instruction requires him to already be retreating when the ball arrives and a full-back who starts high cannot do that.

The near-side central midfielder shades the entry lane rather than pressing the passer, for the same reason it appears in every wide-defending plan in this series: the point is not to intercept but to force the pass to arrive slightly under-hit or slightly behind, which produces a controlling touch instead of a running one. Against this profile that single delay is worth more than usual, because a carrier who has to take a settling touch has given away one of the very few touches the duel contains.

Phase two is the reception. If the reception is facing his own goal, the full-back is tight, at a metre or a metre and a half, from behind the shoulder, contesting the turn. This is the highest-value moment in the entire plan and it should be treated as such in the meeting.

If the reception is facing forward, the full-back is already retreating at the corrected distance, body angled to force a curve, feet set. He does not engage. He does not reach. He is buying the covering defender time to reach the correct depth.

Phase three is the carry, and this is where the touch-count work happens. The full-back's angle forces the curve. The covering defender is set at eight to twelve metres, inside, defending the lane. The near-side central midfielder is recovering into the space the curve is bending toward, which means he is running to a place rather than at a player.

Phase four is the engagement, and its trigger is precise: the touch taken while changing direction. Against a long-striding carrier, the straight-line touches are safe from the defender's point of view because the ball is either far ahead and unreachable or under control. The touch taken during a change of direction is different, because the ball has to be redirected rather than pushed, which means it stays closer to the feet and the carrier's weight is momentarily committed. That is the window. Everything before it is patience and everything after it is recovery.

Phase five is the outcome and it has three branches.

If the carrier is forced into the congested area near the touchline, the plan has succeeded and the double comes: the full-back holds him on the line and the near-side midfielder arrives to take the ball, because in congestion the touch count rises and the windows arrive quickly.

If the carrier cuts back inside and finds the covering defender, that is the second duel the plan was designed to produce, and the covering defender's instruction is the same as the full-back's was: distance, angle, patience, and the change-of-direction window.

If the carrier beats both, the sequence is now a shooting situation and the goalkeeper's preparation, described below, is what is left.

Phase six is the recovery, and there is a specific instruction that most sides do not give. When the carrier releases the ball, the full-back does not stop; he continues into the box to take the far-post arrival, because a wide carry that ends in a delivery frequently ends with a runner attacking the space he vacated. Full-backs who watch the pass leave and then jog back are conceding the second phase of their own successful defending.

## Outside or inside: which route to concede

Every wide-defending plan has to answer which side to show a carrier, and against this profile the answer is unusual enough to need its own treatment, because the standard reasoning gives the wrong result.

The standard reasoning says show him outside, because the touchline is an extra defender and because the outside route ends in a cross rather than a shot. That reasoning is sound against most wide attackers and it has a specific weakness here.

The weakness is that the outside route is the longest straight line available on a football pitch, and a straight line is exactly what a long-striding carrier converts most efficiently. Showing him outside on a pitch with thirty metres of touchline ahead of him is showing him into the acceleration lane and hoping the touchline arrives before he does. It frequently does not, because the touchline is not a defender when a carrier is running parallel to it rather than into it.

The inside route is shorter, more congested, and contains more of your players. It is also where the shot is, which is the reason it is normally refused.

The resolution is that the question is asked wrongly. The choice is not between outside and inside; it is between straight and curved. The plan wants a curved run in either direction, because a curve costs touches, and it wants the curve to end in a populated area. Where that populated area is depends on your shape and on the phase of the match, not on a universal preference.

The practical rule that follows is a positional one. When the duel starts high, near the halfway line, show him outside, because the ground ahead of him on the outside eventually runs out and your covering players have time to arrive. When the duel starts in your defensive third, show him inside, because outside now means the byline and the cutback, and inside means a congested penalty area with your bodies in it.

That reverses the usual instruction in the zone where it matters most, and it should be given as a zone rule rather than a general preference, because a defender given the general instruction will apply it in both zones and will be conceding the byline in the one that hurts.

There is a third condition worth naming: when the duel starts in the middle third with your shape intact and a covering defender already set, show him toward the covering defender regardless of which side that is. The second body is worth more than the geometry.

The general version of the inside-or-outside decision is a substantial question in its own right, and this article deliberately limits itself to the stride-specific adjustment. The comparison between what pace and what positioning actually contribute in a defensive duel, which is the deeper question underneath this whole plan, belongs to the treatment of [whether pace or positioning is what really beats defenders](/2024/02/15/pace-or-positioning-beating-defenders/).

## The transition case

A carry in transition is a different problem from a carry against a set defence, and it is the version that produces the most goals.

The difference is that in transition the covering depth cannot be preset, so the plan's central mechanism, the deep covering defender defending the lane, has to be constructed while the sequence is running.

The instruction is a priority order rather than a set of positions. The first recovering player is not the one nearest the ball; it is the one nearest the lane, meaning the player who can most quickly reach a position between the carrier and the centre of the pitch. That player does not press the carrier. He runs to the lane and holds it, and he is the equivalent of the covering defender in the settled version of the plan.

The second recovering player takes the ball, from behind and slightly inside, and his job is delay rather than a tackle from behind. This is where the safety boundary matters most, and it should be stated to the players directly: a recovering defender arriving from behind a sprinting carrier does not go through the back of him. He runs alongside, applies shoulder contact if the angles permit, and forces the carrier wider or toward the lane defender. The challenge from behind into the legs of a player at full speed is dangerous, it injures people, and no counter-plan in this series asks for it.

The third recovering player takes the far-post arrival, because a transition carry down one flank almost always has a runner attacking the opposite side of the box, and that runner is the least-tracked player in football.

The instruction to the goalkeeper in transition is to hold his position and set early rather than to advance, because a long-striding carrier approaching from wide has a genuine option to strike early from distance and a goalkeeper who is moving forward at the moment of the strike is beaten by an ordinary shot.

There is one preventive instruction that is worth more than all of the above and it belongs in the possession phase. The carry cannot start unless somebody plays the pass that releases it, and against this profile that pass is usually a first-time ball into the space outside your full-back at the moment of a turnover. The forward closest to the passer at that moment presses the passing lane rather than the ball, standing in the diagonal rather than approaching head on. That single positional habit prevents more of these carries than any defensive adjustment resolves.

## The goalkeeper's job

Three goalkeeping adjustments matter against this profile and they are all about timing rather than positioning.

The first is the early strike. A carrier who covers ground quickly can arrive at shooting distance while the goalkeeper is still adjusting, which means the goalkeeper's set has to happen earlier and further from his line than his default. An unset goalkeeper facing a struck ball from twenty-two metres is beaten by a shot he would ordinarily save comfortably, and the difference is entirely a matter of when he stopped moving.

The second is the one-on-one, which arrives differently against a long-striding carrier than against a short-striding one. A carrier taking long touches arrives at the goalkeeper with the ball further from his feet, which sounds like an advantage for the goalkeeper and creates a specific trap: the goalkeeper sees the ball a long way from the carrier's feet, reads it as a heavy touch, and commits. The touch was not heavy; it was normal for that stride, and the carrier reaches it first with the goalkeeper stranded.

The correct instruction is to judge the commitment by the carrier's stride pattern rather than by the distance from the ball, and the practical version is simpler: against a long-striding carrier, do not commit to a ball that is more than a stride ahead of him unless you are certain you arrive first. Stay big, stay set, delay, and make him make the decision.

The third is the near-post angle when the duel has been forced outside. A carrier who has been shown down the line and strikes from a narrow angle is producing the plan's intended outcome, and the goalkeeper's job is to hold the near post and force the far-post finish. The failure mode is the goalkeeper drifting toward the middle in anticipation of a cross, which is the same failure that appears against every wide attacker and which is more costly here because the strike arrives earlier than expected.

## The variance question, framed as a football problem

No discussion of this profile online lasts long before arriving at the claim that his output varies from match to match, and the claim needs handling because it has genuine planning value and because the way it is usually stated has none.

The version with no planning value treats variance as a property of the person. That version is not analysis, it is not observable, and this series does not make claims of that kind about anyone. It is also, practically speaking, useless to a coach: even if it were true, there is no instruction that follows from it, because you cannot build a defensive plan on an assumption about what an opponent will feel on a given afternoon.

The version with planning value treats variance as a product of three structural conditions, all of which a defending coach can influence.

The first condition is service pattern. A carrier whose threat depends on receiving with ground in front of him is entirely dependent on somebody delivering the ball into that situation. If his side is building slowly against a compact block, the receptions arrive with a defender already set and no ground available, and the carry that produces his highest-value actions never occurs. His involvement can look identical in both cases while the value of the involvement is completely different, which is why raw touch counts mislead here.

The second condition is opponent shape. Against a high line, the ground in front of him is available on every reception and the profile looks devastating. Against a medium block with a deep covering defender, the same reception produces a duel rather than a carry, and the same actions produce nothing. That is the plan in this article working, and when it works the result gets described as an off day, which is a description of a defensive success attributed to an attacking failure.

The third condition is role. A wide carrier asked to hold width and wait for a specific supply pattern will have long periods with no involvement by design, because the pattern is not always available. A wide carrier given freedom to drift infield and receive between the lines is involved more often and in lower-value situations. Neither is better in the abstract and they produce very different-looking performances from the same player.

Put those three together and the honest statement is this: output from this profile varies with conditions that are largely outside the carrier's control, and a defending coach's job is to create the conditions that suppress it rather than to hope for one of the quiet afternoons. The quiet afternoons are usually somebody else's plan working.

The practical translation is a warning about preparation. A coach who watches two matches in which this profile was contained and concludes that containment is straightforward has watched two matches in which somebody did the work described in this article. A coach who watches two matches in which he was decisive and concludes he cannot be contained has watched two matches against high lines with tight covering defenders. Both conclusions are about opponents, and both will be applied to the player.

### What actually predicts a quiet afternoon from this profile?

The opponent's covering depth and line height, more than anything else. A medium block with a covering defender eight to twelve metres behind the duel produces duels instead of carries, and duels are ordinary. A high line with a tight covering defender produces carries, and carries are not.

## What to concede deliberately

The concession list is short and two items on it will feel wrong to players who have not been told in advance.

Concede the ball. The plan does not attempt to win it in the wide areas outside the specific engagement window, and a full-back who ends the match with no tackles in that zone has executed correctly. This is the concession that most needs saying out loud, because a defender's self-assessment is built almost entirely on duels won and a defender who believes he is failing will start lunging.

Concede the first duel sometimes. The deeper covering position means the full-back gets beaten more often than he would with tight support, and that is a deliberate purchase: the price is more beaten first duels and the return is that being beaten no longer means conceding a shot.

Concede the cross from wide. If the plan forces a curve toward the touchline and ends with a delivery from a position you chose into a box you organized, that is the intended outcome.

Concede possession in the middle third generally, since a medium block with a compact vertical distance is not a pressing arrangement and territory is not the objective.

Refuse three things. Refuse the straight-line carry into the acceleration lane. Refuse the reception facing forward with no covering defender set. And refuse the back-to-goal turn, which is the one genuinely free window the profile offers and which no plan should give away.

## The fallbacks

Three fallbacks in order of cost, and one that should not be used.

The first is a covering-depth adjustment. If the carrier is consistently cutting inside and finding the covering defender too easily, the covering player is too tight; push him deeper by three or four metres and accept more beaten first duels. If the carrier is consistently getting past both and reaching the box, the covering player is too deep; bring him in. This is a single-variable adjustment that can be made from the touchline and it fixes most problems the plan encounters.

The second is a zone reassignment. Move the covering job from the near-side centre-back to the near-side central midfielder, who is quicker in a straight line and who does not have to abandon the defensive line to do it. This costs central compactness and preserves the back four's integrity, and it is the right adjustment when the covering centre-back is being run past rather than being positioned wrongly.

The third is the shape change to a back five with a wide centre-back covering behind the wing-back. This resolves the geometry cleanly and costs the middle third, and it should be made as a planned substitution rather than a reshuffle for the reason that applies throughout this cluster: an improvised back five has no rehearsed handovers.

The fallback that should not be used is doubling in the wide area with two defenders arriving simultaneously. Against a long-striding carrier, two defenders converging is two defenders in the same space and one straight line past both of them. The plan's whole logic is sequential rather than simultaneous: one defender delays, a second defends the lane behind, a third recovers. Sides that convert that into a double are removing the second layer to reinforce the first, which is the opposite of what the arithmetic requires.

## Measuring a carrier's touch count and stride profile without any equipment

The whole plan depends on classifying the carrier correctly, and the classification is available to anyone with a recording and a piece of paper. This is the most transferable exercise in the article, because it applies to every ball carrier your side will ever face.

The touch-count measurement is the primary one. Pick ten carries in which the player travels roughly ten metres or more with the ball at speed. For each, count the touches from the first contact to the moment he releases or is stopped. Write the ten numbers down. The average is the classification: five or more per ten metres is a short-stride, high-control carrier; three to four is average; two or fewer is the long-stride profile this plan is built for.

Two refinements make the count more reliable. Count only carries at genuine speed, because a player walking the ball forward takes many touches regardless of stride length. And count carries in open ground rather than in congestion, because congestion forces short touches on everybody and will disguise the profile you are trying to identify.

The stride measurement is secondary and useful as a cross-check. Watch the carrier's feet during a straight-line run and count the strides taken between two fixed reference points on the pitch, most easily the edge of the penalty area and the halfway line, or the two lines of a marked pitch if the camera angle allows. Compare that number against a defender running alongside him. A carrier taking noticeably fewer strides than the defenders around him over the same ground is a long-strider, and the plan applies.

The third observation is not a measurement and it is the most useful of the three: watch what happens to his touch count when a defender makes contact. Some carriers shorten their touches under contact and become harder to dispossess. Others maintain their touch length and become easier, because the contact disrupts the timing of a push that was calibrated for clean running. Which of the two he does determines how aggressively your full-back should engage, and it is visible in three or four sequences.

The fourth is the change-of-direction touch, which is the engagement window the plan is built around. Watch specifically for the moment he redirects, and note how close the ball stays to his feet during it. A carrier whose redirection touch is tight has a narrow window and your defender needs to be patient. A carrier whose redirection touch drifts has a wide one and your defender should be ready to take it every time.

Those four observations take one viewing and they produce a defensive plan that is specific to the opponent rather than generic. Keeping them somewhere they survive the week is the part that usually fails, since notes made on a Tuesday are rarely findable on a Friday, and a coach who wants the duel profile, the covering depth, and the engagement trigger held together in one place can [build the opposition plan free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/football-match-planner.html) rather than rebuilding the same analysis every time the fixture returns.

## The amateur and youth version

This is the article in the batch with the most direct transfer to ordinary football, because tall, fast, long-striding wide players exist at every level and the standard coaching instruction fails against all of them.

The single most valuable thing an amateur coach can take from this article is the corrected distance, delivered as a comparison rather than as a number. Telling a defender to stand three metres off is meaningless, because nobody measures metres during a match. Telling him to stand one full step further back than he normally would, against this specific opponent, is an instruction he can execute.

The second is the setting-earlier instruction, and at amateur level it should be given in the simplest possible form: start going backward before the ball gets to him. That sentence contains the whole idea and it is the correction that produces the biggest immediate improvement, because the most common amateur defensive failure against a fast wide player is a full-back standing still as the ball arrives and then being beaten from a standing start.

The third is the covering depth, which requires the amateur adaptation of being explained rather than assumed. Amateur centre-backs cover tight because covering tight looks like helping. Explain the arithmetic once: if you stand close and he goes past both of us, there is nobody left, and if you stand deep and he goes past me, you are a second problem for him. Players understand it immediately when it is put as a sequence rather than as a position.

The fourth is the contact instruction, with its safety boundary stated clearly. Shoulder-to-shoulder engagement is fair, it shortens the carrier's touches, and it is a legitimate part of defending. A challenge from behind into the legs of a sprinting player is not, it injures people, and it should not be part of any amateur plan regardless of what the match situation is.

The fifth applies specifically to youth football and it is a development point rather than a defending one. A coach teaching jockeying to young players should teach the distance as a variable rather than a fixed rule from the start, because the fixed rule creates a habit that becomes very hard to break later and because young players are facing an enormous range of stride lengths within a single age group. The instruction that works is to teach the principle behind the distance, which is that you stand far enough back to reach the ball if it is pushed past you, and then let the player calibrate it against each opponent. That takes longer to install and produces defenders who adjust rather than defenders who apply a number.

There is a sixth point specific to youth and it deserves care. Tall, long-striding young players are often the most physically developed in an age group and their advantage will change as their peers grow, so a young defender who cannot cope with one today may cope comfortably in two years without anything having changed in his technique. Coaches should be careful about drawing conclusions about a young defender's ability from duels against a physically advanced opponent, and should coach the adjustment rather than treating the outcome as a verdict on the player.

### What is the one adjustment an amateur side should make first?

Start the retreat before the ball arrives. Against a fast wide carrier, most amateur goals begin with a full-back standing still at the moment of the reception and being beaten from a standing start. Moving backward before the pass lands fixes more of these sequences than any other single instruction.

## What each player hears in the meeting

Distilled instructions by position, because a meeting that gives everyone everything gives nobody anything.

The near-side full-back hears four sentences, and they are the most important four in the meeting. Start deeper than you want to, level with your centre-back. Start going backward before the ball reaches him. Stand one full step further off than you normally would. Engage only when he changes direction, and never otherwise.

The near-side centre-back hears three. Cover deeper than you want to, eight to twelve metres behind the duel rather than five. Defend the lane, not the ball. When the full-back is beaten, you are the second duel, not the rescue.

The far-side centre-back hears one. Hold the central lane and do not slide toward the ball, because the runner arriving from the opposite side is unmarked in every one of these sequences.

The near-side central midfielder hears two. Shade the entry lane rather than pressing the passer. When the carry starts, run to the place the curve is bending toward, not at the carrier.

The far-side full-back hears one. Tuck to the far post immediately, because the far-post arrival is the least-tracked runner in this pattern.

The nearest forward at the moment of a turnover hears one, and it prevents more of these sequences than anything a defender does. Stand in the diagonal passing lane rather than approaching the passer head on.

The goalkeeper hears three. Set earlier and further from your line than your default, because the strike arrives sooner than you expect. In a one-on-one, do not commit to a ball that is more than one stride ahead of him, since a long touch for him is a normal touch. Hold the near post when he has been forced outside.

The whole side hears one sentence about the duel itself, which replaces the instruction they are expecting. We are not winning the ball off him in the wide areas, and nobody should try. We are making him take more touches and we are defending the second duel.

## Six ways this plan fails

A review checklist, which is more useful after a conceded goal than a narrative.

The first is the standard distance. The full-back applies the two-metre rule he has used his whole career, the first touch travels four metres, and he is beaten without having done anything wrong by the standards he was taught. Cause: the correction was given as a principle rather than as a physical instruction. Fix: give it as one full step further back, and rehearse it against a specific opponent rather than in the abstract.

The second is the late set. The full-back is still arriving at his position when the first touch lands, spends the duel moving backward off balance, and never recovers his feet. Cause: the instinct to hold a higher starting position and close the reception. Fix: the deeper starting position and the instruction to move before the pass.

The third is the tight cover. The covering centre-back sits at five metres, is inside the acceleration lane, and is run past along with the full-back, converting one beaten defender into two. Cause: covering tight feels like helping and covering deep feels like abandoning. Fix: explain the sequence rather than the position, and score it in training on whether a second duel occurred.

The fourth is the lunge. The full-back engages on a straight-line touch because the ball looks reachable, misses, and is out of the sequence. Cause: a long touch looks like a heavy touch to a defender calibrated for average stride lengths. Fix: the engagement trigger stated as a specific event, the change of direction, rather than as a judgment about whether the ball is reachable.

The fifth is the simultaneous double. Two defenders arrive at the carrier together, occupy the same space, and are beaten by one touch through the gap between them. Cause: the instinct that two defenders are better than one, which is true when they are layered and false when they are level. Fix: the explicit instruction that the second defender never arrives level with the first.

The sixth is the abandoned back-to-goal window. The full-back stands off a reception with the carrier facing his own goal, out of respect for the carrier's speed, and allows a free turn into open ground. Cause: a general instruction to stay off a fast player, applied in the one situation where it is exactly wrong. Fix: the zone rule, tight on a back-to-goal reception and deep on a facing-forward one.

The pattern is the one this series keeps finding. Five of the six are decision failures rather than ability failures, and every one of the five is fixed by a prior decision rather than by a better defender.

## Reading whether the plan is working

Four indicators are visible from the technical area inside twenty minutes.

The first is the number of times he takes a settling touch on reception. A settling touch means the entry pass arrived awkwardly, which means the shading is working, and it also means one of his very few touches has been spent on control rather than progress. Count them; three or four in a half is a plan functioning well.

The second is the shape of his runs. Straight runs mean the full-back's body angle is wrong and the touch count is staying low. Curved runs mean the angle is right and the windows are being manufactured. This is visible from fifty metres away and it is the most diagnostic single thing on the pitch.

The third is where the second duel happens. If it is happening at all, the covering depth is correct. If the carrier is either being stopped by the full-back or arriving in the penalty area with nobody in between, the covering player is in the wrong place in one direction or the other, and the adjustment is a single variable.

The fourth is your full-back's tackle count, which should be close to zero in the wide areas and which most coaches read as a problem. Tell the bench in advance that a low tackle count in that zone is the plan working, or somebody will suggest the full-back needs to be more aggressive at exactly the wrong moment.

The failure markers are equally short. Any straight-line carry of fifteen metres or more. Any sequence where the covering defender and the full-back are beaten by the same touch. Any reception with his back to goal where he turned unopposed. One of any of those in a half is normal; two is an adjustment.

## Reading the debate

Five positions recur about this profile and each contains an observation worth separating from the inference attached to it.

The position that he can be waited out gets the observation right and the instruction dangerously wrong. It is true that periods pass without high-value involvement, for the structural reasons already given: service pattern, opponent shape, and role. The inference that a defending side can therefore be passive is the most expensive mistake available, because the quiet periods are produced by the conditions rather than by the passage of time, and a side that relaxes its covering depth is removing the condition. Waiting works only while you are doing the work that makes waiting look like it is working.

The position that he is only effective in transition is half right and the half that is wrong matters for planning. Transition is where the carry is longest and the acceleration lane is most available, so the highest-value actions cluster there. Against a set defence with a high line, the same actions are available in settled play, because a high line supplies the same open ground that a transition does. The accurate statement is that he is effective wherever there is ground in front of him, and transition is simply the most reliable supplier of it. A side that prepares only for transitions and then holds a high line has prepared for the symptom.

The position that a physical defender unsettles him is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing, and the mechanism is more specific than the position states. Physicality in the sense of size and strength is not the operative quality. Contact is, and specifically contact that arrives during the carry rather than at the reception, because contact during the carry is what shortens the touch length and raises the window count. A large, strong defender who defends at arm's length produces none of that; a smaller defender willing to run alongside and apply fair shoulder contact produces all of it. The position is right about the effect and wrong about which quality causes it.

The position that he drifts out of matches is the one that most often shades into a claim about a person, and it should be returned to the football. What is observable is that involvement varies, and involvement in a wide carrier is almost entirely a function of whether his side is producing the receptions his role depends on. A wide player holding width in a side that is building slowly against a compact block will have long periods with no meaningful action, and that is a description of the team's structure and the opponent's shape rather than of the player. The defensive plan that produces those periods is the one in this article, and when it succeeds it should be credited to the plan.

The position that his numbers understate his impact is the most interesting of the five and it is largely correct, for a reason that is specific to this profile. A carrier who advances the ball thirty metres and then releases it has done the most valuable thing in the sequence and appears in no attacking statistic, because the goal and the assist attach to the two actions after his. Progressive carrying is chronically undervalued in the way most people read a player, and a profile built on it will always look less productive than it is. For a defending coach this is a warning rather than a debate: judge the threat by ground gained rather than by end product, because the ground gained is what your defensive shape has to survive.

## The wider correction: how jockeying should be taught

The specific number in this article matters less than the general point underneath it, and the general point is a correction to something that is taught as universal.

Jockeying distance is presented on coaching courses, in defending sessions, and in youth curricula as a fixed rule, usually with a number attached. The rule is derived from an average, the average is real, and the presentation of it as fixed produces defenders who apply it against opponents it does not fit and who are then criticized for a failure that belongs to the instruction.

The correct teaching is the principle rather than the number. Stand far enough back that a ball pushed past you is a ball you can still reach in one recovery stride. That formulation contains the number for an average opponent and it automatically produces the corrected distance for a long-strider, because a defender who has been taught the principle will observe that this opponent's pushes travel further and will adjust without being told.

The reason the principle is not taught in that form is that it is harder to coach, harder to assess, and harder to correct in a large group, whereas a number is easy on all three. That is a real practical constraint and it produces a real cost, and coaches who work with small groups or with individual defenders should teach the principle.

There is a second and broader version of the same point, which is worth stating because it applies to a great deal of defensive coaching. Almost every defensive rule of thumb, the jockeying distance, the covering depth, the line height, the pressing trigger distance, is a number derived from a population average, and almost every one of them is taught without the derivation. A defender who knows the derivation adjusts; a defender who knows only the number applies it uniformly. The single highest-return change available to most coaching environments is to teach the reason alongside the rule, and the wide duel is where the cost of not doing it is most visible, because the opponents vary most.

The detailed technique of the duel itself, the footwork patterns, the body positions, and the moment of engagement, is developed at length in [the dedicated treatment of the one-on-one duel against a wide attacker](/2021/08/15/defending-inverted-wingers-1v1/), which owns that material and which this article adjusts rather than repeats.

## The set-piece and second-phase dimension

Two dead-ball considerations attach to this plan.

The first is your own attacking corners. A side with a long-striding carrier waiting on the halfway line is a side whose counter-attack begins with thirty metres of ground gained before anyone touches the ball a second time. The standard two-back arrangement is inadequate, and the reason is specific to this profile rather than general: two defenders retreating against a carrier who covers ground in few touches will not be able to delay long enough for the rest of the side to recover, because delay against this profile requires a defender to be set and a retreating defender is not.

The correct arrangement is three back with one of them starting deep rather than on the halfway line, so that a covering position already exists rather than having to be constructed while retreating. It costs an attacking body in the box, which is a genuine cost, and it is smaller than the alternative.

The second is the free kick conceded in a wide area of your own half, which is the outcome the plan's non-tackling instruction is designed to avoid. A full-back who is not lunging is a full-back who is not fouling, and that is a secondary benefit of the plan worth naming to the players, because the delivery into your box from a set position is a higher-value situation for the attacking side than the carry you would have prevented.

There is a third point about defending your own corners. A long-striding carrier left up the pitch during a defensive set piece is the outlet, and the marking scheme should account for him with a defender who does not enter the box rather than with the assumption that the clearance will find him under pressure. A player left free at the halfway line during a corner is a player with the entire pitch available.

## The centre-back's version of the same duel

The article has treated the full-back as the primary defender, which is correct for the majority of sequences and leaves out the situation that produces the most alarming ones: the long-striding carrier attacking a centre-back directly, either after cutting inside or on a central transition.

The duel changes in three ways and each requires an adjustment.

The first change is that there is no touchline. Every reason for showing a carrier outside disappears when the duel happens in a central lane, because both directions lead to goal and neither has a boundary that helps. The centre-back's forcing decision is therefore purely about population: he shows toward whichever side has more of his teammates, exactly as the no-weak-side principle described elsewhere in this cluster requires, and he does it with his body angle rather than by moving across.

The second change is that the space behind him is the goal rather than the byline. That raises the cost of being beaten enormously and it changes the correct distance in the opposite direction from the full-back's correction. A centre-back facing a long-striding carrier in a central lane should stand closer rather than further off, because the ground behind him is too valuable to trade and because a push past him at three metres is a shooting position. The correct behaviour is a tight, patient, retreating stance with no engagement at all until support arrives, accepting that the carrier will get a shot from distance and refusing to let him get closer.

That reversal is worth stating carefully because it looks like a contradiction and is not. The corrected distance for the full-back exists to preserve the possibility of contesting the ball. The tighter distance for the centre-back exists to preserve the distance to goal, which is worth more than the ball in that zone. Different objectives, different distances, same underlying reasoning.

The third change is that the covering player is now a midfielder recovering from in front rather than a defender waiting behind. That is a materially worse arrangement, because a recovering midfielder arrives late and from the wrong side, and it is the reason central carries are more dangerous than wide ones despite being defended by better duel defenders.

The instruction that follows is preventive rather than reactive: the near-side central midfielder's screening position exists specifically to prevent the carrier reaching the central lane in the first place, and his job when the carry starts wide is to make the inside route unattractive rather than to chase the ball. Sides that let a wide carrier reach the central lane have already lost the phase where the sequence was defensible.

## Installing the correction in a session

The full session design belongs elsewhere in this series and the minimum needed to make the correction real is short.

The core practice is a wide one-versus-one into a target zone, with a covering defender starting at a fixed deep position, and with one rule change that does the teaching: the defender is scored on whether a second duel occurred, not on whether the ball was won. That scoring rule produces the covering depth, the patience, and the retreat angle without any of the three having to be explained repeatedly, because a defender who lunges will lose the point even when he wins the ball.

The distance correction is installed with a physical constraint rather than an instruction. Mark a line on the ground at the corrected distance from the reception point and require the defender to be behind it when the ball arrives. Players calibrate against a visible reference in a way they never calibrate against a number, and after two sessions the reference can be removed and the distance persists.

The setting-earlier instruction is installed by delaying the pass. Have the server hold the ball an extra beat while the defender is already retreating, so that the retreat-before-the-pass habit forms as a rhythm rather than as a decision. A defender who has spent a session retreating before every pass will do it in a match without thinking about it, which is the only way it survives the seventieth minute.

The touch-count work is installed by making it explicit. Run the same practice with the carrier told to reach the target zone in as few touches as possible, and have somebody count. The defenders will immediately start doing the things that raise the count, because they can see the score, and they will discover the retreat angle and the covering body themselves, which is a far stronger installation than being told.

Run it under fatigue at least once, because every failure mode in this plan appears when the legs are tired and none of them appear in a fresh eight-minute block.

## What the plan costs you going forward

Every defensive plan is an attacking decision and this one is moderately expensive on one flank.

The near-side full-back is starting deeper, is not overlapping, and is spending his running budget on eccentric retreats rather than on forward runs. Whatever your attacking plan is, it does not include him, and a side that expects width on that flank while running this plan will get neither the width nor the containment.

The near-side centre-back is covering deeper and more laterally than usual, which reduces his ability to step out with the ball into midfield and therefore removes one of the more common modern build-up routes.

The near-side central midfielder is shading rather than pressing and recovering into wide areas, which means he is rarely the player receiving in a productive position when you win the ball.

That is three players on one side of the pitch substantially withdrawn from your attack, which is a real cost and one that has a specific and attractive compensation available.

The compensation is the same one that appears against every side built around an advanced wide attacker. A carrier who holds a high, wide starting position and does not track back is a permanent absence in his own side's defensive shape, and the space behind him is available at every turnover. Your opposite full-back is the player to exploit it, and the instruction is that he stays high while you defend rather than tucking in, accepting that your defensive numbers on that side are one short.

That is a genuine risk and it is the trade most sides should take, because the plan's numbers on the containment side are adequate by design and the extra defender would be the least useful body in the box. A clearance played long into the channel behind an advanced wide attacker, with your full-back already running, converts a defensive sequence into an attacking one in a single action and does it at the moment the opposition is least organized.

Prepare that route explicitly, tell the goalkeeper and the centre-backs where the clearance goes, and the plan stops being damage limitation and starts being a match plan.

## What the plan does not solve

An honest counter-plan states its limits, because a coach who believes a plan is complete will not prepare for the situations it leaves open.

The first thing this plan does not solve is the two-route problem. A carrier who is genuinely dangerous going outside and cutting inside cannot be forced onto a low-value option, because both options are live. Everything in this article is an attempt to make the route curved and populated rather than to make it harmless, and the honest description of a good outcome is that the carry ends in a contested delivery or a distant strike rather than that it ends in nothing. Sides expecting to remove the threat will be disappointed and will then abandon a plan that was working.

The second thing it does not solve is the early release. A carrier who advances twenty metres and then plays a simple pass into a teammate has done the damage before any duel occurs, and no jockeying distance affects that. This is the reason the article keeps returning to the pass that starts the sequence: the only complete answer to a progressive carrier is to prevent him receiving with ground in front of him, and everything after that is management. A coach whose preparation time is limited should spend it on the entry pass rather than on the duel.

The third is the second-phase arrival. The plan is built around the carry and it deliberately sends the full-back into a deep, wide defensive position, which means the space he vacated at the near post is available to a runner. That runner is assigned to the near-side central midfielder in this plan, and a midfielder recovering forty metres is a weaker answer than a defender holding a position. It is a genuine seam and sides that have watched this plan closely will attack it.

The fourth is the aerial dimension. Nothing in this article addresses what happens when a tall attacker attacks a delivery, because the plan is a duel plan, and a side that contains the carry excellently and defends crosses poorly has contained nothing. The box organization is a separate problem with a separate scheme and it has to be prepared separately.

The fifth is fatigue, which the plan is unusually sensitive to because its central mechanism is a physically expensive retreat pattern. A plan that requires a defender to perform maximal eccentric work for ninety minutes is a plan that degrades, and the degradation is not gradual: it arrives as a step change when the defender loses the ability to brake cleanly. Substitution planning is therefore part of this plan rather than an adjunct to it, and a side without a second full-back capable of executing the correction should think carefully before committing to it at all.

The sixth is the thing no plan solves, which is that the carrier gets to choose the moment. A defensive plan is a set of prepared responses and the attacker holds the initiative in every sequence. Preparation shifts the odds; it does not remove the possibility that a carrier of this quality produces something the plan had no answer to. Coaches who present a counter-plan to their players as a guarantee will lose the players' trust the first time it is beaten, and the honest framing is that the plan turns a high-probability chance into a low-probability one, repeatedly, across ninety minutes, which is what defending actually is.

There is a seventh limit that belongs in a different category, because it is about the plan's dependence on information rather than about football. Everything in this article assumes the carrier has been classified correctly, and the classification comes from a touch count taken off a recording. If the recording shows carries in congestion rather than open ground, or carries at walking pace rather than at speed, the count will be too high and the plan will be built for the wrong profile. A side that applies the corrected distance to a carrier who is not a long-strider will stand a metre too far off, concede the pass and the shot for nothing, and conclude that the correction does not work. The measurement is cheap and it is not optional, and the failure it prevents is the quiet kind that gets attributed to execution rather than to preparation. Ten carries and a piece of paper is the entire cost, and the ten minutes it takes are the highest-value ten minutes in the preparation week, because every other decision in the plan is downstream of getting the classification right.

Stating those six limits in the meeting is not a hedge. It is what stops a side abandoning a functioning plan after the first sequence that beats it, and abandoning a functioning plan after one sequence is the most common way that good preparation produces a bad result.

## Prioritising the preparation week

Most sides have three sessions and a meeting, and a plan with this many components will not install completely in that time. Ranking the components by return per minute is therefore part of the plan rather than an afterthought.

The highest return is the entry pass, and it is worth roughly half the total preparation value on its own. The carry cannot start without a release into space, and the release is prevented by two habits that take one session between them: the forward standing in the diagonal lane rather than pressing the passer head on, and the near-side midfielder shading the entry rather than chasing it. Neither is a duel skill, neither requires a specific player, and both transfer to every other fixture in the season. Start here.

The second highest is the setting-earlier instruction for the full-back, which is a single habit installed by rhythm rather than by explanation and which takes twenty minutes of a session with a delayed serve. It is the correction that converts the most obviously losing duels into contested ones, and it holds under fatigue better than anything else in the plan because it becomes automatic rather than remaining a decision.

The third is the covering depth. This needs explaining rather than drilling, because the resistance is conceptual rather than physical: covering defenders cover tight because tight looks like helping, and they change once the sequence is described to them. Ten minutes of a meeting and one practice with the scoring rule that rewards a second duel will produce it.

The fourth is the engagement trigger, which is genuinely difficult to install and which should be attempted only if the first three are secure. Recognizing a change-of-direction touch under pressure is a perceptual skill and perceptual skills take longer than a week. What can be installed in a week is the negative half of the instruction, which is not to engage on straight-line touches, and the negative half delivers most of the benefit.

The fifth is the distance correction, which is quick to install with a marked reference and which is worth less than the four items above despite being the article's headline number. That ranking is deliberate and it is worth saying plainly: the distance is the most memorable part of this plan and it is not the most valuable part. A defender who sets early and covers deep at the standard distance will do better than a defender who stands at the corrected distance and arrives late.

The sixth and last is the outside-or-inside zone rule, which is the component most likely to be applied wrongly under pressure and which should be simplified for any side that cannot rehearse it properly. The simplification is to give one instruction for the whole pitch rather than two for different zones, and the instruction to give is to show him toward your own teammates, which produces roughly the right answer in both zones without requiring a player to identify which zone he is standing in while sprinting backward.

That ranking has one implication worth stating for coaches with even less time than three sessions. If you have one conversation and no sessions, have it about the entry pass and the setting-earlier habit, and say nothing about jockeying distance at all. Those two together are most of the plan, they cost nothing, and they do not depend on a defender executing an unfamiliar technique in the seventy-fifth minute.

## The plan across ninety minutes

The load profile of this plan is unusual and it should be planned rather than discovered.

The near-side full-back is performing repeated maximal retreats followed by recovery sprints, which is the most demanding movement pattern available to a defender because it is eccentric rather than concentric: he is decelerating and absorbing load rather than driving forward. That pattern fatigues the legs faster than an equivalent volume of forward running and it produces the specific decline that hurts this plan, which is a loss of the ability to change direction cleanly at the end of a retreat.

The decline is visible before it is costly, and the marker is the depth of the retreat. A tiring full-back retreats further than instructed, because retreating further feels safer and requires less braking. The moment you see him giving up an extra five metres per duel, he is finished as an executor of this plan and the substitution should already be prepared.

The covering centre-back's load is lower and his decline is different. He starts covering tighter as the match progresses, because holding a deep covering position while a duel happens in front of you is psychologically uncomfortable and the discomfort accumulates. Watch for it specifically and correct it verbally, because it is fixable with a shout in a way that the full-back's fatigue is not.

The substitution planning has one specific recommendation. The replacement full-back should be briefed on the corrected distance before he goes on, using the same physical formulation, because a fresh defender applying the standard rule is more dangerous than a tired one applying the corrected one. Sides that substitute a full-back in this fixture and give him no instruction beyond the general shape are the sides that concede in the last twenty minutes.

The match-state profile is straightforward. Leading, the plan tightens by dropping the block five to ten metres, which shortens the acceleration lane further at the cost of territory you no longer need. Level, the plan runs as written. Trailing, the plan is compromised because your full-backs have to be higher, and the honest adjustment is to move the covering responsibility from the centre-back to a midfielder and to accept that the wide channel is now a genuine weakness rather than a managed one.

The opposition change to watch for is a change of the supplying player rather than of the carrier. The carry cannot start without the pass that releases it, so a fresh midfielder capable of that pass changes the fixture more than a fresh forward would.

## The second fixture

The plan will be studied and the responses are predictable.

The likely response to the corrected distance is to attack the space it concedes, which means more shots and passes from the position the deeper stance permits rather than more carries. That is the trade the plan made deliberately and it should be accepted rather than reversed, with one adjustment: the near-side central midfielder's shading position moves from the entry lane to the shooting lane, which costs a fraction of the entry pressure and closes the concession.

The likely response to the deep covering defender is a second runner attacking the space between the full-back and the covering player, which is the seam the depth creates. That runner is defended by the near-side central midfielder, whose recovery destination changes from the curve to the seam, and it needs naming explicitly because it is a different destination.

The likely response to the non-tackling instruction is to invite contact, carrying the ball closer to the defender to draw a foul in a dangerous area. The counter is the shoulder-first contact instruction and the explicit statement that the defender does not use his hands or trail a leg, which is the technical detail that separates a fair contact from a free kick.

The instruction for the second fixture is the same as it is across this series: change the condition, keep the concession. If you used a covering centre-back in the first fixture, use a covering midfielder in the second, which produces similar depth from a different player and defeats the rehearsed seam.

The prerequisite for any of that is a record of what you actually did, in the detail of distances, triggers, and concessions rather than the general form of "we sat off him." Plans that are not written down get re-invented, and re-invented plans revert to the standard jockeying distance within twenty minutes, because the standard is what everybody defaults to under pressure.

## The verdict

The plan against a long-striding carrier is a set of numbers and one habit, and the numbers matter less than the habit.

Stand one full step further off than the standard jockeying distance, which puts the full-back at roughly three metres rather than two, because a longer stride produces a longer push and the standard distance was derived from an average push that does not describe this opponent. Set earlier, meaning the retreat begins before the pass arrives rather than after it, so the duel does not start with the defender off balance. Hold the retreat once cover is set, switching from staying in front to staying on one side, rather than backing off indefinitely toward your own goal.

Cover deeper, at eight to twelve metres behind and inside the duel rather than the usual five, and defend the lane rather than the ball, so that a beaten first defender produces a second duel rather than a shot. Accept that this costs first duels; it buys the sequence.

Engage only on the change-of-direction touch, which is the one reliable window the profile offers, and never on a straight-line touch, where a long push looks reachable and is not.

Manufacture the touches you need rather than waiting for them: force a curve with the retreat angle, place a covering body in the intended line, and apply fair shoulder contact during the carry, which shortens his touches and raises the window count. Fair contact means shoulder to shoulder alongside him; the challenge from behind into a sprinting player's legs is dangerous, injures people, and has no place in any plan.

Show him outside when the duel starts high and inside when it starts in your defensive third, because the choice that matters is between a straight run and a curved one rather than between the two flanks, and the curve should end where your players are.

Be tight on a back-to-goal reception, at a metre or so, because the turn is the one action his stride advantage does nothing for and it is the only free window in the match.

Concede the ball, concede the first duel sometimes, and concede the cross from a position you chose. Refuse the straight-line carry into the acceleration lane, refuse the facing-forward reception with no cover set, and refuse the free turn.

If it fails, adjust the covering depth by three or four metres before changing anything else, then move the covering job to a quicker midfielder, then change shape as a planned substitution. Do not double simultaneously, which puts two defenders in one space and concedes the line between them.

The reason all of it hangs together is the claim this article has argued from the beginning. The number of touches a carrier takes across ten metres is the number of chances his defender gets, and against a profile that halves the number of touches, the plan cannot be built on patience. It has to be built on creating the windows that the carry will not supply, which is an active defensive job and one that almost nobody is taught.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Rafael Leao?

Stand one full step further off than the standard jockeying distance, start retreating before the pass arrives rather than after it, and engage only on the touch he takes while changing direction. Cover deeper than usual, eight to twelve metres behind and inside the duel rather than five, with the covering defender defending the acceleration lane rather than the ball, so that a beaten first defender produces a second duel instead of a shot. Force a curved run with your retreat angle, place a body in his intended line, and use fair shoulder contact during the carry, since all three raise the number of touches he has to take and therefore the number of chances you get. Concede the ball; refuse the straight-line carry.

### Q: What is Rafael Leao's biggest weakness?

The reception with his back to goal, which is the one situation his stride advantage does nothing for. The turn is a low, controlled action taken from a standing position, it is guaranteed to happen on every back-to-goal reception, and it is therefore the only reliable engagement window a defender is given for free. A full-back who stands off that reception out of respect for the carrier's speed has declined the single best moment in the match. The second and larger structural point is dependency on the entry pass: a progressive carrier cannot create the situation himself, so denying the reception with ground in front of him removes the threat entirely, while defending the duel only manages it.

### Q: How do you defend a winger who covers ground in very few touches?

By manufacturing the touches rather than waiting for them. The number of touches a carrier takes across ten metres is the number of engagement windows his defender gets, so a profile that halves the touch count halves the chances. Three mechanisms raise it and all are available at any level. Retreat on a diagonal that forces a curved run rather than a straight one, since each contact on a curve has to redirect the ball as well as advance it. Place a covering defender in the intended line so the ball must be moved around him. And apply fair shoulder contact during the carry, because a carrier being physically engaged has to shorten his touches to protect the ball.

### Q: What jockeying distance works against Leao?

Roughly three metres rather than the standard two, which is about one full step further back. The standard rule is derived from an average push of two to three metres, which is what an average carrier produces when accelerating, and it assumes the defender can cover that distance in one recovery stride. A longer stride permits a longer push, perhaps four metres, and a defender standing at two metres cannot reach a ball that lands four metres beyond him. Give the correction as a physical instruction rather than a number, since nobody measures metres in a match. There is a ceiling: beyond about three and a half metres the defender has stopped defending the duel and is conceding the pass and the shot for nothing.

### Q: Which full-back profile copes with Leao?

Recovery speed over fifteen to twenty-five metres matters more than acceleration over five, because the defender will be beaten sometimes and the question is whether he is still in the sequence three seconds later. Explosive defenders who are quick over two steps and ordinary thereafter are less useful than defenders who are unremarkable early and genuinely fast at full extension. Height and a longer stride help for a reason unrelated to aerial duels: a taller defender retreating at the corrected distance covers ground in fewer steps, so his feet are set for more of the duel and he can change direction. Contact tolerance matters, since the plan requires shoulder engagement. The decisive quality is the willingness to be beaten without lunging.

### Q: How do you stop Leao carrying the ball in transition?

With a priority order rather than a set of positions, since the covering depth cannot be preset. The first recovering player is not the one nearest the ball but the one nearest the lane, meaning whoever can most quickly reach a position between the carrier and the centre of the pitch; he holds that lane and does not press. The second takes the ball from behind and slightly inside, and his job is delay rather than a tackle, running alongside with fair shoulder contact rather than going through the back of a sprinting player. The third takes the far-post arrival. The preventive instruction is worth more than all three: the nearest forward at the turnover stands in the diagonal passing lane rather than pressing the passer head on.

### Q: Should you engage Leao early or delay him?

Delay, with one specific exception. Early engagement against a long-striding carrier is a lunge, because the ball is either far ahead and unreachable or under close control, and a defender who commits on a straight-line touch is beaten by the next one. The exception is the change-of-direction touch, which is the only reliable window: the ball has to be redirected rather than pushed, so it stays closer to his feet and his weight is momentarily committed. Engage there, every time, and nowhere else. The second exception, which is a different situation rather than a different timing, is the back-to-goal reception, where the defender should be tight at a metre or so and should contest the turn rather than delaying at all.

### Q: How do you defend Leao when he goes outside?

By deciding where the duel is happening before choosing which route to show. When the duel starts high, near the halfway line, showing him outside is correct, because the ground on the outside eventually runs out and your covering players have time to arrive. When the duel starts in your defensive third, showing him outside means conceding the byline and the cutback, so the inside route into a congested penalty area is the better concession. The framing that resolves it is that the real choice is between a straight run and a curved one rather than between the two flanks, because a straight line is what a long stride converts most efficiently. Show him toward your own players, wherever they happen to be.

### Q: Why is Leao described as inconsistent?

Because output from a progressive carrier varies with three structural conditions, all of which sit outside his control. Service pattern is the first: a carrier whose threat depends on receiving with ground in front of him is entirely dependent on his side producing that reception, and a slow build-up against a compact block never produces it. Opponent shape is the second: against a high line the ground is available on every reception, and against a medium block with a deep covering defender the same reception produces an ordinary duel. Role is the third, since a player holding width waits for a pattern that is not always available. When containment works, the result gets described as a quiet performance, which is a defensive success credited as an attacking failure.

### Q: How do you stop the pass that releases Leao into space?

Press the lane rather than the passer. The nearest forward or midfielder positions himself in the diagonal passing line and approaches from the side that closes it, rather than running at the passer head on, since a head-on press leaves both diagonals open and buys nothing. That single positional habit prevents more of these carries than any defensive adjustment resolves, because the carry cannot begin without the release and the release needs time and a clear sight line. The second layer is your near-side central midfielder shading the entry lane so the pass arrives under-hit or behind, forcing a settling touch. Against a carrier who takes very few touches, spending one of them on control is a meaningful defensive win.

### Q: How do goalkeepers deal with Leao one on one?

By judging the commitment on his stride pattern rather than on the distance from the ball to his feet. The trap against a long-striding carrier is specific: the goalkeeper sees the ball a long way ahead, reads it as a heavy touch, commits, and discovers the touch was normal for that stride and the carrier reaches it first. The practical rule is not to commit to any ball more than one stride ahead of him unless arriving first is certain. Stay big, stay set, delay, and force the decision. The other adjustment concerns the early strike: set sooner and further from the line than the default, because a carrier covering ground quickly arrives at shooting distance while an ordinary goalkeeper is still adjusting.

### Q: What do you accept conceding in order to contain Leao?

The ball, first of all, which needs saying out loud because a defender's self-assessment is built on duels won and one who believes he is failing will start lunging. A full-back ending the match with no tackles in the wide zone has executed correctly. Accept losing the first duel more often, since the deeper covering position is bought with exactly that. Accept the cross from a wide position you chose into a box you organized. Accept possession and territory in the middle third, because a medium block is not a pressing arrangement. Refuse three things only: the straight-line carry into the acceleration lane, the facing-forward reception with no covering defender set, and the free turn from a back-to-goal reception.

### Q: What is the fallback if the plan against Leao fails?

Adjust the covering depth before changing anything else, since it is a single variable and it fixes most problems the plan encounters. If he is cutting inside and finding the covering defender too easily, push that defender three or four metres deeper and accept more beaten first duels; if he is getting past both and reaching the box, bring him in. Second, move the covering job from the near-side centre-back to a quicker central midfielder, which preserves the back four and costs central compactness. Third, change to a back five as a planned substitution rather than a reshuffle. Do not double simultaneously: two defenders arriving level occupy the same space and are beaten by one touch through the gap between them.

### Q: How should an amateur side handle a big quick winger like Leao?

Start with one instruction, given physically rather than numerically: begin retreating before the ball reaches him. Most amateur goals in this pattern begin with a full-back standing still at the reception and being beaten from a standing start, and that single habit fixes more sequences than anything else. Add the distance correction as one full step further back than normal against this specific opponent. Explain the covering depth as a sequence rather than a position: if you stand close and he goes past us both, there is nobody left. Coach fair shoulder contact alongside him, with the explicit boundary that a challenge from behind into a sprinting player's legs is dangerous and is not part of the plan at any level.

### Q: What do analysts watch when scouting Leao?

The touch count, which anyone can measure with a recording and paper. Take ten carries of ten metres or more at genuine speed in open ground, count the touches from first contact to release, and average them: five or more is a short-stride high-control carrier, three to four is average, two or fewer is the long-stride profile this plan is built for. Cross-check it by counting his strides between two fixed pitch markings against a defender running alongside. Then watch two behavioural markers: whether his touch length shortens under contact, which tells you how aggressively to engage, and how tightly the ball stays to his feet during a change of direction, which tells you how wide the engagement window is.

### Q: Which sides have coped with Leao and what did they do?

The sides that cope share a structural signature rather than a personnel one. They hold a medium block with a compact vertical distance rather than a high line, which shortens the acceleration lane without surrendering the middle third. They cover deep and defend the lane instead of the ball, accepting more beaten first duels in exchange for a genuine second duel. They engage only on the change of direction and almost never lunge. They stay tight on back-to-goal receptions rather than standing off out of respect for pace. They press the release pass in the diagonal rather than pressing the passer. And they treat the flank as a two-phase problem, with the full-back following the sequence into the box to take the far-post arrival rather than stopping when the ball is released.
