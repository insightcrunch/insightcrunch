The communist movement in India was present at nearly every stage of the colonial period's political life, and its history cannot be told as a footnote to the national movement because the two were entangled from the start. Communists organised in the mills and on the railways while the national movement organised in the courts and the councils, and for long stretches the same towns, the same unions and sometimes the same families contained both kinds of activist. The relationship was never settled. It was cooperative in some years, competitive in others, and at one decisive point it ruptured altogether, when the communist party stood aside from, and indeed against, the largest nationalist mobilisation of the colonial era. After independence the movement took a third form, becoming an electoral force that governed particular states for long periods while never approaching power at the centre. A history of the movement therefore has to hold three stories at once: a story of organisation, a story of argument with nationalism, and a story of governments that were always regional and never national.

That triple character explains why the movement's history is so often written badly. Party accounts tend to keep the first story and soften the second. Textbook chronologies tend to keep the dates and drop the reasoning, so that decisions arrive without the arguments that produced them and read as accidents or betrayals. The method used here is the one the series applies to any political record: date the decisions, state the reasoning offered at the time before judging the outcome, and let each party to a dispute speak in its own terms before the record is weighed. Applied to this material, the method produces a single organising instrument, the turning points ledger set out later in the article, and a single organising judgement, named in the tradition's own retrospective vocabulary as the 1942 cost: the decision to support the Allied war effort and to stand against the mass nationalist campaign of that year separated the movement from the central experience of Indian nationalism at its decisive moment, and no later electoral success in any state repaired the damage at the national level. Everything else in the movement's history, its foundings, its trials, its insurgency, its splits and its coalition episodes, is best understood in relation to that judgement, either as preparation for it or as consequence of it.

## The communist movement in the colonial decades

Communism arrived in India through several doors at once, and the plurality of the arrivals matters for everything that followed. One door was exile. Indian revolutionaries who had left the country before and during the First World War gathered in Berlin, Kabul, Moscow and other capitals, where they argued about the means of freeing India and encountered the organised Marxism of the Communist International. A second door was the printing press at home. In Bombay, S. A. Dange published work on Gandhi and on socialism and in 1922 began a journal, The Socialist, that discussed Indian conditions in Marxist terms for an Indian readership. In Calcutta, Muzaffar Ahmad built a circle around journals that addressed workers and peasants in Bengali. In Madras, M. Singaravelu, a lawyer turned labour organiser, founded the Labour Kisan Party of Hindustan in 1923 and used May Day platforms to argue that political freedom without economic change would leave the mass of Indians unfree. In Lahore, Ghulam Hussain and his associates published Inqilab and worked among railway and press workers. A third door was the labour movement itself, which had grown rapidly in the strikes that followed the First World War and had acquired an all-India federation, the All India Trade Union Congress, in 1920. The communist groups formed where these doors opened onto the same street: among mill hands in Bombay, jute and railway workers in Bengal, and the educated unemployed of the provincial towns.

The world these groups addressed was one in which the national movement set the terms of politics. The Non-Cooperation and Khilafat movements had shown that mass action against British rule was possible, and every political current in the country had to define itself against the Indian National Congress, whether as ally, critic or rival. The communists defined themselves as the current that asked the social question inside the national one. Freedom, on their account, had to mean freedom for the worker from the mill owner and for the peasant from the landlord as well as freedom from the foreign ruler, and a national movement that postponed those questions until after independence would, they argued, deliver power to the Indian possessing classes. That argument won them hearing among workers, students and a section of the Congress rank and file, and it also guaranteed friction with the Congress leadership, for whom the communists were at once useful organisers and a standing reproach. The field within which the movement worked, the theory it brought and the wider Indian Marxist tradition it belonged to are treated in the series guide to [Marxism in India: the complete guide](/2017/12/01/marxism-in-india-complete-guide/), and the comparative setting, in which anti-colonial movements across Asia faced the same choice between national unity and social revolution, is treated in [anti-colonial Marxism case studies](/2017/05/01/anti-colonial-marxism-case-studies/). The concern here is narrower: the dated record of what the Indian movement did.

The exile strand had an older root that the founding story should acknowledge. Before there were Indian communists there were Indian revolutionaries abroad, the Ghadar movement of the Pacific coast, the Berlin committee of the war years, the provisional government at Kabul, and the men who staffed them carried into the communist movement of the 1920s a tradition of conspiracy, martyrdom and transnational organisation that predated their Marxism and coloured it permanently. Roy himself came out of that world, a teenage revolutionary of the Bengal underground before he was a theorist, and the muhajirin who reached Tashkent came out of another, the religious protest migration of 1920, so that the party proclaimed there was from its first day a coalition of the subcontinent's several rebellions wearing a single doctrine. The coalition's heterogeneity explains both the early movement's reach, since its emissaries could speak to constituencies no single tradition could have gathered, and its chronic difficulty in agreeing on what it was, since the traditions it had fused, nationalist conspiracy, religious protest, trade unionism and Marxist doctrine, never fully merged in the minds of the men who carried them. The founding dispute between Tashkent and Kanpur is, among other things, the bureaucratic form of that unmerged plurality, each date honouring a different strand of the coalition as the true source.

Two features of the early movement deserve emphasis before the chronology begins, because they recur at every turning point. The first is that the movement was never merely an import. Its exiles took direction and doctrine from the Communist International, sometimes to their cost, but its Indian groups grew out of Indian strikes, Indian journals and Indian grievances, and its strongest later bases, in Andhra, Kerala, Bengal and the Bombay mills, were built by organisers whose authority was local and earned. The second feature is that the movement's relation to Moscow was a standing theme of its politics from the beginning. Opponents made it the whole story, alleging that Indian communists were agents of a foreign centre. The record supports something more complicated: a movement genuinely internationalist in conviction, genuinely connected to an external organisation, and repeatedly driven by Indian conditions into positions its own members then had to defend or regret. The wartime episode examined below is unintelligible unless both halves of that sentence are kept in view.

## The road debate of the 1920s: what kind of revolution, in what kind of colony

The founding generation inherited an argument it never finished, about the character of the revolution India required, and the argument's terms governed choices made decades later by men who had forgotten its origin. At the Communist International's Second Congress in 1920, the colonial debate between Lenin and Roy had posed the question in its classical form: whether the communists of the colonies should support the national movement of the bourgeoisie as a stage on the road, or build an independent movement of workers and peasants against both imperialism and the native possessing classes. The congress's answer had been a deliberate ambiguity, support for national liberation combined with organisational independence, and the ambiguity was thereafter available to license either the alliance strategy or the independent one, as the movement's circumstances and the international line required. In India the argument acquired local content through the figure of the peasant. A doctrine formed on the industrial proletariat confronted a country of villages, and the Indian communists' practical work, in the kisan associations of the 1920s and the peasant movements that followed, kept forcing the question of whether the peasantry was an ally, a class in dissolution, or the revolution's main body in a country where the factories employed a fraction of the people. The international movement's colonial theses of 1928, adopted at its Sixth Congress, hardened the independent line into the sectarianism described earlier in this article, and the decade that followed, of alliance rebuilt under the united front, was the argument swinging back. The movement's whole strategic history can be written as the oscillation of this single debate, between the nation as the unit of liberation and the class as the unit of revolution, and the dated episodes of the ledger are the points at which the oscillation struck the ground: 1942, when the international pole prevailed over the national; 1948, when the class pole prevailed over the national settlement; 1951 and 1957, when the national and constitutional frame was accepted as the field of work. A reader who grasps the debate possesses the key that the chronology alone withholds, the reason the same movement, with the same sincerity, could defend each of its opposite courses as the only scientific one.

## Two foundings: Tashkent and Kanpur

The movement has two birthdays, and the dispute between them is not a pedantry but a compressed version of the argument about what the movement was. One founding took place in exile. In 1920, Indian émigrés gathered at Tashkent, in Soviet Central Asia, where a school for Indian revolutionaries operated and where recruits drawn from the religious migration movement of that year, the muhajirin who had left India in protest at British policy toward Turkey, were receiving political and military training. There, under the leadership of M. N. Roy, the most prominent Indian in the Communist International, a group proclaimed the formation of the Communist Party of India. Roy had arrived in Moscow that year and had debated the colonial question with Lenin at the Second Congress of the Communist International, his supplementary theses on the national and colonial questions standing alongside Lenin's own in the congress record. From Tashkent, the émigré party issued appeals, trained cadres and attempted to send organisers into India through the mountain passes of the north-west, a route that soon brought its travellers into the hands of the colonial police. The successor party that traces its descent from this act keeps its foundation observance in December of 1920 and regards the Tashkent proclamation as the moment the Indian party came into existence.

The other founding took place at home. Through the early 1920s the communist groups in Bombay, Calcutta, Madras and Lahore worked separately, in touch with one another and with the émigrés but without a common centre on Indian soil. In December 1925, at Kanpur, communists from these groups met in an open conference, organised principally by S. V. Ghate, Muzaffar Ahmad and Dange, at which a central executive was elected and the scattered groups were drawn into a single organisation bearing the name Communist Party of India. The conference was public, held in a tent beside a Congress gathering in the same city, and its organisers included men who had never left India and whose communism had been formed in Indian unions and Indian jails. The party claiming descent from the Kanpur act observes its foundation in the last days of December and regards the conference as the founding, on the ground that a party exists when its members inside the country organise themselves under a common leadership, not when its name is proclaimed abroad.

### What did the émigré group at Tashkent actually establish?

It established a proclaimed party: a name, a programme in outline, a claim to represent Indian communism in the Communist International, and a channel through which trained organisers and literature reached India. What it could not establish from Central Asia was an organisation inside the country, which is precisely the gap the rival founding claim points to.

### What happened at the Kanpur conference?

Delegates from the communist groups in the great port and industrial cities met openly, constituted a central executive committee, adopted a constitution and rules for the organisation, and gave the separate circles a single name and centre. Its defenders describe it as the moment scattered propaganda groups became one party working on Indian soil.

The dispute has a third claimant in the footnotes, and completeness requires his mention because he exposes what the two main claims share. Satya Bhakta, a Kanpur activist of heterodox views, had convened a conference of Indian communists at Kanpur a year before the 1925 meeting and had proclaimed a Communist Party of India of his own, nationalist in temper and hostile to the Communist International's authority. The organised communists treated him as an impostor and a police-adjacent nuisance, manoeuvred him out of the 1925 conference he had helped to summon, and wrote him out of the founding story; he responded with pamphlets claiming priority that historians have mined ever since for the founding period's detail. His significance for the ledger is diagnostic. Both successor parties, whatever else divides them, agree in excluding the Satya Bhakta claim, because both claims are claims of legitimacy within the international communist tradition, the one deriving authority from the International's own émigré centre and the other from the organised movement the International recognised. A founding defined by Indian initiative alone, without reference to Moscow, is the one founding neither party can use, and the exclusion tells the reader as much about what the dispute is for as either date does.

A third position exists in the literature and in the movement's own reflective writing, and it accommodates both dates by treating them as different kinds of act. On this view, Tashkent in 1920 was the proclamation of a party by its exiles, an act of intention backed by an international organisation, and Kanpur in 1925 was the organisational founding, the point at which communists inside India constituted themselves as one party with an elected leadership. Both events happened. The dispute is over which kind of act counts as a founding, and that is a question about the nature of a party rather than about the calendar. Parties that emphasise international continuity and the authority of the émigré generation tend to the earlier date. Parties that emphasise indigenous organisation and suspicion of direction from abroad tend to the later one. Since the two successor parties divided in 1964 broadly along lines that included exactly that difference of emphasis, the founding dispute is also a proxy: to ask when the party was founded is to ask, in miniature, whose party it was.

For the purposes of a dated history, the resolution matters less than the discipline of stating the dispute correctly. What can be said without taking a side is that Indian communists were organised in groups from the first years of the 1920s, that an émigré leadership claimed the party's name from 1920, that a unified organisation was constituted inside the country at the end of 1925, and that the movement thereafter possessed both a foreign connection and a domestic base, the two facts between which its whole later history oscillates. The founding period of the movement, treated on its own terms, is the subject of the series article on [the communist movement in India](/2017/12/15/communist-movement-india-history/) and its documentary record, while the narrative here proceeds to the events that made the movement a national presence: the trials through which the colonial state, intending to destroy it, introduced it to the Indian public.

## The union decade: the movement's first mass school

Between the Kanpur conference and the Meerut arrests, the movement passed through its first experience of mass leadership, and the experience set its expectations for a generation. The vehicle was the Workers' and Peasants' Party, formed in the later 1920s in Bombay, Bengal, Madras and the Punjab as a legal, open organisation through which communists, whose own party was small and harassed, could address workers and peasants in a nationalist vocabulary. The WPPs demanded complete independence at a time when the Congress still formally sought dominion status, organised unions, contested municipal politics and published papers that reached far beyond the party's circles. Their success was greatest where the unions were: in Bombay, communist organisers built the Girni Kamgar Union of textile workers into the city's dominant labour organisation, and in 1928 led a general strike of the mills that held for six months against the mill owners and the government, an action that made the union's red flag a feature of the city's landscape and its leaders, Dange among them, figures of all-India standing. In Bengal the movement organised among the jute workers of the Hooghly mills and the railwaymen; in the Punjab its kisan work began among the canal colony peasants. By the end of the decade communists and their allies had acquired commanding influence in the national trade union federation, and the older moderate leadership of the Congress-aligned unions seceded rather than accept the new majority, splitting the national labour movement for the first time.

The decade's school taught two lessons that the movement never unlearned. The first was that Indian workers would follow communist leadership in struggle on a scale the party's size could not predict, provided the leadership organised honestly around their grievances; the 1928 strike remains the movement's founding parable of mass work. The second lesson was the converse: that such leadership was hostage to the party's line, and the line was about to change in a direction that would waste the asset. The international movement's turn at the end of the 1920s to the strategy of class against class condemned the united front tactics that had built the WPPs, denounced the national reformists as the chief enemy and instructed the Indian party to break with its allies and build rival unions. The Indian communists dissolved their WPPs, split the unions they had united and entered the 1930s, the decade of the great nationalist campaigns, in a posture of sectarian defiance toward the movement that was mobilising the country. The Meerut arrests, which fell in March 1929 at the precise moment of the turn, completed the isolation by removing the leaders who might have argued against it. The sequence is the ledger's pattern in embryo, fifteen years before 1942: mass success achieved by an indigenous strategy of alliance, surrendered to a doctrinal instruction whose origins lay in the international movement's internal quarrels.

## Conspiracy trials: repression as publicity

The colonial state understood the new movement early and chose a characteristic instrument against it: the conspiracy trial. Rather than prosecute communists for specific acts of violence, of which there were few to prosecute, the government charged them with conspiring to wage war against the King-Emperor and to deprive him of the sovereignty of British India. The legal theory matched the political theory. Communism, on the prosecution's account, was not an Indian opinion but a foreign plot, hatched in Moscow and smuggled into the country by emissaries, and the courtroom was the place to exhibit the plot to the public. The first proceedings of this kind were the Peshawar conspiracy cases of the early and middle 1920s, directed against returning muhajirin who had passed through Tashkent and were intercepted on the road home. Then, in 1924, came the Cawnpore conspiracy case, in which Dange, Muzaffar Ahmad, Shaukat Usmani and Nalini Gupta were tried for their part in organising communist work inside India. The sentences were severe, and the trials were meant to be exemplary.

The instrument had a defect that its authors appreciated too late. A conspiracy trial is a stage. It assembles the accused in one place, obliges the prosecution to summarise the doctrine it wishes to condemn, admits journalists, and gives the defence the right to speak at length on the record. The accused used the stage. In statements from the dock and in the evidence they compelled the court to hear, they described the conditions of Indian workers and peasants and presented communism not as a foreign intrigue but as an answer to those conditions. The government had intended to prove that the doctrine came from abroad. What the trials proved to a newspaper-reading public, many of whose members had never encountered the word communism outside official warnings, was that the doctrine existed, that Indians held it, and that the state feared it enough to stage elaborate proceedings against a handful of journalists and union organisers. The state's own intelligence summaries conceded the mechanism even while recommending its repetition: prosecution advertised the party it prosecuted.

### Why did the colonial state prosecute communists through conspiracy trials?

Because the movement's offence was organisational and doctrinal rather than a series of discrete crimes. Conspiracy charges let the state present communism itself as the crime, link the Indian accused to an external centre, and seek long sentences without proving any single violent act. The strategy also reflected the state's reading of the movement as directed from abroad.

Repression also shaped the movement's internal character in ways that lasted. A party repeatedly driven underground learns habits of secrecy, centralised discipline and suspicion of open disagreement, and the Indian communists learned those habits young. The conspiracy cases removed experienced organisers for years at a time, which concentrated authority in whoever remained free and accustomed the membership to accepting direction without the correctives of open debate. When later turning points arrived, the 1941 reversal of line, the insurrectionary turn of 1948, the splits of the 1960s, the party's capacity to argue with itself in public was limited by habits formed under prosecution. The trials are therefore not a prelude to the movement's history but one of its causes, and the ledger at the end of this article enters them as such.

## The Meerut case and the making of a national presence

The largest of the conspiracy proceedings, and the one that fixed the movement in the national imagination, began in March 1929. Acting on a plan prepared over months, the police arrested communist and trade union leaders across India, in Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Lahore and a dozen smaller centres, and brought them to Meerut, a garrison town in the United Provinces, to stand trial together. The accused numbered in the dozens and included the cream of the movement: Dange and Muzaffar Ahmad, the trade union organisers who had built the great Bombay textile unions, and three English communists, Philip Spratt, Benjamin Bradley and Lester Hutchinson, whose presence let the prosecution embody its theory of a foreign-directed plot in three actual foreigners. The government's timing was deliberate. The arrests fell on the eve of a new wave of nationalist mobilisation and at the height of communist influence in the unions, and they were intended to decapitate the labour movement before the two forces could combine.

The trial ran for four years. Its length was partly the law's doing, since the prosecution's evidence filled volumes and the defence contested everything, and partly the accused's strategy, since they understood that every month of proceedings was a month in which the Indian press reported their arguments. A defence committee drew support well beyond the movement: nationalist lawyers and political figures, including Jawaharlal Nehru among those who assisted and publicised the defence, treated the case as a civil liberties question on which the national movement could not be silent, even where they disagreed with the accused's politics. The accused's general statement to the court, a long exposition of the communist view of Indian society, was printed and circulated as a pamphlet. When sentences were finally delivered in January 1933 they were heavy, transportation and long terms of imprisonment for most of the convicted, and on appeal to the Allahabad High Court they were substantially reduced, several of the accused being released on the strength of time already served. By the middle of the decade the Meerut prisoners were free men, and they returned to a movement that the trial had, paradoxically, made.

### How did the Meerut proceedings change the movement's public standing?

Before the arrests, communism was a word the newspaper reader met mainly in official denunciations and in the reports of Bombay strikes. The trial put named Indian communists, their biographies and arguments into the press for four years. The movement emerged with a national profile, a martyrology and a cadre of leaders known far beyond the unions.

The case also had an international resonance that the movement valued and the government regretted. The League Against Imperialism and the defence organisations of the European left took up the Meerut prisoners' cause, questions were asked in the British Parliament, and funds and counsel arrived from abroad, so that the trial intended to exhibit communism as a foreign conspiracy succeeded in exhibiting India to the world as a place where trade unionists were tried for sedition in proceedings of extraordinary length. British labour opinion, whose government's officials in India were conducting the prosecution, was embarrassed in ways that complicated the case's management and contributed, on the defence's account, to the appellate reductions. The episode taught the movement the uses of international solidarity at the very moment its doctrine was teaching it the dangers of international direction, and the two lessons, solidarity as a resource and direction as a liability, were never reconciled in its theory even as its practice continually distinguished them. It is a distinction the reader should carry forward: the movement's tragedy was never that it had foreign friends, but that at the decisive moments the friendship's price was set in a currency of strategy.

The paradox of Meerut is the first entry in the ledger's pattern: the movement repeatedly grew through episodes that were intended, or that appeared, to destroy it. But the growth came at a price that the celebration of the trial sometimes obscures. The years 1929 to 1933, during which the leadership was in the dock or in prison, were also the years of the Civil Disobedience movement, the great nationalist campaigns of the salt march and the boycotts, and the communists, leaderless and committed to a sectarian reading of the Congress as a party of the possessing classes, stood largely aside from them. The movement's international centre was then in its most uncompromising phase, the period of class against class, in which social democrats and national reformists were treated as the main enemy, and the Indian party's application of that line left it isolated from the national upsurge of the early 1930s. The isolation mattered doubly. It confirmed the Congress leadership's suspicion of the communists at the very moment when the trial had given the national movement reason to respect them, and it habituated the party to a posture, standing outside a mass movement on doctrinal grounds and explaining why the masses were mistaken, that it would resume in 1942 with far graver consequences. The ledger therefore records Meerut twice over: as the making of the movement's public presence, and as the first performance of its characteristic failure.

## Workers, peasants, and the united front years

Recovery came through a change of method. As the Meerut prisoners emerged in the middle 1930s, the international movement was abandoning the sectarian line for the policy of the united front, the alliance of communists with socialists, nationalists and other anti-fascist and anti-imperialist forces. In India the policy found its instrument in the Congress Socialist Party, founded in 1934 by a generation of younger nationalists who wanted the Congress to adopt a socialist programme, and communists entered the socialist party in numbers, working within it while retaining their own organisation. The result was a brief, fertile period of collaboration. Communists and socialists together built the All India Kisan Sabha, founded at Lucknow in 1936 under the presidency of Swami Sahajanand Saraswati, which carried the agrarian programme of rent reduction and the abolition of landlordism into the villages. The All India Students' Federation, founded the same year, recruited a generation of students who would staff every left organisation of the following decades. In the trade unions, the rival federations of the early 1930s moved back toward unity, and communist organisers regained in the mills of Bombay and the jute belt of Bengal much of the ground lost at Meerut.

The Dutt-Bradley thesis of 1936, written by Rajani Palme Dutt and Ben Bradley for the Indian party, supplied the strategy's canonical statement: the anti-imperialist struggle required a united national front in which the Congress was the central organisation, and the communists' task was to work within and alongside it, pressing it toward mass action and a social programme while building their own independent organisations of workers and peasants. Kerala's later communist strength dates from this period, when organisers such as P. Krishna Pillai built the movement in Malabar inside the socialist party's structures, village by village, among agricultural labourers and toddy tappers. Andhra's dates from it too, where communist-led peasant associations in the Krishna and Godavari deltas began the work that would culminate in the Telangana struggle. The united front years were the movement's apprenticeship in mass politics, and the bases it laid in those years explain the geography of Indian communism for the rest of the century.

### What did the ban on the party change about its methods?

The party had been declared unlawful in 1934, so its members worked through legal fronts: the socialist party, the unions, the peasant and student organisations. Illegality made the movement dependent on allied organisations for its public existence, which taught it coalition skills and simultaneously left its independent identity blurred in the eyes of the public it was trying to win.

Collaboration had limits that both sides recognised. The Congress leadership watched communist growth inside its socialist wing with mounting unease, and the socialists themselves divided over whether the communists were comrades or a disciplined fraction bent on capturing their organisation. By the end of the decade the arrangement was fraying: communists were expelled from the socialist party's executive bodies, and the approach of war raised the question that would break the movement's relations with nationalism altogether. For the historian, the united front years stand as the first demonstration of the pattern the ledger tracks. Given a strategy of alliance and mass work, the movement grew impressively and laid down durable regional bases. Given a strategy dictated by a doctrinal reversal elsewhere, it could surrender in a season much of what alliance had built. The surrender came with the war.

## The wartime reversal: from imperialist war to people's war

When the Second World War began in September 1939, the Indian party's line was the one its international movement had prepared: the war was an imperialist war, a conflict between rival groups of imperialist powers over the division of the world, in which the Indian people had no interest and from which they should extract the price of their freedom. Communists campaigned against recruitment and war production, led anti-war strikes in the industrial centres in 1940, and were answered with arrests that drove the leadership underground and silenced the party's press. The Congress, which had resigned its provincial ministries in protest at India's inclusion in the war without consent, regarded the communist position in those years as parallel to its own in effect, whatever the difference in reasoning. On 22 June 1941, Germany invaded the Soviet Union, and the premise of the line collapsed. A war in which the socialist state fought for its survival against the most aggressive form of imperialism could not, in the movement's own categories, remain a war between equivalent predators. Through the second half of 1941 the party argued the question out, and by the end of the year its central committee had adopted the new line: the war had changed its character and become a people's war against fascism: the duty of Indian communists, on the new line, was to support the Allied effort, defend the Soviet Union, and subordinate every other campaign to that end. P. C. Joshi, the party's general secretary, was the line's tireless advocate, and his name remains attached to it in the movement's memory.

### What was the people's war line?

It was the position adopted once Germany had invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, holding that the war had transformed from an imperialist conflict into a people's war against fascism. On that basis the party supported the Allied war effort, opposed strikes and campaigns that impeded it, and treated the defence of the Soviet Union as the overriding priority.

The consequences inside India followed with the logic of a trap. In July 1942 the government, valuing the communists' support for the war effort, lifted the ban that had been in force since 1934, and the party emerged into legality with its newspaper, its offices and its organisers free to work. Weeks later, in August 1942, the Congress launched the Quit India movement, demanding an immediate end to British rule, and on the arrest of its leaders the country erupted in the largest mass uprising of the colonial period: strikes, attacks on the apparatus of the Raj, parallel governments in some districts, and a repression that filled the prisons. The communist party opposed the movement. It campaigned against the uprising as an obstacle to the war effort, argued that the demand for immediate British withdrawal, at the moment of fascism's greatest advance, would open India to invasion and destroy the ally on whose survival the future of socialism depended, and called instead for the release of the Congress leaders, a national government formed by agreement, and the conversion of India into an armed camp against fascism. Its members were instructed to keep production moving and to stand aside from, and where necessary argue against, the movement that surrounded them.

### Why did the communists oppose the mass nationalist campaign of 1942?

because the party had adopted a line supporting the Allied war effort after Germany invaded the Soviet Union, reclassifying the war as a people's war against fascism. Opposing the nationalist campaign that followed cost the party lasting credibility within the independence movement.

### What changed for the party when the ban was lifted?

Legality transformed its material position. It could publish openly, hold conferences, recruit without concealment and expand its unions and peasant organisations at a pace impossible underground. The party grew several-fold during the war years, built a cultural front of writers and artists, and held its first congress in Bombay in 1943, all while the nationalist leadership sat in prison.

The party also extended the logic of its wartime position to the other great national question of the decade. Arguing that India contained several nationalities entitled to self-determination, it accepted the Muslim League's demand for Pakistan as the expression of such a right, a position set out in the writings of G. Adhikari and endorsed in the party's resolutions. To nationalist opinion, already scandalised by the party's stand on Quit India, this completed the picture of a movement that had placed itself outside the nation at the nation's supreme moment: it had opposed the independence campaign of 1942, and it had blessed the division of the country that the campaign's leaders were resisting with all their strength. The communists replied that they had supported the imprisoned leaders' release and sought a united national government, which was true, and that their position followed from the primacy of the anti-fascist war, which was also true. Neither truth repaired the damage, because the damage was not argumentative. It was experiential. A generation of nationalists remembered, in their bodies, who had been in the prisons and who had been free to organise while the prisons filled.

## The cost of the wartime reversal

The reckoning this article names the 1942 cost can be stated precisely, because its components are distinct. The first component is reputational and immediate. The Congress leadership emerged from prison in 1945 convinced that the communists had betrayed the struggle at its climax, and it acted on the conviction: in the months after the war the Congress organisation moved to expel communists from positions within it, ending the united front collaboration of the 1930s and fixing the communists' public identity, for the nationalist mainstream, as the party that had stood with the British in 1942. The second component is generational. Political loyalties in India were formed in the 1940s on a scale unmatched before or since, and the cohort that joined politics through Quit India, the naval mutiny and the final campaigns carried into independent India a settled distrust of the communist movement that no subsequent record in government ever fully dissolved. The third component is structural. A movement that aspires to lead a nation must be able to claim the nation's founding experience as its own. The communists' claim was compromised at the source. They had been present at the creation in every sense except the one that mattered most in public memory: they had not been part of the final mass struggle against the British, and their own account of why not required an argument, where their rivals required only a recollection.

Against this, the wartime years had brought the party real gains, and an honest ledger records them on the other side. Legality had multiplied its membership and its union and peasant organisations several times over. It entered the postwar period with a press, a cultural apparatus of writers, actors and filmmakers grouped in the Indian People's Theatre Association, and a cadre of full-time organisers beyond anything it had possessed in the 1930s. When the postwar upsurge came, the strikes of 1946, the Royal Indian Navy mutiny in Bombay harbour, which the communists supported when other parties counselled surrender, the Tebhaga sharecroppers' movement in Bengal and the rising in Telangana, the party was equipped to lead struggles that partially restored its militant reputation. The paradox is exact: the wartime line bought the movement its greatest organisational expansion and its greatest political isolation in the same transaction. What the expansion could not buy back was the one asset the isolation had spent, standing within the national story.

The mechanics of the estrangement deserve a paragraph, since estrangement is too often treated as a mood rather than a sequence of acts. Through 1945 the two organisations manoeuvred around the question of the communists' place in the national movement with the war's end approaching and elections in prospect. The Congress, reconstituting itself after the release of its leaders, confronted the fact that during its own imprisonment the communists had built unions, peasant associations and a press in districts where the Congress organisation had been driven underground, and that communist members sat inside Congress committees by virtue of the united front arrangements of the 1930s. In December 1945 the Congress's central bodies resolved that communists could no longer hold office in the organisation, and the provincial committees proceeded to expel them, ending the dual membership on which the previous decade's collaboration had rested. The communists protested the decision as a gift to reaction and continued to call for a united national movement against the British, but the institutional bridge was burned, and the elections of 1946 were fought by the two organisations as rivals. From that point the estrangement had a machinery of its own: separate unions competing for the same mills, separate student organisations competing for the same campuses, and a polemical literature on each side that trained its readers to regard the other as the nation's betrayer or its gaoler. When the movement's historians ask why the wartime error could not be repaired by the postwar militancy, the answer lies partly in 1942 itself and partly in this machinery, which converted a decision into an institution.

### How did the party explain its wartime position to its own members?

Through the primacy of the Soviet Union's survival. Members were told that fascism was the main enemy of every progressive cause, that a German victory would extinguish the socialist state and darken the world, and that India's freedom would be won in the settlement that followed an Allied victory rather than in a rising timed to fascism's convenience.

### What did the party itself later concede about the wartime decision?

Later party assessments, including the reviews after the splits of the 1960s, conceded that the political cost had been severe, that the party had underestimated the depth of the national upsurge and the damage of standing apart, and that its wartime conduct had burdened its relations with the national movement for decades. The anti-fascist reasoning itself was not renounced.

The interlocking of the two judgements, organisational gain and national loss, is why the episode resists the simple verdicts passed on it. It was not a blunder in the sense of a miscalculation that better information would have prevented: the party knew the nationalist campaign would be large, knew its stand would be unpopular, and chose the stand anyway on grounds that were, within its scheme of values, compelling. Nor was it a betrayal in the sense of service to the British, whose rule the party continued to oppose and whose officials continued to watch it with suspicion even while using its support. It was the subordination of a national commitment to an international one, executed openly and defended honestly, and its cost was the price of that subordination, paid over the following half century whenever the movement reached for a national role and found the memory of 1942 waiting for it.

## The famine years: the movement in the districts while the nation looked away

One wartime chapter belongs to the cost's other side, and it is usually omitted from both the prosecution and the defence of the period: the movement's work during the Bengal famine of 1943. While the province's countryside starved under a combination of harvest failure, war demand, hoarding, boat denial and administrative paralysis, the legal communist organisation was among the few political forces working in the districts at scale, running relief kitchens and food committees, campaigning against hoarders, and reporting conditions that the wartime censorship kept out of the metropolitan press. Its cultural workers carried the famine into the country's conscience; the images and plays that fixed the famine in Indian memory came substantially from the movement's own artists, working through the people's theatre and the illustrated press the party had built in its legal years. The episode does not alter the ledger's verdict on 1942, and the movement's own historians have not claimed that it should. It does complicate the moral arithmetic in a way the honest account must preserve. The same legality that freed the party to oppose the national uprising freed it to feed the starving when the national movement's leaders were in prison and its organisations suppressed, and the cadres who ran the relief kitchens of 1943 were, in many cases, the same men and women who had argued against Quit India in 1942 and would argue for Telangana in 1946. Political judgement in the movement's history is rarely a sorting of persons into the loyal and the disloyal. It is a sorting of decisions, each with its price and its purchase, and the famine work is the wartime decision's purchase at its most concrete.

The famine also shaped the movement's postwar geography. The Bengal organisation that emerged from the relief years had roots in districts it had never reached before, among peasants who associated the party not with the abstractions of its line but with the kitchens and the anti-hoarding campaigns, and the Tebhaga movement of 1946 drew on exactly that capital. In Andhra, wartime food campaigns built the district organisations that would sustain Telangana. The pattern generalises into one of the article's recurring findings: the movement's durable assets were almost always accumulated in work whose value did not depend on the correctness of the line of the moment, relief, union organisation, the defence of tenants, while its durable liabilities were accumulated in the lines themselves. Cadres understood the distinction instinctively, which is why the movement's internal arguments, even at their most doctrinal, keep returning to the mass work as the ground on which every leadership finally asks to be judged.

## The defence of the wartime line, stated at full strength

Any assessment of the episode has to begin where the brief for this article insists it begin: with the defence in its proponents' own terms, at the strength they gave it, before any verdict is returned. The defence, as Joshi and the party's wartime publications made it, ran as follows. Fascism was not one imperialism among others. It was a qualitatively distinct enemy, a regime type that annihilated the labour movement wherever it conquered, extinguished every democratic space, and practised racial extermination as policy. Its victory would not merely rearrange empires; it would close the historical future. The Soviet Union, whatever criticisms Indian communists might privately entertain, was the socialist state whose armies were bearing the main weight of the war against that enemy, and in 1941 and 1942 its survival hung in genuine doubt, with German armies deep inside its territory. In that extremity, every movement in the world owed the anti-fascist war its first allegiance. An Indian rising in 1942, whatever its justice in the abstract, would in practice weaken the one front on which fascism could be beaten, invite the collapse of the eastern war effort, and expose India itself to Japanese invasion, which the party argued would be a worse servitude than the British rule it replaced. Freedom, on this account, was not being postponed out of cowardice. It was being pursued by the only road that led to a world in which freedom was possible at all, and the proof of the argument's sincerity is that the party paid for it knowingly, accepting unpopularity in India as the price of solidarity in a global emergency.

The defence also had an Indian chapter, which its critics less often quote. The party argued that the war had transformed Indian politics as well as the world's: that the demand should be for a national government formed by the agreement of the Congress and the League, the release of the imprisoned leaders, and the arming and mobilisation of the people for the defence of the country, a programme that would convert the war effort into an instrument of Indian self-rule. It pointed to its own legal work as evidence of good faith: the unions it led kept war industries running while winning wage increases against inflation, its peasant organisations campaigned against hoarding and for food distribution in a countryside sliding toward the Bengal famine, and its cultural front carried anti-fascist argument into villages that no other party reached in those years. On the party's telling, it had not left the nation in 1942. It had defended the nation's future against a short-sighted campaign that mistook the moment of fascism's maximum danger for the moment of freedom's maximum opportunity.

The defence was also conducted personally, at the highest level, and the episode belongs to the record because it shows the line's authors attempting repair in real time. In 1944 and 1945 Joshi corresponded with Gandhi, newly released from prison, in an exchange the party published: the communist general secretary argued the people's war case to the one man whose judgement the nation trusted, sought his blessing for a united movement on the party's terms, and received in reply courtesy, disagreement and a refusal to endorse the party's wartime course. Joshi's parallel approaches to the Muslim League's leadership, in support of the party's Pakistan position, met a warmer reception and confirmed nationalist suspicions in equal measure. The correspondence is remembered less for its arguments than for its pathos: the leader of the legal, growing, confident party of 1944 explaining himself to the leader of the imprisoned movement of 1942, and discovering that the explanation, however able, could not transfer the one asset the other possessed, the moral credit of shared suffering. The party's wartime leadership understood the cost while it was being incurred, which acquits it of blindness and convicts it of the harder charge the objection presses: that it counted the cost and paid it, because the priority that demanded payment was not India's.

Stated at that strength, the defence explains why intelligent and courageous people held the line, and why the party's own later reviews, however regretful about the cost, never simply disowned the reasoning. It also locates the exact point where the defence fails to reach. The argument establishes that the choice was defensible. It does not establish that the choice was costless, or that a movement hoping to lead India could afford it. Between a defensible decision and a sustainable one there is a gap, and the movement's subsequent history is largely the story of that gap.

## The strongest objection: subordination and its price

The strongest objection to the episode is not the crude charge of treason, which the record does not support, but the structural one made by nationalist critics at the time and developed by historians of the national movement since. It runs as follows. A movement that opposed the greatest nationalist mobilisation of the colonial period, that reached that position in step with the strategic requirements of an external state, and that then presented the position as its own reasoned conclusion, demonstrated that its national commitments were subordinate: real, sincerely held, and liable to be overridden whenever the international centre's priorities demanded it. The proof, for the critics, lies in the sequence. The party's line on the war reversed within months of the German invasion of the Soviet Union, and reversed on no Indian event. The independence of India was the professed goal of every Indian political organisation; an organisation whose decisive commitments could be reordered from outside had, on the critics' account, disqualified itself from national leadership in advance, and the subsequent record, state-level competence without national ascent, is the working out of that disqualification. Historians sympathetic to the national movement, among them Bipan Chandra in his studies of the freedom struggle, treated the episode as the moment the communists forfeited the nationalist mainstream's trust, a forfeiture whose effects outlasted everyone who had taken part in the decision.

The answer available to the movement is the one already given in the defence: that the anti-fascist priority was not an external imposition but a conclusion its own members reached and defended, that the Soviet Union's survival was an Indian interest as well as a socialist one since fascism threatened every people's future, and that the party's subsequent self-criticism, its acknowledgements of political misjudgement and of the depth of the national feeling it had misread, shows a movement capable of learning. This answer is genuine as far as it goes, and it should be recorded that the party's wartime growth and postwar militancy complicate any picture of a movement simply discredited. But the objection identifies something the answer does not remove. Whether the priority was imposed or adopted, it was a priority in which India's national moment ranked second, and the Indian public of the 1940s, forming its loyalties in prison and in struggle, registered the ranking and remembered it. The concession this article makes, following the brief's instruction to concede where the evidence points, is that the objection identifies the decisive and lasting cost of the episode, and that the movement's failure to become a national force, despite decades of state-level government examined in the articles on [Kerala's communist experiment](/2018/01/01/kerala-communist-experiment/) and the [Left Front in Bengal](/2018/01/15/west-bengal-left-front-analysis/), cannot be explained without it.

## The postwar upsurge and the recovery of militancy

Between the wartime isolation and the independence settlement stands a short period, 1945 to 1947, in which the movement partially recovered the standing it had surrendered, and the recovery matters to the ledger because it shows the surrender was not the whole of the movement's character. The war's end released a subcontinent in motion: demobilised soldiers, inflation, food scarcity and a British government visibly preparing to leave. The communists, legal, organised and staffed by the cadres the wartime expansion had trained, were positioned to lead struggles that the imprisoned national leadership had missed and that the restored Congress leadership often sought to restrain. In February 1946 the ratings of the Royal Indian Navy mutinied in Bombay harbour and beyond, and the communist party was the one political organisation that supported them without qualification, calling a general strike in Bombay in their defence while Congress and League leaders negotiated the surrender that ended the mutiny. In Bengal the Tebhaga movement of 1946 and 1947 mobilised sharecroppers to claim two thirds of the harvest against the landlords' half, in a campaign the party led through the kisan sabha and which ended in repression and partial legislative concession. In the princely state of Travancore, the rising at Punnapra and Vayalar in 1946, led by communist-organised coir workers against the dewan's regime, was put down by the state forces with heavy loss of life, an episode the region remembers as martyrdom. In Telangana the struggle described below was already under way.

The upsurge years complicate the wartime verdict in a way the honest ledger must register. A movement capable of leading the Bombay strike for the mutineers and the Bengal sharecroppers' campaign was not a movement that had forgotten how to stand with mass struggle; it was a movement whose wartime subordination had been a specific decision, taken on specific grounds, whose costs it was already beginning to repay in the only currency available to it, militancy in the struggles at hand. The recovery was nonetheless incomplete, and its limits were set by the memory it was repaying. When independence came, the Congress took power as the nation's acknowledged author, and the communists, for all their strikes and martyrs, took their place as its most organised opposition. The party's furious internal argument over how to characterise the settlement, the argument that produced the insurrectionary line, is best read against this background: the leadership that denounced the freedom as false was a leadership haunted by the knowledge that the freedom's real authors had excluded it, and determined that history should not record the movement as a spectator at the founding. The determination was understandable. Its consequences were the subject of the next section.

## Partition in the districts: the movement's hardest year

The transfer of power came with partition, and partition tested the movement in the districts in ways no line could govern. The party's wartime acceptance of the Pakistan demand, argued as the self-determination of Muslim-majority regions, had been intended to outflank communal politics by conceding its national form; the concession's practical harvest, in 1947, was a subcontinent in flames and a movement whose intercommunal organisations, built over decades in the unions and mohallas of Lahore, Calcutta, Bombay and Delhi, were torn apart by the killing like every other institution that crossed the communal line. In the Punjab, where the party had real strength among Sikh and Hindu and Muslim peasants of the canal colonies, its cadres organised relief and protected neighbours where they could and were overwhelmed where they could not, and the provincial organisation itself divided along the new border, its western half passing into a Pakistan in which a separate communist party was constituted in 1948 and banned within six years. In Bengal, the party's relief work among the refugees who streamed into Calcutta across the following years built the refugee colony movement that became one of its durable bases, the squatters' colonies organised, defended against eviction and gradually regularised, a politics of shelter that bound a generation of the displaced to the movement that had housed them.

The movement's historians have been candid that 1947 found it without a politics adequate to communal violence. Its theory treated communalism as a ruling-class diversion from the real struggle, an analysis that explained the phenomenon's uses without touching its power over the people who killed and died in it, and the party's district organisations, which understood their neighbours better than the theory did, improvised a politics of protection and relief that the doctrine had not prescribed. The gap between the theory of communalism and the experience of partition runs through the movement's later history as an unclosed account: its strongholds were precisely the regions where class organisation had partially displaced communal loyalty, Kerala and the Andhra delta above all, while in the regions where the displacement failed, the movement remained a spectator of the politics that actually mobilised the majority. A full treatment of that failure belongs to the series' treatment of caste and class in Indian Marxism; its place in the dated record is here, in the year when the nation was founded in a form the movement had predicted, opposed in part, and could not prevent, and when its own wartime concession on Pakistan returned to it as the wreckage of its Punjab organisation.

## Independence and the insurrectionary turn

Independence arrived in August 1947 in the form the communists had spent a decade warning against and failing to prevent: a partitioned subcontinent, power transferred to the Congress in India and the League in Pakistan, and mass killing and displacement along the new borders. The party's assessment of the settlement divided its own ranks. Joshi's leadership read the transfer as a compromise that nonetheless opened a democratic space in which mass movements could advance, and counselled work within the new order. The opposing current, gathering strength through 1947, read the settlement as a bargain between imperialism and the Indian possessing classes that left the substance of power unchanged, a false freedom that mass struggle should now overthrow. At the party's second congress, held in Calcutta in February and March 1948, the second current won. B. T. Ranadive replaced Joshi as general secretary, and the congress adopted a line of imminent revolutionary confrontation: the new government was to be treated as an enemy regime, strikes were to be driven toward insurrection, and armed struggle on the model of the peasant war then advancing in China was held up as the road for the Indian countryside. The slogan remembered from those years, that this freedom was false, condensed the line into a phrase.

### Why was an insurrectionary line adopted after independence?

Because the leadership installed in 1948 judged the transfer of power a compromise that left imperialist influence and landlord power intact, and read the postwar strike wave and the Telangana struggle as signs the masses were ready for a revolutionary offensive. The judgement mistook a militant minority mood for a national one, as the party's own later reviews concluded.

The consequences were swift. The government of independent India, led by men who had been the party's wartime antagonists and who regarded its new line as an invitation to civil war, answered with bans, arrests and police action. The party was declared unlawful in several provinces, its offices were raided, its press suppressed, and thousands of its members passed through prison. In the countryside the armed actions the line encouraged were defeated in detail, since a peasantry willing to fight its landlords was not thereby willing or able to fight the new state's army. Within the party, resistance to the line grew from the quarter that was actually conducting an armed struggle, Andhra, whose leadership argued that the Chinese road of protracted peasant war required patient base-building rather than the urban insurrectionism the centre was preaching, while other critics charged that the leadership had mistaken the temper of the country. By 1950 the line stood discredited in practice. Ranadive was removed, a new central leadership under Ajoy Ghosh took shape, and in 1951, after consultations that included a celebrated journey by a party delegation to Moscow, the party adopted a programme and a statement of policy that withdrew the Telangana struggle, committed the organisation to the defence of the democratic space the constitution offered, and resolved to contest the approaching general elections.

The mechanics of the 1951 reversal repay attention, because they show the movement correcting itself by the only instruments it trusted, and the instruments' character explains much about its later history. The correction began in print and in prison. An editorial in the international movement's journal in January 1950, addressed formally to the parties of several countries, criticised the course of armed adventurism in terms the Indian leadership could not mistake, and the party's internal opposition, the Andhra secretariat whose alternative thesis had circulated since 1948, acquired thereby an external warrant for the argument it had been losing. Ranadive's replacement in 1950 by a secretariat associated with the Andhra leader Rajeshwar Rao began the turn; Rao's own tenure, marked by an attempt to continue the Telangana struggle under new management, satisfied neither the struggle's critics nor its defenders; and the settlement came only when a four-man delegation travelled to Moscow in early 1951 and discussed the Indian party's course with the Soviet leadership, returning with the authority to adopt the programme and statement of policy that Ajoy Ghosh, as general secretary, then carried through the party. The sequence deserves to be stated without varnish, since the movement's own historians state it so: an Indian party's strategy, adopted by its own congress in 1948, was abandoned in 1951 after armed failure at home and consultation abroad, in proportions the participants themselves disputed then and since. Sympathetic readers emphasise the failure; hostile readers emphasise the consultation. The ledger records both, and notes that the movement's capacity for self-correction, on which its survival depended, was real but was never, in this period, wholly its own.

The reversal deserves a ledger entry of its own because it displays the movement's characteristic oscillation in its purest form. Within four years the party had moved from subordinating the national struggle to an international priority, to declaring the national settlement worthless and summoning an insurrection the country did not want, to accepting the constitutional order and preparing to work it. Each position was argued sincerely and defended in print. Each was abandoned with a self-criticism that blamed the previous leadership's reading of the situation. The pattern, in which strategy swung between extremes as leaderships changed, rather than being corrected incrementally by debate, is the organisational signature that the splits of the 1960s would formalise into rival parties, each claiming to have escaped it.

## Telangana: the armed struggle and its withdrawal

The Telangana struggle was the largest armed peasant movement in the movement's history, and the brief for this article is explicit that it must not be treated as a minor episode. It arose in the Telangana region of the princely state of Hyderabad, where the Nizam's government presided over a countryside of extreme agrarian oppression: great landlords, the deshmukhs and doras, exacted forced labour, illegal levies and rents in kind from a peasantry that included large numbers of bonded and landless labourers, while the state protected the exactions and forbade political organisation. Communist organisers, many of them formed in the Andhra delta districts across the border in British India, had built village associations in the region from the early 1940s, and when, in July 1946, a landlord's men killed a village activist who had resisted an exaction, the resistance ignited. Villages rose, drove out the landlords' agents, and established committees that took over the land, cancelled debts, abolished forced labour and redistributed the surplus land of the great estates among the landless. Guerrilla squads defended the liberated villages against the Nizam's police and his irregular militia, the Razakars, and for two years a parallel authority governed a large part of the Telangana countryside.

The scale of the struggle should be conveyed as its participants and historians convey it, in kind rather than in contested numbers. At its height the movement covered a substantial region of the princely state, ran village governments in place of the landlord's writ, and redistributed land on a scale that altered the agrarian structure of the districts it held. Estimates of the villages involved, the land redistributed and the dead on all sides vary greatly between sources with different affiliations, official, party and academic, and the honest course, which this article follows, is to record that the struggle was large, that its land redistribution was real and remembered in the region for generations, and that precise figures belong to the contested-figures protocol of the series rather than to assertion here.

In September 1948 the Indian Union absorbed Hyderabad by military action, the police action that ended the Nizam's rule, and the struggle's situation transformed. The enemy was no longer a princely despotism but the army of the Indian republic, commanded by the government the movement's national leadership had just resolved to overthrow. The Andhra leadership chose to continue the armed struggle, now against the Indian state, holding that the gains of the liberated villages could only be defended by arms and that the struggle was the embryo of a wider peasant war. The decision split the movement's judgement for three years. The continuation brought severe repression: military occupation of the struggle districts, mass arrests, and a campaign that gradually broke the guerrilla squads' capacity to operate, even as the village committees' social work continued to command loyalty. The debate inside the party, between those who saw in Telangana the Indian road of the Chinese revolution and those who saw a regional struggle being sacrificed to a doctrine, was the sharpest internal argument the movement had conducted, and it was settled less by argument than by exhaustion and by the general reversal of 1951. In October of that year the struggle was formally withdrawn, its remaining squads stood down, and its cadres turned to the elections of the new republic, in which the communists and their allies in Telangana won a striking success among the very electorate that had lived through the struggle.

The village order the struggle built deserves description in its own right, because it was the movement's most complete attempt to govern and because its memory, rather than the guerrilla war's, is what the region kept. In the liberated villages the committees abolished vetti, the forced unpaid labour owed to the landlord, cancelled the peasants' debts to the moneylender and the estate, fixed wages for agricultural labour, and took over the uncultivated and surplus lands of the great houses for distribution to the landless, with the village assembly as the authority and the squad as its guard. Grain was levied for the struggle's support, disputes were settled in the committees rather than in the landlord's court or the distant thana, and schools and volunteer organisations carried the new order's literacy and its songs into hamlets the state had never served. Participants' memoirs and the scholarly literature agree on the transformation's depth for those who lived it: for a population that had known the dora's authority as a fact of nature, the experience of a power answerable to the village was the revolution itself, whatever the flag. They agree less on the order's shadows, the coercion that accompanied collection and recruitment, the fate of those the committees judged enemies, and the burden of a war economy on a poor countryside, and the honest account records the disagreement rather than resolving it, since the witnesses remain parties to the argument.

The withdrawal of 1951 therefore posed a question no other Indian movement has had to answer in the same form: what becomes of a social revolution when its own party orders it to stop? The committees dissolved or were suppressed, the distributed land was contested field by field through the following decade, and a share of the redistribution survived in fact and more of it in expectation, since the republic's own land reforms in the region were legislated under the pressure of the memory that the thing had once been done by the peasants themselves. The struggle's veterans became the Andhra movement's elder generation, and the argument between those who had ordered the withdrawal and those who had obeyed it under protest ran through the movement for decades, surfacing in the split of 1964, when the Andhra organisation's weight was thrown behind the departing left, and again in 1969, when the Maoist current claimed Telangana as its own inheritance betrayed. Few episodes in the century repay study as richly, because few compress so much of the movement's whole problem into one district's fields: a genuine social transformation, achieved by arms, ended by a decision taken in the name of a wider strategy whose wisdom the participants disputed at the time and dispute still.

### How did the Telangana struggle end?

It was withdrawn by the party's own decision in October 1951, as part of the general change of line that replaced insurrection with constitutional and electoral work. By then military repression had badly weakened the guerrilla squads, and the leadership judged that the struggle's gains could be better defended politically in the new republic than by arms against its army.

Posterity has treated Telangana more kindly than it treated the wartime line, and the contrast is instructive. The struggle is remembered in Andhra and Telangana as a heroic chapter of peasant resistance, commemorated in song, memoir and a substantial literature of which P. Sundarayya's account, written by the movement's own Andhra leader, is the best known. Its land redistribution anticipated reforms that the republic itself later legislated in diluted form, and its village committees stand in the region's memory as a brief experience of self-government. Critics, including voices within the later movement, have questioned the continuation after 1948, holding that the decision to fight the Indian state cost the peasantry dearly for gains that the withdrawal itself showed could be defended by other means, and the debate remains open in the literature. What is not debated is the struggle's weight in the movement's own scale of values: Telangana was its proof that Indian communists could lead a mass armed movement of the rural poor, and the proof was carried into every later argument about strategy, cited by the advocates of the parliamentary road as a glorious episode happily concluded and by the advocates of armed struggle, a decade and a half later, as a betrayal happily to be avenged. The fullest treatment of that second reading belongs to the article on [Naxalism and the Maoist current in India](/2018/03/15/naxalism-maoist-current-india/).

## The discovery of the vote: the first elections as a turning point

The general elections of 1951 and 1952 deserve to stand in the narrative as a turning point in themselves, and not merely as the insurrectionary line's funeral, because they taught the movement something about India that none of its texts had contained. Universal franchise, adopted by the constitution over the doubts of many of its framers, made the Indian peasant and the Indian mill hand into electors before it made them into citizens of any other effective kind, and the communist movement, which had organised both as classes, discovered that it had also, without intending it, organised them as constituencies. The party contested the elections through fronts and alliances adapted to each province, its candidates drawn from the struggle generations, Telangana's veterans in Andhra, the kisan sabha's organisers in Bengal, the union men in Bombay and Madras, and it campaigned on the programme of 1951 with the confidence of a movement that expected the electorate to ratify the struggles it had just been conducting. The results, a commanding opposition presence in the first Lok Sabha, with assembly strengths that made government in Kerala and near-government in Andhra and Bengal imaginable within the decade, astonished the movement's opponents and, on the evidence of its own documents, its leadership as well.

The discovery had two edges, and the movement spent the rest of the century exploring both. The first edge was legitimation: a party that had been banned, tried and shot at within living memory now sat in the republic's legislatures as the recognised opposition, its leaders' speeches reported, its questions answered by ministers, its right to exist conceded by the state that had lately proscribed it, and the concession was never withdrawn even at the Emergency's height, when the party in opposition was jailed but not abolished. The second edge was discipline of a new kind. Elections punished exactly the qualities the cadre form prized, doctrinal precision, the long view, the refusal of the popular mood, and rewarded exactly the qualities it suspected, local brokerage, candidate selection by winnability, the patient accumulation of caste and community arithmetic. The movement's adaptation to the vote was therefore always double: it became, in its strongholds, a formidable electoral machine, and it never ceased to regard the machine's requirements with an internal suspicion that surfaced at every congress in the ritual denunciation of parliamentarism by the very men who contested the seats. The oscillation this article has tracked in strategy, between the nation and the doctrine, reappears inside the electoral turn as a permanent tension between the party the voters met and the party the members joined, and no subsequent leadership resolved it, since resolving it in either direction would have dissolved the movement into a social democracy it despised or a sect it could not afford to be.

## The turn to elections and the government that was dismissed

The elections of 1951 and 1952, the first held under universal franchise in the new republic, gave the movement its first measurement in the currency it would use for the remainder of the century. The communist party and the fronts allied with it emerged from the insurrectionary years to become the leading opposition presence in the first Lok Sabha and a governing possibility in several states, strong in West Bengal, in Madras and Hyderabad, and above all in the Malayalam-speaking country that in 1956 became the state of Kerala. The result astonished observers who had written the movement off in 1950, and it settled the strategic argument of the insurrection years by demonstration: whatever the countryside might one day do, the Indian electorate in its first exercise had made the communists a parliamentary force, and a party that could win elections had acquired an interest in the constitutional order it had lately proposed to storm. Through the middle 1950s the party built on the opening, contesting its internal argument, the argument that would eventually split it, over how to characterise the Congress government and the space it allowed.

In March 1957 the argument acquired a government. The communists won the assembly election in Kerala and formed a ministry under E. M. S. Namboodiripad, the first time in India, and among the first times anywhere in a large polity, that a communist party had taken office through a contested election under universal franchise. The ministry's programme was deliberately constitutional and demonstrative: an agrarian relations bill to fix rents, give tenants security and impose ceilings on holdings, and an education bill to bring the state's dense network of privately managed, publicly aided schools under public regulation. Both measures struck at entrenched interests, the landlords, the Catholic Church which managed many schools, and the caste associations of the propertied, and the interests answered with a mass agitation, the so-called liberation struggle, that combined church, caste organisations and the Congress in a campaign to bring the ministry down. In July 1959 the central government dismissed the Kerala ministry and imposed president's rule under the constitutional provision for the failure of state government, on the stated ground that the administration had broken down. The dismissed ministers and their supporters held that a lawfully elected government pursuing lawful legislation had been removed because its legislation threatened the propertied, and the episode entered the country's constitutional folklore as an early and precedent-setting use of the centre's power against an opposition state government.

The ministry's twenty-eight months repay a moment's detail, because they set the template every later communist state government inherited. Namboodiripad's cabinet was small, austere and conspicuously incorruptible by the standards of the time, and it governed with a self-consciousness about precedent: its members understood that they were being watched as a demonstration, by supporters hoping the demonstration would spread and by opponents hoping it would discredit itself. The agrarian bill proposed to fix fair rents, confer ownership rights on hutment dwellers and tenants of long standing, and impose ceilings whose surplus would vest in the state for distribution. The education bill proposed to regulate the aided schools, standardise teachers' pay through the government and curb the managements' power to treat public money as private patronage. Each measure was, in form, the kind of reform that non-communist governments elsewhere enacted without drama. In Kerala each was received as the opening of a social transformation, because the interests affected, the great landlords, the church as the largest school manager, the caste associations that brokered between property and politics, believed the ministry's professions of constitutionalism to be a tactic, and said so. The liberation struggle that followed, processions, school boycotts, clashes and deaths in police firing, was the first mass movement in independent India directed at removing an elected government by pressure from the street, and its success taught lessons in both directions that the country has not finished learning.

### Why was the elected government in Kerala dismissed?

Formally, the centre held that law and order and constitutional government had broken down amid the liberation struggle's agitation. The ministry and its party held that the agitation had been organised by the interests its land and education bills threatened, precisely to manufacture the breakdown that would justify dismissal, and that the centre had obliged them.

The sequel in Kerala itself added a refinement to the lesson. Fresh elections after the dismissal returned a Congress-led coalition, and the communists went back to opposition with their vote substantially intact and their organisation hardened by the belief that they had been robbed rather than beaten. Through the 1960s the state oscillated, communist-led fronts returning to office in 1967 and repeatedly thereafter, and the dismissal's precedent entered the national argument over the centre's powers, cited by every opposition-governed state that feared the same treatment and examined by the commissions that later reviewed the constitution's working. For the movement nationally, 1959 also fixed the terms of the split argument in advance: the party's right could point to Kerala as proof that a communist government, however lawful, would be destroyed by the ruling class unless the movement broadened its alliances to the point of partnership with the Congress; the left could answer that the dismissal proved the opposite, that no alliance would protect a government that touched property, and that independence was the only protection. The episode thus armed both sides of 1964 with their favourite evidence five years before the split, and the historian can watch, in the polemics of the early 1960s, the Kerala ministry being buried under the weight of the two morals.

The Kerala episode belongs in the ledger with its meaning double-sided. For the movement, it proved two opposite things at once, and its members have quoted both ever since. It proved that the parliamentary road could deliver office, legislation and a demonstration effect no insurrection had achieved, the spectacle of communist ministers governing a state within the constitution and being removed, in the movement's telling, for governing too well in the interests of tenants and labourers. It also proved the road's ceiling: a state government held office at the centre's sufferance, and the first time a communist ministry seriously legislated against property, the constitutional machinery of the union removed it without a shot. The governing record of the movement in the states, of which the Kerala ministry was the first example and the West Bengal government the longest, is treated in the companion article on [the Kerala experiment](/2018/01/01/kerala-communist-experiment/) and in the study of [Bengal's Left Front](/2018/01/15/west-bengal-left-front-analysis/). What belongs here is the episode's place in the national argument: after 1959, no communist strategist could discuss the parliamentary road without Kerala, and no critic of that road could discuss it without the dismissal.

## The opposition decade: the movement in the states of the 1950s

Kerala was the summit of a decade in which the movement functioned, across several states, as the republic's principal opposition, and the opposition decade deserves its record because it formed the cadre that later governed and split. In Andhra, the movement fused with the campaign for a Telugu-speaking state, Visalandhra, and when Andhra state was formed in 1953 and enlarged into Andhra Pradesh in 1956, the communists stood as its alternative government in waiting, dominant in the delta districts and in Telangana's memory, led by figures of the Telangana generation whose authority no other party could match locally. In West Bengal, the movement inherited a province traumatised by famine and partition, organised the refugee colonies that ringed Calcutta in a famous assertion of squatters' rights, led the movements against tram fare increases and for food that repeatedly shook the Congress ministry, and built the municipal and union presence that made it the state's permanent second force. In Madras and in the Punjab, in the PEPSU region's peasant movement against the landlords' exactions, in Assam's tea gardens and in Tripura's hills, smaller organisations held their ground in the same posture: the disciplined opposition that a Congress ministry had to answer in the assembly and feared in the street.

Opposition politics taught the movement a repertoire its governments later used: the assembly as a theatre of exposure, the mass organisation as an instrument of pressure between elections, the local committee as a court of appeal for citizens failed by the administration. It also fixed the movement's social ceiling for the decade. The communists were the opposition of the literate and the organised, of the delta peasant, the jute worker, the schoolteacher and the refugee, and their appeal travelled poorly into the constituencies the Congress held by patronage, caste brokerage and the memory of the freedom struggle. The argument that would split the party was, at bottom, an argument about that ceiling: whether it was the product of an insufficiently independent line, to be broken by sharper opposition, or of the country's real distribution of loyalties, to be dissolved by patient alliance. Kerala's ministry and dismissal gave both readings their evidence within a single term of office, which is why the 1950s, for all their distance, remained the decade to which both successor parties appealed when they wanted to show what the undivided movement had been capable of.

## The road to the split

The split of 1964 had three causes, and the article's discipline requires them to be distinguished, because the parties' later accounts each elevate one and bury the others. The first cause was the attitude to the governing party. The Congress under Nehru was pursuing a state-led development programme with a socialist vocabulary, non-alignment abroad and an expanding public sector at home. One current in the communist movement held that this made the Congress, or at least its dominant leadership, a national and progressive force with which communists should cooperate against the right and against imperialism, even to the point of supporting its governments. The opposing current held that the Congress was the party of the Indian bourgeoisie and landlords, that its socialist vocabulary disguised class rule, and that cooperation meant tailing a ruling class and disarming the movement. The second cause was the border war of 1962. When Indian and Chinese forces fought in the Himalayas in October and November of that year, the party divided over the response: its right wing supported the national defence effort without qualification, while its left wing refused to place sole blame on China, criticised the government's forward policy and opposed the emergency measures under which, in the event, many left-wing communists were themselves arrested and held without trial under the Defence of India Rules. The third cause was international. The dispute between Moscow and Beijing, over de-Stalinisation, the strategy of peaceful coexistence and the road to socialism, had by the early 1960s become a contest for the allegiance of the world's communist parties, and the Indian party contained organised partisans of both camps who read every domestic question through it.

### What role did the border war play in the split?

It converted disagreement into grievance. The left's refusal to endorse the official account of the war was treated by the leadership as near disloyalty, and the arrest of left communists under emergency rules, while the party's chairman supported the government, gave the left a martyrology and a charge of collaboration that it carried into the split and never withdrew.

To the three causes, the immediate quarrel added a document. In 1964 there appeared in print a set of letters attributed to Dange, the party's chairman, purporting to show that in 1924, from prison, he had offered his cooperation to the colonial authorities. Dange and his supporters denounced the letters as forgeries or as misrepresented, and historians have disputed their provenance and meaning since. Their function in 1964 is not in dispute: they gave the left a weapon against the chairman's authority at the exact moment the substantive argument came to a head, and they gave the leadership's supporters a conviction that the campaign against them was unscrupulous. In April 1964, after a meeting of the party's national council broke down over a resolution and over the handling of the letters question, thirty-two members of the council associated with the left walked out. Through the following months parallel organisations formed, each claiming to be the party. In the autumn the left held its congress in Calcutta, numbering it the seventh congress of the Communist Party of India to assert continuity with the party's congresses of the past, and constituted itself as the Communist Party of India (Marxist). In December the remainder held their congress in Bombay under Dange's chairmanship, equally asserting continuity, as the Communist Party of India. The movement that had been founded twice had now divided twice over, and this time the division was permanent.

## The Nehru question: the argument at the split's domestic core

The first cause of the split, the question of the governing party, deserves isolation as the domestic heart of the matter, because it was the argument Indian communists had with one another in Indian terms, and it turned on a single figure. Jawaharlal Nehru presented the movement with a problem no other leader of the national bourgeoisie, in the doctrine's gallery, quite matched: a prime minister who spoke the language of socialism, built a public sector against his own party's right, kept India out of the Western alliance system, recognised the people's republics and irritated Washington as reliably as he irritated Moscow's enemies. At its Avadi session in 1955 the Congress adopted the goal of a socialistic pattern of society, and the question for the communists was whether this was the vocabulary of deception, the ruling class clothing itself in the movement's own words, or the sign of a genuine national project with which the movement should cooperate against the right at home and imperialism abroad. The party's right argued the second, pointing to the Second Five-Year Plan's heavy industry under public ownership, to non-alignment, and to Nehru's battles with his own conservatives, and it read the Kerala dismissal of 1959 as the work of the Congress right against Nehru's better instincts. The party's left argued the first, pointing to the same dismissal as the system's truth, to the police firings on workers and peasants that marked every year of the republic, and to the plan's actual beneficiaries, the private houses that grew fat on public investment's subcontracts.

The argument's interest for the historian is that both readings were argued from evidence the other side acknowledged, and that the choice between them was finally temperamental and organisational as much as analytical: a judgement about whether history in India was moving through the Congress or around it. Nehru's own conduct fed both. He campaigned personally against the communists in Kerala, authorised the intelligence watch on their party, and spoke of them as a movement whose head lay in Delhi and whose heart, in his famous insinuation, lay elsewhere; he also protected the constitutional space in which they operated, received their delegations, and treated their parliamentary presence as a legitimate feature of the republic he was building. The movement never decided whether he was its most dangerous opponent, because he disarmed the anti-imperialist argument, or its most valuable shield, because his republic gave it room no successor regime reliably would. The split of 1964 can be stated, in its domestic form, as the institutionalisation of that undecided question: one party built its strategy on the shield reading, the other on the opponent reading, and the decades since have supplied each with its confirmations, which is why the argument, unlike so many of the century's others, has never been settled by events to both sides' satisfaction.

## The international schism comes home

The third cause of the split, the quarrel between the Soviet and Chinese parties, deserves a fuller statement than it usually receives in Indian accounts, because its Indian career shows how an external dispute acquires domestic force. The quarrel began in the later 1950s over questions that appeared remote from Indian conditions: the Soviet leadership's repudiation of Stalin, its doctrine that war between the camps was not inevitable and that socialist transition might proceed by parliamentary means in some countries, and its pursuit of coexistence with the Western powers. The Chinese party's answer, developed in polemics that the whole communist world read, held that these doctrines disarmed the movement, that imperialism's nature had not changed, and that the Soviet leadership was restoring capitalism at home while preaching surrender abroad. Each side courted the uncommitted parties, and the Indian party, one of the largest outside the socialist states, was courted intensely. Indian communists visited Moscow and Beijing, received delegations, and read the polemics in a party press that could not report the dispute honestly without taking a side in it.

The dispute's Indian mechanism was translation. Every position in the international argument had a domestic equivalent waiting for it. Peaceful transition translated into the attitude to the Congress: if a parliamentary road was conceivable in principle, then cooperation with a national bourgeois government pursuing progressive policies was strategy rather than betrayal, and the party's right held exactly that brief. The Chinese insistence on the universality of revolutionary struggle translated into the left's characterisation of the same government as an organ of class rule with which no durable cooperation was honest. The personalisation of the dispute, the attacks on revisionism as a heresy with named carriers, licensed the Indian argument's descent from programme to person, from the assessment of Nehru's government to the authenticity of Dange's letters. When the border war of 1962 fused the international and national questions into one, Indian communists who looked to Beijing found their country's government in armed conflict with the state whose party they admired, and Indian communists who looked to Moscow found their loyalty to the international centre comfortably aligned with their country's cause. The asymmetry embittered everything that followed: one current could be simultaneously patriotic and orthodox, while the other was forced to choose, and was observed choosing, in public. The split, when it came, was dressed in the international dispute's vocabulary, revisionism against dogmatism, but the vocabulary was the least Indian thing about it. The stakes, the Congress, the war, the organisation, were India's own.

## The years of attempted unity and their failure

Between the Kerala dismissal and the split stand five years in which the party tried, formally and repeatedly, to hold itself together, and the failure of those attempts is itself evidence about the split's causes. The decade had opened with the international movement's own earthquake, the Soviet party's 1956 repudiation of Stalin's cult and crimes, which reached the Indian party as both liberation and threat: liberation, because it licensed the Indian leadership to discuss its own dogmas; threat, because the authority that had arbitrated the movement's internal arguments since the 1920s had publicly revised the terms on which it claimed obedience. The Indian party's Palghat congress of 1956 attempted a moderate synthesis, and its special congress at Amritsar in 1958 adopted documents designed to bind the two emerging currents into one framework, conceding to the left a sharper characterisation of the Indian state and to the right a continued openness to cooperation with the Congress's progressive aspects. The synthesis held on paper. In practice the two currents were building separate apparatuses of loyalty inside the one party: separate study circles, separate understandings in the state units, separate channels to the two quarrelling international centres, so that by the time the party met at Vijayawada in 1961 the argument was no longer about resolutions but about which current's people would hold which posts.

Two accelerants then destroyed the remaining margin. The first was Kerala's dismissal in 1959, over which the currents divided on the moral: whether the lesson was that the parliamentary road required broader alliances, including with the Congress against the communal right, or that it required a harder independence, since the Congress had itself wielded the dismissal. The second was the border war, treated in the previous section, which gave each current a charge of national disloyalty to lay against the other and made compromise readable as surrender. A party that had survived illegality, prison and its own insurrectionary episode could not survive the combination of an international schism, an unresolved national question and two leaderships that had ceased to believe the other acted in good faith. The historian who asks why the split came in 1964 rather than 1958 or 1971 will find the answer in that accumulation rather than in any single congress: the attempted unity of the Amritsar documents postponed the reckoning by exactly as long as no external event forced the currents to choose between their country and their camp, and the border war was that event.

## The split in the parties' own accounts

The discipline this article applies to contested episodes is to give each party its account in its own terms before weighing the record, and no episode in the movement's history needs the discipline more than the split, since the two parties' histories of it remain, half a century later, mutually exclusive documents. The account of the party formed in 1964 runs as follows. The split, on its telling, was a defence of the movement against revisionism. The leadership around Dange had, in this account, abandoned the party's revolutionary programme: it had converted support for progressive aspects of the government's policy into political subordination to the Congress, it had suppressed the party's independence in the name of national unity during the 1962 war and stood by while left communists were jailed, and it ran the organisation by bureaucratic command, stifling the inner-party discussion through which such a course might have been corrected. The walkout of April 1964 was therefore not a factional manoeuvre but the refusal of the party's majority in spirit, representing its strongest state organisations, to accept a leadership that had changed the party's character. The Calcutta congress, on this account, did not found a new party; it rescued the old one, adopted a programme that restored the analysis of the Indian state as an organ of bourgeois-landlord rule, and took the name with the parenthetical addition only because the usurping leadership retained the apparatus and the original name.

The account of the party that retained the name runs in mirror image. On its telling, the split was a factional act prepared over years by a group that had organised itself as a party within the party, maintained its own discipline and its own channels, and taken its orientation not from the Indian situation but from the Chinese party in the international dispute. The left's refusal to support national defence in 1962 showed, in this account, where its allegiance lay. The campaign over the Dange letters, documents of disputed authenticity produced at the decisive moment, showed its methods. And the walkout from the national council, a body in which the dissidents could not command a majority for their positions through the party's own procedures, showed its respect for the inner-party democracy it invoked. The Bombay congress, on this account, was the continuation of the party founded at Kanpur, the party of the trade unions and the anti-imperialist struggle, and the seceders were a breakaway that the international situation had manufactured and that time would expose. The party pointed, in support, to its continued strength in the unions and to the seceding party's alignment with positions, on the Soviet Union and on China, that events would soon make untenable.

Set side by side, the accounts agree on nothing except the fact of division, and the historian's task is to say what the record shows beneath them. It shows, first, that the three substantive disputes were real: the character of the Congress, the response to the border war, and the international schism divided Indian communists as they divided every communist party in the world, and a split somewhere along those seams was a genuine possibility rather than a manufactured one. It shows, second, that organisational rivalry did autonomous work: two leaderships, each with its strongholds, its press and its memory of grievance, reached a point at which coexistence inside one apparatus cost more than separation, and the letters affair supplied the occasion rather than the cause. It shows, third, that the division, once made, was reproduced by everything that followed, since each party built its identity on the other's error, trained its cadres in its own account, and found in every subsequent event, the further split of the late 1960s, the Emergency, the coalition episodes, fresh evidence for the history it already believed. The reader who wants to know why there are several communist parties in India rather than one will find the answer in this mechanism as much as in any doctrine: the split created two institutions, and institutions perpetuate the accounts that justify them.

What the split divided, concretely, was a going concern, and the division's mechanics explain its permanence better than any doctrine. The party's central apparatus, its publishing houses, its newspaper offices and its funds were litigated and apportioned in practice by possession, each side keeping what its people physically held. The mass organisations divided unevenly. The trade union federation remained with the party that had kept the old name, until the other party founded a rival centre of Indian trade unions in 1970 and the labour movement acquired two competing national voices where it had had one. The peasant front split along the same seam, the student and youth organisations likewise, and the cultural front's unity, the achievement of the 1940s, dissolved into the parties' separate establishments. State organisations divided by majority: Kerala and West Bengal and Tripura went predominantly with the departing left, which is why that party emerged as the larger and the governing one, while the older name retained Andhra's remnants, pockets in the unions and the allegiance of those for whom the Soviet connection remained the movement's anchor. A cadre choosing a side in 1964 was choosing a newspaper, a union, a set of comrades and a version of the past in a single act, and such choices, once made at the price of broken friendships and divided families of activists, are not unmade by later congresses passing resolutions on unity.

### Why are there several communist parties rather than one?

Because the splits were institutional as well as ideological. Each separation produced a party with its own leadership, press, unions and founding history to defend, and reunification would have required one side to concede that its account of the past, the account its cadres were raised on, was wrong. Splits in this movement have therefore never been reversed.

A further split at the decade's end completed the pattern. Within the party constituted in 1964, the most radical current had never accepted the parliamentary road that its leadership, in government in West Bengal from 1967 as part of a united front, was practising. In May 1967, peasants at Naxalbari, in the Darjeeling district, under local communist leadership associated with Charu Majumdar's writings on the Chinese road, rose in an armed action that the state government, including its communist ministers, answered with police. The radicals drew the conclusion that the party had itself become an obstacle to revolution, broke away, and in April 1969 constituted a separate party, the CPI (Marxist-Leninist), committed to armed struggle and the boycott of elections. Its campaign of the early 1970s, and the severe repression that broke it, belong to the history of the Maoist current and are handled in the companion study of [the Maoist current and Naxalism](/2018/03/15/naxalism-maoist-current-india/). For the ledger the episode's significance is its demonstration that the movement's oscillation between parliament and insurrection, which the 1951 settlement was supposed to have ended, had instead been institutionalised: after 1969 there existed, simultaneously, communist parties working the constitution and a communist current at war with it, all claiming the inheritance of the same founders.

The breakaway's genesis inside a government deserves one further paragraph, since it is the movement's tragedy in miniature. The radicals of Naxalbari were not intruders into the party formed in 1964; they were its own district cadres, formed by its training, armed with its doctrine, and radicalised by its victory, who watched their party take office in a united front and administer, through its ministers, the police action against the peasants whose rising the party's programme appeared to bless. Majumdar's documents, circulated inside the party before the break, had argued that the Indian revolution's road was the Chinese road and that the party's parliamentary practice was revisionism in power; the leadership's answer, expulsion of the dissidents and defence of the government, repeated the choice of 1964 with the parties' positions reversed, the new establishment now suppressing the new left in the name of the movement's hard-won positions. The pattern is the article's recurring one at the level of the faction: every settlement the movement reached generated, from its own cadres and its own texts, the revolt against the settlement, and the energy of Indian communism across the century is in good part the energy of that cycle, founding, settling, revolting, and founding again.

## The united front governments and the argument of the late 1960s

The elections of 1967, in which the Congress lost power in half the states of the union, gave both communist parties their first experience of coalition government and reopened every argument the split had formalised. In West Bengal a United Front of left and other opposition parties took office with the party formed in 1964 as its largest component, its leaders holding the land and other key portfolios, and the government survived, fell and returned within three turbulent years. Its land minister encouraged the occupation and redistribution of illegally held surplus land by the peasants themselves, a policy its supporters celebrated as the countryside taking what legislation would never have given and its critics, including its coalition partners, condemned as licensed disorder. In Kerala a seven-party front under the same party's leadership governed from 1967 to 1969 and enacted land reform legislation whose tenancy provisions, completed by the succeeding ministry, abolished landlordism in the state in law. The experiments ended in dismissal, defection and dissolution, and each party drew its moral. For the party formed in 1964, the united fronts proved that state governments could be used as platforms of struggle and relief within strict limits, the doctrine it would carry into 1977. For the party that retained the old name, which sat in some of the same fronts and then moved toward its own understanding with the Congress, the decade proved the opposite: that fronts with bourgeois parties dissolved on contact with power, and that the road ran through alliance with the Congress's progressive self-image against the right.

Beneath the governmental experiments, the countryside was conducting its own argument, and Naxalbari was its loudest sentence. The food movement in West Bengal, the struggles over surplus land and the radicalisation of a student generation that watched its party administer a state and fire on peasants at Naxalbari produced, by 1969, the third party of the movement's fragmentation, committed to the proposition that both older parties had become parliamentary frauds. The established parties answered in kind, and the early 1970s in Bengal were years of violence between the communist currents as much as between communists and the state: cadres of the rival parties killed one another in the lanes of Calcutta and the villages of the districts, in a fratricide that the Congress government of the period, returned to office in 1972 in an election the left has always disputed, exploited and extended with severe police action. The ledger records the years without distributing emphasis, since the sources themselves are parties to the quarrel. What can be said neutrally is that the movement entered the 1970s divided into three, that its divisions were now armed, and that the decade's great test would find its two principal parties on opposite sides of the question of democracy itself.

## Alignment and its price in the 1970s

The party that retained the original name spent the 1970s in the posture its account of the split had prepared: alliance with the Congress, justified as the defence of the national, secular and non-aligned state against the right. It supported the Congress government through the war of 1971 that created Bangladesh, a position with genuine popular warrant, and through the increasingly authoritarian middle of the decade, a position whose warrant thinned as it lengthened, until the support extended to the Emergency itself. The party formed in 1964 spent the same years rebuilding in opposition, its Bengal organisation recovering from the violence of the early decade through mass work among peasants and the urban poor, its national posture uniting it tactically with parties of every other colour against authoritarianism. The contrast of the decade is the split's two theories tested in practice. The alliance theory held that proximity to the ruling party would let the movement shape the state from beside the throne. The independence theory held that a communist party's capital was its credibility, and that credibility spent in another party's service did not return. By 1977 the country's electorate had delivered its opinion between them, in the only test either theory recognised as final.

## The Emergency and the two parties' opposite choices

The constitutional emergency of June 1975, when the government of Indira Gandhi suspended civil liberties, jailed the opposition and ruled by decree for twenty-one months, divided the two main parties along the seam the split had cut. The Communist Party of India supported the Emergency. Its reasoning, stated in its own terms at the time, was that the opposition movement against the government had become a vehicle for right-wing and externally encouraged forces bent on destabilising the country, that the Emergency's economic programme contained progressive measures against hoarders, smugglers and monopolists, and that the defence of a government pursuing non-alignment and public-sector development took precedence over the liberties of a campaign it regarded as reactionary in essence. The Marxist party opposed the Emergency. Its leaders and cadres were arrested, its press censored and its organisation driven semi-underground, and it emerged in 1977 with its democratic credentials burnished by imprisonment suffered in company with the rest of the opposition.

### How did the two parties differ over the Emergency?

One supported it as a necessary blow against what it characterised as a right-reactionary offensive, accepting the suspension of liberties as temporary and justified by the government's progressive measures. The other resisted it as authoritarian rule, paid for the resistance in arrests and censorship, and treated the defence of democratic rights as a commitment not subject to tactical exchange.

The sequel gave each party its verdict. When the Emergency ended and elections were held in March 1977, the Congress was swept from power nationally, and the party that had supported its emergency shared in the discredit, losing ground it never recovered. Three months later the Left Front led by the party formed in 1964 won the West Bengal assembly election and began the longest incumbency of any communist-led government in the country. The later party's account of 1977, in its own terms, is that the victory vindicated two decades of its method: it presented the government it formed not as a step to revolution, which a state government within the Indian union could not be, but as an instrument for defending democratic rights, carrying out land reform and giving the working people relief, a limited and explicitly constitutional conception that it defended against its own left critics as the only honest one available. The supporting party's later account of the same period is the more remarkable document. At its Bhatinda congress in 1978, the Communist Party of India reviewed the Emergency and acknowledged that its support had been a serious error, a failure to defend the democratic rights whose defence it now recognised as inseparable from its socialist aims. The acknowledgement belongs in the ledger beside the party's post-1942 reviews: the movement's tradition of self-criticism, on paper, is real and sometimes unsparing. Its limitation, which the ledger exists to expose, is that the self-criticisms are retrospective. Each was delivered after the damage was entered, and none has yet been delivered in time.

## After the Emergency: the realignment and the long incumbency begins

The years from 1977 to the early 1980s rearranged the movement's pieces before the long incumbency settled them. The Janata government at the centre, a coalition of the anti-Emergency opposition, held office for less than three years, and the left parties' relations with it ran the full cycle of the period's possibilities: cooperation in the restoration of civil liberties and the release of prisoners, friction over economic policy and the treatment of strikes, and abstention from the coalition's internal quarrels as it dissolved. The party that had supported the Emergency, chastened by Bhatinda, moved by stages into opposition to the Congress it had served, and when the Congress returned to power in 1980 the two communist parties found themselves, for the first time since the split, on the same side of the central argument, a tactical convergence that produced joint actions and even shared platforms without ever approaching reunification, since the organisations, properties and histories divided in 1964 remained divided and each party's identity continued to require the other's error. In West Bengal the Left Front's victory of 1977 was confirmed and enlarged, and the front under Jyoti Basu began the work that made its name: the registration of sharecroppers, the distribution of vested land, the panchayat elections that carried the left's organisation into every village of the state and fixed its incumbency for a generation. Kerala in the same years perfected its alternation, the left front and the Congress-led front exchanging office with a regularity that made Kerala the republic's most evenly contested state.

The realignment's significance for the ledger is that it closed the movement's period of national ambition in its older form. After 1980, neither party again proposed, as a practical horizon, the conquest of national power by the movement's own growth; the horizons became the defence and extension of the state strongholds, the shaping of national coalitions from positions of regional strength, and the preservation of the movement's organisations against a changing economy. Judged by the founding generation's aims, the contraction is stark. Judged by the standards of Indian political survival, it was an achievement: the two parties entered the republic's second half-century as its most durable organisations after the Congress itself, with governments, unions, presses and memories intact, in a country that had dissolved or absorbed most of the other formations of the freedom struggle era. The tension between those two judgements, contraction as failure and survival as achievement, is the unresolved question the movement's history hands to its readers, and the ledger's remaining rows are best read as its successive attempts at an answer.

## Governing in the states: a pointer to the record

From 1977 the movement's centre of gravity shifted decisively to the states. The Left Front's victory in West Bengal began an incumbency that ran, through repeated re-election, for more than three decades, and Kerala developed the opposite rhythm, an alternation in which communist-led and Congress-led fronts replaced each other at nearly every election with the communists a permanent party of government or near it. A third state, Tripura in the north-east, sustained long periods of Left Front rule. These governments operated within tight constitutional and fiscal limits, since an Indian state controls neither the currency, nor the commanding heights of economic policy, nor its own borrowing without the centre's consent, and their records have to be read against those limits: land reform and the registration of sharecroppers in West Bengal under Operation Barga, tenancy and land legislation in Kerala, the decentralisation of administration to elected village and district bodies, systems of public distribution and public health and education that the development literature has studied intensively. The full treatment of that record, its achievements, its stagnation and the arguments of its critics, belongs to the companion studies of [the experiment in Kerala](/2018/01/01/kerala-communist-experiment/) and of [the Left Front's Bengal](/2018/01/15/west-bengal-left-front-analysis/), and the disambiguation discipline of the series reserves the detail for them.

What belongs in this history is the structural fact the state governments created. From the late 1970s, the Indian communist movement was no longer primarily an opposition current. It was a governing establishment in its strongholds, with the assets and the liabilities of incumbency: control of state administrations and police forces, responsibility for industrial stagnation or agricultural performance, patronage to distribute and opponents to answer. Incumbency changed the movement's sociology, drawing its cadres into administration and its politics toward the defence of office, and it changed the meaning of its national weakness. A party that governs a large state for a generation is not failing in any simple sense; it is succeeding at a level below the nation, and the gap between its state-level durability and its national ceiling is the central puzzle the ledger's later entries pose. The two episodes that follow, the declined prime ministership and the coalition that ended in withdrawal, are the movement's two encounters with that ceiling, and they frame it from opposite sides: in the first, national power was offered and refused; in the second, national influence was exercised and then surrendered on a point of principle.

## The prime ministership that was declined

The general election of 1996 produced a hung parliament and a brief, genuine possibility that India would be governed by a communist. The Congress had lost, the Bharatiya Janata Party's first government had fallen within days for want of support, and the United Front, a coalition of regional and left parties, assembled a majority on the understanding that its leader would be chosen by consensus. The name the consensus produced was Jyoti Basu, the chief minister of West Bengal, the movement's most experienced administrator and its most nationally acceptable face. The choice required the consent of his party's central committee, since the party's doctrine held that it joined governments only where it could decisively shape their programme. The central committee deliberated and declined. The stated reasoning, in the party's own terms, was that a prime minister from its ranks would head a government whose constituent parties did not share its programme, that it would bear responsibility for policies it could not control, and that the movement's interests were better served by supporting such a government from outside, where its independence of judgement survived. Basu accepted the decision publicly and loyally. The United Front turned to another leader, and the moment passed.

### Why did the party decline the prime ministership for its own chief minister?

On its stated reasoning, because office without the strength to implement its programme would have made the party answerable for a government it did not control. Its doctrine permitted joining governments only where it could decisively influence policy, and a coalition assembled from a hung parliament did not meet that test in the central committee's judgement.

The episode's afterlife has been longer than the government that resulted from it. Basu himself, in retirement, described the decision as a historic blunder, and the phrase entered the country's political vocabulary as the standard summary of the movement's national predicament. Defenders of the decision answer in the party's terms: that a communist prime minister presiding over a coalition committed to economic liberalisation would have been destroyed by the office, that the party's conditions were known in advance and honestly applied, and that the blunder, if any, lay in a doctrine framed for state governments being applied to a national opportunity that would not recur. Critics from outside the movement treat the refusal as the confession of its ceiling: a party that had organised for three quarters of a century to transform India declined, at the moment of maximum possibility, to attempt the government of India, and did so on grounds of doctrinal propriety that its rivals read as fear of responsibility. Either way, the ledger enters 1996 as the second great entry in the movement's account with the nation, the counterpart of 1942. In 1942 the movement stood aside from the nation's founding struggle for reasons of principle. In 1996 it stood aside from the nation's government for reasons of principle. The principles differed. The structural position, outside the national story at the moment of decision, was the same, and the movement's own most senior figure named the second occasion an error in terms his party has never formally adopted and never quite refuted.

## Outside support and the withdrawal

The movement's final encounter with national power took the opposite form. After the general election of 2004, the left parties held a large parliamentary contingent, the largest they have ever held in the republic's history, and the Congress-led United Progressive Alliance needed their support to govern. The parties chose the arrangement their doctrine preferred: support from outside, without ministerial office, in exchange for a common minimum programme that committed the government to measures on employment, public investment, social welfare and the restraint of privatisation, and to consultation with the supporting parties on policy. For four years the arrangement gave the movement a degree of influence over national legislation unmatched in its history. Measures it prized were enacted, measures it opposed were delayed or diluted, and its leaders were received as principals in the country's government without bearing office in it. Its critics, including critics within its own ranks, observed that the arrangement reproduced at the national level the asymmetry its opponents had always alleged: influence without responsibility, the power to obstruct without the duty to administer, exercised by a party whose parliamentary strength rested on two or three states.

The end came over foreign policy, the ground on which the movement's commitments had always been least negotiable. The government pursued an agreement on civil nuclear cooperation with the United States, which it presented as the key to India's energy security and international standing. The left parties opposed the agreement in principle, arguing in their own terms that it subordinated India's independent foreign policy to an American strategic design, compromised the country's freedom to develop its own nuclear programme, and bound India into an alignment its non-aligned tradition forbade. Consultation failed to close the gap. In July 2008, when the government resolved to proceed to the international safeguards stage of the agreement, the left parties withdrew their support, forcing a vote of confidence which the government survived with the help of other parties. The alliance was over, and at the general election that followed the movement's parliamentary strength fell sharply, beginning a decline in its national position from which, in structural terms, it has been managing the consequences since: its state strongholds came under pressure it had historically resisted, and its role in national politics contracted toward the regional bases from which it had begun.

### What issue ended the Left's support for the coalition?

The nuclear cooperation agreement concluded with the United States. The left parties held that the agreement subordinated India's independent foreign policy and compromised its strategic autonomy, the government held that it served energy security and national standing, and when the government proceeded with the agreement's international safeguards stage in 2008 the supporting parties withdrew.

Read against the ledger, the withdrawal of 2008 is a smaller, cleaner repetition of the movement's oldest pattern, and sympathetic and hostile readers divide over it in the old way. To its defenders, the episode proves the movement's consistency: it surrendered real power rather than endorse a strategic subordination, exactly as it had claimed it would, and paid the electoral price without complaint. To its critics, the episode proves the subordination objection in a new form: a movement that could stake its national position on the foreign-policy question of a superpower relationship, when its own electorate's concerns were wages, land and prices, was still conducting politics by priorities formed in another century's international movement. The article's discipline is to enter both readings and to note the fact they share: after 2008 the movement's long twentieth century, the century of Tashkent and Meerut, of Telangana and the splits, was effectively over, and what remained was its inheritance, regional parties of government with a national memory, whose history this article has told.

## The international connection across the century

No theme runs through the movement's whole history as persistently as its relation to the international movement, and the ledger's entries make little sense without a section that treats the connection as a subject in itself rather than as a recurring embarrassment. The connection began as a lifeline. The émigrés at Tashkent existed because the Communist International maintained them, the early groups at home received literature, funds and couriers through it, and the doctrine the movement preached, its analysis of imperialism, its organisational forms, its very vocabulary of party, front and cadre, was the international movement's common stock. To describe this as foreign direction, full stop, is to miss that Indian communists sought the connection as eagerly as it was offered: they were a handful of publicists and organisers in a subcontinent, and the International gave them a world, a theory of history in which their country's struggle had a place and a prospect of allies in every port. Roy's standing in Moscow, where his theses were debated beside Lenin's, was a source of legitimate pride to a movement accustomed to being treated by the British as a criminal nuisance and by the Congress as an auxiliary.

The connection's costs were the mirror of its benefits, and they were paid at the movement's decisive moments. The sectarian line of the early 1930s, which isolated the party during the Civil Disobedience years, was the International's line before it was India's. The wartime reversal of 1941 followed the invasion of the Soviet Union, and required no instruction from Moscow to be adopted, since Indian communists drew the conclusion from their own internationalism, which is precisely what made the subordination objection so difficult to answer: the subordination was internalised, a matter of conviction rather than of command, and therefore proof against the remedy of simply disobeying an order. The insurrectionary turn of 1948 similarly echoed an international hardening, the two-camp doctrine of the early Cold War, and its reversal in 1951 was eased by consultations in Moscow at which the Indian delegation's new course was discussed with the Soviet leadership. The split of 1964 cannot be told without the Sino-Soviet dispute, and the further split of 1969 cannot be told without the Cultural Revolution's example and the Chinese party's blessing of the Indian Maoists. For half a century, there is no major turn in the movement's strategy that does not have an international co-author.

The exceptions are as significant as the rule, and they cluster where the movement was strongest. The united front work of the 1930s, the Telangana struggle in its village phase, the Kerala ministry of 1957 and the Left Front's doctrine of the limited state government were Indian constructions, worked out against Indian conditions by organisers answerable to Indian electorates and Indian landlords' resistance, and the international movement's response to them ranged from endorsement after the fact to discomfort. The Kerala experiment in particular, a communist government administering a state of a bourgeois republic and planning to be re-elected, fitted no international template and was defended by its authors in an idiom of Indian constitutionalism that owed little to any external centre. A balanced history therefore refuses both simplifications. The movement was not a puppet, since puppets do not build Telanganas or win Keralas. It was not autonomous in the way its own national self-image sometimes claimed, since its gravest decisions track the international movement's turns with a fidelity that coincidence cannot explain. It was a national movement with an international nervous system, and its history is the record of what that combination made possible and what it cost.

The ending of the international movement is the quiet fact behind the movement's later history. The Sino-Soviet dispute had already destroyed the idea of a single centre before 1964 gave the destruction Indian form. The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 removed the pole around which the party keeping the original name had oriented its internationalism, and that party's subsequent congresses acknowledged a changed world in documents that stressed multipolarity and the diversity of roads. The party formed in 1964, which had defined itself against Soviet revisionism, found its critique deprived of its object by the same event. Neither party's Indian bases were much moved by the fall of a state most of their voters had never expected to see, a fact that itself measures how indigenous the movement's roots had become. The international connection had formed the movement, marked its worst decisions, and then departed, leaving the parties to conduct, in their states and their unions, a politics whose references were increasingly their own past.

## The cultural front and the movement's second organisation

A history confined to congresses and governments would miss the institution through which the movement reached deepest into Indian life: its cultural front. From the Progressive Writers' Association of the 1930s, which gathered the subcontinent's Urdu, Hindi and Bengali literary modernists around a manifesto against decadence and for social purpose, to the Indian People's Theatre Association founded in 1943, the movement built a parallel organisation of writers, actors, musicians and filmmakers whose work carried its sensibility far beyond its membership. IPTA's squads toured the famine districts of Bengal and the mill towns with plays and songs that reported what official India preferred unreported, and its Bombay film unit and its theatre people fed talent into the postwar cinema for a generation. The names associated with this world, poets such as Kaifi Azmi, actors such as Balraj Sahni, filmmakers and theatre directors across the regional languages, were not all party members, and the front's strength was precisely its looseness: it offered artists a cause, an audience and a network, and asked in return a sympathy rather than a card.

The cultural front matters to the political history for two reasons. First, it was a principal channel of the movement's national presence. In years when the party was banned, divided or electorally confined, its songs, plays and journals kept a left vocabulary in circulation among students, teachers and the reading public of every province, and recruited to the broader movement many who never joined a party. The movement's influence on Indian intellectual life, on its universities, its publishing and its historiography, has always exceeded its vote, and the cultural front is a large part of the explanation. Second, the front reproduced the movement's central tension in aesthetic form. Artists chafed under doctrinal supervision, and the party's cultural managers, armed with the international movement's theories of socialist realism, periodically attempted to discipline the front's exuberance into correctness, losing talent in the process. The oscillation between alliance and control, between the front as a home for sympathy and the front as an instrument, is the united front argument restated in the key of culture, and it was never resolved, only managed, with the balance shifting as the party's political fortunes rose and fell.

For the ledger, the cultural front is the entry most often omitted and the one that best explains the movement's survival. Parties that lose their cultural presence become electoral machines and die with their vote. The Indian movement, through every catastrophe of its chronology, retained a presence in the country's imagination, its martyrs sung, its struggles novelised, its historians installed in the universities, that carried its name across the decades when its politics foundered. When the movement's own chroniclers ask why a party so often defeated remained a permanent feature of Indian life, the answer lies as much in the theatre squads of 1943 as in any congress resolution.

## The movement and the constitution it learned to work

One relationship in the movement's history changed so completely across the century that the change itself deserves recording: its relationship to the Indian constitution. The movement's first encounters with the founding settlement were hostile. In the Constituent Assembly, the communist presence was token, and its best-known spokesman there, Somnath Lahiri, attacked the draft constitution as a document framed under imperialist and capitalist tutelage that would entrench property against the people. The insurrectionary line of 1948 treated the constitutional order as a facade to be swept aside. Yet the same movement, from 1951, contested the constitution's elections, and from 1957 governed under its provisions, and over the following decades became, by the testimony of its opponents as much as its friends, one of the constitutional order's most scrupulous operators: forming governments and surrendering them on lost votes, litigating its dismissals, defending federalism against the centre with a constitutional lawyer's precision, and treating the defence of democratic rights, after the Emergency, as a first principle rather than a tactic.

The transformation was not merely opportunism, though opportunism played its part, since a party that can win states has reasons to love the rules that let it win them. It was also an education. Governing Kerala and Bengal taught the movement the constitution's real distribution of power, its protections and its traps, the president's rule provision above all, and taught it that the document's democratic spaces were not a facade but a terrain on which its own survival depended, as the Emergency proved to the party that had to be jailed to learn it and to the party that learned it in the review afterward. By the century's end the movement's constitutionalism had become one of its distinguishing marks in Indian politics, a source of genuine strength in its strongholds and of a certain pathos nationally, since the constitution it had learned to work so well had also fixed the arena in which its ceiling was set: a federal republic in which a regional party, however accomplished, governs states and influences coalitions but does not become the nation. The ledger's national entries are, in that sense, constitutional entries too.

## The turning points ledger

The narrative above can be compressed into the instrument the series uses for a political record: a ledger in which each turning point is entered with its date, the case made for it at the time, what followed from it, and the verdict the movement afterwards passed on its own act. The compression is deliberate. A ledger forbids the two vices of party history, which are celebration and prosecution, because a row must contain the reasoning and the consequence side by side, and a movement that reads its own rows in sequence can see the pattern that no single episode reveals: strategies succeeding at the regional and organisational level while failing, repeatedly and for identifiable reasons, at the level of the nation. The ledger is the article's findable artifact, designed to be lifted into teaching and revision, and the entries below follow the order of the narrative.

Read down the consequence column and the pattern declares itself. Every row in which the movement acted from an Indian assessment of Indian conditions, the united front years, Telangana in its village phase, Kerala, the state governments, ends in construction that lasted. Every row in which a decision was governed by a priority set elsewhere, the sectarian isolation, the wartime reversal, the turn to insurrection, the terms of the international schism, ends in a cost the movement spent years repaying. The ledger does not prove that subordination was the movement's only failing, since the rows also record misjudgements, of the country's temper in 1948 and of a coalition's worth in 1996, that were entirely its own. It proves the narrower and more useful thing: that the movement's national failures were not accidents of personality or of fate, but the recurring price of a recurring structure, and that its regional successes were not consolations but demonstrations of what the same movement could do when the structure did not intervene.

## Reading a movement's history against its own accounts

A word is owed to the reader about sources, because this history is unusually dependent on the movement's own paper. The Indian communist movement was a movement of writers. Its leaders published journals, programmes, self-criticisms and multi-volume documentary histories, and its parties maintain archives and official histories in which the splits are recorded from each side. The outsider literature is thinner: colonial intelligence reports, of which the Meerut proceedings generated shelves; a small shelf of academic histories, of which the early scholarly account by Gene Overstreet and Marshall Windmiller long set the terms; and the memoirs of participants, Sundarayya's Telangana narrative among them, which combine testimony with advocacy in proportions the reader must continually estimate. The method used throughout this article follows from that source situation. Where a party's account of a split is quoted, it is quoted as the party's account, evidence of what the party believes about itself, and weighed against the sequence of decisions that both sides record identically. Where the parties agree on the facts and differ only on the meaning, the article gives both meanings and says so. Where a figure is contested between sources of different affiliation, membership, votes, casualties, the article either omits it or marks it as contested, in accordance with the series' contested figures protocol.

The state's archive requires the same double reading. The colonial intelligence establishment watched the movement from its first years, and its officers produced accounts, of which the 1927 report on communism in India prepared for the government by a senior intelligence official is the founding document, that historians still mine for the early period's detail. The reports are exact on meetings, journeys and publications, since the watchers were competent, and systematically misleading on motive and allegiance, since the watchers' institutional need was to find the conspiracy their theory required. The republic's intelligence inherited the watch and, during the long years when the movement was the principal internal subject of its attention, generated a parallel archive whose eventual opening will revise parts of this history again, particularly the record of the splits, in which the role of forged and planted material, the Dange letters above all, cannot be finally assessed until the files are read. The researcher should therefore treat every precise claim about the movement's internal affairs that rests on a single archive's authority, the party's or the state's, as provisional, and prefer the points at which the two archives, watching the same men for opposite reasons, reluctantly agree.

The self-criticisms deserve particular handling, because they are the sources most likely to mislead a sympathetic reader and most likely to be dismissed by a hostile one. The movement's tradition of reviewing its errors is genuine: the reversals of 1951, the post-Emergency review of 1978 and the concessions over 1942 were formal acts, debated and adopted, not journalistic asides. They are also, without exception, retrospective and leadership-shaped, distributing blame to predecessors in ways that conveniently validate successors. The student who learns to read a self-criticism twice, once for what it concedes and once for what the concession protects, has acquired the single most useful skill this topic teaches, and it transfers to every other movement history in the series.

## Misreadings this history corrects

Four misreadings circulate widely enough to be worth correcting in one place. The first holds that the movement was uniformly opposed to the nationalist struggle. For most of the colonial period it was not: it sought alliance with the national movement, worked inside its socialist wing, supported the naval mutineers of 1946 when the national leadership counselled surrender, and measured its own wartime stand as a tragic exception rather than a general rule. The second misreading is the mirror image, that the movement was uniformly supportive of the national struggle, which the wartime line and the acceptance of the Pakistan demand contradict on the record. The third is that the split of 1964 was purely a product of the Soviet and Chinese parties' international dispute. The international dispute supplied one of its three causes and much of its vocabulary, but the argument over the Congress and the grievance of 1962 were Indian, and the organisational rivalry was Indian beyond argument. The fourth is that Telangana was a minor episode, a footnote of banditry in a princely state. It was the greatest armed peasant movement of the Indian twentieth century's middle decades, it governed a countryside, and its memory structures the politics of its region to this day in the sense that parties and movements there still argue in its shadow. A fifth misreading completes the set, and it is the one the movement's friends most often commit: that the movement's history is a story of betrayals by its leaderships, in which a heroic rank and file was repeatedly sold by the men at the top. The dated record does not sustain it. The wartime line was debated and carried through the party's committees by men the membership trusted and re-elected; the insurrectionary line was adopted by a congress; the split was made by two leaderships each commanding the loyalty of half the movement's heartlands; the declined premiership was a committee's decision defended by its intended beneficiary in public. The movement's errors were corporate, argued, voted and owned, which is precisely what makes them historically instructive rather than merely scandalous: they show what a serious organisation, reasoning in good faith from its premises, can decide, and what such decisions cost. A reader who carries the corrections rather than the misreadings into the examination hall or the archive will find the rest of the record falling into place around them.

## The regional pattern: why these states and not others

Any map of the movement's strength poses a question the national narrative can slide past: why did communism take root in Andhra and Telangana, in Kerala, in West Bengal and in Tripura, and remain thin in the great Hindi-speaking interior? The answers are local, and they repay attention because they show what the movement's growth actually consisted of when it succeeded. In the Andhra country, the movement grew through the delta districts' rich peasant associations and the cultural politics of the Telugu language, and it fused with the demand for a linguistic state, Visalandhra, so that to be a communist in the delta was also to be a patriot of the Telugu nation. In Malabar and later Kerala, it grew through the socialist party's village networks into a society of high literacy, sharp caste oppression and dense associational life, where agricultural labourers, toddy tappers, coir workers and schoolteachers formed a public that read party journals and expected politics to address rent, caste humiliation and the price of rice. In Bengal, it grew through the Calcutta intelligentsia and the jute unions into a bhadralok culture that prized doctrine and debate, and it was fed by the traumas of famine and partition, which delivered to the movement a generation of refugee and student recruits. In Tripura, it grew through the tribal population's resistance to princely and then settler pressure, a politics of land and identity in which the party's defence of tribal land made it the vehicle of a people's self-protection.

The common factors are visible once the cases are laid side by side. The movement flourished where it fused with a grievance that was simultaneously economic and cultural: language in Andhra, caste and literacy in Kerala, famine and partition in Bengal, land and tribal identity in Tripura. It flourished where an intermediate public existed, schoolteachers, clerks, journalists, students, to carry doctrine into everyday speech. And it flourished where the Congress was weak, divided or identified with the local possessing classes, leaving the representation of the poor to whoever would organise them. Where those conditions failed, in the interior districts where the Congress's nationalist legitimacy was unchallenged and the social structure offered the movement no cultural grievance to fuse with, communism remained a doctrine of small circles. The pattern carries a lesson that the ledger's national entries confirm from the other side. The movement grew when it became the vehicle of a people's particular wrongs, in the people's own language. It faltered when it addressed the people in the vocabulary of a universal schedule whose priorities had been set elsewhere. Regional history, usually treated as the residue of the national story, is in this case the explanation of it.

## The cadre party: organisation as strength and as weakness

The movement's organisational form was itself one of its historical facts, and a section is owed to it because several turning points are unintelligible without it. The party was built as a cadre organisation: a membership selected by application and probation, organised in cells and committees ascending to a central committee and a politburo, bound by democratic centralism, the rule that decisions debated beforehand are binding afterward, and served by a stratum of full-time functionaries, the whole-timers, who gave the movement its continuity through bans and prisons. Around the party stood the mass organisations, unions, peasant associations, student and youth federations, women's organisations and the cultural front, each formally independent and each led in practice by party members, so that the movement's real strength was always several times its membership and its presence in a workplace or a campus could survive the party's own illegality.

The strengths of the form were displayed at every crisis of the chronology. It survived the conspiracy trials, the ban of 1934, the repression of 1948 to 1950 and the Emergency with its organisation intact, a record no looser Indian party of the left can match. It produced organisers of legendary stamina and incorruptibility, men and women who lived on party allowances in party communes and whose personal example recruited as effectively as any pamphlet. It gave the movement a memory: decisions, self-criticisms and programmes were written down, debated and preserved, which is why this article can be written from the record at all. The weaknesses were the same qualities inverted. Democratic centralism, under the pressure of illegality and then of habit, decayed into command from above, in which the bindingness of decisions outlasted the debate that was supposed to precede them, and the great reversals of line, 1941, 1948, 1951, arrived at the membership as accomplished facts to be defended rather than as questions to be judged. The cadre form also made splits total: since the party was a single disciplined apparatus with one press, one union leadership and one account of itself, a disagreement that could not be contained became a division of everything, apparatus, property, fronts and history, and the divided halves inherited the form that had made the division necessary. To understand why the movement split rather than argued, the reader has to understand that its organisation left no institutional space in which a defeated minority could remain.

## The agrarian thread through the century

Beneath the chronology of congresses runs a single continuous thread, the agrarian question, and following it from end to end gives the century a shape the political narrative alone does not. The movement began in the cities, among mill workers, but its destiny was decided in the countryside, because India was a peasant country and every strategy the movement adopted was finally a strategy about land. The kisan sabha of the 1930s made the first translation of doctrine into agrarian programme: abolish landlordism, reduce rents and debts, tax the rich peasant lightly and the landlord out of existence. Telangana was the thread's armed chapter, in which the programme was enacted by village committees before any legislature had voted it. The Kerala and Bengal governments were its legislative chapters, enacting tenancy reform, ceilings and the registration of sharecroppers against the resistance of the landed, and discovering the limits of what a state government could enact against courts, centre and landlord. The Maoist current was its insurgent continuation, holding that the legislative chapters were a surrender and that the village war of the 1940s had been abandoned, not concluded. Even the coalition episodes of the national period had an agrarian substrate, since the movement's parliamentary weight rested on states whose politics had been formed by land movements two generations old.

The thread also exposes the movement's deepest unresolved argument, which is about the peasant himself. Classical doctrine, formed on European material, treated the peasantry as a class destined to dissolve into landlords and labourers, and the Indian movement's practical organisers found instead a countryside of tenants, sharecroppers, small holders and labourers whose grievances were immediate and whose horizons were the village and the harvest. Every successful chapter of the movement's history was written by those who organised the grievance in front of them. Every failure of the thread, the insurrectionary line's assumption that the countryside was ready for a war it had not declared, the later governments' discovery that land reform administered from a secretariat exhausts its own momentum, came from substituting the doctrine's schedule for the village's. The agrarian thread is therefore the strongest evidence available for the ledger's general pattern, since in agrarian work the movement could not take its priorities from any external centre for long: the harvest would not wait for a congress, and the landlord's men did not read resolutions.

## The movement and the working class in the republic

The movement began in the unions, and a history that follows it into parliaments owes the unions a section of their own, since the industrial working class remained the movement's most reliable constituency and its slowest-changing problem. The communist-led unions inherited from the colonial period a stronghold structure: dominance in particular industries and cities, textiles in Bombay, jute in Bengal, the docks, the railways' workshops, the plantations and mines, rather than a uniform national presence. The AITUC, which the communists effectively led by the late 1940s, faced from 1947 a state-sponsored rival, the Indian National Trade Union Congress founded by the ruling party, which competed for recognition with the government's favour behind it, and later a socialist and then a Hindu-nationalist federation joined the contest. Union rivalry in India thus became an extension of party rivalry, with recognition, check-off and the favour of managements and labour ministries distributed politically, and the communist unions' history in the republic is a history of defending organised strongholds in a fragmenting field.

The split of 1964 divided the unions as it divided everything else, and the foundation of a rival trade union centre in 1970 by the party formed in 1964 institutionalised the division in the workplace: two communist union federations, each regarding the other's existence as the split's continuing injury, competing for the same mills and mines. The deeper challenge arrived from the economy itself. From the 1980s the stronghold industries contracted or transformed: the Bombay textile industry, the movement's oldest fortress, collapsed through the great strike of 1982 and the long closure of the mills, a strike led from outside the established federations and lost over eighteen months, after which the mill districts were redeveloped and their working-class public dispersed. Jute declined, the organised sector's share of employment shrank, and the republic's workforce became overwhelmingly informal, casual and unorganised, a terrain on which the cadre union model, built for the factory gate and the recognised bargaining agent, worked poorly. The movement's unionism in its later decades is a record of adaptation attempted under adverse conditions: organising construction and agricultural labour, domestic and home-based workers, often through the women's and peasant fronts rather than the classical union form. The ledger's perspective puts the union story in its place in the pattern. The unions were the movement's school and its pension, the institution that trained its cadres and sustained its vote in the industrial towns; and the economy's transformation dissolved the school's premises faster than the movement, ageing and state-bound, could rebuild them elsewhere.

## Students, teachers, and the movement's pipeline

Every Indian political formation recruits, but the communist movement recruited distinctively, through the school and the university, and its history cannot be understood without the pipeline that carried teenagers from the student federation to the whole-timer's life. The All India Students' Federation of 1936 was the first vessel; after independence the parties built their own student and youth organisations, which contested campus elections as seriously as parliamentary ones and treated the student years as the movement's apprenticeship. The recruits were disproportionately the children of schoolteachers, clerks and small peasants, the literate poor of the stronghold states, for whom the movement offered a complete world: a library, a hostel network, a marriage market of comrades, a career of honourable poverty and a doctrine that explained their families' condition as history rather than fate. Kerala's and Bengal's famous literacy and their communist strength were not parallel facts but connected ones, each feeding the other across generations, since the movement's teachers taught the children who became the movement's students.

The pipeline explains features of the movement that puzzle outsiders. It explains the movement's literary quality, the density of poets, teachers and journalists in its leadership, unusual in a party of workers and peasants. It explains the ferocity of its internal arguments, conducted in the idiom of the seminar and the little magazine, with programmes and counter-programmes circulated like examination answers. It explains, too, the movement's characteristic lag: a party staffed through universities absorbs the questions of the educated young a generation before it absorbs the questions of the informal workforce, and the Indian movement's long difficulty in speaking to the unorganised, to migrants, to the new service proletariat of the cities, is partly a pipeline's difficulty. The food movements of 1959 and 1966 in Bengal, in which students and the urban poor marched together over the price of rice and the movement paid in lives under police firing, mark the pipeline's heroic period; the campus radicalism that fed the Maoist current at the decade's end marks its crisis, when the students concluded that the party of their teachers had become a party of government and took the doctrine's more violent chapters literally. Both episodes belong to the same institution's biography.

## The women's organisations and the movement's household

The movement's record on women is a subject its own later self-criticism treats with unusual candour, and the dated record supports a mixed entry. Women were present from the movement's first decades as organisers, prisoners and martyrs, in the unions of Bombay and Bengal, in the kisan sabha and supremely in Telangana, where peasant women fought in the squads, carried the struggle's intelligence and, in the person of commanders whose names the region still honours, led armed units against the landlord's men and the state's forces alike. The women's front acquired national form in the National Federation of Indian Women, founded in 1954, and the parties later built their own mass women's organisations, which in the stronghold states enrolled members on a scale few Indian women's organisations have matched, campaigning on prices, liquor, violence and the enforcement of the laws the republic had already passed. The movement's governments legislated and administered within the limits of their powers, and its public culture celebrated the woman comrade as an emblem of the new society it proposed.

Against that record stands the movement's household, which its women members have described in memoirs and internal debates with a consistency that forbids dismissal. The cadre life the movement honoured, the whole-timer's total availability, was built on the unpaid labour of wives and mothers who kept the commune's kitchen and the movement's children while the doctrine of women's emancipation was preached from its platforms. Women rose through the fronts in numbers and through the party's leading committees in a trickle, and the questions the women's organisations raised, dowry, domestic violence, the double burden, were chronically ranked below the questions of class strategy in the movement's order of business. The honest ledger entry is therefore double, and it is the entry the movement's own best historians now write: a movement that mobilised women on a scale and with a seriousness unmatched by its rivals, gave many of them public lives their society had foreclosed, and reproduced inside its own walls a substantial part of the subordination it existed to abolish. The series treats the theoretical side of that contradiction in its feminism cluster; the historical side belongs here, as one more instance of the gap between the movement's programme and its practice that its self-criticisms periodically discover.

## The adversary's portrait: anti-communism in Indian politics

A movement's history is incomplete without the portrait its adversaries painted of it, since the portrait was itself a political force that the movement had to answer at every election. Indian anti-communism had several workshops. The colonial state built the first, communism as foreign conspiracy, in the conspiracy trials, and bequeathed its archive and its idiom to the republic. The Congress right built the second during the freedom struggle and the early republic: the communist as the nation's internal enemy, the party that had blessed partition and opposed the final struggle, a charge the Congress deployed whenever the communists grew strong in a state, most consequentially in Kerala in 1959, where the liberation struggle's propaganda fused the party with foreign allegiance and godlessness in a single indictment addressed to a deeply religious electorate. The Hindu nationalist movement built the third, from the 1950s onward, in which communism figured as one term of a civilisational enemy, alien in doctrine, hostile to the nation's religion and aligned with its external adversaries, a portrait that sharpened after the border war of 1962 into the durable charge that the left's first loyalty lay across the Himalayas. Employers and the press of the industrial cities maintained a fourth, workaday portrait, the communist as the professional disturber of industrial peace, which needed no doctrine at all.

The movement's answers to the portrait were as various as its workshops, and the ledger shows their unevenness. Against the conspiracy charge it deployed its martyrs and its mass work, the trials' accused, Telangana's dead, the cadres of unimpeachable local standing whose biographies refuted the foreign agent in the only court that mattered, the neighbourhood. Against the national charge it deployed its self-criticisms and its constitutional record, which persuaded the persuadable without ever reaching the founding memory of 1942 in the form the charge preserved it. Against the civilisational charge it deployed its cultural front and its rootedness in the regional languages, with diminishing returns as the century turned. The historian's interest in the portrait is not in its fairness, which varied from the arguable to the fabricated, but in its function: anti-communism was the solvent that repeatedly united the movement's otherwise divided opponents, Congress and caste associations and church in Kerala, employers and the state in the mill districts, the centre and the street in 1959. A movement whose existence reliably assembles its enemies into coalition pays a tax on growth that its own accounts, focused on doctrine, rarely itemise, and the tax belongs in this article's accounting of why the national ceiling held.

## A comparative glance: the Indian movement among the world's parties

Set among the communist parties of the twentieth century, the Indian movement is an anomaly that the comparison clarifies. It was among the largest parties of the non-ruling communist world, and it is nearly alone in having built its strength entirely within a competitive democracy it did not control: no liberation war delivered it a state, no occupying army installed it, and the two occasions on which armed struggle presented itself as a road, 1948 and 1969, it either abandoned the road or watched a fragment break away down it and be destroyed. Its European contemporaries, the great parties of France and Italy, shared the electoral predicament and the subordination problem in milder forms, and both dissolved with the century; the Indian parties persisted, diminished but intact, into the next one, carried by state governments and mass organisations of a density the European parties never possessed. Its Asian contemporaries mostly chose or were forced onto the armed road, with results that ranged from state power to annihilation. The Indian movement's distinctiveness is therefore not its doctrine, which it shared, but its environment and its adaptation: a federal democracy with a literate stronghold here and a peasant movement there rewarded exactly the skills the movement's better half possessed, organisation, patience, the long incumbency, and punished exactly its worse half, the imported schedule, with a consistency no argument could have matched.

The comparison returns the article to its opening judgement by a longer road. The movement's national ceiling was not the product of a single error, and the ledger does not ask the reader to believe that a different choice in 1942, or 1964, or 1996, would by itself have made the communists the government of India. Structural facts stood against any such outcome: the Congress's founding legitimacy, the caste and religious organisation of the electorate, the federal dispersion of the movement's strength, the economy's refusal to produce a classical industrial proletariat of the size the doctrine imagined. What the dated record adds to the structural account is agency and its price. Within the space the structures allowed, the movement repeatedly chose, at the decisive moments, the option that cost it the nation and saved something else, its international faith in 1942, its doctrinal purity in 1964, its doctrinal propriety in 1996, and it repeatedly chose, in the long intervals between the decisive moments, the patient Indian work that built its states and its name. A reader who holds both halves of that sentence holds the movement's history as its own best chroniclers, in their reflective rather than their polemical moods, have come to hold it: as the record of the most substantial attempt made in India to organise the poor as a political subject, flawed at its centre by a subordination it could neither defend nor renounce, and preserved in the country's life long after grander projects were forgotten.

## What the movement proposed: the economic programme across the century

Beneath the strategic arguments ran a programme, and its evolution is a quieter index of the movement's history than the splits. The early movement's economic demands were the international movement's standard articles applied to India: abolition of landlordism without compensation, nationalisation of foreign capital and of the commanding industries, the eight-hour day, and a planned development that would industrialise the country under public direction. The programme of 1951, adopted with the turn to constitutional work, translated the articles into the idiom of a people's democracy: land to the tiller through the abolition of intermediaries, nationalisation of foreign enterprises and of key industries, protection and extension of democratic rights, and a foreign policy of peace and non-alignment, a programme the party offered as the agenda of a government it might join but not yet lead. The striking fact of the following decades is how much of the programme's outer shell the republic itself enacted under other management: zamindari abolition in the 1950s, bank nationalisation and the public sector's expansion in the 1970s, non-alignment as the state's own doctrine. The movement watched its programme adopted in dilution by its principal opponent and drew opposite conclusions in its two halves, the one holding that history was moving its way and cooperation was wisdom, the other that a ruling class enacting reforms to forestall revolution confirmed the need for an independent force to complete them.

The programme's inner core, the abolition of landlordism with its full consequence of land to the tiller, was enacted nowhere by the centre and only partially by the movement's own state governments, whose land reforms, real in Kerala and Bengal, ran into the limits of courts, records and the resistance of the rural propertied within the movement's own electorates. From the 1980s the programme's terrain shifted under it. The movement's state governments, needing investment in economies they did not command, courted the private capital their programmes proposed to supersede, and the contradiction between the programme of transformation and the practice of administering a state within a liberalising federation became the governing tension of the left's incumbency, fought out in the Bengal government's industrialisation drive of the 2000s and in the land acquisition conflicts that ended its long rule. The movement's opposition to the economic liberalisation begun in 1991 was the last occasion on which its programme functioned as a national platform: the parties opposed the new policy in parliament, in their states' fiscal practice as far as they could, and in the common minimum programme they imposed on the coalition they supported, and the opposition, though it delayed and moderated particular measures, did not reverse the direction. A programme that had begun as the design of a new India ended the century as a standard of criticism, a list of the republic's defaults, and the distance between those two functions is the economic form of the story the ledger tells in political entries.

## The movement and linguistic India

The movement's relationship to India's languages is one of its under-recorded strengths, and it shaped the strongholds as surely as any doctrine. The communists were early and consistent champions of the linguistic reorganisation of the provinces, holding that democracy required administration and education in the people's own tongues and that the nationalities of India, whose self-determination the party's wartime resolutions had proclaimed, were in the Indian case most concretely linguistic. The demand for Visalandhra was carried by the Andhra movement as its own cause; in the Malayalam country the party grew inside the Aikya Kerala movement for a united Kerala of the Malayalam speakers; in Bengal its defence of the Bengali language and its refugees fused; and when the republic reorganised its states on linguistic lines in 1956, the map that resulted bore a marked resemblance to the map of the movement's hopes, since the new states of Kerala and Andhra Pradesh were precisely its two southern fortresses and the reorganisation delivered electorates unified by language to parties that had organised in the language for decades.

The linguistic commitment was organisational as much as programmatic. The party worked and published in the regional languages, its journals and its theatre and its songs vernacular from the start, and its internal life was federal in practice long before its programmes conceded federalism in theory: state committees with real authority, leaderships rooted in their languages, a centre that coordinated more than it commanded in the stronghold states. This is one reason the party, for all its centralism, never became a Delhi organisation with provincial branches in the manner of its rivals, and one reason its splits divided along state lines so cleanly, Kerala and Bengal and Tripura departing as blocs in 1964. It is also a reason for the movement's national ceiling, stated now from the other side: a movement whose strength was a federation of linguistic patriotisms, Telugu, Malayalam, Bengali, could speak to India only through translation, and the Hindi-speaking heartland, where the national question was posed in its simplest form, remained the region where its organisation was thinnest and its idiom most foreign. The founders at Tashkent had imagined a party of all India. The party that history actually built was a league of regional movements under a common flag, and much of what followed, the strongholds' durability, the centre's weakness, the splits' geography, is legible in that sentence.

## Prisons and the making of the movement's mind

The colonial and early republican state educated the movement in an institution it did not choose: the prison. From the conspiracy trials onward, jail-going was a normal stage in a communist career, and the movement converted the sentence into a resource with a thoroughness that became part of its identity. The Meerut prisoners studied and argued through their four years, emerging with the doctrinal formation the trial record displays. In the detention camps and central jails of the 1940s and of the repression that followed independence, cadres of different provinces, who would never otherwise have met, lived together for years, ironing out the regional idioms of their communism into a common party culture and forging the personal loyalties, and the personal enmities, that structured the leadership for decades. Leaders wrote in prison or in hiding the texts by which the movement knew itself, histories, programmes and polemics produced in cells and safe houses, and the movement's respect for the written word, its congress documents debated like scripture, descends from that carceral scholarship. The jail also fixed the movement's moral economy: suffering for the cause, borne without complaint and without personal enrichment, became the standard against which leaders were judged, and the conspicuous incorruptibility of the movement's senior figures, men who held state power and died poor, was a prison-formed virtue carried into the secretariat.

The institution had its costs, and the movement's reflective literature names them. A leadership formed in prison and underground learned to value loyalty over initiative and to treat security as the first virtue of organisation, habits that outlasted the conditions that produced them and that contributed, in the free republic, to the party's slowness in renewing itself and its suspicion of the unvetted newcomer. The prison generation also aged in place. Because authority in the movement was earned by sacrifice and seniority, and because the sacrifices of the 1940s were unmatched by any later generation's, the men of the conspiracy trials and the Telangana years, and then their immediate juniors of the 1950s, retained the leadership into extreme old age, and the movement acquired its reputation for gerontocracy honestly: it was governed, into the 1990s, by the survivors of its heroic age, whose authority no internal challenge could match and whose memories were the party's constitution in a sense no document rivalled. The connection between the prison and the split belongs here too. Men who had suffered together in the 1940s divided in 1964 with the bitterness of a family quarrel precisely because the shared suffering had made them a family, and each side experienced the other's choice as a betrayal of the cell and the camp, which is why the split's polemics have a personal heat that the doctrinal differences alone cannot explain.

## Succession: the generations of the movement

The movement's century divides by generations as cleanly as by congresses, and a brief generational history sharpens the record. The founders' generation, the men of Tashkent and Kanpur and the conspiracy trials, were autodidacts and journalists formed before the movement had an Indian tradition to inherit, and they carried its authority into the 1960s: Dange presiding over one party until his death's approach, Muzaffar Ahmad revered in the other as a living link to the founding, their contemporaries filling the central committees of both. The second generation, formed in the 1930s and 1940s, in the kisan sabha, the student movement, Telangana and the unions, supplied the movement's great organisers and its state leaderships: Namboodiripad and his Kerala contemporaries, the Andhra veterans of the armed struggle, the Bengal organisers who built the Left Front, and Jyoti Basu, whose career, from the railway unions of the 1940s to the chief ministership and the declined premiership, spans the movement's whole arc from insurgency to incumbency in a single life. The third generation, recruited in the 1960s and 1970s through the student movement and the food struggles, inherited the state governments and the divided parties as going concerns, and its history is the history of administration and defence rather than of founding.

Generational succession in the movement was never a formality, because each generation's formative experience set its strategic instincts. The founders had known the movement as a conspiracy and never wholly trusted the open; the second generation had known it as a mass movement and a government and trusted the electorate as the founders never could; the third knew it as an establishment and defended it as one. The splits can be re-read in this key with profit: 1964 divided the founders' generation against itself, while the second generation chose sides by state and by loyalty; 1969 was the third generation's revolt against the second's incumbency before the third had fully arrived. The movement's later difficulty in renewing its leadership and its appeal is, in this reading, a succession problem of a classic kind: a revolutionary organisation whose founding generations were formed by sacrifices no successor generation can replicate must either routinise its charisma into institutions or watch it expire with its carriers, and the Indian movement, for all its documentary habits, never solved the problem to its own satisfaction.

## The coalition grammar: the movement's alliances across the century

Step back from the sequence of alliances and a grammar becomes visible, a set of rules the movement applied, with variations, from the 1930s to the 2000s, and the grammar explains episodes that the chronology leaves looking arbitrary. The first rule was that the alliance is always justified by an enemy: the imperialist, the fascist, the communal right, the reactionary offensive, and the identity of the enemy determines the breadth of the front, so that the wider the enemy's threat, the wider the company the movement would keep. The second rule was that the movement must never dissolve into the alliance: it kept its own organisation, its own press and its own mass fronts inside every front it joined, from the Congress Socialist Party to the United Progressive Alliance's support arrangement, and it treated the preservation of that independence as the lesson of its history. The third rule held the alliance temporary by definition, a stage on the road to a realignment in which the movement expects to lead, and allies who understood the rule, as the Congress always did, treated the movement's friendship as an armed truce, while allies who forgot it were periodically surprised by the withdrawal of support at the moment of the movement's choosing, as the coalition of 2004 was surprised in 2008.

The grammar's costs are written across the ledger. Because the alliance was always instrumental, the movement's allies learned to discount its commitments, and the discount raised the price of every subsequent alliance: partners demanded office-sharing, common programmes in writing, and public pledges, the apparatus of distrust visible in the common minimum programme of 2004. Because the movement kept its independence inside every front, its partners accused it, in every decade, of running a party within the party, and the accusation, from the Congress socialists of 1939 to the coalition managers of 2008, was never wholly false. And because the enemy defined the front, the movement's politics acquired a permanent negative pole: it was always clearest about what it was against, and its positive programme, the society it proposed, receded into the documents as the fronts succeeded one another. A historian of the alliances alone could reconstruct the movement's century from these rules, and the reconstruction would show a formation superbly adapted to opposition, to pressure, to the support and withdrawal of support, and correspondingly maladapted to the one role the grammar never contemplated: sole responsibility for a national government, which it declined, when the role was finally offered, on grounds the grammar supplied.

## Martyrdom and memory: the movement's dead

The movement kept its dead with a care that amounted to an institution, and the memory work was load-bearing. The Kayyur martyrs, four young communists of Malabar hanged by the colonial state in 1943 after a clash in which a policeman died, became the Kerala movement's founding sacrifice, commemorated in an annual observance that continues in the district's political calendar and in the names of its institutions. The dead of Punnapra and Vayalar, of Telangana's squads and villages, of the food movements shot down in Calcutta's streets, of the fratricidal years in Bengal and of the repression that broke the Maoist campaign, were entered in the parties' calendars, their portraits hung in the party offices, their families pensioned by the movement's informal welfare, and their stories retold in the songs and plays of the cultural front. The practice had a sociology as well as a sentiment. A cadre movement asking its members for lifetimes of poorly paid work requires a currency beyond wages, and martyrdom supplied it: the assurance that sacrifice is remembered is the whole-timer's pension scheme, and the parties administered the memory as carefully as they administered their funds.

Memory also policed the splits. Each party kept its own martyrology and read the other's as evidence, the Emergency's prisoners against the Emergency's supporters, the victims of the Bengal fratricide assigned to the opposing party's account, so that commemoration, which in an undivided movement would have been its glue, became in the divided one a border fence. The Maoist current built its identity on a counter-memory, recovering the dead of Telangana and Naxalbari as martyrs of a revolution the older parties had betrayed, and the contest over the dead of 1946 to 1951, whose sacrifice all three currents claim, is as exact an emblem as the century offers of a single inheritance divided three ways. For the historian the martyrologies are sources of a particular kind: unreliable on the circumstances of any single death, and wholly reliable on the question of what each party needed its past to mean.

## The problem of strength: what the movement's size never settled

Every account of the movement reaches a point at which it wants a number: how many members, how many votes, how large the unions, and this article's refusal of the contested figures requires a word of explanation that is also a finding. The numbers in this history are not merely uncertain; they are interested. Membership figures were instruments in the movement's internal politics, inflated for congresses, deflated for the police, and claimed differently by the successor parties for the same undivided years. Vote shares, in a first-past-the-post system with fronts and alliances, measure the alliance's arithmetic as much as the party's appeal, and the movement's historians have quarrelled over them accordingly. Casualty and participation figures for the struggles, Telangana above all, were compiled by the state to minimise and by the parties to consecrate. The series' protocol, describe scale qualitatively and flag contest where scale matters, is therefore not an evasion but the only treatment the evidence supports, and the reader who finds it unsatisfying is invited to notice that the quarrel over the numbers is itself one of the movement's historical products.

What can be said without numbers is the shape of the strength. The movement's membership was always small relative to its influence, since the cadre form multiplied each member through the fronts into union members, peasant association members, students, readers and voters in ratios that no rival formation matched. Its vote, concentrated by state and by social bloc, was always larger than its membership by orders of magnitude and always smaller than its ambitions. Its real strength, the quantity that survived every reversal, was organisational density in particular places: the capacity, in the stronghold districts, to put a committee in every village, a union in every workplace, a candidate in every election and a mourner at every funeral, a density that took decades to build and that explains both the strongholds' durability under national decline and the impossibility of replicating them where they had never been built. A movement's history measured in that currency is less dramatic than a history measured in votes, and more truthful: the Indian communists were never close to being India's largest party, and they were, for most of a century, its most thoroughly organised one, and the distance between those two facts is the space in which their whole history happened.

## The movement in print: newspapers, publishing houses, and the paper empire

No Indian political formation invested in print as the communists did, and the paper empire was a strategic asset whose loss in the splits was mourned as keenly as the loss of any union. The movement's journalism began with the little journals of the 1920s, Dange's Socialist, Muzaffar Ahmad's Bengali papers, the Inqilab of Lahore, cyclostyled and confiscated and reborn under new names as the press laws closed on them. Legality in 1942 gave the movement its first great newspaper, the weekly People's War, later People's Age, edited under Joshi's direction with a flair for illustration, reportage and the famine photograph that made it a national institution beyond the party's membership, and the regional dailies followed as the movement's state organisations matured, each stronghold acquiring the daily paper that remains, in the strongholds, a fixture of the morning. The publishing houses, the party's own and the sympathising firms that printed its classics, made Marxist literature available in Indian languages at prices students could pay, and the cheap edition, the pamphlet programme and the study circle's syllabus together built the movement's characteristic reader: the young person radicalised not by a meeting but by a bookstall.

Print shaped the movement's internal life in ways the history has already touched. A party that argues in documents produces members who expect documents, and the great internal controversies were conducted as literature, thesis against thesis, with the membership as jury. The splits were therefore publishing events as much as organisational ones: each new party founded its press, issued its programme and its history, and raced to place its version of the past in the bookstalls before the other's hardened. The documentary history projects, the multi-volume collections of the undivided party's records that the movement's historians edited over decades, were the paper empire's monument, preserving the evidence on which any later history, including this one, depends, while arranging it under the successor parties' competing custodies. The historian's gratitude and suspicion, extended in equal measure, are the correct response to an archive built by its subject.

## The movement and the princely states

A third of British India was not British at all but princely, and the movement's history in the states forms a distinct strand, since the politics of the states combined the agrarian oppression of the countryside with the autocracy of the durbar and forbade the legal organisation the provinces allowed. Communist work in the states proceeded through the states' people's movements, the praja mandals that demanded responsible government, and through the peasant associations that grew where the states' landlordism was rawest. Travancore gave the strand its martyrdom at Punnapra and Vayalar. Hyderabad gave it its revolution in Telangana, a struggle directed first against a princely order whose anachronism, a Muslim sovereign ruling a Hindu-majority peasantry through landlord intermediaries in the age of the Congress ministries, made it the purest available target for the movement's agrarian programme. Kashmir gave it a different role, as communist organisers worked inside the national movement of the valley and influenced the programme of land reform that the state's government enacted after accession, one of the most radical such reforms in the subcontinent, carried out under a leadership the communists advised but did not control.

The strand matters beyond its episodes. The states were the movement's laboratory of the agrarian revolution precisely because their oppression was least mediated: where the landlord was also the state, the distinction between the social and the political struggle collapsed, and the party's village committees in Telangana acquired the character of a counter-government more completely than anywhere in British India. The lesson the movement drew, that its programme advanced fastest where the enemy was most feudal and least legitimate, was true and treacherous in equal measure: true of Hyderabad and Travancore, treacherous when generalised to a republic whose government, whatever its class character, held an electoral legitimacy no durbar had possessed. The insurrectionary line of 1948 can be read as the princely-states lesson misapplied to the republic, and the parliamentary turn of 1951 as its correction, which is one more reason the ledger dates the movement's education from its struggles rather than from its congresses.

## The missing national leader: leadership and the ceiling

One structural fact about the movement is so familiar that its significance escapes notice: in a century of Indian politics conducted substantially through personalities, the communist movement never produced a national leader. It produced organisers of genius and administrators of reputation, Dange in the unions, Namboodiripad in Kerala, Basu in Bengal, Sundarayya in Andhra, but each man's authority was regional or sectoral, and none became a figure in whose name the whole country could be addressed. The absence was partly doctrinal. A cadre party that prized collective leadership and suspected the cult of personality, having watched the cult's career in the international movement and repudiated it in 1956, was institutionally incapable of manufacturing a national tribune, and its leaders were trained to distrust the ambition that national leadership requires. The absence was partly sociological. The movement's strongholds were linguistic, and a leader's eloquence, the currency of national politics in India, did not translate: the Malayalam or Bengali or Telugu orator addressed his own, and the Hindi heartland heard the movement's case through interpreters and pamphlets. And the absence was partly the 1942 cost in biographical form, since the generation that might have supplied a national figure, the men who had organised the wartime expansion, carried the wartime record into every national room they entered, while the succeeding generations were formed in the states, where the movement's real business lay.

The consequence is written into the ledger's national rows. National politics in India rewards the formation that can personify itself, and a movement that could not personify itself could compete nationally only as a coalition partner, a supporting bloc, a pressure on other men's governments, which is precisely the sequence of roles the later entries record: outside supporter in 2004, decliner of the premiership in 1996 on the ground, among others, that its leader would head a government of other parties' making. The party's defenders reply, in its own idiom, that the absence of a supreme leader is a virtue misread as a defect, that the movement's durability derives from institutions rather than from charisma, and that the republic's experience of charismatic leadership gives the defence a force its authors did not always intend. The historian can leave the valuation open and record the mechanism: a movement organised against the cult of the leader governed states for generations and never governed India, and the two facts are not unrelated.

## The balance sheet: what the record shows when the rows are read together

The article closes its argument where the ledger closes its rows. Read together, the entries describe a movement of genuine and unusual substance: the organiser of the subcontinent's first modern unions and its largest peasant struggles, the author of land reforms that outlasted its governments, the keeper of a cultural and intellectual presence that shaped the republic's universities and letters, and a political formation whose local honesty and organisational stamina set a standard its rivals privately acknowledged. The same entries describe a movement with a recurring structural fault, located not in its members' courage or its doctrine's content but in the ordering of its loyalties at the moments when loyalties conflicted. Three times in the century, in 1942, in the terms of the 1964 division, and in 1996, the movement at the national moment chose the claim that was not the nation's, the international war, the international schism's categories, the doctrine of permissible office, and three times it paid with the national standing that no subsequent state-level success restored. Between those moments, its Indian work built everything it is remembered for. The final judgement the evidence supports is therefore neither of the two the movement's friends and enemies keep in stock. It is not the story of a foreign agency unmasked, since agencies do not build Telanganas or register sharecroppers or die poor in office after thirty years. It is not the story of a revolution betrayed by its rivals, since the decisive defeats were self-administered and are recorded as such in the movement's own hand. It is the story of a national movement with an international nervous system, whose history is the longest Indian experiment in what that combination can build and what it must cost, and whose record, kept honestly, is the best argument its inheritors possess and the hardest one they have to answer.

The record also has a use beyond the inheritors, and the article's method was designed for it. Every political movement keeps two sets of books, the programme it publishes and the decisions it takes, and the distance between them, measured over a century with the reasoning of each decision stated before its consequence, is the truest biography a movement can be given. The Indian communists, to their credit and their cost, wrote more of both sets down than any of their rivals, which is why their history can be audited in a way that theirs cannot. The audit's conclusion is the ledger's: build where the assessment is your own, beware the moment when another capital's war, another centre's quarrel or another doctrine's schedule asks for the nation's place in your order of loyalties, and keep the books honestly enough that your successors can find the entries you would rather have hidden.

## The century's end: the movement after its world ended

The last decade of the movement's century posed a question no congress resolution could answer in the old vocabulary: what is an Indian communist party for, in an India liberalising its economy, after the disappearance of the socialist camp whose existence had anchored its internationalism since 1920? The parties' answers divided along the familiar seam. The party keeping the old name, whose orientation toward the Soviet Union had been the steadiest fact of its identity, absorbed the disappearance as a civilisational loss to be mourned and analysed, and repositioned itself as a party of the democratic and secular opposition, its programme rewritten for a world of one superpower it opposed and a domestic right it opposed more. The party formed in 1964, born in the quarrel against Soviet revisionism, discovered that anti-revisionism without its object left its own doctrine in need of the restatement its congresses supplied, in documents that conceded a changed world while insisting that the Indian analysis of 1964 stood. The Maoist current, underground and fragmented, treated the collapse as the vindication of its oldest charge, that the established parties' world had been a fraud, and drew from the wreckage a recruitment among the dispossessed of the forest districts that belongs to its own article's record.

Beneath the doctrinal adjustments, the strongholds were changing in ways the movement's categories handled awkwardly. The Bengal government's long incumbency had made it responsible for an industrial decline it had inherited and could not reverse within its powers, and its late turn toward courting private industry for the factories its electorate demanded brought it into conflict, over land acquisition in the districts, with the peasantry its history had made its own, a conflict whose mishandling in the following decade cost it the state after a generation in office. Kerala's alternation continued, its governments administering a remittance economy whose prosperity owed little to any party's programme. Tripura's front endured longest of the three. The movement entered the new century as it had entered the old one's second half, formidably organised in its places, eloquent in its documents, and confronted by an India whose questions, of caste assertion, religious mobilisation, informal work and aspirational consumption, were not the questions its theory had been built to answer. Whether that confrontation is the movement's ending or merely another unfavourable terrain of the kind it has crossed before is a judgement this article's discipline forbids, since it concerns the living present rather than the dated record. What the record establishes is that the movement has been written off at intervals of roughly twenty years since 1929, by opponents who mistook each of its defeats for its dissolution, and that the obituaries have so far shared a single defect: they reckoned without the organisation, the memory and the local rootedness that this history has tried to weigh.

## The other lefts: socialists, and the argument over the inheritance

The communist movement never held the Indian left to itself, and its relations with the socialist formations that shared its vocabulary are a subplot with its own lessons. The Congress Socialist Party of 1934 was the first and most consequential of these formations: founded by a generation that included Jayaprakash Narayan and Ram Manohar Lohia, it gave the communists their united front opening and then, as the decade closed, expelled them from its counsels in a quarrel over exactly the questions this article has tracked, the communists' discipline, their external allegiance and their wartime line, on which the socialist leaders' indictment, drafted in the 1940s, remains the most formidable statement of the subordination objection from within the left itself. After independence the socialists constituted themselves as parties of democratic socialism, opposed to Congress rule and to communist discipline alike, and the two lefts competed for the same constituencies, the unions, the kisans, the students, with a bitterness that the common enemy rarely softened. Lohia's socialism, with its attack on caste as the central Indian reality and its suspicion of both Congress and communists as parties of the twice-born, posed the question the communists were slowest to answer, and the argument between the communist analysis of class and the Lohiaite analysis of caste runs under the whole later history of the Indian left, surfacing in the caste and class debate treated elsewhere in this cluster.

The practical consequence of the divided left was felt in the republic's arithmetic. Where the lefts combined, as in the united fronts of 1967 and the Left Front's long Bengal incumbency, they governed; where they divided, the Congress and later its successors harvested the constituencies both claimed. Reunification was periodically attempted and periodically wrecked on the same rocks as the communist splits themselves, the rival accounts of 1942, of the Emergency, of the Soviet Union, each formation's identity being mortgaged to its version of the shared past. The movement's own historians treat the socialist formations with a respect their polemics did not always show, acknowledging that the democratic socialists kept alive, through the decades of the movement's own authoritarian temptations, a version of the left's promise that the communists' record could not supply, and that the Indian left's tragedy was distributive as much as doctrinal: it possessed, in its several parts, the mass organiser, the democratic conscience, the agrarian base and the intellectual system, and never succeeded in holding them in one organisation at one time. That judgement, which no single party's history would print, is among the conclusions this article's method exists to make stateable.

## The Emergency underground and the movement's education in democracy

One experience of the Emergency years deserves separate record, because its effects on the opposing party outlasted the Emergency itself. For the cadres of the 1964 party, the twenty-one months of underground and prison were a second education, administered this time not by a colonial state but by an Indian government their rivals supported. They shared cells and safe houses with socialists, with trade unionists of every federation, with members of organisations their doctrine classed as enemies, and the shared persecution built a practical democratic solidarity that the party's theory had not envisaged: men and women who had agreed on nothing except the illegitimacy of rule by decree. The party emerged speaking a language it had not used before with the same conviction, the language of democratic rights as ends in themselves, of the constitution as a patrimony to be defended rather than a form to be used, and the change registered in its subsequent practice, in the Left Front's self-presentation as a guardian of democratic norms in its states and in the party's national role as a coalition partner whose conditions were constitutional as much as programmatic. Historians of the party note the irony without embarrassment: the movement that had treated bourgeois democracy, in its founding texts, as a form of class rule to be transcended, was educated into its defence by an Indian government's attempt to suspend it, and the education held. The party that supported the Emergency learned the same lesson by the opposite road, in the Bhatinda review, and the convergence of the two parties, after 1978, on the defence of the democratic order is the closest the movement came, in the century, to a shared conclusion drawn from a shared experience differently undergone.

## The movement and the world after independence

The movement's internationalism did not end with the colonial period; it changed its object, and the post-independence record completes the theme this article has tracked from Tashkent. In the republic's first decades the movement's foreign policy was a politics of solidarity with the decolonising world and of support, in the main, for the Indian state's own non-alignment, which the parties of both halves praised as an independent course while pressing it toward firmer alignment with the socialist camp, the right as a matter of conviction, the left as a matter of anti-imperialist strategy tempered by its quarrel with China. The border war of 1962 broke the theme's unity for a generation, since the two parties' foreign policies diverged precisely where the Indian state's interests were sharpest, and the divergence shadowed every subsequent argument: the party of the old name could present itself as the left that had stood by the nation in its wars, while the party of 1964 carried into each decade the suspicion its 1962 position had earned, a suspicion its own later nationalism, in office in the border states and in its defence of Indian positions against external pressure, never wholly dispelled.

The war of 1971, which created Bangladesh, showed the theme in repair and is the strongest evidence against a simple subordination reading of the later movement. Both parties supported the Indian intervention and the liberation of Bangladesh, the left party with its Bengal organisation working among the refugees who flooded its state, and the alignment of the movement's internationalism with the nation's cause was on that occasion complete and unforced, since the cause was simultaneously anti-imperialist in the movement's categories and Indian in every voter's. The Emergency complicated the record again, the supporting party's internationalism extending to a defence of the government's foreign friends against its domestic critics. The coalition decades returned the theme to the centre in its oldest form. The left's opposition to the nuclear agreement of 2008, the act that ended its national influence, was argued in the vocabulary of 1947, independent foreign policy against subordination to a superpower, and whatever the merits of the position, its genealogy is the article's point: a movement formed by an international connection, whose gravest wounds were inflicted by that connection's demands, ended its century still conducting its national politics in the grammar of the world's alignment, and still paying, and refusing to regret, the grammar's price. The historian who wants the movement's foreign policy in one sentence can take that one; the one who wants it in full must read the ledger's rows in sequence, which is what they are for.

## The Gandhi question: the argument the movement never finished

No account of the movement's relation to the nation is complete without its longest argument, the argument with Gandhi, which ran from the 1920s to his assassination and resumed, in altered terms, for decades afterward. The communist critique was formed early and changed little in its essentials: Gandhi's leadership, on the movement's reading, mobilised the masses magnificently and restrained them fatally, calling off movements at the moment of their greatest pressure, preaching trusteeship to the mill owner and patience to the mill hand, and clothing a bourgeois national programme in the language of renunciation that disarmed the poor. The reading had evidence, the withdrawals of 1922 and the settlements of 1931 and the Congress ministries' police firings of 1937 to 1939, and the movement's polemics deployed it for decades. Gandhi's counter-judgement, delivered across the same decades in interviews and letters, was more personal and in its way as damaging: he praised the communists' sacrifice and their service of the poor, doubted their Indian rootedness, and rejected class war as a foreign method that would corrupt the nation's freedom in the winning of it, a judgement the nationalist mainstream absorbed as common sense and the movement never succeeded in answering at the level at which it was made.

The argument outlived both parties to it because it was never only about tactics. It was about the moral form of Indian politics, whether the nation's liberation required the saint's discipline of self-suffering or the organiser's discipline of class interest, and the two movements divided the freedom struggle's inheritance along that line, the Congress taking the state and the communists taking the unions, the villages of Telangana and the seminar rooms. Later generations of the Indian left, including voices inside the communist parties, returned to the argument with less confidence than the founders had brought to it, conceding that the Gandhian idiom had achieved a moral reach into the Indian countryside that the movement's vocabulary never matched, and that the dismissal of trusteeship and village industry as mere ideology had cost the movement the allegiance of strata it needed. The concession belongs to the ledger's general pattern in its cultural form: the movement's theory, formed elsewhere, persistently undervalued the indigenous moral languages in which India actually conducted its politics, and paid for the undervaluation at every national moment, in 1942 most of all, when the nation followed the man its theory had classified and the movement stood, with its correct analysis, outside the crowd. A history of Indian communism that omits the Gandhi argument omits the explanation of its tone, the note of embattled correctness that its friends loved and its country could not.

## For students and exam candidates

This history is examined, in political science and modern history papers, through its episodes rather than as a narrative, and the article is built to be disassembled accordingly. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The turning points ledger is designed for the seminar room as a decision-analysis exercise, and it works best when the rows are separated from their outcomes. Give a group one row's date and reasoning, the founding dispute, the wartime reversal, the insurrectionary turn, the declined premiership, and require a defence of the decision from the information available at the time, before the consequence column is revealed. The exercise teaches the article's central distinction, between a decision that was wrong and a decision whose price was known and paid, and it disciplines the retrospective moralising to which movement histories are prone. The splits repay the complementary exercise: distribute the two parties' accounts of 1964 as unattributed documents, and have the group identify, from internal evidence alone, which account belongs to which party and what each account cannot afford to say. A comparison with the anti-colonial movements treated in [the comparative case studies of anti-colonial Marxism](/2017/05/01/anti-colonial-marxism-case-studies/) extends the seminar to the question the Indian case poses in its sharpest form, of what a movement owes its nation when its doctrine is international. Assessment should reward the separation of accounts from record and penalise the resolution of disputes the evidence leaves open.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Two entry points serve the working researcher best. The founding dispute opens the movement's documentary archive at its most instructive point, since the rival claims require the handling of émigré records, colonial police abstracts and party commemoration as three different kinds of evidence, and the dispute's literature is compact enough to master and rich enough to teach method. The wartime episode opens the question of the movement's sources at their most sensitive, since the record includes the party's own published defence, the nationalist indictment, the intelligence watch kept on a legal party by the state it was supporting, and the later self-criticisms, four archives that agree on the sequence and on nothing else. Journalists covering the movement's successor parties will find the contemporary formations unintelligible without the split's mechanics, the division of unions, presses and martyrologies described in this article, and without the grammar of alliance and withdrawal that still governs their conduct in coalitions. Throughout, the caution of the sources section applies: the movement wrote its own record with unmatched industry, and the researcher's task is neither to believe it nor to discount it, but to read each document as an act by the party that produced it, dated, interested and, for that reason, evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When was the Communist Party of India founded?

The honest answer is that the movement has two claimed foundings and the dispute is part of its history. A party was proclaimed by Indian émigrés at Tashkent in 1920, under M. N. Roy's leadership, and a unified organisation was constituted inside India at the Kanpur conference of December 1925, where the communist groups of the great cities elected a central executive. One successor party keeps the earlier date, the other the later. A third account treats 1920 as the proclamation and 1925 as the organisational founding, and accepts both as real events of different kinds.

### Q: Was the party founded at Tashkent or Kanpur?

Both events happened, and the parties divide over which kind of act constitutes a founding. The Tashkent claim rests on the proclamation of the party's name and programme by its émigrés under the Communist International, with cadres and literature then sent into India. The Kanpur claim rests on the constitution of a single organisation on Indian soil by the Indian groups themselves, with an elected leadership. The dispute persists because it encodes a deeper argument about the movement's identity, between international continuity and indigenous organisation, which the split of 1964 inherited.

### Q: What was the Meerut Conspiracy Case?

It was the largest of the colonial conspiracy trials, opened in 1929 after the arrest of communist and trade union leaders across India, and it ran for four years. Dozens of accused, including three English communists, were charged with conspiring to deprive the King-Emperor of sovereignty. The accused used the proceedings as a platform, stating their doctrine on the record to a national press, and the trial gave the movement a publicity and a consolidated leadership it had never possessed. Sentences in 1933 were heavy and were substantially reduced on appeal.

### Q: Why did the communists oppose the Quit India movement?

Because, following Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941, the party had adopted the people's war line, reclassifying the conflict as a people's war against fascism in which the Allied effort had to be supported and nothing allowed to impede it. When the Congress launched Quit India in August 1942, the party therefore campaigned against the rising, called instead for the leaders' release and a national government by agreement, and accepted legality and growth under a government whose prisons were filling with nationalists. The stand cost the party its standing in the national movement for a generation.

### Q: What was the Telangana armed struggle?

It was the armed peasant movement conducted by the communists in the Telangana region of Hyderabad state from 1946 to 1951, against the landlord order of the princely state and, after Hyderabad's absorption in 1948, against the Indian Union. Village committees abolished forced labour, cancelled debts and redistributed the landlords' surplus land across a large countryside, defended by guerrilla squads. Figures for villages, land and casualties vary sharply between sources of different affiliations and are treated in this series as contested. The party withdrew the struggle in October 1951 as part of its turn to electoral work.

### Q: Why did the Communist Party of India split?

Three substantive causes and an organisational occasion produced the split of 1964. The causes were the attitude to the governing Congress, whether it was a progressive national force to cooperate with or the party of the possessing classes to oppose; the stand taken toward the border war of 1962, during which left leaders were jailed while the party chairman supported the government; and the dispute dividing the Soviet and Chinese parties, which supplied the vocabulary of revisionism and dogmatism. The occasion was a leadership quarrel, sharpened by disputed letters attributed to the chairman. The departing wing claimed to defend the movement against revisionism; the remaining leadership claimed to defend it against factionalism.

### Q: What happened to the left during the Emergency?

The two main parties chose opposite courses, and the opposition between them is the episode's whole significance. One supported the Emergency of 1975 to 1977, in its own terms as a blow against a right-reactionary offensive, and shared in the discredit when the Emergency fell. The other opposed it, suffered arrests and censorship, and emerged to lead the Left Front to power in West Bengal in 1977. The supporting party's congress at Bhatinda in 1978 formally acknowledged its support as a serious error.

### Q: Did a communist almost become Prime Minister?

Yes, in 1996, and the episode is the standard illustration of the movement's national ceiling. After a hung parliament, the United Front coalition's consensus choice for prime minister was Jyoti Basu, the communist chief minister of West Bengal. His party's central committee declined to permit it, reasoning that the party could not take responsibility for a government whose programme lay beyond its shaping, and the coalition turned to another leader. Basu accepted the decision at the time and later described it, in a phrase that entered the language, as a historic blunder. The party has neither adopted his verdict nor formally withdrawn its reasoning.

### Q: Why did the Left leave the coalition government?

The left parties supported the Congress-led coalition from outside after the 2004 election, under a common minimum programme, and withdrew in July 2008 over the agreement on civil nuclear cooperation with the United States. In their own terms, the agreement subordinated India's independent foreign policy to an American strategic design and compromised the country's strategic autonomy; in the government's terms, it served energy security and national standing. When the government proceeded with the agreement's international safeguards stage, the supporting parties withdrew, the government survived the resulting confidence vote with other support, and the left's parliamentary strength fell sharply at the following election.

### Q: What are the main turning points in the movement's history?

The turning points ledger in this article lists thirteen: the dual founding of 1920 and 1925; the Meerut trial of 1929 to 1933; the united front recovery of the later 1930s; the wartime reversal of 1941 to 1942; the Telangana struggle and its withdrawal; the insurrectionary line and its abandonment by 1951; the Kerala government and dismissal of 1957 to 1959; the division over the border war of 1962; the split of 1964; the breakaway of 1967 to 1969; the opposite choices of the Emergency; the declined premiership of 1996; and the coalition support and withdrawal of 2004 to 2008. Each is entered with its reasoning and its consequence, which is the article's method in miniature.

### Q: How does the cost of the wartime reversal grade the movement's record?

It grades it as the record's heaviest single debit, against which the credits have to be entered separately. The wartime decision bought legality, growth and the organisation that led the postwar struggles, and those credits are real. But the decision separated the movement from the nation's founding experience at its climax, fixed, for the nationalist mainstream, its identity as the party that stood aside in 1942, and capped its national development for the remainder of its century. On the ledger's method, the episode is therefore neither a footnote nor the whole story: it is the entry the other entries have to be read around.

### Q: What should a student separate between a party's account of a split and the record of it?

Three things. First, the sequence of decisions, dates and documents, which the rival accounts usually record identically and which constitutes the record. Second, each account's selection, what it elevates and what it buries, since the parties to the Indian split each elevate one of the three causes and bury the others. Third, each account's function, since an official history is written to hold an organisation together and will concede exactly what the concession protects. A student who reads a party's history for the record will be misled; a student who reads it as evidence of what the party needs to believe will learn the split twice over.

### Q: Why did the movement oppose the mass campaign and then reverse course?

The opposition of 1942 followed from the people's war line adopted after Germany's invasion of the Soviet Union; the reversal of course came in stages rather than as a single recantation. The party defended the line through the war, conceded the political cost in its postwar reviews, and by the 1950s had absorbed the episode into its self-criticism while continuing to defend the anti-fascist reasoning. A second reversal, the abandonment of the insurrectionary line of 1948 by 1951, shows the same rhythm at higher speed: a line adopted on an international reading of the moment, tested against Indian conditions, and abandoned with a self-criticism that assigned the blame to the reading rather than to the readers' successors.

### Q: How does the turning points ledger order the movement's history?

By decision rather than by period. Each row takes one dated decision, states the justification offered at the time in the decision-makers' own terms, records what followed, and closes with the verdict the movement later passed on the decision. Ordered so, the history shows a pattern that chronological narrative alone conceals: rows decided on an Indian reading of Indian conditions close in durable construction, while rows governed by a priority set elsewhere close in costs repaid over years. The ledger is the findable artifact the article builds, made to be reused in teaching and revision, and its columns, not its verdicts, are the method a student should carry to other movements' records.

### Q: What separates a national commitment from a subordinate one in this debate?

On the critics' formulation, the test is behaviour at the moment of conflict: a national commitment is one that prevails when it collides with an external allegiance, and a subordinate one yields. The movement's defenders answer that the test is rigged, since the anti-fascist priority of 1941 to 1945 was itself adopted by Indian communists as their own conclusion about India's interest in a world at war, and that a commitment does not become foreign by being shared. The debate is not resolvable from the documents, because both sides accept the sequence and divide over its meaning, and the article therefore leaves it where the record leaves it: as the question the movement's whole history poses, and the one its self-criticisms circle without closing.
