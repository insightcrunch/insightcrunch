The Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 rests on a single, radical legal move: it treats a wild animal not as a resource waiting to be used, nor as a pest waiting to be destroyed, but as property of the government, placed beyond private taking altogether. From that one move everything else in the Wildlife Protection Act follows. Hunting a scheduled animal is a criminal offence rather than a regulated sport. A trophy or an animal article belongs to the state unless a citizen holds a certificate saying otherwise. Trade in the most protected species is prohibited outright. And the habitat of those animals is fenced off, in law if not always on the ground, by a graded system of protected areas that reaches from the strict national park down to the community reserve declared over private land with its owners' consent.

![The Wildlife Protection Act 1972, its hunting ban, schedules and protected area network explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Yet the statute carries a tension it has never resolved, and the tension is the key to reading it well. The Act was drafted in 1972 against a single, identifiable enemy: the hunter, the poacher and the trader, who between them had driven the tiger, the elephant, the rhinoceros and dozens of other species towards disappearance. Against that enemy the Act is complete and severe. The threat that defines wildlife policy in practice is a different one: the leopard that takes livestock at the edge of a village, the elephant herd that flattens a paddy field in a night, the wild pig that makes a smallholding uneconomic to farm. For that threat the Act has almost nothing. Its only instruments addressed to an animal living among people are a permission to kill it, granted case by case by the chief wildlife warden, and a power in the Central Government to strip a whole species of protection in a named area by declaring it vermin. Compensation for the farmer who loses a crop belongs to state schemes that sit outside the Act entirely.

This article gives the full working model of the statute: how Parliament came to enact a law on a subject that belonged to the states, what the prohibitions actually cover, how the schedules worked before the restructuring of 2022 and how they work after it, what each category of protected area permits and forbids, which institutions approve and enforce, and where the courts have redrawn the lines. It also gives the honest assessment the subject requires. The recovery of the tiger under this regime is a genuine achievement, built on the statute's severity. The cost of that recovery is borne overwhelmingly by poor rural residents who live beside protected areas, lose crops and livestock and sometimes their lives, and have the least voice in the structures that decide. A serious account has to hold both halves of that record at once.

## The India the Act was written for

To understand why the statute takes the absolute form it does, a reader has to picture the legal landscape it replaced. Before 1972, wild animals in India were governed by a patchwork. A central statute of 1912, the Wild Birds and Animals Protection Act, protected a short list of species against hunting in closed seasons, and the Indian Forest Act, 1927 allowed forest officers to regulate hunting inside reserved forests, but across most of the country the taking of wild animals was a matter for state game rules, princely-state shikar departments, and private arrangements between landowners and sportsmen. Hunting was a licensed recreation, a source of revenue, and for the rulers of the former princely states a mark of status. The animal itself had no legal standing of its own; the law regulated who might kill it, when, and on payment of what fee.

The results were visible in the population figures that conservationists assembled at the end of the 1960s. The tiger, hunted for sport, for bounty in some districts, and increasingly for the value of its skin, had fallen from the tens of thousands estimated at the start of the century to a remnant. The first country-wide tiger census, conducted in 1972 using the methods of the period, produced an estimate of about 1,827 animals, a figure historians treat with care because the counting method was crude, but whose direction nobody disputed. The Asiatic lion survived in a single forest in Gujarat. The one-horned rhinoceros of the Brahmaputra floodplain had been reduced by poaching for its horn. The cheetah had been declared extinct in India in 1952, the last recorded animals shot in the years after independence. Against that record, the argument that regulation had failed was difficult to answer.

The states had not been idle in the interval; they had been uneven. A few produced serious protection statutes of their own, of which the Bombay Wild Animals and Wild Birds Protection Act, 1951 is the standard example, with closed seasons, licensing and game reserves, while others retained rules under which crop protection and bounty payments rewarded the killing of the same predators the naturalists were counting down. The patchwork's defect was not only weakness but arbitrage: a species protected across one border was lawful quarry across another, and the skin trade organised itself around the seams. A uniform national floor was the Board's answer long before it was the government's, and the Article 252 device was the only constitutional road to it in 1972.

The institutional groundwork for a national law had been laid two decades earlier, when the Government of India constituted the Indian Board for Wild Life in 1952 as an advisory body, with the naturalist E. P. Gee among its early influential members. The Board pressed for uniform protection across the states, and the political moment arrived with the Stockholm Conference on the Human Environment in 1972, which India attended and after which environmental legislation acquired a new urgency in New Delhi. The Wild Life (Protection) Bill was framed against that background: alarm at the measured decline of large mammals, embarrassment at the extinction of the cheetah within living memory, and a determination that the remaining megafauna would be protected by a single national code rather than by the uneven game rules of the states.

The year after enactment, the statute acquired its operational flagship. Project Tiger was launched on 1 April 1973, begun at Corbett National Park in what is presently Uttarakhand, covering nine tiger reserves and directed initially by Kailash Sankhala. The project supplied the money, the management doctrine and the publicity; the Act supplied the legal machinery, the offence, the protected area categories and the presumption of state ownership that made enforcement possible. The two have been intertwined ever since, and the tiger's fortunes became the measure by which the whole statute is publicly judged.

The nine founding reserves, Bandipur, Corbett, Kanha, Manas, Melghat, Palamau, Ranthambore, Similipal and the Sundarbans, were chosen to sample the tiger's Indian range rather than to maximise early numbers, and the management doctrine Sankhala carried into them, a strictly protected core from which incompatible uses were withdrawn, surrounded by a buffer in which regulated human use continued, became the template Indian conservation has argued about ever since. It is recognisably the ancestor of the core and buffer structure that the amendment of 2006 wrote into Section 38V for tiger reserves, and its premise, that a breeding population of a large carnivore needs an inviolate space at the centre of its landscape, is the premise the fortress reading of the Act rests on. Its critics, a line of scholarship and activism associated with researchers of forest communities, answer that the core was made inviolate in landscapes that had supported settled cultivation and grazing for generations, and that the doctrine priced the tiger's security in other people's displacement from the start.

The counting question shadowed the project from its first census. The 1972 estimate rested on pugmark tracing, a method whose confidence exceeded its reliability, and when camera trapping and capture-recapture statistics replaced it in the estimation of 2006, the national figure fell to 1,411, a number that measured a better method as much as a worse population. That methodological break matters to any honest use of the recovery figures quoted later in this article: the rise from 1,411 in 2006 to the 2022 estimate was counted by substantially consistent modern methods and is real growth, while comparisons reaching back to 1972 cross a change in the instrument and prove less than they appear to. A statute that made counting the state's business also made the state the referee of its own success, and the estimation exercises, conducted by the National Tiger Conservation Authority with the Wildlife Institute of India and opened in their modern form to independent statistical scrutiny, are the system's attempt to keep that refereeing honest.

## A central law on a state subject

The first technical puzzle the Act presents is constitutional. In 1972, the protection of wild animals and birds stood in the State List of the Seventh Schedule, which meant that only state legislatures could ordinarily legislate on it. Parliament had no direct entry to rely on. The route it used was Article 252 of the Constitution, which permits Parliament to legislate on a state subject where the legislatures of two or more states resolve that it should, with the resulting Act applying to the resolving states and open to adoption by others.

The Act records that route in its own preamble history. Eleven state legislatures passed the resolutions that allowed Parliament to act, and the resulting statute applied at first to those states, extending to others as they adopted it. This is why the statute book describes the Wild Life (Protection) Act as enacted under Article 252, and why, for a period, its territorial reach depended on adoption rather than on the automatic operation that a Concurrent List statute enjoys. Jammu and Kashmir, which had its own wildlife protection legislation, stood outside the central Act for decades and came within it only through the reorganisation of 2019. The settled constitutional position is stated fully in the framework survey of [India's environmental law as a connected system](/2014/08/01/environmental-law-india-complete-guide/), where this Act appears as the Article 252 member of the family.

The constitutional atmosphere of the enactment deserves a paragraph, because the courts still breathe it when they read the Act. The Stockholm Conference at which the statute's urgency was formed is remembered in Indian law for the Prime Minister's address arguing that poverty itself was the deepest pollution and that environmental protection in a poor country had to be built through development rather than against it, a framing that explains the Indian environmental statutes' recurring structure of strong prohibitions administered through discretionary permissions. Four years after the Act, the Forty-second Amendment wrote the environment into the constitutional text itself: Article 48A directs the state to endeavour to protect and improve the environment and to safeguard the forests and wild life of the country, and Article 51A(g) makes it the duty of every citizen to have compassion for living creatures. Neither provision is enforceable by itself, but together they became the interpretive lens through which the higher judiciary reads this Act, cited in the lion translocation judgment, in the animal welfare cases and in the climate judgment alike, and they are the reason a statute of 1972 is so often construed in the courts as if it were a constitutional text in miniature.

The arrangement lasted only four years in its original form. The Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976 transferred forests and the protection of wild animals and birds from the State List to the Concurrent List, inserting them as new entries alongside the transfer that is traced fully in [the account of the 42nd Amendment and its reconstruction of Centre and state powers](/2010/02/15/42nd-amendment-act-1976-explained/). From 1977, Parliament and the state legislatures shared competence over wildlife, and the central Act applied across the country as ordinary concurrent legislation rather than as an adopted statute. The change mattered in practice as well as in doctrine. It allowed the Centre to amend the Act for the whole country at once, as it did in 1991, 2002, 2006 and 2022, and it made the national institutions the Act later created constitutionally straightforward. It also left a permanent federal texture inside the statute: the day-to-day machinery of protection, the chief wildlife wardens, the state boards and the declaration of sanctuaries and national parks, remained with the states, while the schedules, the trade controls and the apex institutions sat in New Delhi.

The Act as enacted is the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, Act 53 of 1972, which received the assent of the President on 9 September 1972. Its formal identity repays attention because the statute is constantly miscited. The short title retains the two-word spelling "Wild Life", a drafting survival that the amended text has kept. The long title originally promised an Act for the protection of wild animals, birds and plants; the amendment of 2022 recast it to speak of the conservation, protection and management of wild life, a small textual sign of the shift from a purely prohibitory code towards a management code that the later amendments carried through.

## The architecture of the statute

The Act is organised by function rather than by species, and a reader who grasps the functional map can find any provision in it. The opening chapter fixes the definitions, of which four do the heavy lifting. A "wild animal" is an animal specified in the schedules and found wild in nature, which ties the entire protective regime to the schedule lists. "Hunting" is defined with a breadth examined in detail below. "Trophy" and "animal article" extend the Act's grip from the living animal to everything made from it after death. And "protected area" collects the four notified categories, sanctuary, national park, conservation reserve and community reserve, into a single defined term.

The chapters that follow distribute the work. One chapter constitutes the authorities: the Director of Wild Life Preservation at the Centre, the chief wildlife wardens in the states, and the boards that advise them. A long chapter states the core prohibitions, the hunting ban, the narrow permissions, and the rule of government ownership. A further chapter deals with trade and commerce in wild animals, animal articles and trophies, built around declarations of stock, certificates of ownership and licences. The protected areas occupy their own chapter, which runs from the declaration of a sanctuary through the settlement of rights inside it to the declaration of a national park and, in subdivisions added later, to conservation reserves, community reserves and tiger reserves. The institutions added by amendment, the Central Zoo Authority, the National Tiger Conservation Authority and the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, sit in inserted chapters of their own. The closing chapters supply the enforcement machinery: entry, search, seizure and arrest; penalties; the presumption that reverses the burden of proof on possession; and the miscellaneous powers, among them the vermin declaration, that give the executive its safety valves.

A reader opening the Act itself will find the chapter arrangement confirming the functional map. Chapter I carries the short title, the commencement structure, which originally ran by adoption, and the definitions. Chapter II constitutes the officers and boards. Chapter III states the hunting prohibition, the permission provisions and the ownership rule, and a short inserted chapter from 1991 governs the Central Zoo Authority's recognition and standards regime for zoos. Chapter IV contains the protected areas: sanctuaries, then an inserted set of sections on conservation and community reserves, then national parks, and after 2006 the tiger chapter and the crime bureau chapter stand as lettered insertions inside the same region of the book. Chapter V carries the trade and commerce provisions, with the CITES chapter of 2022 inserted within it. Chapter V-A, an earlier insertion, prohibits trade in articles derived from certain animals altogether. Chapter VI supplies prevention and detection of offences, the entry, search, seizure and arrest powers, and Chapter VIA adds the forfeiture of property derived from illegal hunting and trade, a proceeds-of-crime layer aimed at the trade's organisers rather than its foot soldiers. Chapter VII, the miscellaneous chapter, houses penalties and the presumption alongside the vermin power, the reward provision, the power to make rules and the repeal of inconsistent state laws. The lettered insertions are the archaeology of the statute made visible: every era of Indian wildlife policy has written itself into the book as a new stratum rather than a rewrite, which is why the pillar reader navigates by function and amendment year together.

Two structural features deserve emphasis because they explain the Act's character. The first is that protection is schedule-driven. The statute does not protect wild animals in the abstract; it protects the species Parliament has listed, at the level the listing prescribes, and an unlisted species stands largely outside the code. That design makes the contents of the schedules the true battleground of wildlife policy, and it is why the restructuring of 2022, which rewrote the lists wholesale, was more than a tidying exercise. The second feature is that the Act separates the species question from the place question. Schedules answer which animals may never be taken. Protected areas answer where human activity is restricted for habitat's sake. A tiger in a paddy field and a tiger in a national park carry the same species protection and entirely different land consequences, and the failure to keep those two questions apart is the source of most public confusion about the Act.

## Who and what the Act binds, and where it stops

The Act's reach is wide but it is not a general animal welfare code, and marking its edges prevents the commonest citizen error, which is to assume it governs every cruelty or every animal. It binds every person in India, citizen, company and government department alike, and the animal it governs is the scheduled one: a wild animal means an animal specified in Schedule I or Schedule II and found wild in nature, with captive animals, animal articles, trophies and meat drawn in by their own definitions. A domesticated species is outside the schedules and outside the Act, however it is treated; cruelty to it is the province of the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960, a statute with a different structure and far weaker penalties, and the boundary between the two codes is the first question in any real case involving a captive or a farm animal. The elephant illustrates the boundary's oddity: wild or captive, it sits in Schedule I, so the ownership, transfer and possession provisions of this Act govern the temple elephant and the circus elephant as they govern the wild herd, which is why the litigation over captive elephants has been fought inside this statute.

The plant kingdom enters narrowly. Specified plants listed in Schedule III may not be picked, uprooted, damaged, acquired or collected except under licence for education, scientific research or propagation in recognised institutions, and their trade is regulated; the rest of India's flora is protected, where it is protected, by the forest statutes and not by this Act. Aquatic life enters through the schedules rather than through any general principle: listed fish, molluscs and marine mammals are protected as wild animals, while fisheries as an industry remain under fisheries legislation. Habitat, as distinct from the creatures in it, is protected only inside the notified categories; the Act contains no general prohibition on destroying the habitat of a scheduled species outside a protected area, a gap that has mattered in litigation over grasslands and wetlands, where the great Indian bustard's display grounds and the lesser florican's fields long enjoyed no area protection of their own, and which the eco-sensitive zone device and the forest conservation regime have had to fill from outside the statute.

One boundary the Act draws only in practice, and uneasily, is at customary hunting. In parts of the north-east and in tribal belts elsewhere, hunting carries subsistence and ritual meanings that predate the statute by centuries, and community hunts and the taking of animals for festivals sit squarely inside Section 9's prohibition while sitting equally squarely inside the customary life the Constitution's Fifth and Sixth Schedule arrangements elsewhere protect. The working settlements have been local and various: enforcement concentrated on commercial offtake and on the most threatened species, negotiated restraint with village councils of the kind the community reserve chapter later formalised, and periodic public controversy when a ritual hunt of a scheduled species is prosecuted or conspicuously not prosecuted. The statute contains no customary exception, and the gap between its text and its administration in these landscapes is the largest single divergence between the Act as written and the Act as lived, larger even than the compensation gap, because it is a divergence about legitimacy rather than about capacity.

The Act also binds the state against itself in ways worth noticing. Its offences are not softened for official actors: a forest guard who hunts a scheduled animal hunts within Section 9, and the government ownership rule in Section 39 captures animals killed by mistake, a formula aimed at the official encounter and the claimed accident alike. Immunity exists, but it is the ordinary one: Section 60 protects action taken in good faith under the Act, and it has never been read to protect a killing the Act does not authorise. Against other statutes, the Act asserts primacy in its field through the ordinary rules of special legislation, while its Section 56 preserves the operation of other laws except where they conflict, which is why a single poaching episode can lawfully attract proceedings under this Act, the Arms Act and the customs law together, as the organised trade prosecutions routinely showed.

## The hunting prohibition and its definition

Section 9 of the Act states the central prohibition in one sentence: no person shall hunt any wild animal specified in the schedules, except as provided under the two permission provisions that follow it. Everything depends on what "hunt" means, and the definition in Section 2(16) is drawn far wider than the ordinary sense of shooting an animal for sport.

### What counts as hunting under the Act?

Hunting includes killing or poisoning a wild animal, and also capturing, coursing, snaring, trapping, driving or baiting it, together with every attempt to do any of these. For birds and reptiles it extends to taking or destroying their eggs or disturbing their nests. Injuring an animal, destroying it, or taking any part of its body is hunting as well.

The consequences of that breadth are practical. A villager who sets a snare for a wild pig has hunted within the meaning of the Act even if the snare is never sprung, because the attempt is itself within the definition. A person who drives deer towards others with nets has hunted without touching a weapon. A collector who takes eggs from a nest has hunted a bird that was never in his hands. The definition also governs the permission provisions: because capture is hunting, a forest department that wishes to trap and relocate a leopard needs the same statutory authority as one that proposes to shoot it, and the paperwork runs through the same section.

The original Act of 1972 did not impose the absolute ban in one step. As enacted, it prohibited hunting of the species in the highest schedules and allowed hunting of lesser-scheduled animals under licence, preserving a regulated form of the old game regime for species Parliament thought abundant. The amendment of 1991, Act 44 of 1991, closed that door: it restructured the schedules, extended the prohibition across them, and converted the Act into the near-absolute code it remains. The licensed sport hunting that had survived from the princely era ended with that amendment, and with it the last legal remnant of the shikar economy. The same amendment banned trade in ivory, striking at the commodity that had driven elephant poaching, and inserted a sixth schedule protecting specified plants against picking and uprooting.

Against the blanket prohibition the Act keeps three doors open, and each is deliberately narrow. The first, in Section 11, is the chief wildlife warden's permission. For an animal in the highest schedule, the warden may permit hunting where satisfied that the animal has become dangerous to human life or is so disabled or diseased as to be beyond recovery. For animals in the lesser schedule, the power extends also to danger to property, expressly including standing crops, and may be exercised by an authorised officer. The second door, in Section 12, is permission for education, scientific research and scientific management, granted at the Centre for the highest-schedule species and by the warden otherwise; scientific management is itself defined to include translocation and, remarkably, population management without killing or poisoning. The third door is not a permission at all but a defence: Section 11 also provides that killing or wounding a wild animal in defence of oneself or of another person is no offence, and that an animal so killed becomes government property.

Section 11 is, by a wide margin, the most contested single power in the Act, because it is the point at which the absolute rule meets the individual problem animal. Its discipline is case-by-case: the warden must be satisfied about this animal, on material placed before him, and the order is expected to record why capture, tranquilisation or translocation would not serve, a requirement the courts and the National Tiger Conservation Authority's standard procedures have read into its exercise for tigers and leopards. The controversies that recur in public life are almost all Section 11 controversies. When a tigress in the Yavatmal district of Maharashtra was shot in November 2018 after being blamed for a series of human deaths, the legal question that outlasted the event was whether the warden's order had genuinely exhausted the non-lethal options the section contemplates, and litigation over the order reached the courts before and after the shooting. When state governments press for wider culling of wild pigs or monkeys, the argument is really about whether Section 11's case-by-case discipline should yield to the blanket instrument of the vermin declaration examined later in this article.

The permission system's other door, Section 12, carries the conservation state's constructive work, and its history shows the Act operating in reverse, as an instrument for putting animals back rather than keeping people out. The reintroduction of tigers to Sariska, begun in 2008 with animals moved from Ranthambore, and to Panna in 2009 after that reserve's tigers were lost entirely, were scientific management operations of exactly the kind the section contemplates, capture and translocation authorised at the highest level because a Schedule I animal was involved. The cheetah introduction at Kuno, carried out in 2022 and 2023 with animals brought from Namibia and South Africa under the international arrangements the CITES framework governs, ran through the same conceptual door at a larger scale, and its contested early mortality figures became a public argument about whether Section 12's research and management permissions are being asked to bear experimental programmes of a size the provision's drafters never imagined. The statutory text does not answer that argument; it fixes who must sign, and the signature, for the highest schedule, is the Central Government's on the advice of its wildlife directorate. What the permission record shows across five decades is a consistent asymmetry that defenders and critics read differently: permissions to kill a problem animal are granted by a state officer on a local file, while permissions to move, study or restore an animal travel upward through committees and ministries, so that the lethal use of the Act is administratively its easiest use, a fact about institutional design rather than about any individual decision.

The protocol layer that has grown around Section 11 for the big cats deserves separate statement, because it is where case-by-case discipline is either real or theatrical. The Tiger Conservation Authority has issued standard operating procedures for dealing with tigers straying into human dominated landscapes and for leopards in conflict, which prescribe a sequence: verification that the animal is the one responsible, attempts at capture by trained teams with veterinary support, the constitution of a committee to advise the warden, and lethal action as the terminal step on recorded satisfaction, with the carcass and the inquiry documented afterwards. For elephants, state-level protocols and the project machinery prescribe driving, early warning and capture in defined circumstances. The procedures bind administratively rather than as statute, and their legal significance is that a warden who departs from them without reasons writes an order a court can dismantle, as the litigation over individual orders has repeatedly shown. Their practical significance is distributional: a state that maintains capture teams, veterinarians and a functioning committee can actually run the sequence, while a state without them runs the signature. The Section 11 debate, conducted at that level of honesty, is not about the text at all. It is about which states possess the apparatus the text presupposes, and the answers are written in establishment budgets rather than in the Act. The section's defenders, among them serving and retired forest officers who administer it, make the point that a power to remove a genuinely dangerous animal, exercised on recorded reasons, is what keeps affected communities inside the law at all; its critics answer that in practice the recorded reasons thin out under political pressure, and that the order becomes a formality between a demand for a killing and its authorisation.

## Government property and the prohibition on trade

Section 39 performs the quietest and most consequential operation in the Act. It declares that a wild animal, other than vermin, which is hunted or kept or bred in captivity or found dead or killed by mistake, together with animal articles, trophies, uncured trophies and meat derived from it, and the ivory imported into India or made from an Indian elephant, shall be the property of the government: of the state government where the animal is taken or found, and of the Central Government in the cases the section specifies. No person may acquire, keep, sell, offer for sale or destroy such property except with the previous permission of the chief wildlife warden or an authorised officer.

The ownership rule does three kinds of work. It destroys the finder's and the hunter's title, so that a poached skin is not merely evidence of an offence but state property in the poacher's hands, which is why the possession of wildlife articles is presumptively unlawful under the Act's burden-of-proof provision. It grounds the licensing system: because the default owner is the state, private possession of a trophy or a captive animal is lawful only inside the documentary structure of declarations and certificates of ownership that Chapter V builds, under which a person holding a scheduled animal or article when the provisions commenced had to declare the stock, and transfers thereafter require permission and, for the listed species, are restricted to inheritance and gift rather than sale. And it supplies the theory of the whole statute in one sentence: wild fauna is a public asset held by the state, not an ownerless thing open to the first taker, which is the precise inversion of the common law position that prevailed under the game regime.

The trade provisions that complete the chapter prohibit, for the most protected species, what the ownership rule makes presumptively unlawful for all. Dealing in trophies and animal articles derived from the highest-schedule animals is banned; taxidermy and manufacture are licensed activities elsewhere; and the import of ivory was closed by the amendment of 1991. The constitutional validity of the ivory ban was tested by the trade in Indian Handicrafts Emporium v Union of India, where the Supreme Court in 2003 upheld the prohibition against the argument that it destroyed the dealers' fundamental right to trade, holding that a trade in articles derived from a species the law protects without exception could be prohibited in the public interest and that no vested right survived in a business the statute had extinguished. The judgment matters beyond ivory, because it established that the Act's trade bans would be read as reasonable restrictions judged against the conservation object, a position that has sheltered later prohibitions from similar challenge.

The enforcement theory is completed by the presumption in Section 57: where, in a prosecution, it is established that a person is in possession, custody or control of a captive animal, animal article, meat, trophy or uncured trophy specified in the schedules, the court shall presume that the person is in unlawful possession until the contrary is proved. Combined with Section 39, the effect is that the prosecution need prove only the fact of possession and the identity of the specimen; the paperwork burden falls on the holder. That reversal, examined again in the enforcement section below, is the engine of most wildlife prosecutions, and the feature of the Act that the celebrated trade prosecutions, including the long proceedings against the poaching syndicate led by Sansar Chand, convicted by the courts in Delhi for offences built around skins and derivatives in his network's possession rather than around witnessed kills, actually turned on.

### What may a person lawfully possess as a trophy or animal article?

Possession is lawful where the holder declared the article in the statutory window when the trade provisions commenced, or holds a certificate of ownership for it, and any later transfer came by inheritance or gift with the warden's permission recorded. Commercial sale of articles from the highest-schedule species is prohibited altogether.

In practice the certificate is the citizen's entire title. An inherited pair of antlers with a certificate is furniture; the same antlers without one are government property unlawfully held, and the distinction explains both the care with which old hunting families preserved their ownership certificates and the recurring seizures of uncertified trophies from hotels, farmhouses and antique dealers who assumed that age alone legitimised possession. It does not: the Act knows no doctrine of regularisation by passage of time for scheduled specimens, and amendments have repeatedly reopened declaration windows precisely because each generation of enforcement discovered a new class of holders who had missed the paperwork.

## The paper regime: declarations, certificates and licences

Because Section 39 makes the state the owner, the citizen's relationship with every scheduled specimen is documentary, and Chapter V builds the paperwork in three layers. The first layer is the declaration: when the trade provisions were brought into force, and again at each amendment that widened them, every person holding a captive animal of the listed species, an animal article, a trophy or meat was required to declare the stock to the chief wildlife warden within the window the law prescribed, thirty days in the original scheme, and the declaration window has been reopened by amendment as enforcement uncovered new classes of holders. The second layer is the certificate of ownership, issued on the declaration for the specimens the Act allows a private person to retain, which functions as the holder's title deed and without which possession is exposed to the Section 57 presumption. The third layer is the licence system for those who deal: dealers in animal articles and trophies, manufacturers of articles from scheduled species, taxidermists and keepers of zoos and circuses operate under licences whose conditions regulate stock registers, premises and transactions, and whose breach is itself an offence. Section 49 draws the hard line inside this system: no person may purchase, receive or acquire an animal article, trophy or meat derived from the highest-schedule species from a source other than the authorised channel, which is the provision that finally made the retail trade in tiger and leopard skins, ivory and rhino horn unlawful at the customer's end and not merely at the dealer's.

Movement is the regime's choke point, and the Act polices it as such. A restriction on transportation, carried in Section 48A, forbids the acceptance of a wild animal, animal article or trophy for transport except with the previous permission of the chief wildlife warden or an authorised officer, so that a carrier, a railway parcel office or a courier who takes a scheduled consignment without papers commits an offence independently of the sender. The provision converts the transport network into an inspection network, and the celebrated seizures of the trade, at airports, at railway stations and in courier hubs, are disproportionately transport seizures, because a skin in a godown is a rumour while a skin in transit is a document trail. Inter-state movement of live animals multiplies the permissions, since the warden at each end holds a piece of the transaction, and the elephant transfers examined later in this article became legally interesting precisely at this choke point, where the paper of two states had to agree about one animal and repeatedly did not.

The paper regime's stresses are instructive about the statute's limits. Its premise is a closed universe: every lawful specimen documented, everything undocumented unlawful. Reality supplied undocumented specimens continuously, through inheritance of trophies from the hunting era, through the live trade in birds and reptiles that flourished at the edge of the licence system, and through the conversion of wild-caught animals into paper captives by false declarations of captive birth. Two commodity histories show the regime learning. Shahtoosh, the wool of the Tibetan antelope woven into shawls of extreme value, stood for years in a legal twilight in Jammu and Kashmir, whose own law had permitted the trade while the central Act and the species' international listing forbade it; in Balram Kumawat v Union of India the Supreme Court in 2003 declined to disturb the prohibition framework, and the trade's suppression followed as the state law was aligned with the Act. Ivory ran the other way, from open trade to the 1991 ban upheld in the Handicrafts Emporium judgment, leaving dealers with stock the law had made unsaleable and a long tail of litigation over compensation and over whether existing stock could be marked and retained. The certificate, in each history, is the battlefield: whoever controls the paperwork controls the boundary between a lawful heirloom and contraband, and the Bureau's intelligence work concentrates on the forgery and laundering of exactly these documents.

## The schedules and the restructuring of 2022

For its first five decades the Act carried six schedules, and the sixfold structure is the version most examination material and most older commentary still describes. Schedule I listed the species under absolute protection, big cats, elephants, rhinoceros, and the rest of the charismatic and endangered fauna, split into parts by class of animal. Schedule II did the same for a second tier. Schedules III and IV covered species protected at lower levels, hunted under licence until 1991 and protected against hunting thereafter. Schedule V was the vermin schedule, listing a handful of animals, the common crow, fruit bats, mice and rats, that anyone might kill. Schedule VI, added in 1991, listed specified plants. The penalty and permission provisions keyed themselves to these lists, so that the schedule in which a species sat determined the punishment for killing it, the authority that could permit its capture, and whether the Centre could declare it vermin.

The Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2022, Act 18 of 2022, which received the assent of the President on 19 December 2022 and came into force on 1 April 2023, rewrote the structure into four schedules. Schedule I contains the animal species with the highest level of protection. Schedule II contains animal species with a lesser level of protection, absorbing the former third and fourth schedules into one list. Schedule III contains the protected plant species, the descendant of the old sixth schedule. Schedule IV is entirely new in kind: it lists the specimens covered by the appendices of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora, CITES, and supports the new chapter the amendment inserted to give that convention domestic legal form.

The CITES chapter is the amendment's centre of gravity. India had been a party to the convention since 1976, yet for nearly five decades the obligations were discharged through customs law, export policy and administrative arrangement rather than through the wildlife statute itself. The amendment inserted a chapter on the regulation of international trade in endangered species, providing for a Management Authority to grant permits and certificates for the export and import of scheduled specimens, a Scientific Authority to advise on whether trade in a specimen would harm the survival of its species, and a registration regime under which the possession, transfer, birth and death of living specimens of listed species must be recorded. For the first time, the cross-border movement of a listed orchid or a flask of captive-bred reptiles runs through the Wild Life (Protection) Act rather than around it, and the officers of the wildlife machinery, rather than customs alone, hold the permit power. In the working arrangement the Management Authority's functions are discharged through the wildlife directorate of the Union environment ministry, and the Scientific Authority's through the country's zoological and botanical survey institutions, so that a permit application turns on a scientific opinion about detriment to the species, recorded by the survey body, rather than on a customs classification alone. The registration duty for living scheduled specimens, which reaches the private holder of a listed parrot or tortoise as much as the commercial breeder, is the chapter's most intrusive element and the one whose enforcement will test the system's capacity: it converts an uncounted population of exotic pets and collection animals into a register the state must maintain, and the prescribed online registration process is the instrument on which the whole exercise stands or falls.

The lists are not frozen between amendments. Section 61 empowers the Central Government, where it is of the opinion that it is expedient, to add an entry to, omit an entry from, or transfer an entry between the schedules by notification, laying the change before Parliament. The power is the statute's admission that taxonomy and threat move faster than parliamentary time, and it has been used for individual species as evidence accumulated, a mechanism of particular importance for the CITES schedule, whose contents track the convention's appendices as conferences of the parties revise them. The discipline the power needs is the one its critics demand of the vermin system: a recorded scientific basis, since a listing decision by notification carries the same legal force, the same offences and the same permit consequences, as a listing enacted in the schedule itself, and a delisting quietly executed can strip a species as completely as a vermin declaration, for the whole country and without a time limit. The asymmetry is worth noticing. Parliament built a notification route for the lists because science moves, and the same logic, applied to protection's removal, is what makes Section 62's notification route defensible in principle and contested in practice; the difference lies in the presence or absence of a recorded scientific inquiry behind the signature.

The plant schedule carries the listing logic into its least enforced corner. Specified plants under Schedule III may not be picked, uprooted, possessed or traded except as the chapter permits, and the provision was aimed at the commercial collection of wild orchids, medicinal plants and endemic species from protected and unprotected land alike. In practice the plant provisions have run far behind the animal provisions in enforcement attention, a gap conservation botanists have documented in the trade in wild-collected medicinal species, and the schedule's presence in the Act is best understood as a jurisdictional claim, that flora of conservation concern belongs inside the wildlife code, awaiting an enforcement generation that takes it up. The contrast instructs: the same section numbers, the same presumption machinery and the same penalties attach to a smuggled orchid as to a smuggled skin, and the difference in outcomes is administrative priority, not law.

The same amendment made three further changes a careful reader should separate. It empowered the Central Government to regulate or prohibit the import, trade, possession and proliferation of invasive alien species, creatures not native to India whose spread threatens native wildlife, a power the original Act had lacked entirely. It raised the financial penalties across the statute, discussed in the enforcement section below. And it amended the captive elephant provision, a change so contested that it is treated separately later in this article. What it did not change is equally telling: the hunting ban, the ownership rule, the protected area chapters and the institutional skeleton of 1972, 2002 and 2006 all survived, so that the Act after 2022 is best read as the old code with a new index and a new international chapter, not as a new statute.

The rebuilt lists also changed the Act's taxonomic centre of gravity. The old schedules were, in substance, a roll of the hunted and the traded: mammals and birds first, reptiles and amphibians behind them, insects and marine creatures barely present. The new schedules were compiled from far wider taxonomic advice and take in, among much else, marine species and invertebrates that the old lists ignored, running to a substantially larger number of entries than the six lists they replaced. Conservation biologists welcomed the widening, since a protection code that began at the tiger and ended at the butterfly had left most of biodiversity to other instruments. A contrary criticism, recorded in commentary on the amendment and in the research community's response to it, is that listing on this scale, without a matching expansion of identification capacity in the enforcement machinery, loads the permit and prosecution system with species that a frontline officer cannot reliably name, and converts scientific collecting itself into a licensed activity whose permissions researchers find slow. Both observations can be true at once, and the working out of that tension, in the permit offices rather than in Parliament, is where the new schedules will be made or marred.

One restructuring consequence deserves the scrutiny the Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy gave it. The vermin power, examined in its own section below, was recast so that the class of species exposed to declaration changed with the consolidation of the schedules: because the former third and fourth schedules folded into a single Schedule II, and the declaration power reaches the Schedule II list while Schedule I stands outside it, hundreds of species that had sat in the lower schedules, among them very large numbers of birds, reptiles and insects, came within the notifiable class. The Centre for Legal Policy's estimate put the newly exposed mammals alone in the dozens, with birds in the hundreds. Whether that widening was an intended policy choice or an arithmetic accident of consolidation is a question the parliamentary debate never squarely addressed, and it remains the sharpest technical criticism of the 2022 exercise.

## Protected areas under the Wildlife Protection Act: sanctuaries and national parks

The species chapters of the Act travel with the animal; the protected area chapter stays with the land. A state government that considers an area to be of adequate ecological, faunal, floral, geomorphological, natural or zoological significance may declare it a sanctuary under Section 18, and may declare it a national park under Section 35 where the significance is of the higher order those provisions describe. The distinction between the two is the distinction most often garbled in public discussion, and it is precise: it concerns the treatment of private rights and of human use inside the boundary.

A sanctuary is declared over land in which rights may continue. The declaration begins with a notification of intention, after which the Collector inquires into and determines the rights of affected persons, a settlement process the Act borrows from the land acquisition tradition: claims are invited, heard and either admitted, acquired on compensation, or excluded by carving the land out of the boundary. Pending final settlement, the area enjoys interim protection, and the final notification issues when the process completes or the prescribed period runs. Within a sanctuary, the chief wildlife warden controls entry, residence, the grazing of livestock and the movement of persons through a permit system, and certain acts, destroying or diverting habitat, diverting or stopping the flow of water, lighting fires, are prohibited outright. But admitted private rights that were not acquired survive, grazing may be regulated rather than excluded, and the human footprint inside a sanctuary remains lawful in the forms the settlement recorded.

A national park takes the further step. When a national park is finally declared, the Act requires that the rights settlement be complete in the stricter sense: no right may continue to be exercised unless it has been through the acquisition process, grazing of livestock is prohibited, and no person may destroy, exploit or remove wildlife or habitat, or divert water, except under a permit granted by the chief wildlife warden for the purposes the Act permits. Alteration of a national park's boundary, and equally of a sanctuary's, requires a recommendation of the National Board for Wild Life, a lock inserted to prevent state governments from quietly shrinking protected areas under development pressure. In short, a sanctuary is a protected area negotiated with the rights that existed inside it; a national park is a protected area from which incompatible rights have been bought out or extinguished. That is the whole difference, and it explains why national park declarations take longer, generate more litigation, and cover a smaller share of the protected estate.

### How is a new sanctuary declared, step by step?

The state government issues a notification of intention to constitute the area a sanctuary. The Collector then inquires into the existence, nature and extent of rights in the land, invites and hears claims, and admits, acquires or excludes them. A final notification declares the sanctuary, whereupon the warden's permit regime and the habitat prohibitions begin to apply.

Inside the boundary, the Act runs a detailed interior regime that the declaration sections introduce and Sections 27 to 34 operate. Entry into a sanctuary is restricted to the classes of person the Act names, public servants on duty, persons permitted by the warden, persons with rights in immovable property inside the area, and those using a public highway through it. Weapons may not be carried in without the warden's written permission, and a person holding an arms licence who resides within ten kilometres of a sanctuary or national park is required by Section 34 to register with the chief wildlife warden, a provision whose rationale is that the gun nearest the boundary is the one the poacher's network most readily rents. Fire is prohibited except as the warden permits; injuring or endangering wildlife by any means, and the use of chemicals and explosives that pollute the water flowing through the area, are barred; and the warden's control of grazing in a sanctuary, exercised under the management plan, is the daily point of friction between the interior regime and the pastoral households whose herds have used the same grazing for generations. National parks apply the same interior discipline with the grazing door closed. The protected network that these provisions govern runs to more than a hundred national parks and more than five hundred wildlife sanctuaries, together covering a significant single-digit share of the country's land area, with conservation and community reserves adding smaller numbers of sites year by year: a substantial estate, administered by state forest departments whose protection staff vacancies are a recurring subject of parliamentary questions, which is the recurring arithmetic behind the gap between the interior regime on paper and the patrol strength on the ground.

The boundary lock has its own instructive history. Before the amendment of 2002 inserted the requirement of the National Board's recommendation, a state legislature could attempt by resolution, and a state government by notification, to redraw a sanctuary around the interests pressing on it, and the Kolleru lake sanctuary in Andhra Pradesh became the standing example: a vast wetland sanctuary whose boundary the state sought for years to reduce under pressure from the fish tank economy that had occupied the lake bed, with the contest running through committees and courts while the encroachments it sought to regularise continued on the water. The 2002 amendment's answer was procedural rather than substantive: it did not forbid shrinkage, it nationalised the decision, so that a boundary alteration must survive the National Board's scrutiny on a national record rather than the originating state's politics alone. The lesson generalises across the Act's design. Wherever the statute distrusts the level of government closest to a decision, it does not remove the decision; it adds a recommending authority above it, and the quality of protection in any given decade is the quality of that recommending layer's independence.

The rights-settlement machinery produced one of the Act's formative judgments. In Pradeep Krishen v Union of India, the Supreme Court in 1996 addressed the collection of tendu leaves and other forest produce by local communities, many of them tribal, in and around the sanctuaries and national parks of Madhya Pradesh, where settlement proceedings had dragged on for years without completion. The Court directed the state to complete the acquisition of rights within the prescribed frame and restrained the continuation of interim arrangements that had left residents in legal suspension, neither settled nor evicted. The case stands for a proposition that runs through the whole protected area system: the settlement process is not a formality to be left unfinished, because until it finishes, nobody inside the boundary, resident, officer or animal, has the legal certainty the Act promises. Decades on, unfinished settlement remains a live administrative fact in many sanctuaries, and it is the quiet reason why the formal map of protection and the map on the ground diverge.

The six categories of protected geography that the statute and its surrounding instruments recognise are compared in the table below. Five are creatures of the Act itself; the sixth, the eco-sensitive zone, is declared under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and its rules, and is included because no account of a protected area's legal perimeter is complete without it.

| Category | How it is declared | Who owns the land | Rights of residents that survive | Activities permitted | Who approves a project inside it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sanctuary | State government notification under Section 18, after settlement of rights | Government and private, as settled | Admitted rights not acquired by the state continue | Entry, residence and grazing under permit from the chief wildlife warden | National Board for Wild Life recommendation for boundary change or diversion, with the warden's permit regime inside |
| National Park | State government notification under Section 35, rights extinguished or acquired | Government, on completion of settlement | None inconsistent with the park; grazing prohibited | Tourism and research under permit; no exploitation or removal of wildlife or habitat | National Board for Wild Life recommendation for any boundary alteration; stricter scrutiny than a sanctuary |
| Conservation Reserve | State government notification under Section 36A over government land, after consulting local communities | Government | Existing uses continue under the conservation management committee's plan | Sustainable use consistent with the committee's management plan | State government on the committee's advice; lighter regime than sanctuary or park |
| Community Reserve | State government notification under Section 36C where a community or individual volunteers private or community land | Private or community, unchanged | All ownership rights continue; land use change needs committee resolution and state approval | Conservation and traditional use as the community management committee resolves | The community reserve management committee, with state government approval of land use change |
| Tiger Reserve | State government notification under Section 38V on the recommendation of the National Tiger Conservation Authority | As in the underlying sanctuary or national park | Recognised forest rights in the buffer; relocation from critical habitat only on the statutory conditions | Core kept inviolate; buffer managed for coexistence under the tiger conservation plan | Tiger Conservation Authority approval of the plan; National Board for Wild Life for boundary change or de-notification |
| Eco-Sensitive Zone | Central government notification under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and its rules | Unchanged; public and private | Existing lawful uses continue, regulated by category | Activities listed as permitted run freely; regulated activities need clearance; prohibited activities are barred | Monitoring committee for the zone, with environmental and forest clearance processes for listed projects |

Two clarifications protect readers against the commonest category errors in this field. The first is marine: the protected area chapters apply to territorial waters as they do to land, and the network includes marine national parks and sanctuaries, of which the Gulf of Kutch and the Gulf of Mannar are the best known, where the interior regime must be translated into rules about fishing, anchoring and collection that the land-born text handles awkwardly and the notifications handle specifically. The second concerns labels that are not in the Act at all. A biosphere reserve is a designation layered over existing protected areas and surrounding landscapes for research and sustainable development purposes; it creates no offence and no permit regime of its own, and its core zone is protected because it is a national park or sanctuary under this Act, not because the biosphere label says so. Ramsar listing of a wetland likewise adds international recognition without adding a domestic prohibition. Project reports and examination answers that treat these labels as statutory categories misdescribe the law, and the confusion matters practically, because a landowner inside a biosphere reserve's transition zone faces no restriction from the designation, while his neighbour inside the core national park faces the full interior regime.

The table rewards one careful look. Approval inside a protected area is never a single signature: the warden's permit, the National Board's recommendation on boundaries and diversion, and the clearance processes under the environment and forest statutes interlock, and a project that has cleared one gate has not cleared the others. The interlock is deliberate. Where land inside a protected area is sought for a mine, a road or a dam, the wildlife approval runs alongside, and does not replace, [the forest diversion regime that governs habitat loss outside the protected estate as well](/2014/11/01/forest-conservation-act-1980-explained/), and a proponent who treats a wildlife clearance as a forest clearance, or the reverse, discovers the error at the hearing stage.

## The consent categories of 2002

By the turn of the century the protected area model faced a structural embarrassment. The strongest categories could be declared only where the state owned or could acquire the land, yet much of India's ecologically valuable habitat, its wetlands, its grasslands, its community forests and its coastal stretches, lay in government land outside reserves or in private and community hands altogether. The Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2002, Act 16 of 2003, in force from 1 April 2003, answered with two new categories designed for land the fortress model could not reach.

The conservation reserve, under Section 36A, is declared by a state government over government-owned land, particularly land adjacent to national parks and sanctuaries or linking one protected area with another, after consultation with local communities. A conservation management committee, drawn substantially from local panchayats, the forest department and non-governmental expertise, advises on its management. The category was designed for corridors and buffers: stretches of revenue or forest land whose protection completes a landscape but which could not survive the rights-extinguishing rigour of a national park. The Centre acquired a parallel power to notify conservation reserves on its own land through later amendment.

The community reserve, under Section 36C, goes a step further and inverts the declaration logic. It may be declared where an individual or a community has volunteered to conserve wildlife and habitat on private or community land which is not part of any existing national park, sanctuary or conservation reserve. Ownership does not change. A community reserve management committee, elected substantially from the village community with official representation, prepares the conservation plan, and after notification no change in land use pattern may be made except in accordance with a resolution of that committee approved by the state government. The category's best-known successes are community-conserved wetlands and nesting sites in the north-east and village-protected heronries in the south, places where the conservation impulse preceded the legal form and the statute's contribution was to give the community's own restraint legal durability against outsiders and against future temptation.

Tourism cuts across all the categories and deserves separate mention because it is the use the public most associates with protected areas and the one the statute regulates most obliquely. The Act itself says little about tourism beyond the warden's permit control over entry; the operative discipline came from the tiger chapter's requirement that tourism follow the Authority's normative standards, and from litigation. In 2012, in proceedings brought by the conservationist Ajay Dubey, the Supreme Court first restrained tourism in the core areas of tiger reserves altogether, and then, on the Authority's guidelines being placed before it, permitted regulated tourism to resume under them, with a ceiling on the share of the core open to visitors and a local conservation levy. The episode is the protected area system in miniature: a statutory silence, an executive guideline drafted under judicial pressure, and a compromise between the tourism economy that funds goodwill for conservation in gateway towns and the disturbance case against vehicles around breeding animals, in which both sides could point to reserves that supported their account.

The two categories differ in the one thing that matters: whose land, and whose initiative. A conservation reserve sits on government land and is declared by the state after consultation; a community reserve sits on private or community land and can exist only because the owners volunteered. Between them they mark the statute's first admission that protection could be negotiated rather than imposed, an admission the tiger provisions of 2006 would extend, under much harder conditions, to the question of people living inside the most valuable habitat of all.

The uptake record of the consent categories is geographically lopsided in a way that teaches something about the statute. Community reserves have multiplied most where community institutions over land were already strong, above all in the north-eastern states, where village councils hold and govern land in a manner the category's design fits almost without translation, and where community-declared reserves protect stretches of hill forest and river that the state could not have notified as sanctuaries without acquiring land it had no customary route to. In the plains states, where private land is fragmented into small holdings and the community as a landholding unit barely exists, the category has been used thinly, mostly for wetlands and village tanks protected by panchayat resolution. Conservation reserves have served a different niche, the formal protection of corridors and reservoir catchments on government land, where the alternative was no status at all. Read together, the two categories show the Act learning to protect land it does not own, at a pace set by the land institutions it encounters rather than by its own text.

## Tiger reserves, Sariska and the authority of 2006

For three decades Project Tiger ran as a centrally sponsored scheme resting on the Act's ordinary categories: a tiger reserve was, in law, a national park or sanctuary with a project label, a management plan and central funding. The arrangement's weakness was exposed in 2004 and 2005, when the tigers of Sariska Tiger Reserve in Rajasthan were found to have been poached out entirely, a disappearance first forced into public view by conservationists and the press and then confirmed by official inquiry, in a reserve that on paper had staff, funds and the full protection of the Act. The scandal destroyed the comfortable assumption that notification plus funding equalled protection, and the Tiger Task Force appointed by the Prime Minister in 2005, chaired by Sunita Narain of the Centre for Science and Environment, recommended in its report Joining the Dots that tiger conservation be given a statutory authority with power to set binding standards. Its diagnosis went beyond machinery: the Task Force argued that Sariska had been lost not to any shortage of law but to a breakdown of the relationship between the reserve and the people inside and around it, and it pressed coexistence and the honest settlement of rights as conservation measures in their own right. The Authority was duly created, and the rights conditions later written into Section 38V bear the report's mark.

The Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2006, Act 39 of 2006, wrote that recommendation into the Act. It inserted a chapter constituting the National Tiger Conservation Authority, chaired by the Union minister for environment and forests, charged with approving the tiger conservation plan each state prepares for every tiger reserve, laying down normative standards for tourism and management, and evaluating and assessing the programme that the scheme had run on administrative instruction alone. It gave the tiger reserve itself statutory form under Section 38V: a state government notifies a tiger reserve on the Authority's recommendation, and its area is organised as a core or critical tiger habitat, kept inviolate for tiger conservation without affecting the rights of forest dwellers in the manner the statute specifies, and a buffer or peripheral area, managed expressly for coexistence between wildlife and human activity under a tiger conservation plan. No alteration of a tiger reserve's boundaries may be made except on the Authority's recommendation and the approval of the National Board for Wild Life, and no de-notification except in public interest with both approvals, a double lock against the quiet erosion that boundary politics had practised on sanctuaries.

Section 38V then performs the Act's most delicate negotiation, between inviolacy and rights. The statute provides that while earmarking the core, the state shall not affect the rights of the Scheduled Tribes or other forest dwellers, and it lays down cumulative conditions before any relocation from a critical tiger habitat may proceed: the process of recognition and determination of rights under the forest rights legislation must be complete, it must be established that the community's presence causes irreversible damage and that coexistence is not possible, the informed consent of the Gram Sabha and of the affected families must be obtained, and a resettlement package providing a secure livelihood must be prepared and the facilities and land allocation at the resettlement site completed before anyone moves. The relocation question is where this statute meets the forest rights regime head-on, and [the recognition of forest dwellers' rights and the claims process that produces them](/2017/03/15/forest-rights-act-2006-explained/) is the subject that article owns; the point for the wildlife statute is that Parliament wrote the safeguard into the tiger chapter itself, in 2006, in the same legislative season that produced the rights statute, so that the two laws stand or fall together on the honesty of the rights-recognition process that precedes any move.

The courts have kept the negotiation honest at its most visible point. In Centre for Environment Law, World Wide Fund for Nature India v Union of India, the Supreme Court in 2013 directed the translocation of Asiatic lions from Gir to a second home at Kuno in Madhya Pradesh, reasoning from the species' interest rather than from any state's proprietary pride in its lions, and the judgment remains the leading statement that the Act's object is the survival of species, not the convenience of the governments that host them. The same period saw the Court insist, in the continuing forest litigation, that tiger reserves notify their core and buffer areas as the 2006 text required, converting a statutory duty that states had treated as aspirational into a monitored obligation. Where the relocation safeguard has been tested in practice, the findings of researchers and of official evaluations divide: relocations conducted with completed rights recognition, genuine consent and delivered packages, studied in reserves such as Sariska's receiving villages and in the Bhadra landscape in Karnataka, are cited by conservationists and by many relocated families themselves as improvements in security, services and income; relocations pressed forward on contested recognition and thin consent are cited by rights advocates as dispossession wearing a conservation uniform. The statute cannot settle which description fits a given reserve. It can only fix the conditions, which it has done with unusual specificity, and leave their observance to be policed village by village.

## The institutional layer

The amendment of 2002 replaced the purely advisory Indian Board for Wild Life, which had existed since 1952 outside the statute, with a statutory apex body. The National Board for Wild Life, constituted under Section 5A, is chaired by the Prime Minister, with the Union environment minister as vice-chairperson, and includes members of Parliament, representatives of state governments, serving officers and a body of non-official members drawn from conservation organisations and individual experts. Its functions under Section 5B run from framing policy for the conservation of wildlife to advising on the selection and management of protected areas and on the restriction of activities within them. Section 5C constitutes a Standing Committee of the Board, chaired by the environment minister, to which the Board's working business is delegated.

### Who chairs the National Board for Wild Life, and who else sits on it?

The Prime Minister chairs the Board. The Union minister for environment and forests sits as vice-chairperson. The remaining membership mixes members of Parliament, nominees of state governments, the senior forest and wildlife officers of the Centre, and non-official members drawn from conservation organisations and independent experts in wildlife and ecology.

The Standing Committee is where the Board touches the ground. Projects that require land within a protected area, or that fall within an eco-sensitive zone and attract its scrutiny, come before the Committee for recommendation, and its site appraisals and conditions shape what a highway, a transmission line, a mine or a resort may look like at a sanctuary's edge. The charge most often laid against the arrangement is structural: a committee of a large board, meeting on the files the ministry places before it, under the pressure of infrastructure timelines, will tend towards clearance with conditions rather than refusal, and critics of particular decisions, from river valley projects in the Himalaya to mining leases in central Indian corridors, have argued that the recommendation function has become a processing function. Defenders of the system answer that the alternative, no single national forum at all, was worse, that the Committee's conditions have redesigned real projects, and that the Board's statutory existence gives objectors a forum whose refusal or deferral the government must reckon with. Both descriptions are true of different meetings, which is precisely why the institution rewards scrutiny decision by decision rather than verdict in the abstract. Researchers and consultants preparing the maps, species inventories and site appraisals that go before the Committee handle large volumes of protected area documents, and keeping those papers in order matters: a scholar assembling a presentation for a committee briefing or a classroom can open the deck in the browser itself, without desktop software, through [the browser-based presentation viewer from ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), and check the slides against the notification record.

At the state level the Act pairs the administrative head with a deliberative body of its own. The chief wildlife warden, appointed under Section 4, heads the wildlife wing and exercises the permission powers, the permit regime and the enforcement command within the state. The State Board for Wild Life, under Section 6, is chaired by the Chief Minister, with the state forest minister as vice-chairperson and a membership that includes legislators, officials and non-official experts, charged with advising the state on the selection and administration of protected areas and on policy for harmonising the needs of people and wildlife. The amendment of 2022 introduced standing committees of the state boards, a change that opposition members in Parliament and conservation commentators criticised as diluting the full board's deliberation, on the ground that a small committee meeting frequently would decide what the full board, meeting rarely but deliberating publicly, had been designed to slow down. The government defended the change as administrative practicality, pointing to the National Board's own standing committee as the working model. The dispute is a small instance of the statute's recurring argument between speed and deliberation, and it is not resolved by the text.

The state boards carry a weakness their composition predicts. A body chaired by a Chief Minister meets when the chair's calendar allows, which in most states has meant rarely, and its statutory agenda, the selection of areas, the review of policy, the harmonisation of people's needs with protection, is exactly the agenda that suffers from infrequent meeting, because none of it arrives as a single file demanding a signature. The non-official members' complaint, recorded across states and decades, is of boards that ratify rather than deliberate. The 2022 standing committees were defended as the cure for this paralysis, and their critics' fear, that the cure converts deliberation into disposal, describes the same body at two speeds. The National Board's own history supplies evidence for both readings, having passed long quiet periods in its advisory existence before 2002 and having acquired, as a statutory body with a working committee, a decisional weight its predecessor never held. Institutional design in this Act is a recurring wager that a smaller, oftener body will decide better than a grander, rarer one; the wager's results differ by state and by decade, and the wager itself is never re-examined in the amendments, which simply repeat it.

Below the boards, protection is delivered by people, and the Act's personnel design explains much of its field record. The Director of Wild Life Preservation heads the Centre's wildlife administration and anchors the Bureau. In the states, the chief wildlife warden commands a wing that in most states is a small cadre inside the territorial forest department, so that the officer responsible for a division's timber and plantation targets and the officer responsible for its wildlife protection are frequently the same person wearing successive hats, a structural conflict the Tiger Task Force identified and the creation of dedicated wildlife divisions in the larger states only partly answers. The amendment of 2006 prompted the raising of a Special Tiger Protection Force in tiger bearing states, drawn from local youth and ex-servicemen for frontline patrol, an acknowledgment that the offence chapters are only as strong as the guard who reaches the snare line first. Honest accounts of enforcement begin here, with establishment registers and vacancy returns, rather than with the penalty sections.

Two specialist bodies complete the layer. The Wildlife Crime Control Bureau, constituted under the chapter the amendment of 2006 inserted, is a multi-agency intelligence and coordination body built from the forest service, the police and the customs administration, charged with collecting and collating intelligence on organised wildlife crime, maintaining a centralised data bank, coordinating enforcement among states and assisting prosecutions. Its creation recognised a fact the poaching record had made unavoidable: the trade that the trade chapters prohibit is run by networks that cross state and national borders, while the Act's ordinary enforcement stops at the boundary of a single forest division. The Central Zoo Authority, inserted by the amendment of 1991, performs a quieter function, recognising zoos, setting standards for the housing and care of captive animals, and regulating transfers between collections, so that the Act's concern extends to animals in captivity as well as in the wild. Its chapter also carries the Act's only express welfare offences addressed to captivity, including the prohibition on teasing, molesting or feeding animals in a zoo except as permitted, a provision whose modesty of penalty beside the hunting offences states the era's hierarchy plainly. The recognition power proved the chapter's real instrument: derecognition closes a zoo as effectively as prosecution, and the Authority's evaluations of substandard collections, and the long campaign over elephants in zoos and circuses in which its standards were invoked, moved more animals than the offence provisions ever did. The zoo chapter is the Act's bridge to the animal welfare code it otherwise leaves alone, and its existence explains why arguments about captive wildlife so often begin inside this statute even where the operative cruelty provision lies in the Act of 1960.

One jurisdictional note matters to practitioners. The National Green Tribunal, constituted under its own Act of 2010, draws its environmental jurisdiction from a schedule of listed enactments, and the Wild Life (Protection) Act is not among them, so challenges to wildlife decisions travel to the High Courts and the Supreme Court under writ jurisdiction rather than to the Tribunal whose remit is mapped in [the profile of the National Green Tribunal and the limits of its schedule](/2014/11/15/national-green-tribunal-act-2010/). The omission is deliberate in origin: wildlife was thought to need the constitutional courts' direct attention. In practice it means wildlife litigation proceeds by public interest petition to the higher judiciary, with the Godavarman continuing mandamus as the standing example of what that route makes possible.

## The courts and the shape of the statute

Read as a line of cases, the jurisprudence does three things to the Act: it confirms the severity of the prohibitions, it disciplines the permissions, and it polices the boundary between the wildlife statute and the rights of the people inside its geography. The earliest of the leading decisions belongs to the severity line. In State of Bihar v Murad Ali Khan, decided in 1988, the Supreme Court treated the killing of an elephant by a professional hunting party as exactly the offence the Act's scheme contemplated, rejecting the attempt to soften the prosecution through the procedures the accused invoked, and the case entered the textbooks as the standing authority that the hunting prohibition means what it says against organised sporting parties and not only against village poachers. The trade prosecutions of the following decades extended the same severity to the networks, and in Sansar Chand v State of Rajasthan in 2010 the Court, dealing with the syndicate that had moved tiger and leopard skins out of India's reserves for years, recorded the scale of organised wildlife crime and the inadequacy of treating it as petty offending, an observation the enforcement agencies quote when they argue for the Bureau model of intelligence-led prosecution.

The discipline line runs through the Section 11 litigation and the protected area cases collected earlier in this article, Pradeep Krishen on unfinished rights settlement and the Godavarman orders on boundaries and eco-sensitive zones. Its theme is constant: the permissions and procedures of the Act are not decorative, and an executive that treats them as formalities will find the courts completing the inquiry the file should have contained. The boundary line is the youngest and the fastest moving. The Nagaraja judgment of 2014 supplied the moral vocabulary, reading the Act with the constitutional duty of compassion towards living creatures; the constitution bench of 2023, in upholding the state amendments that permitted the traditional bull events, marked where that vocabulary stops against a legislature's considered cultural judgment; and the Ranjitsinh litigation carried the boundary question into energy policy, the Court in 2024 recognising a right against the adverse effects of climate change in a case whose operative dispute was the routing of power lines through the great Indian bustard's last strongholds. A reader who follows only the statute's text will miss the extent to which its operative meaning in any given decade is set by this case law layered over it, which is why the cluster's judicial treatment of environmental doctrine, surveyed in the framework article, repays reading alongside this pillar.

## Eco-sensitive zones: protection beyond the boundary

A protected area ends at its notified boundary, but the pressures on it do not. Hotels crowd the gate of a famous park, quarries open on the ridge above a sanctuary, and a highway pushed to a boundary fence achieves by proximity what diversion inside the fence would have been refused for. The eco-sensitive zone is the legal answer to that geometry: a belt of land around a national park or sanctuary, notified by the Central Government under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the rules under it, in which activities are classified as prohibited, regulated or permitted, and in which a monitoring committee watches the boundary the wildlife statute itself cannot police.

The doctrine grew by stages. The Wildlife Conservation Strategy adopted in 2002 treated the land within ten kilometres of a protected area boundary as an eco-fragile zone for planning purposes, and the environment ministry's guidelines of 9 February 2011 converted the idea into a working method, listing prohibited activities such as commercial mining and large hydroelectric projects, regulated activities such as the erection of hotels and the felling of trees, and permitted activities such as agriculture and rainwater harvesting, with each zone to be notified site-specifically rather than by a uniform rule. Litigation then hardened the default. In the continuing Godavarman proceedings, an order of the Supreme Court in June 2022 directed that every protected area carry a minimum eco-sensitive zone of one kilometre from its boundary. The direction proved difficult of universal application in landscapes where habitations, towns and long-settled uses sit hard against the boundary, and by an order of April 2023 the Court modified it: a uniform one-kilometre width would not be imposed where zones had been notified or were moving through the guideline process, while mining was held impermissible within a national park or sanctuary and within one kilometre of its boundary in any event.

Mining supplied the doctrine's hardest case before the general width question was reached. In the Goa Foundation litigation, the Supreme Court in 2014, dealing with the iron ore belt that presses against Goa's sanctuaries, directed that mining within one kilometre of a protected area boundary in that state would not be permitted and that the leases' environmental clearances reckon with the buffer, a state-specific holding whose logic the 2022 and 2023 Godavarman orders later generalised for mining across the country while leaving the wider zone widths to the notification process. The sequence shows the courts building the buffer doctrine in layers: a planning concept in the 2002 strategy, a guideline in 2011, a state-specific judicial buffer in 2014, a uniform minimum in 2022, and a differentiated settlement in 2023, each layer narrowing the discretion of the layer beneath it.

For the resident and the landowner, the zone's arrival changes land value and building rights at a stroke, which is why eco-sensitive zone notifications generate more correspondence with legislators than any other instrument in this field. The grievance is real: a family whose land falls inside a newly notified zone finds construction, industry and even tree felling regulated by a committee it did not elect, for the benefit of a park it may experience mainly as a source of crop-raiding animals. The conservation answer is equally real: without the belt, the boundary is a hard edge between a protected interior and an unregulated exterior, and edge effects, light, noise, effluent, traffic, do the damage the interior rules exist to prevent. The 2023 modification, which kept the regime but refused the uniform width, is best read as the Court's attempt to keep both truths in view, insisting on the belt while letting its width be argued site by site on the guideline criteria rather than fixed by a single number.

The zone has also become the meeting point between the wildlife statute and the energy transition, a friction the drafters of neither regime foresaw. Wind turbines and solar parks seek exactly the open, elevated, sparsely settled land that surrounds many protected areas, and transmission lines must cross the belts whatever route is chosen, which is how the great Indian bustard's habitat in Rajasthan and Gujarat came to be litigated as a question about burying power lines rather than about hunting or diversion. The bustard proceedings, culminating in the climate judgment of 2024 with its expert committee and its differentiated directions for priority habitat, show the eco-sensitive idea mutating under pressure: from a belt of prohibited and regulated activities fixed by notification, towards a continuing, expert-supervised negotiation between two public goods, species survival and decarbonisation, that the notification format handles clumsily. That mutation, more than the width controversy, is the zone's future, and it will be worked out in the bustard committee's reports and their successors rather than in any amendment of this Act, which does not contain the zone at all.

## Enforcement and penalties

The enforcement chapters give the Act its teeth, and their design explains the shape of wildlife crime statistics better than any account of patrol strength. Officers of the wildlife and forest machinery, and police officers of prescribed rank, hold powers of entry, search, seizure and arrest without warrant where an offence under the Act is reasonably believed to have been committed, together with a power to require assistance and a duty laid on the public to report. Vehicles, weapons, traps and tools used in an offence are liable to forfeiture. Offences are prosecuted on complaints by the authorised wildlife authorities or, for a private complainant, only after the statutory notice process the Act prescribes, which keeps the criminal machinery in official hands.

### Why do wildlife prosecutions so often turn on possession?

Because the Act makes possession the pivot of proof. Section 39 vests ownership of scheduled animals and their derivatives in the government, and Section 57 requires the court to presume that a person found in possession of a scheduled animal, article, trophy or meat holds it unlawfully unless the contrary is proved.

The amendment of 2002 added a financial weapon aimed above the poacher's head. The inserted chapter on forfeiture of property derived from illegal hunting and trade, modelled on the proceeds-of-crime chapters of the narcotics and smuggling statutes, empowers the state to trace, attach and forfeit property acquired through wildlife crime, on proceedings before a competent authority, where the scale of a syndicate's assets makes prosecution of individuals an inadequate answer. Its premise is that the trade's organisers are deterred by balance sheets rather than by sentences they can arrange for others to serve. The chapter has been used sparingly beside the ordinary prosecution route, and its sparing use is itself instructive about the enforcement system's habits: the machinery of complaint, seizure and trial is familiar to every forest officer, while asset forfeiture demands financial investigation skills the department must borrow, which returns the analysis to the Bureau's design and to the multi-agency staffing that was supposed to supply exactly those skills.

The penalty ladder under Section 51 is graduated by the schedule of the species and by repetition. A general contravention of the Act is punishable with imprisonment of up to three years, or a fine, or both, with the maximum fine raised by the amendment of 2022 from twenty-five thousand rupees to one lakh rupees. Where the offence relates to an animal in Schedule I, or to hunting inside a sanctuary or national park or altering its boundaries, the punishment rises to imprisonment of not less than three years and up to seven, together with a fine which the amendment of 2022 set at not less than twenty-five thousand rupees. A second or subsequent conviction for such an offence carries the same three-to-seven-year band with a higher minimum fine. The structure is deliberately asymmetric: the Act treats the killing or trading of a tiger as a different order of wrong from a procedural lapse under a licence, and prices them accordingly.

Two smaller mechanisms soften the ladder's edge and deserve notice because practitioners rely on them. Section 54 empowers the chief wildlife warden and authorised officers to compound offences, accepting a payment in composition, except offences for which a mandatory minimum imprisonment is prescribed, which after the 2022 fine revisions excludes the Schedule I core of the Act; compounding disposes of the mass of technical and licence offences without a trial, and its exclusion of the serious core is the deliberate line between an administrative settlement and an impunity market. Section 60A empowers a court to grant a reward, payable from a fine it imposes, to a person who assists in the detection of an offence or the apprehension of an offender, a small statutory recognition that wildlife crime is defeated by informers inside the trade more often than by patrols outside it. The gap between the Act's deterrent design and its courtroom yield, discussed next, is therefore not a drafting gap; the draftsman supplied severity, forfeiture, reversed burdens and informer rewards, and the shortfall lies in investigation quality, identification evidence and time.

The honest assessment of this machinery must record its two-sided record. On paper the Act is among India's most demanding environmental statutes, with reversed burdens, warrantless search and mandatory minimum imprisonment of a kind the pollution statutes never acquired. In court, wildlife prosecutions have historically suffered the familiar pathologies of Indian criminal process, long trials, witnesses turning hostile, cases collapsing on chain-of-custody and species-identification failures, and conviction rates that researchers and parliamentary answers have described as low without agreeing on a single stable figure. The gap is widest exactly where the trade is most organised, because organised networks litigate procedure skilfully and intimidate witnesses effectively, which is why the Bureau's intelligence-led model and the prosecution of networks rather than individual poachers became the system's strategic answer to its own courtroom record. Readers who need advice on a live matter should take it from a practitioner: this article states the general position of the statute, and individual cases turn on facts and limitation that general writing cannot assess.

## The vermin power and the conflict the Act cannot answer

Section 62 holds the Act's most politically explosive power. The Central Government may, by notification, declare any wild animal specified in Schedule II to be vermin for a specified area and for a period the notification states, and while the declaration runs, the animal is treated for the purposes of the Act as if it stood outside the protected schedules: it may be hunted without the permission Section 11 demands, and Section 39's vesting of ownership does not attach to it. The word "vermin" itself is defined by reference to the notification, so the category exists only as, when and where the Centre creates it.

The provision's history explains its placement in central hands. As enacted in 1972, the declaration power lay with the state governments, and a short Schedule V carried a standing list of vermin, the common crow, fruit bats, mice and rats. The amendment of 1991 transferred the power to the Centre, a centralisation defended on the ground that states under local pressure would declare too freely, and the Delhi High Court upheld the transfer against a farmer's challenge that Parliament had invaded the state field. The amendment of 2022 deleted Schedule V altogether, so that no standing vermin list exists, and confined the declaration power to Schedule II, which, because Schedule II absorbed the former third and fourth schedules in the same exercise, is a far larger pool of species than the phrase suggests. The two-step is worth holding in mind, because public argument usually notices only the first: the 2022 Act abolished the standing vermin list while enlarging the class of animals the Centre may strip of protection on demand.

The power is exercised episodically and always controversially. In the middle of the last decade the Centre, acting on state requests, declared wild pigs vermin in Uttarakhand, nilgai and wild pigs in identified districts of Bihar, and rhesus macaques in specified areas of Himachal Pradesh, in each case for a limited period and area. Each declaration drew the same argument in the same two voices. State governments and farmers' organisations, and the members of Parliament who carried their case, described animals whose numbers and boldness had made farming untenable in affected villages, crop losses running season after season, compensation that arrived late or not at all, and the absurdity, as they presented it, of a legal regime under which a farmer must watch a nilgai herd eat a crop because the animal's protection outranked the family's subsistence. Conservation scientists and animal welfare advocates answered that the declarations were made without population studies adequate to show that culling would reduce damage, that indiscriminate killing of social species disrupts group structure in ways that can increase conflict, that no declaration carried a duty to record how many animals were killed or with what effect, and that the political convenience of a notification was displacing the slower work of fencing, guarding, habitat management and prompt compensation. Kerala's long argument over the wild pig shows the politics of the power at their most developed. Successive state governments, pressed by farmers in the hill districts where pigs destroy tapioca and banana on a scale that shows in the state's politics, sought declarations and wider culling authority; the Centre's responses were measured and area-specific, and the state's political class turned to its own devices, including resolutions and local orders whose legality under the central Act was itself contested, since after 1991 the declaration power is the Centre's and a state instrument that achieves the same removal of protection invites the repugnancy question the Concurrent List always carries. The episode generalises: vermin is where the Act's centralisation is felt most sharply by elected state governments, because the demand arrives at the state capital while the signature sits in New Delhi, and the recurring federal friction over the power is the predictable result of the 1991 transfer.

In the Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy's analysis of the declaration system, the structural objection is the sharpest: Section 11 requires case-by-case justification tied to an individual animal and a recorded inquiry, while Section 62 requires none of that, no evidence threshold, no expert review, no outcome reporting, so that the statute's safeguards vary inversely with the scale of the killing authorised. That critique, and the farmers' rejoinder that case-by-case process has never fed a family whose crop is gone, state the vermin debate at its strongest on both sides, and the statute leaves the debate exactly there, to be settled by each notification's politics.

Beneath the episodic declarations sits the permanent fact that the Act was not designed for: human and wildlife conflict as a daily condition rather than an emergency. The numbers that recur in official discussion, of human deaths from elephant and tiger encounters running into the hundreds across the country in a year, of crop and livestock losses spread across every state with significant wildlife, are quoted with wide variation and should be handled as orders of magnitude rather than precise counts. What is structurally certain is the allocation of the burden and the remedy. The burden falls on households at the forest edge, among the poorest in their districts. The remedies that exist, ex gratia payments for death and injury and state compensation schemes for crop and livestock loss, are creatures of executive scheme and state budget, not of the Wild Life (Protection) Act, which contains no compensation chapter at all. The courts have filled part of the silence from constitutional ground: in Animal Welfare Board of India v A. Nagaraja in 2014 the Supreme Court read the Act together with the constitutional duty of compassion to give animals a dignity-protecting interpretation, while a constitution bench in 2023 upheld state legislation permitting traditional bull events, marking the boundary of that expansion; and in M. K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India in 2024 the Court recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change while shaping relief to protect the great Indian bustard's habitat from overhead power lines. These judgments widen the Act's moral frame without altering its central gap, which is institutional rather than doctrinal: the statute protects animals from people with great sophistication and protects people from animals hardly at all.

The research record on the compensation side of this gap is unusually concrete, and it cuts against complacency in both directions. Field studies by Krithi K. Karanth and her collaborators around the reserves of Karnataka documented the pattern household by household: crop and livestock losses concentrated in a minority of households nearest the boundary, compensation claims filed by only a fraction of those who lost, payments delayed and partial, and a measurable share of households absorbing losses in silence because the claiming cost exceeded the expected award. Studies of failed compensation as a driver of retaliatory killing, including the work associated with M. D. Madhusudan on the ecology and economics of conflict around protected areas, supply the causal half of the point: an uncompensated loss is not only an injustice but a conservation failure, since the poisoned carcass and the snare set for the pig that takes the leopard are how unpriced conflict returns to the protected population. The policy literature's conclusion, that prompt, adequate and simple compensation is among the highest-yield conservation expenditures available, has been absorbed unevenly by state schemes, some of which have digitised claims and raised rates substantially while others retain the paper gauntlet the studies described. One further instrument has been bolted on from the insurance side: the national crop insurance scheme has been opened in a number of states to treat wild animal attack as an insurable localised peril for enrolled crops, which converts an ex gratia favour into a contractual claim, though uptake among the marginal farmers who bear the heaviest losses, and the assessment of damage by a wild species as opposed to weather, remain the practical obstacles the field studies would predict.

Around the statute, and largely outside it, an administrative conflict apparatus has grown up to supply what the Act omits: primary response teams of trained local volunteers in high-conflict landscapes, rapid response units with tranquilisation capacity attached to territorial divisions, standard operating procedures issued by the Tiger Conservation Authority for tigers straying into human habitation and for leopard management, elephant early-warning systems by message alert in the tea country of the north-east and the Western Ghats, and barrier engineering from elephant-proof trenches to solar fencing. None of this machinery has a statutory home in the Wild Life (Protection) Act. It runs on scheme guidelines, authority protocols and state budgets, which is why it varies so sharply between a well-funded tiger state and a poor one, and why the recurrent reform proposal, pressed by conflict researchers and by parliamentary committees examining the subject, is to write a conflict chapter into the Act itself: duties of prevention, a compensation entitlement with timelines, and data reporting that would make Section 62 declarations answerable to recorded outcomes. Parliament has not taken that step in any amendment so far, including the CITES amendment of 2022, and the omission is the clearest single evidence for the claim this article has pressed: the statute's architecture still faces the hunter it was built against, not the neighbour it has acquired.

## The captive elephant exception of 2022

No single clause of the amendment of 2022 attracted more argument than the proviso it added to Section 43, the provision that bars the transfer of captive animals by sale or other commercial transaction. The proviso provides that the transfer or transport of a captive elephant for a religious or any other purpose by a person holding a valid certificate of ownership shall be subject to such terms and conditions as the Central Government prescribes. The elephant is a Schedule I animal, at the summit of the Act's protection, and the general rule since the trade amendments has been that a captive elephant may change hands by inheritance or in the narrow ways the ownership chapter permits, but may not be commercially traded.

The criticism, made in the Rajya Sabha debate by members including Sushmita Dev and outside Parliament by animal welfare organisations such as PETA India, is that the words "any other purpose" are without visible limit, that a transfer for any purpose on prescribed conditions is in substance a reopening of the elephant trade, and that a lawful channel for moving elephants between owners will launder the unlawful capture of wild elephants into documented captivity, as the Wildlife Rescue and Rehabilitation Centre's long litigation over captive elephant welfare had shown the documentation system being gamed through gift deeds and paper transfers. The supporting case, made by the government in defending the clause and by owners' associations, particularly in Kerala where temple and private elephants form a distinct institution, is narrower than the clause's opponents allow: the bill introduced in 2021 had proposed a far wider exemption, which the enacted text cut back to a conditional proviso; owners who can no longer maintain an ageing elephant, in a period when the costs of upkeep have outrun many private owners' means, need a lawful route to place the animal with an institution able to keep it; and a regulated transfer on prescribed conditions is preferable to the unregulated market in deeds of gift that the prohibition had in fact produced. Both cases identify something real: the documentary system has been abused, and the prohibition without a transfer route had generated its own abuses. The enacted proviso chose regulation over prohibition for this one species. Whether the prescribed conditions are policed with the rigour the choice presupposes is an implementation question on which the statute's defenders and critics will be reading the same transfer registers for years.

The litigation history explains why the registers matter. In proceedings brought by the Wildlife Rescue and Rehabilitation Centre and heard over years in the Supreme Court, the ownership certificates of Kerala's captive elephants came under sustained scrutiny, with the Centre reporting that certificates had been issued for elephants whose lawful acquisition could not be shown, and transfers between states, including the movement of elephants from the north-east to buyers elsewhere, repeatedly surfacing as commercial sales dressed in the paperwork of gift. A Gauhati High Court decision of 2021, arising from the interception of two elephants moving from Arunachal Pradesh towards Odisha on a claimed religious purpose, found prima facie evidence of a paid transaction behind the certificates and kept the animals in departmental custody while investigation proceeded. That case, decided a year before the amendment, is the critics' best exhibit: the pre-2022 law already contained the certificate system, and the certificate system is what failed. The government's answer is that the proviso adds a condition layer the old law lacked and that prohibiting transfer outright did not stop movement, it only stripped it of records. Both readings of the same record are available in the material, and the choice between them is a judgment about administrative capacity rather than about elephants.

## The text in force: amendments and versions

A final orientation concerns the Act's text itself, because readers meet it in versions of very different vintage. The statute in force is the 1972 text as amended repeatedly over five decades, substantially in 1991, 2002, 2006 and 2022, a palimpsest in which the schedule references in any unrevised reprint will mislead: a bare Act printed before April 2023 still shows six schedules and the old penalty figures, and commentary written from it will misstate the fine for a Schedule I offence, the composition of Schedule II and the very existence of the CITES chapter. The discipline this imposes is unglamorous and absolute. Work from the updated text, check the commencement date of the amendment against the date of the events in question, and treat any secondary account that describes the vermin schedule or the sixfold structure as the living law as a source about the Act's past rather than its present. Transitional questions are real ones: an offence committed under the old schedules is governed by the law of its date, and the renumbering of 2022 does not retrospectively regrade it, a point on which prosecutions spanning the change have had to be careful. The Concurrent List position adds a second layer of version discipline. Since 1977 the states share competence over wildlife, and a state legislature may enact provisions in the field, with a state amendment that conflicts with the central Act requiring the President's assent under the repugnancy rule of Article 254 to prevail within that state. State amendments and state rules under the Act therefore exist at the margins of the national scheme, touching matters such as the composition of state machinery and the handling of particular species, and the officer or advocate who works from the central text alone will occasionally be caught by a state variation that the file, but not the book, contains. The pillar records the national scheme; the working lawyer confirms the state layer before advising on it.

## Reading the Act as a practitioner and as an aspirant

Two reading disciplines extract the statute's value fastest, and they are worth stating plainly because the Act's organisation defeats casual reading. The practitioner reads by transaction. A client holding an inherited trophy needs the ownership chapter and the certificate trail, not the hunting sections. A developer with land near a sanctuary needs the protected area chapter, the boundary lock and the eco-sensitive zone notification, in that order. A farmer facing raids needs Section 11 and Section 62 and the state compensation scheme, and needs to be told that the second of those three is not in the book. A prosecutor needs Sections 39, 50, 51 and 57 as one machine. Each transaction has its own four or five provisions, and the pillar's function is to make those clusters visible rather than to march the reader through sixty-six sections in order.

The aspirant reads by fault line, because the examination questions cluster where the statute argues with itself. The tested distinctions are the ones this article has drawn: sanctuary against national park on the survival of rights; conservation reserve against community reserve on whose land and whose initiative; Section 11 against Section 62 on case-by-case permission against blanket declaration; the old six schedules against the new four, with the CITES chapter and the abolished vermin list as the changes that matter; the Article 252 origin against the Article 253 origin of the Environment (Protection) Act, two statutes of the same environmental family born by opposite constitutional roads; and the National Board against the Tiger Conservation Authority on advice against approval. A candidate who can state each distinction with its section number and its reason owns the tested core of the subject, and the remainder, the institutional detail and the case names, assembles itself around those six contrasts.

## Assessment: a fortress statute in a landscape of neighbours

Measured against its founding purpose, the Act works. The tiger estimation conducted by the National Tiger Conservation Authority and the Wildlife Institute of India put the country's tiger population at an estimated 3,682 animals in the 2022 exercise, within a range the report states, against the camera-trap based estimate of 1,411 in 2006: a recovery built inside this statute's protected areas, under this statute's offences, and inconceivable under the game regime it replaced. The elephant and the one-horned rhinoceros, each targeted by an organised trade the Act criminalised, persist in numbers their 1972 trajectories did not promise. Any assessment that omits this record, as some of the Act's rhetorical critics do, is not serious.

The counterexamples discipline the celebration, and they share a diagnostic feature. The great Indian bustard, a Schedule I bird under the Act's highest protection for five decades, declined to a remnant population in its desert and grassland range, its losses driven by habitat conversion and collision with power lines rather than by hunting the Act could prosecute, until the Supreme Court's climate judgment took up its cause on energy infrastructure grounds. The vulture collapse of the 1990s, among the fastest declines of any bird group recorded anywhere, was traced to a veterinary drug in cattle carcasses and was answered through drug regulation, not through this statute at all. In each case the species sat at the top of the schedules while the threat operated in a register the Act does not reach: land use outside protected areas, and chemistry inside lawful commerce. Schedule I status, the record shows, is a complete answer to the hunter and a partial answer to everything else, and the statutes and regulators around this Act, forest diversion, environmental clearance, drug control, have had to carry the remainder.

Measured against the problem that dominates wildlife administration in practice, the Act is largely silent, and its silence has a structure. It is a statute of prohibitions addressed to taking, and of areas addressed to habitat. Human and wildlife conflict is neither a taking nor an area; it is a relationship, between a recovering animal population and the settled human population at the protected area's edge, and the statute's only answers to it are the two lethal instruments of Section 11 and Section 62 and a compensation system it does not contain. The community reserve of 2002, the buffer doctrine of 2006 and the coexistence language of the tiger chapter are real gestures towards that relationship, and the amendment of 2022 added local consultation to sanctuary management planning, yet the centre of the Act remains where Parliament put it in 1972: protect the animal without qualification, fence the habitat by area, and treat the animal among people as an exception to be licensed away.

The reform debate around the Act has a stable shape, and a reader who knows it can place any new proposal. One family of proposals would complete the statute on its own terms: a conflict chapter carrying prevention duties, a compensation entitlement with timelines, and outcome reporting for vermin declarations, as the conflict researchers and the Vidhi analysis in their different vocabularies propose. A second family would rebalance its federalism, restoring to the states a share of the declaration and listing decisions that centralisation took from them, a case the states pressed in the 2022 debates and that the state boards' standing committee controversy keeps alive. A third reaches for the schedules themselves, arguing that listing should follow a standing scientific process with periodic revision rather than amendment by omnibus Act, so that the lists track taxonomy and threat status as they change. A fourth, pressed by animal welfare advocates through the elephant clause debate, would tighten rather than loosen: confine the transfer proviso, strengthen the certificate audit, and write welfare standards for captive scheduled animals into the Act with the force the zoo chapter gives them for collections. Against all four stands the ministry's working defence of the 2022 settlement, that the statute's task is protection, that conflict and welfare are governed by scheme and protocol layered over it, and that a code amended five times in five decades has shown it can absorb new chapters when the case is proven. The parliamentary standing committee that examined the 2021 bill had recommended narrowing the elephant provision before enactment, and Parliament enacted the wider text with conditions instead, which is where the burden of proof in this debate presently sits: on the implementation of the conditions, watched by both camps.

That is the claim this pillar leaves with the reader, and it is the thread the cluster's specialist articles pick up: protection was designed for places, not for coexistence. The statute that made every wild animal government property and hunting a crime has, in consequence, an elaborate law for the animal in the forest and almost no law for the animal in the field, and the distance between those two animals is the distance Indian wildlife policy still has to travel.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Wildlife Protection Act prohibit?

The Act prohibits the hunting of every wild animal listed in its schedules, and hunting is defined to include capturing, snaring, trapping, coursing, driving and baiting as well as killing, together with attempts and, for birds and reptiles, taking eggs or disturbing nests. It prohibits trade in trophies, animal articles and meat derived from the most protected species, restricts all dealings in scheduled specimens to a system of declarations, ownership certificates and licences, and bars specified acts inside protected areas, such as destroying habitat, diverting water and grazing without permit. It also prohibits keeping a scheduled animal or article that is government property under Section 39 without the warden's permission, which makes unlawful possession itself an offence.

### Q: What are the schedules under the Wildlife Protection Act?

After the amendment of 2022 the Act has four schedules. Schedule I lists the animal species given the highest level of protection, including the tiger, elephant and rhinoceros, and offences against them attract the heaviest penalties. Schedule II lists animal species given a lesser but still substantial level of protection, absorbing what were formerly the third and fourth schedules. Schedule III lists protected plant species, descended from the plant schedule added in 1991, whose picking, uprooting and trade are regulated. Schedule IV lists the specimens that appear in the appendices of CITES, the international convention on trade in endangered species, and supports the permit system for international trade. Before 2022 there were six schedules, including a standing vermin list.

### Q: What is the difference between a national park and a sanctuary?

The difference lies in the treatment of rights inside the boundary. A sanctuary is declared after a settlement process in which existing private rights are inquired into, and rights that are admitted but not acquired by the state continue; grazing may be regulated by permit, and residents' admitted uses survive. A national park requires the settlement to be completed by extinguishing or acquiring inconsistent rights, prohibits grazing altogether, and allows no destruction, exploitation or removal of wildlife or habitat except under permit for narrow purposes. Boundary alteration in either case requires the recommendation of the National Board for Wild Life. In short, a sanctuary is protection negotiated with existing rights; a national park is protection from which incompatible rights have been removed.

### Q: Who owns a wild animal in India?

The government does. Section 39 of the Act provides that a wild animal, other than vermin, which is hunted, kept or bred in captivity, found dead or killed by mistake, together with animal articles, trophies and meat derived from it, is the property of the government, ordinarily of the state government where the animal is taken or found. A private person may hold a scheduled animal or article only inside the Act's documentary system, through a certificate of ownership or a lawful declaration of stock, and transfers are restricted and require permission. An animal killed in self-defence also becomes government property. The rule inverts the old position under which a wild animal belonged to whoever lawfully took it.

### Q: When can a wild animal be hunted legally?

In narrow circumstances only. The chief wildlife warden may permit the hunting of a Schedule I animal under Section 11 where it has become dangerous to human life, or is so disabled or diseased as to be beyond recovery, and of a Schedule II animal also where it is dangerous to property including standing crops. Permission may also be granted for education, scientific research and scientific management under Section 12. Killing or wounding an animal in defence of oneself or another person is declared no offence. An animal declared vermin for an area and period by the Central Government under Section 62 may be hunted while the declaration runs. Everything else is an offence.

### Q: What did the 2022 Wildlife Protection Act amendment change?

The Wild Life (Protection) Amendment Act, 2022, Act 18 of 2022, in force from 1 April 2023, reduced the six schedules to four: Schedule I for the highest protection, Schedule II for lesser protection, Schedule III for protected plants and Schedule IV for specimens listed under CITES. It inserted a chapter giving CITES domestic effect, with a Management Authority to issue trade permits and a Scientific Authority to advise on the effect of trade on species survival, and a registration regime for living listed specimens. It abolished the standing vermin schedule, recast the vermin declaration power, raised fines, empowered the Centre against invasive alien species, and added the contested proviso permitting transfer of captive elephants by certificate holders for religious or other purposes on prescribed conditions.

### Q: How do conservation reserves and community reserves differ?

They differ in whose land they cover and whose initiative creates them. A conservation reserve is declared by a state government under Section 36A over government-owned land, typically land adjoining a national park or sanctuary or linking protected areas, after consulting local communities, and is managed with the advice of a committee in which local bodies are represented. A community reserve is declared under Section 36C over private or community land only where an individual or a community has volunteered the land for conservation; ownership does not change, an elected community committee prepares the conservation plan, and no change in land use may be made except by a resolution of that committee approved by the state government.

### Q: Which body approves projects inside a protected area?

No single body does, because the approvals interlock. A project needing land inside a sanctuary or national park requires the recommendation of the National Board for Wild Life, whose Standing Committee, chaired by the Union environment minister, appraises such proposals and imposes conditions. Inside a tiger reserve, the tiger conservation plan approved by the National Tiger Conservation Authority governs, and boundary alteration or de-notification needs both the Authority's recommendation and the Board's approval. The chief wildlife warden controls permits for entry and activity within the area itself. These wildlife approvals run alongside, and do not replace, forest diversion approval and environmental clearance under their own statutes.

### Q: Can a farmer kill a wild animal that is destroying crops?

Ordinarily, no. A farmer who kills a scheduled animal that is raiding crops commits the offence of hunting unless an exception applies, however severe the damage. The lawful routes are indirect: the chief wildlife warden, or an authorised officer, may permit the hunting of a Schedule II animal that has become dangerous to property, expressly including standing crops, under Section 11; an animal declared vermin for the area under Section 62 may be killed while the declaration runs; and an animal may be killed in defence of human life, though not in defence of crops alone. In practice the farmer's realistic remedy under the legal scheme is the state compensation scheme for crop loss, which sits outside this Act.

### Q: What is an eco-sensitive zone and what does it restrict?

An eco-sensitive zone is a belt of land around a national park or wildlife sanctuary, notified by the Central Government under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and its rules rather than under the wildlife statute itself. Within the zone, activities are classified as prohibited, regulated or permitted under the environment ministry's guidelines of 2011 and the site-specific notification: commercial mining and large polluting industry are typically prohibited, hotels, construction and tree felling are regulated and need clearance, and agriculture and similar existing uses are permitted. A monitoring committee oversees the zone. The Supreme Court's orders in the Godavarman litigation fixed and then refined the zone's minimum width, while keeping mining barred within one kilometre of a protected boundary.

### Q: What is a tiger reserve and how does it differ from a national park?

A tiger reserve is a statutory overlay, notified by a state government under Section 38V on the recommendation of the National Tiger Conservation Authority, over land that is usually a national park or sanctuary at its core. It differs from a national park in three ways. Its area is organised into a core or critical tiger habitat, to be kept inviolate, and a buffer managed for coexistence between wildlife and people. It is governed by a tiger conservation plan approved by the Authority rather than by the park machinery alone. And its boundaries cannot be altered, nor the reserve de-notified, without the Authority's recommendation and the National Board's approval. A national park is a land category; a tiger reserve is a management regime built on top of one.

### Q: What is the punishment for poaching a Schedule I animal?

Under Section 51, an offence relating to a Schedule I animal, or hunting inside a sanctuary or national park, is punishable with imprisonment of not less than three years and extending to seven years, together with a fine of not less than twenty-five thousand rupees as the amendment of 2022 set it. A second or subsequent conviction carries the same three to seven year band with a higher minimum fine. Lesser contraventions of the Act attract imprisonment of up to three years, a fine of up to one lakh rupees, or both. The court may also order forfeiture of the animal, trophy, weapon, trap or vehicle used in the offence, and the possession presumption in Section 57 eases the prosecution's burden.

### Q: How does the Act put CITES into effect in India?

The amendment of 2022 inserted a chapter on the regulation of international trade in endangered species, keyed to the new Schedule IV, which reproduces the specimens listed in the appendices of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora. The chapter provides for a Management Authority, designated by the Central Government, which grants the permits and certificates required for the export and import of scheduled specimens, and a Scientific Authority which advises on whether a proposed trade would be detrimental to the survival of the species concerned, the test the convention itself applies. Possession, transfer, birth and death of living specimens of listed species must be registered, bringing the domestic holding of exotic listed species inside the Act.

### Q: What does the National Tiger Conservation Authority do?

The Authority is the statutory body constituted by the amendment of 2006, chaired by the Union minister for environment and forests, which converted Project Tiger from an administrative scheme into a legal regime. It approves the tiger conservation plan that each state prepares for every tiger reserve, lays down normative and advisory standards for reserve management and tourism, evaluates the state of tiger conservation including the periodic national estimation of tiger numbers, and carries the statutory functions of assessment and monitoring that the scheme previously performed by executive instruction. A state cannot alter a tiger reserve's boundaries or de-notify it without the Authority's recommendation and the National Board's approval, which makes the Authority a veto point over the erosion of reserves.

### Q: Why did the tigers of Sariska disappear, and what changed in the law after that?

Sariska Tiger Reserve in Rajasthan lost its entire tiger population to poaching by 2004 and 2005, in a reserve that held funds, staff and full statutory protection on paper, a failure confirmed by official inquiry after conservationists forced it into public view. The proximate causes were organised poaching meeting unaccountable management and no honest counting. The Tiger Task Force appointed in 2005 recommended statutory backing for tiger conservation, and the amendment of 2006 answered it: the National Tiger Conservation Authority was constituted, tiger reserves received statutory form under Section 38V with core and buffer areas, tiger conservation plans became mandatory, and the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau was created to pursue the organised networks that a single reserve's staff could not.

### Q: What changes when the government declares an animal vermin?

A declaration by the Central Government under Section 62, made by notification for a specified area and a specified period, removes the declared animal from the Act's protection within that area and period. The animal, which must be a Schedule II species, may then be hunted without the case-by-case permission of the chief wildlife warden, and Section 39's rule that wild animals are government property does not apply to it. Since the amendment of 2022 there is no standing vermin schedule: vermin status exists only through such a notification, and lapses with it. Nothing else about the species changes, and outside the notified area, or after the notified period, full protection returns automatically.

### Q: Can a captive elephant be given or sold to a temple after the 2022 amendment?

The general rule in Section 43 bars the transfer of a captive animal by sale or other commercial transaction, and transfers otherwise run through the ownership certificate system, historically by inheritance or gift with official permission. The amendment of 2022 added a proviso under which the transfer or transport of a captive elephant for a religious or any other purpose, by a person holding a valid certificate of ownership, is permitted subject to such terms and conditions as the Central Government prescribes. A gift to a temple fits the clause plainly; whether a priced transaction does depends on the prescribed conditions, which is exactly the ambiguity critics attacked in Parliament, arguing the words reopen a commercial trade the Act had closed.

### Q: What must happen before a village is relocated from a critical tiger habitat?

Section 38V makes relocation the last step of a sequence, not an administrative starting point. The recognition and determination of the community's forest rights must first be completed under the forest rights legislation. It must then be established, on the prescribed inquiry, that the community's presence results in irreversible damage to the habitat and that coexistence is not a reasonable option. The informed consent of the Gram Sabha and of the families affected must be obtained for the proposed resettlement. A package providing a secure livelihood must be prepared, and the facilities and land allocation at the resettlement site must actually be provided, before the relocation proceeds. The sequence is cumulative: failure at any step stops the move.

### Q: What does the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau do?

The Bureau is the multi-agency body the amendment of 2006 inserted into the Act, staffed across the forest service, the police and the customs administration, and headed by the Director of Wild Life Preservation. Its functions are intelligence and coordination rather than ground patrol: it collects and collates intelligence about organised wildlife crime, maintains a centralised wildlife crime data bank, disseminates intelligence to state and other enforcement agencies for immediate action, coordinates enforcement action across state boundaries, supports the international obligations on wildlife crime, and builds investigative capacity and assists states in carrying prosecutions to success. It exists because the trade the Act prohibits is networked across jurisdictions, while ordinary enforcement under the Act stops at a single forest division.

### Q: Why was Parliament able to pass a wildlife law in 1972 when wildlife was a state subject?

Because the states asked it to. Protection of wild animals and birds stood in the State List, so Parliament used Article 252 of the Constitution, under which it may legislate on a state subject for the states whose legislatures pass resolutions requesting it. Eleven state legislatures resolved in favour of a central law, the Act applied to them, and other states adopted it afterwards, which is why its early territorial reach depended on adoption. The position changed in 1976, when the Forty-second Amendment moved forests and the protection of wild animals and birds to the Concurrent List, giving Parliament direct and continuing competence and allowing the central amendments of 1991, 2002, 2006 and 2022 to operate across the country at once.
