How FOIA Was Amended is best understood as a record of reaction rather than a record of steady expansion. Each major revision answered a defined failure in the statute as agencies, courts, and requesters had experienced that statute in practice. A judicial construction narrowed a right that Congress believed the text had granted. An administrative practice turned a deadline or a fee rule into a barrier that the text had not intended. A change in recordkeeping altered the material to which the entire scheme applied. The amendment pattern therefore reveals the operating condition of disclosure law between revisions: text enacted for one institutional environment had to be defended, corrected, or rebuilt when that environment changed.

The baseline was the Freedom of Information Act enacted in 1966 as Public Law 89-487 and codified at 5 U.S.C. 552. That statute replaced a withholding oriented provision of the Administrative Procedure Act with a general right of access to agency records, qualified by nine exemptions and enforceable in federal court. The baseline account in [the original Freedom of Information Act profile](/2021/10/01/freedom-of-information-act-1966-guide/) sets out the original design in full. The design contained a central tension from enactment. Exemptions drawn in broad category language required later specification, procedures stated without precise consequences invited delay, and judicial review depended on powers that the text described only in general terms. Each later round returned to one of those points of weakness and rewrote the governing language.

![How the Freedom of Information Act was amended over decades, from the veto override that gave the statute teeth to the foreseeable harm standard - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-38.webp)

The major revision sequence contains six acts. In order, the sequence comprises the amendments of 1974; the Government in the Sunshine Act of 1976; the Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986; the Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996; the OPEN Government Act of 2007; the Freedom of Information Act Improvement Act of 2016. Treated together, the six acts altered deadlines, fees, exemptions, formats, mediation, proactive disclosure, and the standard for withholding. Treated separately, each act preserved the circumstances of enactment in statutory form, because the wording of each revision answered the particular decision, scandal, backlog, or technology that had exposed the weakness in the preceding text.

The six round sequence describes major revision rather than every congressional change. The Department of Justice Office of Information Policy counts seven congressional amendments in total, including narrower measures: a technical change in 1978, an enactment in 1984, a restriction concerning foreign requesters in 2002, and a rule concerning designation under Exemption 3 in 2009, the OPEN FOIA Act of 2009. The distinction between the two counts matters for study of the statute. The narrower measures adjusted particular points without rebuilding a major part of the scheme, while each of the six major acts changed how requests were priced, timed, decided, reviewed, or published across the government.

The pattern across the six rounds has a consistent trigger and an inconsistent direction. The amendments of 1974 followed a scandal and a narrowing judicial construction. The Sunshine Act round followed a broadening judicial construction of Exemption 3. The round of 1996 followed a migration of agency recordkeeping into electronic form and a deadline that agencies had missed as a matter of routine. The rounds of 2007 and 2016 followed documented delay, backlog, and dependence on executive policy for the governing standard. The Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986 stands as the countercurrent within the pattern, because that round lowered the cost of requesting while broadening law enforcement withholding and creating exclusions that removed some records from the ordinary response process.

Reading the amendments in sequence also clarifies a distinction that single provision analysis obscures. Some rounds changed the substantive boundary between release and withholding, by narrowing or broadening an exemption or by setting the harm standard that an agency had to satisfy. Other rounds changed procedure without moving that boundary, by creating mediation, assigning tracking numbers, lengthening an appeal period, or requiring proactive posting after repeated requests. A third kind of change preserved an existing boundary against erosion, most clearly in 1996, when electronic records required a statutory statement that coverage followed the record into a new medium. Confusing those kinds of change produces a distorted history in which every amendment appears as a vote for or against openness in the abstract.

One claim about the sequence can be stated plainly and supported by vote and text. The enforceable version of the statute exists because a congressional supermajority overrode a presidential veto in 1974. Without that override, the deadlines, fee limits, in camera review of classified records, segregability duty, narrower Exemption 1, rewritten Exemption 7, and fee shifting for requesters who substantially prevailed would have remained proposals rejected by the executive branch. Later rounds built on that foundation, even when a later round narrowed access in a defined area. The foundation therefore carries more explanatory weight than any single later adjustment, and the six sections that follow examine each round in the order of enactment.

## The First Major Revision After Watergate and Mink

The amendments of 1974 stand first in the sequence of six major revision acts that reshaped the Freedom of Information Act, and the round carried a weight that later rounds rarely matched. The measure, the Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1974, Public Law 93-502, 88 Stat. 1561, carried as bill H.R. 12471, rewrote the relationship among a requester, an agency holding records, and a federal court asked to decide whether withholding had a lawful basis. Before the round, the statute announced a presumption of disclosure and gave a disappointed requester a route into court, yet agencies retained wide room to delay, to charge, to characterize records beyond inspection, and to defend a refusal without exposing the underlying material to independent examination. The elements worked together. A deadline without a fee rule could be defeated by cost, and judicial inspection without segregability could produce an all or nothing contest over a whole file when only part of the file deserved protection.

Two prompts converged to produce that design, one political and one judicial. The political prompt was the Watergate scandal, a crisis over executive secrecy and control of information, which reached a decisive point when President Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974. The judicial prompt was EPA v. Mink, 413 U.S. 493 (1973), in which the Supreme Court barred in camera review of classified material under Exemption 1 and read segregability narrowly. Watergate supplied the will to act against secrecy as a practice of government. Mink supplied a precise doctrinal target, because the decision identified limits in the statute as written that only Congress could remove by amending the text. A scandal alone might have produced hearings or temporary pressure, with behavior drifting back once attention faded. A court decision alone might have produced a narrow technical correction. Together, the scandal and the decision produced a comprehensive revision, because Congress had both a reason to distrust executive claims made without verification and a judicial map showing exactly where verification had been foreclosed.

Watergate mattered in a specific institutional sense, beyond the general atmosphere of distrust. The investigations surrounding the scandal had turned on access to records, on executive claims that certain material lay beyond the reach of investigators and courts, and on the discovery that official accounts could diverge sharply from what contemporaneous documents showed. For legislators weighing a disclosure statute, that experience altered the burden of persuasion. An agency argument that records should rest entirely on executive characterization, without independent checking, carried less weight in a Congress that had watched characterization fail under scrutiny. The resignation in August 1974 did not by itself draft a single provision of H.R. 12471, and legislative work on reform extended across a longer period than the final crisis. The resignation nevertheless fixed the context in which the final votes occurred, because the case for trusting unreviewed executive secrecy had collapsed in public view during the months before the override.

Mink mattered for the opposite reason, because the decision showed what happened when a court attempted to enforce the statute as written and found the text insufficient. The case arose under Exemption 1, covering material properly classified in the interest of national defense or foreign policy, and the questions included whether a district court could examine classified material in camera, in private, to test whether the classification claim covered the whole of the material or only part. The Supreme Court barred that form of review for classified material, leaving the executive determination largely conclusive, and the Court also read the obligation to release segregable portions narrowly, so material intertwined with exempt material could remain withheld as a whole. Judicial review is the enforcement mechanism of the act, and a rule preventing the reviewing judge from seeing the disputed records reduces review to an assessment of affidavits prepared by the party defending withholding. Congress responded by changing the statute, authorizing in camera examination expressly, narrowing Exemption 1 to material properly classified under Executive order criteria, and codifying and extending the duty to release reasonably segregable portions.

The congressional drive joined legislators whose work crossed chamber lines and rested on sustained attention to agency administration of the act. Representative William Moorhead stood among the House sponsors, and Senator Edward Kennedy led the effort in the Senate. The case for the drive documented agency practice under the original statute: requests answered slowly or within no predictable period, fees assessed in ways that discouraged pursuit of records, exemption claims stated in general terms that a requester had no practical means to test, and litigation in which the evidence in dispute sat entirely in agency hands. The argument distinguished two kinds of executive concern. Protection of genuinely sensitive material concerning national defense, foreign policy, law enforcement, and personal privacy was accepted as legitimate, reflected in exemptions that the round narrowed rather than abolished. The institutional preference for controlling the timing and terms of disclosure was treated as a preference rather than a right, and the bill subordinated that preference to enforceable rules.

What the bill contained followed from that diagnosis. Enforceable time limits required a determination on a request within ten working days of receipt and a determination on an administrative appeal within twenty working days. Fee provisions limited charges to reasonable standard charges for document search and duplication and added a public interest fee waiver. Judicial provisions authorized a court to examine records in camera, including classified records, restoring the verification step that Mink had foreclosed. Exemption 1 was narrowed so that material had to be properly classified under Executive order criteria. Exemption 7 was rewritten from investigatory files to records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes, with a six-harm test. Segregability was codified and extended, so reasonably segregable portions had to be released. Attorney fees and litigation costs became available to requesters who had substantially prevailed. Annual reports required agencies to account for administration of the act, and disciplinary referral applied where a court found withholding arbitrary or capricious. The contents describe a statute redesigned around verification, timing, and consequence, and executive resistance focused precisely on those features rather than on the general principle of disclosure.

Executive resistance rested on arguments that deserve full statement, because the override rejected those arguments after consideration. The executive branch contended that judicial examination of classified material placed judges in a position for which the constitutional structure assigned responsibility to the President, and that a court ordering disclosure after private inspection could expose material whose sensitivity depended on context a reviewing judge might not fully appreciate. Fixed deadlines, applied across agencies with different request volumes and classification burdens, could force hasty decisions, divert staff, or produce litigation when a deadline passed for reasons of capacity rather than intent. A further argument concerned candor, the concern that officials expecting records to be examined by courts and released in part will write less fully and deliberate less frankly. The transparency answer held that classification already had criteria, that a judge examining material in private did not publish the material by examining the material, and that deadlines govern a determination rather than demand instant production of every record. The deliberative answer held that the exemptions existed to absorb the candor cost in defined categories, rather than through a general judicial inability to verify claims.

President Gerald R. Ford gave the resistance formal expression when the President vetoed the bill on October 17, 1974, calling the amendments unconstitutional and unworkable and objecting to judicial review of classified material and to the deadlines. The stated grounds identified the same two pressure points that had driven drafting, the constitutional place of classification and the practicality of time limits. A veto on those grounds forced Congress to decide whether the core mechanisms were worth enacting without presidential agreement, because accepting the grounds would have meant abandoning in camera review of classified records and enforceable deadlines, the two provisions that most directly changed the balance between agency and requester. President Ford had taken office after the resignation in August 1974 and had presented the new administration as a break from the secrecy associated with Watergate, which made the veto a test of how far that break extended when a specific institutional power was at stake. The veto also demonstrated continuity in executive positions on disclosure across administrations and party control of the presidency, a pattern repeated in later rounds. Congress responded by returning the same measure to a vote under the constitutional procedure for enactment over a veto.

The override votes settled the question with margins that left little ambiguity. The House voted on November 20, 1974, by 371 to 31 to override, and the Senate voted on November 21, 1974, by 65 to 27, with enactment following on November 21, 1974, as Public Law 93-502. Understanding [the mechanics of a veto override](/2022/04/01/veto-and-override-explained/) clarifies why those tallies carried structural significance beyond the fate of one bill, because an override requires a two-thirds majority in each chamber rather than the simple majority sufficient for ordinary passage. Structurally, enactment over a veto meant the disclosure rules rested on a legislative decision made with full knowledge of the executive position and in deliberate rejection of that position. An agency could not plausibly treat the deadlines as aspirational language adopted without thought about administrative burden, because the burden argument had been presented in a veto message and overridden. Amendments signed after negotiation reflect compromise between branches, while the 1974 amendments reflect a legislative settlement imposed over executive objection, which helps explain why the round reached further than a negotiated measure of the same period might have reached.

### What did the override require and what did the round change?

A veto override required two-thirds of each chamber voting in favor, and the House and Senate both cleared that threshold in November of the amendment year. Enactment without presidential approval then locked time limits, judicial review, fee rules, and narrower exemptions into law rather than leaving disclosure to executive discretion alone.

The time limits addressed delay, the most common and least visible form of denial, since a request answered after the information has lost value has been denied in effect without any exemption being invoked. By requiring a determination within ten working days of receipt and a determination within twenty working days on administrative appeal, the amendments converted timing from agency queue management into a legal obligation with a defined endpoint. A determination is a decision on the request, a disposition that a requester can respond to, appeal, or challenge, rather than a promise that search and review will begin at some point. The deadline was short by design, and later history, in which the determination period was lengthened from ten to twenty working days in the amendments of 1996 after the shorter period had been routinely missed, shows that the tension identified in the veto between deadline and capacity was genuine. A standard that agencies strain to meet nevertheless disciplines behavior differently from no standard, because the standard determines who bears the burden of explanation when time passes.

The fee provisions operated through the price of access, and the round treated price as statutory policy rather than administrative discretion. Limiting charges to reasonable standard charges for document search and duplication only confined the categories for which an agency could bill, excluding broader and less predictable costs, and the word reasonable imposed a standard against which a charge schedule could be measured. The public interest fee waiver added a second layer, under which fees could be waived where disclosure served the public interest, recognizing that the benefit of certain records flows to a broad audience rather than to the individual who filed the request. A journalist, a researcher, or a civic organization seeking records on a matter of public concern produces value for readers and citizens who never learn the name of the requester, and a fee structure charging full cost to the requester alone understates that benefit and deters requests whose value is widely shared.

Judicial in camera examination supplied the enforcement mechanism that made the other provisions credible in contested cases. In camera examination means inspection by the judge in private, outside the presence of the parties, so the court can see the disputed records and decide whether an exemption covers the material without disclosing the material in the course of deciding. The amendments authorized that examination for records generally and, crucially, for classified records, directly reversing the limitation that EPA v. Mink had placed on review under Exemption 1. An agency affidavit describing records in general terms ceased to be the outer limit of what a court could consider, because the court could compare the description against the record and detect overstatement or segregable material within an exempt passage. A requester gained a realistic prospect in classification cases, where the requester by definition cannot describe the contents of records never seen and where a rule against judicial inspection would make the exemption effectively unreviewable. The availability of inspection also altered behavior before any inspection occurred, because an exemption claim drafted in the knowledge that a judge could read the underlying record tends to be drafted more carefully.

The narrowing of Exemption 1 worked alongside in camera review and gave the reviewing court a standard to apply during inspection. The amendments narrowed the exemption so that material must be properly classified under Executive order criteria, tying protection to compliance with the classification system itself rather than to the sensitivity of the subject matter in the abstract. The word properly carried the weight of the change. Material that an agency preferred to withhold, material that would embarrass an administration if released, or material touching defense or foreign affairs at a general level did not qualify merely by association with a sensitive topic. The material had to satisfy the criteria of the governing Executive order and to have been classified in accordance with those criteria, so a court conducting in camera examination could ask whether the classification met the governing standard, instead of asking whether deference was owed to an executive assertion. The change preserved the exemption for material genuinely classified under the system, including material whose release could damage national defense or foreign relations, while removing the argument that classification, once asserted, ended the inquiry.

The rewrite of Exemption 7 changed a heavily used law enforcement protection at two levels, the threshold description of covered material and the test for withholding within that description. As enacted in the original statute, Exemption 7 covered investigatory files compiled for law enforcement purposes, language that invited agencies to characterize an entire file as investigatory and to defend withholding at the level of the file as a whole. The amendments changed the coverage to records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes, shifting the unit of analysis from a file, a container holding a mixture of sensitive and routine material, to records or information that a court could examine individually and an agency had to justify individually. The amendments then attached a six-harm test, under which withholding required that disclosure produce one of six specified harms: interference with enforcement proceedings, deprivation of a fair trial or impartial adjudication, an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy, revelation of a confidential source, disclosure of investigative techniques and procedures, or endangerment of physical safety. A harm test replaces a categorical claim with a causal claim, so an agency had to connect the particular record to a particular injury on the list, and a court could test that connection against the record in camera and against the segregability duty.

Segregability addressed the space between full release and full withholding, where much practical disclosure litigation is decided. The doctrine holds that where a record contains both exempt and nonexempt material, the nonexempt material must be released after the exempt material is removed, unless the two are so intertwined that separation would leave meaningless fragments. Mink had read the obligation narrowly, and the amendments codified and extended the doctrine by requiring that reasonably segregable portions of records be released. The phrase reasonably segregable set the practical standard, asking whether separation could be accomplished through ordinary redaction, the deletion of a name, a paragraph, or a classified passage, without destroying the intelligibility of what remained. A reviewer could no longer rest on the presence of exempt matter somewhere in a document as a ground for withholding the document entire. Each document required a passage level judgment, and each judgment produced a visible artifact, a redacted record whose removed portions showed exactly where exemption claims had been applied. The duty applied across exemptions, not only under Exemption 1, and later rounds retained the discipline, including a requirement in the amendments of 2016 that agencies consider partial disclosure where full disclosure is not possible.

Attorney fees and litigation costs addressed the economics of enforcement, which determine whether rights in the text become rights in practice. Litigation against an agency carries costs that an individual requester, a small organization, or a freelance journalist may be unable to bear even where the legal claim is strong, and an agency deciding how aggressively to resist a request can take that imbalance into account. The amendments provided that a court could award attorney fees and litigation costs to requesters who had substantially prevailed, shifting part of the financial risk from the requester to the government where the requester had achieved the disclosure sought through the lawsuit. The standard of substantial success, rather than complete success on every record and every argument, reflected the reality of disclosure cases, in which an agency often releases records after suit is filed or a court orders release of most material while sustaining a small number of withholdings. The provision changed behavior before judgment. Requesters with meritorious claims could find counsel willing to take a case whose fees might be recovered, and agencies had a financial reason to reassess weak withholdings early rather than after months of litigation.

Annual reporting and disciplinary referral supplied the oversight and accountability layers, operating outside individual lawsuits and reaching behavior that no single requester would ever litigate. Annual agency reports on administration of the act created a regular flow of information to Congress about the volume of requests, the disposition of requests, and the exemptions invoked, converting anecdote about delay and withholding into data that committees could compare across agencies and across years. The disciplinary provision addressed individual accountability. Where a court found withholding arbitrary or capricious, the statute provided for disciplinary referral, under which the responsible conduct could be examined through the disciplinary process. The qualifier mattered, since the provision did not punish every withholding a court later rejected, a result that would have penalized good faith judgments about close exemption questions. Arbitrary or capricious withholding describes a withholding without a reasonable basis in the statute, and the referral marked that failing as a matter for institutional discipline rather than leaving the court order as the only consequence.

The consequences of the round explain why the amendments of 1974 occupy the central place in the six round sequence that runs through the Government in the Sunshine Act of 1976, the Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986, the Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996, the OPEN Government Act of 2007, and the Freedom of Information Act Improvement Act of 2016. The Department of Justice Office of Information Policy counts seven congressional amendments in total, including narrower measures, and the six round sequence remains the major revision sequence against that fuller count. Before the amendments, a requester held a presumption supported by a right to sue in which the court reviewed descriptions of unseen records under deferential exemption readings. After the amendments, a requester held a statute with consequences: deadlines agencies had to meet or explain, fees bounded by law and subject to waiver in the public interest, exemptions defined by checkable standards, a judicial power to inspect records and order release of segregable portions, a prospect of recovering the cost of successful enforcement, published reporting by which Congress could monitor performance, and a disciplinary path for withholding a court found arbitrary or capricious. Each consequence followed from text enacted over a presidential veto by margins of 371 to 31 and 65 to 27, and the durability of the structure across following decades measures the significance of what Congress built in the autumn of 1974.

## The Sunshine Act Round and the Narrowing of Exemption Three

Exemption Three occupies a distinctive place in the structure of the Freedom of Information Act because its content does not come from the Freedom of Information Act itself. The other exemptions describe categories of information in terms set out within the act, such as classified material, trade secrets, deliberative memoranda, personnel and medical files, and law enforcement records. Exemption Three works differently. It protects matters that are specifically exempted from disclosure by another statute, provided that the other statute meets conditions stated in the exemption. The operative decision therefore sits in two places at once. An agency or a court must first read the separate statute to determine what it covers and on what terms, and must then read Exemption Three to determine whether a statute of that kind qualifies as a withholding authority. Disclosure law can consequently change without any amendment to the Freedom of Information Act at all. When Congress enacts a nondisclosure provision for a tax program, a transportation safety program, or a financial regulatory program, the new provision may also redraw the boundary of public access, even though the debate that produced it centered on the substantive program rather than on transparency in general.

That incorporation by reference gives Exemption Three both its utility and its danger. Its utility lies in coherence across the United States Code, because a promise of confidentiality made for one regulatory purpose is not casually undone by a request under a general disclosure law. Its danger lies in dispersion, because the effective scope of withholding can expand statute by statute, in provisions that requesters and legislators may not recognize as amendments to the disclosure regime in practical effect. Litigation under Exemption Three also differs from litigation under most other exemptions. Threshold qualification comes first, and qualification turns on the language of a statute written for a different subject. A broadly worded provision may leave an agency with extensive choice about what to protect. A narrowly worded provision may command protection for a defined class of records and allow no choice at all. Between those poles lie statutes that allow withholding after an objection from a submitter and statutes that instruct an agency to weigh considerations before deciding. Before the amendment of nineteen seventy six, the act supplied little guidance for sorting those variations. The exemption referred to matters specifically exempted by statute, and the adjective specifically carried almost the entire weight of the limitation.

The case that forced the question arose from aviation safety administration. The Federal Aviation Administration operated a program known as the Systems Worthiness Analysis Program, under which the agency gathered and analyzed information bearing on the airworthiness and operational safety of aircraft and air carriers. Reports generated through that program, often shortened to SWAP reports in discussion of the case, contained assessments that drew on information supplied within the aviation industry and on the evaluative work of agency personnel. A requester sought disclosure of those reports under the Freedom of Information Act, and the agency resisted on the basis of a provision in the Federal Aviation Act of nineteen fifty eight. Section eleven oh four of that act authorized the Administrator to withhold information when a person filed an objection to its disclosure and the Administrator determined that disclosure would adversely affect the interests of the objecting person and was not required in the interest of the public. The provision thus linked withholding to three elements: information connected to the regulatory program, an objection by an interested person, and an administrative determination about adverse effect and public interest. It did not list particular documents and order them withheld in every circumstance. It conferred judgment, and the scope of that judgment was the point on which the case turned.

The agency position treated section eleven oh four as a statute specifically exempting material from disclosure within the meaning of Exemption Three. The requester position treated the same provision as too discretionary and too general to qualify, because a statute that merely allowed an official to decide against disclosure after weighing interests had not itself exempted any particular matter. That distinction mattered far beyond aviation. If discretionary authority sufficed, Congress could be understood to have exempted material whenever it gave an agency a choice, and the exemption would expand or contract with the confidence an agency placed in its own assessment. If specificity demanded more, Congress had to do identifiable legislative work in the separate statute, either by removing choice or by defining the protected ground with enough particularity that an agency and a court could apply the definition rather than recreate it case by case. The Supreme Court resolved the dispute in FAA v. Robertson, four hundred twenty two United States Reports page two hundred fifty five, decided on June twenty four, nineteen seventy five. The Court held that section eleven oh four qualified under Exemption Three. In the reasoning of the holding, the provision sufficed because it gave the Administrator broad discretion to withhold material on objection, and that grant of authority counted as a statute specifically exempting material from disclosure. The breadth that the requester treated as a defect became, in the holding, no barrier to qualification at all.

That outcome separated specific exemption from specific description, and the separation alarmed disclosure advocates and legislators who had supported the Freedom of Information Act less than a decade earlier. A statute could qualify without naming particular types of matters in careful terms and without requiring withholding in defined circumstances, so long as the statute addressed disclosure and placed the decision in agency hands. The practical locus of the disclosure boundary then shifted from legislative text to administrative practice. Two agencies operating under similarly broad provisions could reach different results on materially similar records, and a single agency could develop internal habits that hardened into a withholding regime without any further vote in Congress. Judicial review remained available in form, but a court had little statutory criteria against which to measure a discretionary decision, beyond an objection and an administrative determination. Almost any statutory reference to confidentiality, submission, or nondisclosure could be argued into a withholding basis if breadth posed no obstacle. Drafters working on an unrelated bill might insert a routine provision allowing an agency to protect information supplied by regulated parties, and that provision could later be invoked to defeat requests that its drafters never considered. No single committee held responsibility for the whole inventory of potential Exemption Three statutes, and no single debate tested whether each provision deserved to override the general policy of disclosure.

From the agency side, the countervailing concern carried genuine weight and deserved equal attention. Many federal programs depend on information that private parties, state authorities, or foreign counterparts supply under an expectation of limited use. An aviation safety program offers a clear illustration of the underlying logic, without deciding any particular record. If carriers and manufacturers believed that candid reports about defects, maintenance problems, or operational weaknesses would be released in full to competitors and the press, the flow of information to the regulator could thin or turn guarded, and safety analysis could suffer from the very transparency meant to improve public protection. A nondisclosure statute in such a setting is not merely a bureaucratic preference for secrecy. It can be the condition on which a reporting system functions, and an agency that disregards the expectation may honor a disclosure request at the cost of weakening the substantive mission that produced the records. The dispute after Robertson therefore pitted two forms of legislative control against each other: control through a general disclosure statute with predictable exemptions, and control through specialized statutes tailored to particular programs, including programs in which a credible promise of confidentiality induced the candor that regulation required.

### What did the Sunshine Act change in Exemption Three?

The nineteen seventy six amendment narrowed Exemption Three by requiring a separate withholding statute to leave no discretion or to establish particular criteria or identify particular types of matters to be withheld, replacing the broad discretion that the Supreme Court had accepted in Robertson.

Congress placed the response inside a larger measure devoted to open government. The Government in the Sunshine Act, Public Law ninety four dash four hundred nine, was signed by President Ford on September thirteen, nineteen seventy six. The act is associated principally with open meetings requirements for multi member federal agencies, and its title captured a broader legislative judgment that the operations of government should be visible to the public except where a defined reason required closure. Its component under the Freedom of Information Act was narrow in reach and precise in target. The Sunshine Act amended Exemption Three only. It left the other exemptions untouched, left the request and litigation machinery of the act in place, and addressed the single doctrinal problem that Robertson had exposed: the standard by which an outside statute earns recognition as an Exemption Three statute. Congress did not respond by withdrawing Exemption Three or by declaring that specialized nondisclosure statutes had no place in the disclosure system. Specialized statutes retained their role. What changed was the price of admission. A statute seeking recognition had to show, on its face or through its operative terms, either a command that removed choice or a definition that confined choice within stated bounds.

The amendment wrote that price of admission as a two limb test, and the exact phrasing matters because later litigation organized itself around the limbs. Under the revised exemption, a statute qualifies only if it satisfies one of two conditions. The first condition, limb A in the conventional labeling, is that the statute requires that the matters be withheld from the public in such a manner as to leave no discretion on the issue. The second condition, limb B, is that the statute establishes particular criteria for withholding or refers to particular types of matters to be withheld. Limb A covers a true legislative command, where agency judgment has no lawful role in deciding whether a category will be protected. Limb B covers a more common technique, where Congress specifies the criteria an agency must apply or the types of matters a provision covers. Under limb B an agency may retain judgment, but the judgment is guided judgment, exercised inside a frame that Congress supplied and that a court can inspect. Discretion alone, unaccompanied by either a mandate or a frame, fails the test. That single move transformed the adjective specifically from a loose descriptor into an operative standard with two demonstrable routes to qualification.

The Department of Justice Guide to the Freedom of Information Act, the principal executive branch commentary on the act, describes the nineteen seventy six amendment in unusually direct terms. In the account given by the Guide, the amendment was in effect overruling Robertson. The phrase in effect deserves attention. Congress had not reopened the judgment in the aviation case itself, and the amendment did not purport to revise the judgment between the parties to that litigation. Legislation operated on the governing standard, repudiating the construction that the Court had placed on the earlier text and substituting a construction that Congress preferred. The episode belongs to a recurring pattern in the history of the act, in which the Supreme Court reads an exemption in a manner that Congress regards as inconsistent with the legislative design, and Congress answers by amending the text. The nineteen seventy four amendments had followed a comparable sequence after EPA v. Mink narrowed judicial review and segregability under the earlier statute. The nineteen seventy six round repeated the pattern on a smaller canvas, with one exemption, one decision, and one corrective standard.

After the amendment took effect, the practical work of Exemption Three litigation changed in emphasis. Arguments that might once have ended with proof that a statute mentioned disclosure and conferred authority shifted toward two more demanding inquiries. The first inquiry concerned specificity. Counsel for an agency had to identify, in the separate statute, either language of command that left no discretion or language of definition that established particular criteria or referred to particular types of matters. General statements of confidentiality and grants of administrative authority invited closer resistance, because none of those features alone answered the statutory question that the amendment posed. Counsel for a requester gained a textual foothold that Robertson had denied. A requester could concede that a program statute addressed disclosure and yet contest qualification, on the ground that the statute neither compelled withholding nor confined it with the particularity that limb B required. The second inquiry concerned the intent and scope of the separate statute, read on its own terms. Even a qualifying statute protects only the matters that fall within its provisions, so litigation turned to what the specialized law actually covered: the class of records it described, the conditions it attached, the persons or submissions it addressed, and the purposes for which its protection had been enacted. Qualification and coverage became distinct questions, and an agency needed to prevail on both.

That separation improved the quality of justification that an agency had to supply, without denying the agency side of the case. An agency administering a program built on confidential submissions could make a stronger argument under the amended exemption than under the Robertson standard, provided that Congress had drafted the underlying statute with particularity. The agency could point to criteria that the legislature had chosen, explain how a record met those criteria, and show a court that withholding followed from a legislative judgment rather than from administrative preference. Where Congress had instead enacted only a vague assurance that information might be protected, the amended exemption forced a useful clarification. The agency had to return to Congress for a better drafted statute, or accept disclosure under the general law, or defend withholding under another exemption whose terms the record genuinely satisfied. The amendment also altered drafting practice. A provision meant to operate under Exemption Three needed either mandatory language or specific criteria and types of matters, and drafters who wanted reliable protection had an incentive to supply one or the other at enactment. Ambiguity lost its earlier advantage.

A later development completed the logic that the nineteen seventy six amendment had begun, although it arrived more than three decades afterward and belongs to the narrower measures in the executive branch count of amendments rather than to the six major revision rounds. The Department of Justice Office of Information Policy counts seven congressional amendments to the act in total, including a rule concerning designation under Exemption Three enacted in two thousand nine, identified as the OPEN FOIA Act of two thousand nine. The designation rule addressed a problem of recognition that the two limb test had left partly open. A statute could meet the specificity standard and yet give no clear signal at enactment that Congress understood it to be creating an Exemption Three authority, leaving litigants and agencies to reconstruct that understanding after the fact. The two thousand nine rule required statutes enacted after its adoption to state expressly that they operate as Exemption Three statutes. A future Congress seeking to create a new withholding authority had to name the mechanism in the text, making the disclosure consequence visible during the legislative process itself. The rule did not displace the two limb test. It added a threshold of explicitness for newer statutes, layered over the specificity that the Sunshine Act round had demanded.

Read together, the two reforms describe a coherent theory of how incorporation by reference ought to work in a disclosure law. The nineteen seventy six amendment insisted that a borrowed statute be specific, either by command or by criteria and types. The two thousand nine designation rule insisted that a newly borrowed statute be explicit about the borrowing. Congress, across the two measures separated by a generation, required both qualities before a specialized statute could reliably displace the general rule of access. The theory also preserved space for the legitimate agency interest that ran through the entire episode. A safety reporting system or any other program dependent on confidential cooperation could receive durable protection, resting on a statute that named its basis and defined its reach. What the amended exemption withdrew was not protection itself, but protection by implication, protection inferred from a general grant of discretion, and protection broad enough to cover whatever an agency preferred to keep from view. The Sunshine Act round therefore stands as the most compact of the major revisions in the sequence of six: one exemption amended, one construction repudiated, one test supplied, and a drafting discipline established that later Congresses extended rather than abandoned.

## The Countercurrent Round Inside a Drug Enforcement Law

The third major revision of the Freedom of Information Act arrived in a form that explained much of its character before a single provision was read. The Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986 was not enacted as a freestanding transparency measure debated on its own terms and passed on its own vote. Its provisions were carried at sections 1801 to 1804 of the Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986, Public Law 99-570, signed on October 27, 1986, a measure whose central business was crime and drug enforcement. A disclosure statute was therefore amended inside legislation built around the suppression of drug trafficking, the strengthening of criminal penalties, and the expansion of enforcement capacity. The resulting amendments carried both impulses at once, and the round cannot be described accurately as a simple advance for openness or a simple retreat from it.

The vehicle also shaped the politics of passage. A freestanding bill narrowing law enforcement disclosure would have drawn sustained scrutiny from press organizations, public interest groups, and congressional committees. Carried inside an omnibus drug measure in the autumn of 1986, amid intense public concern over crack cocaine and drug related violence, the same provisions moved with a bill that few members wished to be recorded against. The dynamic illustrates a recurring feature of large composite legislation, in which [the omnibus vehicle and its riders](/2022/04/15/omnibus-bills-and-riders/) travel together and provisions that might have faced a separate fight instead take effect as one part of a package assembled around a dominant priority. Opposition to the whole measure registered as opposition to a drug enforcement bill, while support for the whole measure required no separate endorsement of each withholding change it contained.

That mixed origin produced a mixed statute, and the clearest evidence lies in the contrast between the round's two principal subjects. The fee provisions were requester facing reforms, developed through long running disputes over how agencies charged for searches and copies and over how readily agencies waived those charges in the public interest. The law enforcement provisions were agency facing protections, developed through pressure from investigators who maintained that the 1974 version of Exemption 7 and the act's ordinary response obligations exposed investigations to compromise. One set treated cost as a barrier that priced ordinary requesters out of a statutory right and sought to lower it. The other set treated disclosure itself as a hazard to enforcement and sought to widen the ground on which records could be kept from requesters altogether.

The fee restructuring was the most intricate part of the round, and it replaced a relatively flat arrangement with a system that priced a request according to the identity and purpose of the requester. The 1974 amendments had limited fees to reasonable standard charges for document search and duplication and had supplied a public interest waiver, but agencies retained wide discretion in applying charges, and disputes over fees had become a standing source of friction between agencies and the journalists, scholars, and public interest organizations that used the act most heavily. An agency deciding how to assign search, review, and duplication costs held a practical power over whether a request proceeded at all, since a requester quoted a large fee in advance faced paying for a search of unknown yield, narrowing the request under financial pressure, or abandoning it. The 1986 restructuring addressed that power directly by specifying in the statute which categories of requester would pay for which components of the work, taking a measure of discretion away from individual agencies and converting fee practice into a set of entitlements defined by law.

At the top of the structure stood commercial use requesters, the one category charged for every component of processing. A requester seeking records for a commercial purpose paid fees for document search, for review, and for duplication. Review charges distinguished this category most sharply, because review was the most labor intensive and least predictable stage of processing. Congress treated commercial requesters as obtaining a private benefit from records assembled at public expense, so agencies could recover search, review, and duplication costs from them without the free allowances granted elsewhere. The commercial category functioned as the full price tier, and its existence made the concessions to the other categories legible as deliberate subsidies rather than as general reductions.

Below that tier, the statute placed educational institutions and noncommercial scientific institutions, together with representatives of the news media, on duplication only terms. An educational institution or a noncommercial scientific institution paid for duplication and for nothing else, and the first 100 pages of duplication were provided free of charge. Search time cost such a requester nothing, and review time cost such a requester nothing, however long either stage took. Representatives of the news media received the same treatment, paying duplication charges only, again with the first 100 pages free. The pairing of scholars and journalists in a single preferential tier expressed a judgment about the social function of their requests. Both groups characteristically gathered records for analysis and dissemination, turning raw agency material into research, teaching, and reporting for audiences far beyond the requester. By removing search and review charges from that work, Congress lowered the cost of exactly the requests most likely to produce public understanding, while the 100 free pages ensured that a modest request to an agency could often be completed at no charge at all.

The remaining category covered all other requesters, the individuals and organizations that fit neither the commercial definition nor the preferential institutional and press categories. Such a requester paid for search and for duplication, but not for review, and received two free allowances: the first two hours of search time and the first 100 pages of duplication were provided without charge. The two free search hours carried particular practical weight, since many routine requests could be located within that time, leaving an ordinary requester owing nothing for the search and paying only for copies beyond the free page allowance. The residual category thus sat between the scheme's two poles, with the free allowances marking the point at which Congress judged an ordinary request to have consumed a fair share of publicly funded effort.

Taken as a whole, the fee scheme was an instrument for shaping behavior on both sides of the request, and that instrumental quality explains its elaborate design. For agencies, the categories limited the ability to use anticipated fees as a screening device, because the charges available against each class of requester were fixed by statute rather than set case by case, and an agency could not quote a journalist search and review fees that the law forbade it to collect. The free allowances operated as a threshold of civic access, a quantity of search and copying that the government absorbed as the cost of maintaining a disclosure system open to the general public. Fees in this design were never merely accounting. The 1974 Congress had already recognized that a disclosure right burdened by unpredictable charges was a right weakened in practice, and the 1986 Congress extended that recognition into a differentiated schedule that attempted to match the price of a request to the character of the use behind it.

Alongside the categories, the 1986 round clarified and strengthened the public interest fee waiver, the provision under which fees could be reduced or eliminated altogether even for charges the schedule otherwise imposed. The clarified standard asked whether disclosure of the information was likely to contribute significantly to public understanding and whether disclosure was not primarily in the commercial interest of the requester. The contribution had to run to public understanding, not merely to private knowledge, tying the waiver to the disseminating function the preferential categories also rewarded. It had to be significant and likely, demanding more than speculation while stopping short of requiring a requester to prove in advance exactly what unreleased records would show. The commercial interest element guarded the waiver against a requester whose real object was private gain dressed in public interest language. The waiver and the categories reinforced each other: the categories set the default price for each class of request, and the waiver supplied an escape from that price where the public benefit of a particular disclosure justified the agency absorbing the whole cost.

The law enforcement half of the round moved in the opposite direction, and its center was Exemption 7, the exemption protecting law enforcement material. As enacted in 1966, the exemption had covered investigatory files compiled for law enforcement purposes, language that raised recurring questions about whether protection attached to a file as a whole and about which materials counted as investigatory. The 1974 amendments had recast the exemption around records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes and had attached a six part harm test, under which withholding required a showing that disclosure would interfere with enforcement proceedings, deprive a person of a fair trial or impartial adjudication, invade personal privacy, reveal a confidential source, disclose investigative techniques and procedures, or endanger physical safety. Law enforcement agencies pressed the argument that the structure left investigations exposed, because records gathered in enforcement work without a formal investigation behind them sat awkwardly within the statutory words. The 1986 amendments answered that pressure by widening both the threshold description of the material covered and the standard by which harm was judged.

The first change was a single deletion with broad consequences. The 1986 text deleted the word investigatory from the threshold, leaving the exemption to cover records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes. Before the deletion, an agency had to connect the material to an investigatory character, a point of resistance where records arose from routine enforcement activity or monitoring rather than from a discrete investigation. After the deletion, the threshold question became whether the records or information had been compiled for law enforcement purposes, a formulation that reached material gathered across the full range of an agency's enforcement functions. Records assembled by a law enforcement agency in the course of its mandate presumptively satisfied a threshold that records of a purely administrative character might not, and the contested ground in litigation shifted away from the investigatory pedigree of a file toward the second stage of the exemption, the enumerated harms. A one word deletion thereby enlarged the pool of records eligible for withholding before any question of harm was reached at all.

The second change lowered the harm standard itself. Where the 1974 text had required that disclosure would cause one of the enumerated harms, the 1986 text substituted a prospective and probabilistic test, covering records whose disclosure could reasonably be expected to cause such a harm. The distance between the two formulations was the distance between demonstrated consequence and reasonable anticipation. Under a would standard, an agency defended withholding by pointing to a harm that disclosure would produce, and a court could demand a comparatively firm connection between the release and the injury. Under a could reasonably be expected to standard, an agency defended withholding by showing that the anticipated harm was a reasonable expectation, a judgment about probability and foreseeability that an agency familiar with its own investigations was well placed to assert and that a court was correspondingly hesitant to displace. Speculation alone did not satisfy the formulation, since the expectation had to be reasonable, but the amendment eased the agency's burden at the point where Exemption 7 disputes were most often decided. The Department of Justice Guide to the act noted exceptions to the lowered standard, recording that Exemption 7(B), protecting fair trial and impartial adjudication rights, and part of Exemption 7(E), addressing investigative techniques and procedures, were excepted from the change, so the lowered threshold governed most but not all of the exemption's six harms. The combined effect of the two changes was substantial: more records entered the exemption's doorway, and the showing needed to keep them there grew lighter.

The round then went further than broadening an exemption, and created a device different in kind. At 5 U.S.C. 552(c), the 1986 amendments established three exclusions, situations in which records were removed from the act's ordinary operation altogether. The distinction between an exclusion and an exemption is qualitative rather than a matter of degree, and it turns on the response the agency gives. When an agency withholds records under an exemption, the existence of the records is acknowledged, the request is processed under the act, and the agency identifies the exemption on which withholding rests, leaving the requester able to appeal the determination and a court able to review it. When an agency applies an exclusion, the Department of Justice formulation states that the agency treats the records as not subject to the act and responds as if the records did not exist. The requester receives a response indicating that no records were found, in circumstances where records may in fact exist, with no exemption citation to challenge and no description of withheld material to contest. The exclusion thus operates outside the adversarial structure the act otherwise creates. The justification was that in a narrow set of situations, even confirming that records exist would itself cause the harm the law seeks to prevent.

The first exclusion, at section 552(c)(1), addressed records connected to a pending criminal investigation whose subject remained unaware of it. Where a request touched records relating to a pending criminal investigation, where the subject of the investigation was unaware it was underway, and where revealing the existence of the records could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings, the agency could treat the records as outside the act. A pending investigation depends on the subject acting without knowledge of official attention, and a disclosure request could expose it simply by producing an agency response confirming that investigative records existed. The exclusion severed that channel by permitting the agency to answer as though nothing existed, preserving secrecy about the fact of the investigation itself.

The second exclusion, at section 552(c)(2), protected informant records. It covered records maintained by a criminal law enforcement agency that consisted of information identifying an informant, where the informant's identity had not been officially confirmed. Informants occupy the most exposed position in criminal enforcement, supplying information from inside organizations where discovery of their cooperation could bring retaliation, and agencies retain them on assurances of confidentiality that a single disclosure could destroy for an entire network of sources. An exemption claim over informant records would have acknowledged that an agency held records about a named informant in a given matter, and that acknowledgment alone could confirm what the agency had never officially confirmed. The exclusion avoided that trap by allowing the agency to respond as if the records did not exist whenever the predicate conditions held, most critically the absence of official confirmation. Once an informant's identity had been officially confirmed, the rationale for the fiction fell away and the records returned to the act's ordinary framework, where Exemption 7 protections for confidential sources could be argued on their own terms.

The third exclusion, at section 552(c)(3), was confined to a single agency and a defined set of subjects. It covered records of the Federal Bureau of Investigation pertaining to foreign intelligence, counterintelligence, or international terrorism, where the existence of the records was itself classified. Foreign intelligence and counterintelligence work presents the acknowledgment problem in its sharpest form, because the fact that a bureau holds records on a particular matter can reveal targets, methods, and the direction of collection as surely as the contents would. Where the very existence of such records was classified, the 1986 amendments permitted the bureau to treat a request as reaching nothing at all, answering in the same form used when no records exist. The narrowness of the drafting is part of the provision's meaning: Congress limited this exclusion to the bureau rather than extending it across the government, limited it to three enumerated subjects rather than to national security material generally, and conditioned it on the existence of the records being classified, so that records whose existence carried no classification remained within the ordinary exemption framework, where Exemption 1 and Exemption 7 could be invoked and tested.

### Did this round of amendments expand or narrow access to government records?

The round did both at once. Its fee provisions lowered the cost of requesting and strengthened the public interest waiver, while its law enforcement provisions broadened withholding through a wider Exemption 7 threshold and three new exclusions that placed some records entirely outside the act's ordinary response process altogether.

The double direction was not an accident of drafting that a more careful Congress would have avoided. It followed from the conditions that produced the bill, from a disclosure statute amended inside a drug enforcement measure at a moment when enforcement concerns commanded the legislative agenda and fee reform commanded a constituency of its own. Each constituency received provisions addressed to its grievance. Journalists, scholars, and public interest requesters received a fee schedule that removed search and review charges from their work, free allowances that covered modest requests entirely, and a waiver standard written in terms of contribution to public understanding. Law enforcement agencies received a broader Exemption 7, a lighter burden in establishing harm, and, for the most sensitive categories of records, an exclusion device that relieved them of even acknowledging that records existed. Both sets remained in force together, so the act after October 27, 1986 was simultaneously cheaper to use and harder to use against law enforcement. Describing the round by either half alone misstates what Congress enacted.

The exclusions in particular mark the outer boundary of the act's logic, and their presence in the same statute as the fee reforms captures the round's tension in concentrated form. The fee provisions assumed a requester entitled to engage the government, to receive an accounting of charges tied to a statutory schedule, and to seek a waiver under a stated standard with reasons available for review. The exclusions assumed a small class of records for which engagement itself was the danger, where the protective response was a denial that records existed at all. Fees addressed the harm of a right priced beyond reach. Exclusions addressed the harm of investigations compromised and informants exposed. The 1986 Congress weighed both harms in a single enactment and moved against each, which is why the round resists the narrative of an amendment arc progressing steadily from secrecy toward openness. Substantive coverage moved as the politics of each round moved, and in 1986 the politics of drug enforcement pulled coverage backward at the same time as the politics of fee reform pushed access forward.

For the longer history of the statute, the significance of the round lies in that demonstration. Later rounds would codify a presumption of openness, build mediation machinery, and compel proactive electronic disclosure, inviting a reading of the act's development as a single direction traveled at varying speed. The 1986 amendments stand against that reading as evidence that each revision answered the pressures assembled around it, and that a Congress preoccupied with crime could widen withholding in the same enactment with which it cheapened access. The fee categories endured, structuring subsequent requests by the character of their use, and the broadened Exemption 7 threshold and lowered standard likewise endured, structuring subsequent law enforcement withholding by a test friendlier to the agency than the one the 1974 Congress had written. And the three exclusions endured as a reminder that the act contains a category beyond exemption, a zone in which the statute authorizes the government to answer a citizen's request by treating responsive records as though they were not there. Holding those three legacies together is the accurate account of what the Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986 did to the law it amended.

## The Electronic Amendments and the Preservation of an Established Right

The fourth major round of revision, the Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996, Public Law 104-231, 110 Stat. 3048, signed by President Clinton on October 2, 1996, arose from an occasion unlike the occasions behind the earlier rounds. The amendments of 1974 had followed the Watergate scandal and a narrowing judicial decision. The amendment carried in the Government in the Sunshine Act of 1976 had followed a broadening judicial decision. The Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986 had followed law enforcement pressure and long running disputes over fees, and had cut in both directions. The occasion in 1996 was quieter and more fundamental. Agency recordkeeping had migrated into electronic form, while the text of the act had been written around paper files. The question before Congress was not whether more material ought to be disclosed or whether more people ought to be entitled to ask for it. The question was whether a right written for one medium of recordkeeping reached records held in another medium at all.

That question carried consequences out of proportion to its technical appearance. A disclosure statute operates through definitions and procedures: what counts as a record, what counts as a search, where records available for public inspection are kept, how long an agency has to answer, and how an agency reports on its administration of the law. If each element quietly assumed paper, then a change in office practice could shrink the statute without any vote to narrow it. Records created on a computer, stored in a database, and retrieved through a query fit that design only if the statutory language stretched to cover them, or only if courts stretched it case by case. Congress in 1996 chose legislation over litigation to settle the point, and that choice explains the character of almost every provision in the round.

Leaving the matter to inference would have invited an argument about form in every disputed case. An agency resisting disclosure, or simply uncertain about its obligations, could contend that a database was not a file, that querying a database was not a search, or that a printout satisfied a request for the underlying electronic record even where the printout stripped away structure that gave the record meaning. Sensitivity analysis under the exemptions turns on content: a record about contracting, inspections, or grants does not become more sensitive because it is stored electronically. The 1996 round treated coverage, by contrast, as a question about the record and the manner of its keeping, and answered that question in favor of continuity. Continuity also mattered for evenhandedness: a statute reaching only paper would have reached a shrinking slice of agency activity, namely the slice that happened to be printed, and its reach would have varied with the printing habits of individual offices. The amendments tied coverage to the fact of agency maintenance rather than to the medium of maintenance.

### What did the electronic amendments change about access to federal records?

The amendments confirmed that records maintained in electronic form counted as records under the act, treated an electronic search as a search, required reasonable efforts to honor a requested format where readily reproducible, moved reading rooms online, doubled the determination period, and created expedited and multitrack processing.

The format provisions formed the core of the round, and each answered a distinct paper era assumption. The first established that the act reaches records maintained in electronic format. Once stated in statutory text, the provision foreclosed an argument otherwise available in every case involving a database, a spreadsheet, an electronic message, or any other record whose existence was inseparable from a computer system. Coverage followed the record into its new form. An agency could no longer treat electronic maintenance, in itself, as a reason to place material beyond the act, any more than transfer from one filing cabinet to another could have done so under the paper regime. The exemptions continued to govern whether particular electronic records had to be disclosed, and nothing in the provision weakened any exemption.

The second provision addressed the search. A request triggers a duty to search for responsive records, and the adequacy of that search has long been a central subject of dispute between requesters and agencies. The 1996 amendments provided that a search includes an electronic search. An electronic search proceeds through queries and systems whose design determines what a query returns, rather than through folders, boxes, and indexes. Treating the electronic search as a search under the act placed electronic retrieval under the same obligation of reasonableness that governed paper retrieval: an agency had to look in the places, and through the systems, where responsive records were reasonably likely to be found. The provision implied no duty to create records or redesign systems. The duty was to search, honestly and competently, in the medium in which the agency had chosen to keep its records. An agency that gained the efficiency of electronic storage accepted the corresponding burden of electronic retrieval.

The third provision addressed the form of production. The amendments required agencies to make reasonable efforts to provide records in the form or format requested where the records were readily reproducible in that form or format. Each qualifier did real work. The duty ran to the form or format the requester asked for, a recognition that format is not a trivial preference. An electronic record produced only as a printed stack of pages loses the properties that make the electronic version useful: the ability to sort, to filter, to compare entries, to run calculations, and to detect patterns across thousands of entries at once. For data heavy records, production in paper form could amount in practical terms to production in unusable form, even where every page was delivered. The qualifier of reasonable efforts bounded the duty, so that no requester could demand heroic conversions or reconstruction of obsolete systems. The qualifier of ready reproducibility bounded it further: where a record could be reproduced in the requested format without undue difficulty, the format request carried weight; where it could not, the agency retained room to produce in another format.

The electronic reading room provisions extended the same translation to proactive disclosure, the part of the act under which agencies make specified records available for public inspection without waiting for a request. The reading room idea belonged to the original paper design: specified records, including final opinions, statements of policy, and administrative staff manuals, were to be kept available for inspection and copying, traditionally in a physical location. The 1996 amendments required that reading room records created on or after November 1, 1996 be made available electronically. The date line divided the obligation sensibly. Records created after the dividing date entered a system in which electronic availability was the expectation from the moment of creation, while older holdings, created under paper practices, were not subjected to a wholesale conversion mandate.

Alongside the change of medium, the amendments added a new category to the reading room, one that reflected a sophisticated understanding of how requests behave. The new category covered records that had been released under the act and that an agency determined had become, or were likely to become, the subject of subsequent requests for the same records. Such records were to be made available in the electronic reading room, accompanied by an index. A first request produces a search, a review for exemptions, and a production. If the same record is likely to be requested again, repetition of that entire process for each later requester wastes agency effort and delays every later requester behind work already done. Posting the released record once, where later requesters can find it directly, converts repeated individual processing into a single act of publication. The index served the finding function: a collection of posted records without an index would replicate, in electronic form, the frustration of an uncatalogued storeroom, and the requirement of an index recognized that availability without findability is availability in name only.

The design of the new category also contained a measure of institutional discretion. The trigger was a determination by the agency that released records had become, or were likely to become, the subject of subsequent requests. The statute thus relied on agencies to recognize recurring demand for their own records, the records they were best positioned to observe. The expansion therefore differed from a general publication mandate: it targeted the point at which proactive disclosure and administrative efficiency pointed in the same direction. It also changed the position of later requesters. Under a purely request driven system, each requester stands alone at the back of a queue for a record others have already obtained. Under the amended system, earlier releases generated a public copy, and later interest could be satisfied from that copy without a fresh contest over search and review.

The deadline change cut in the opposite direction from the rest of the package, and its candor makes it one of the most instructive provisions in the history of the act. The amendments of 1974 had imposed enforceable time limits, including a determination within ten working days of receipt of a request. The 1996 amendments lengthened the determination period from ten working days to twenty working days. The occasion, at the strength the record supports, was that the ten working day period was routinely missed. President Clinton, in signing the legislation, acknowledged the backlogs and limited resources under which agencies administered the act. The lengthening therefore rested on an admission legislatures rarely write into the justification for a statute: the earlier deadline had failed in practice, across the government, over a long period, and the failure was a fact about institutional capacity rather than about requester entitlement.

The lesson embedded in the change concerns the function of a deadline that goes unmet. A statutory deadline disciplines behavior only where compliance is possible often enough for the deadline to operate as a real standard, against which performance is measured and delay is treated as an exception requiring explanation. Where a deadline is missed as a matter of routine, it loses that force. By doubling the period to twenty working days, Congress traded nominal speed for a standard with some prospect of being met and, in being met, of recovering meaning. The trade was a concession, and making it in statutory text was rare enough to deserve emphasis: a rare statutory admission that an unenforced deadline disciplines no one, neither the agency that misses it without consequence nor the system that treats the missing as unusual. A determination within twenty working days remained a determination, a decision on whether to comply with a request, rather than a license to defer production indefinitely.

Expedited processing was the clearest of those mechanisms. The amendments created expedited processing for cases of compelling need and defined the central cases. The first category covered a failure to obtain records on an expedited basis that could reasonably be expected to pose an imminent threat to the life or physical safety of an individual. The second covered an urgency to inform the public concerning actual or alleged federal government activity, where the requester was a person primarily engaged in disseminating information. The statute also left room for other cases an agency determined warranted expedition. The boundaries of the categories were as revealing as the categories themselves. Expedition did not rest on the importance of a topic in the abstract. The life and safety category required imminence and a threat to an individual. The public information category required both urgency and a disseminating requester, and it concerned actual or alleged federal government activity, the core subject matter of the act itself. The residual category introduced flexibility at the price of consistency, since agencies could differ in generosity, but a closed statutory list would have frozen the categories of urgency as understood in 1996 and denied agencies the ability to recognize a compelling case of a kind the drafters had not foreseen.

Multitrack processing, expressly permitted by the 1996 amendments, addressed queue fairness from the opposite end. Expedited processing identified the rare request that deserved to pass the entire queue. Multitrack processing addressed the ordinary injustice of a single queue in which every request waits behind every earlier request regardless of size. Under a strict first in, first out regime, a request for a single identified document, answerable in an afternoon, could wait months behind a request requiring the search and review of hundreds of thousands of pages. Permitting agencies to process requests on multiple tracks, differentiated by complexity, allowed simple requests to move on a simple track at the speed their simplicity permitted, while complex requests proceeded on a track paced to their genuine demands. The fairness claim of the design is that proportionality is a better principle of queue justice than arrival order alone, because it ties the wait borne by each requester more closely to the work that request actually generates. Even with the risk that complexity judgments could be contested or manipulated, the authorization marked a conceptual shift. Delay stopped being treated solely as a function of agency diligence measured against a single deadline, and started being treated as a problem of queue design, in which the structure of processing itself could be fairer or less fair independent of the effort of the people working within it.

The reporting adjustments attracted less attention than the format and processing provisions, but they completed the translation of the act into its new environment and tightened one long standing strand of accountability. The amendments moved annual agency reports on the administration of the act to a fiscal year basis and required that the reports be made available electronically. The fiscal year basis aligned reporting with the cycle on which agencies plan, budget, and account for operations, making the reported figures more naturally comparable with the rest of agency administrative data. Electronic availability applied the premise of the entire round to oversight itself: a report that exists to inform Congress and the public about the performance of a disclosure system ought itself to be obtainable through the standard channel of the era, rather than resting in a limited distribution of printed copies.

The second reporting change required agencies to list, in annual reports, the statutes relied on as a basis for withholding under Exemption 3. Exemption 3 incorporates by reference other statutes that prohibit or restrict disclosure, and the Government in the Sunshine Act of 1976 had narrowed the exemption by requiring that a qualifying statute either leave no discretion on the issue or establish particular criteria for withholding or refer to particular types of matters to be withheld. That narrowing disciplined the legal standard. The 1996 listing requirement disciplined practice in a complementary way: an agency invoking Exemption 3 had to name, in a public annual accounting, the specific statutes on which reliance rested. Listing exposed the pattern of reliance to view. Without the listing, Exemption 3 reliance remained dispersed across thousands of individual determinations, each visible only to its own requester, and the aggregate shape of withholding under other statutes remained obscure even to diligent overseers. The requirement converted scattered individual decisions into a legible inventory, at the modest cost of a line in a report agencies already had to file.

Considered as a whole, the 1996 round preserved the reach of an established right against a change in the practice of recordkeeping, and that description captures both its ambition and its restraint. The round did not broaden the class of requesters: the act continued to make records available to any person, as it had from the beginning, and the amendments added no new class of entitled requester and removed none. The round did not broaden the grounds of request: a requester needed no reason at all, and the amendments neither imposed a statement of purpose nor rewarded particular purposes with substantive rights to records that others could not obtain, the procedural priority of expedited processing being a matter of timing rather than of entitlement to the records themselves. What the round changed was the fit between the statute and the material world to which the statute applied: the form of the records, the method of the search, the format of the production, the location and content of the reading room, the structure of the queue, and the medium and content of the annual account.

That defensive character distinguishes 1996 in the sequence of major revisions, and it carries a general lesson about the maintenance of rights over time. The round of 1996 would have been necessary even in a world without any dispute about the proper scope of disclosure, because a right to inspect a file means something unclear when the file is a database, and a duty to keep records available in a reading room means something unclear when inspection occurs through a network. Rights stated in statutes do not maintain themselves. The ground shifts beneath them through changes in technology, in administrative capacity, and in the volume of requests, and each shift can enlarge or contract the practical content of a right whose text has not changed by a single word. In each case the 1996 amendments intervened to restore practical content toward the level the text had originally promised, the lengthened deadline standing as the one instance where restoration gave way to honest adjustment of the promise itself.

The Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996 therefore occupy a distinctive place among the six major revision acts. The round lacked a scandal, lacked a great judicial provocation, and lacked a dramatic expansion or contraction of the substantive law of withholding. Its provocations were a filing system in transformation and a deadline in disrepute. Its achievements were a definition of coverage that followed records into electronic form, a duty of reasonable effort toward the format a requester asked for, a reading room rebuilt for electronic access with a new category aimed at records in recurring demand, a candid doubling of the determination period, a fast lane for compelling need, a fairer architecture for the ordinary queue, and a public inventory of the statutes behind Exemption 3 withholding.

## The Fifth Major Revision and the Attack on Delay Through Procedure

The OPEN Government Act of 2007, Public Law 110-175, signed on December 31, 2007, arose from a diagnosis that differed in emphasis from the diagnoses behind the earlier major revisions. The amendments of 1974 had responded to scandal and to restrictive judicial construction, and later rounds had adjusted the boundaries of withholding and adapted the statute to electronic records. By the middle of the first decade of the twenty first century, the documented complaint against the Freedom of Information Act centered less on a single exemption and more on administration. Requests waited in queues for extended periods, agency backlogs persisted as a structural feature rather than an occasional failure, and disputes that might have been resolved through communication hardened into litigation. Delay of that kind operated as withholding in practical effect, whatever the formal legal position, because information tied to a public debate, a business decision, a historical inquiry, or a news report loses value as time passes and can lose value entirely after the moment for use has passed.

That practical equivalence between delay and denial shaped the character of the 2007 legislation. Congress left the nine exemptions and the substantive tests for withholding largely untouched in this round, and instead directed attention to the machinery through which requests moved, stalled, and were contested. The statute sought to make delay visible, to attach a financial consequence to missed deadlines, to give requesters a means of checking where a request stood, to create a neutral office that could mediate disputes instead of a lawsuit, and to restore a path to attorney fees for requesters whose lawsuits had prompted an agency to change position without a court order. Each provision addressed a point in the administrative process where inertia could defeat the disclosure purpose of the act without any official ever signing a denial letter. Considered together, the provisions formed a coherent argument that openness depends not only on the legal standard for release but also on timelines, incentives, communication, and the availability of help when the process breaks down.

The backlog problem deserves emphasis because the 2007 act treated backlog as a governance problem rather than as a misfortune that requesters had to accept. A backlog is not a single decision that a court can review in an orderly fashion. A backlog is an accumulation of unmade decisions, distributed across offices and queues, produced by volume, staffing, record complexity, consultation between agencies, and the absence of consequences for allowing older requests to age. Litigation practice compounded the difficulty. Filing suit required resources, and an agency could respond to a suit by releasing records after the complaint had been filed, a sequence that raised the question whether the requester had prevailed in any sense the fee statute recognized. Before the 2007 amendments, that sequence could leave a requester with the records and with the full cost of the litigation that had produced the release, a result that weakened the incentive to enforce the act and strengthened the incentive for an agency to wait. The round lacked a single precipitating scandal comparable to Watergate. The evidence consisted of documented delay, documented backlog, and recurring problems in litigation practice, accumulated across agencies and across categories of requesters.

At the center of the new machinery stood the Office of Government Information Services, created within the National Archives and Records Administration. Placement mattered. Congress located the office inside the institution charged with the preservation and administration of federal records, rather than inside the Department of Justice, which defended agencies in litigation, or inside any single operating agency whose own compliance might come under review. The office opened in September 2009, an interval after enactment that reflects the practical work of establishing a new federal office, hiring staff, and defining procedures before services could be offered. From the opening onward, the office supplied something the system had lacked: a standing, government wide point of contact for problems in the administration of the act that did not require the requester to file suit and did not require the agency to concede error.

### What can the FOIA ombudsman office do and what can it not do?

The Office of Government Information Services reviews agency policies and compliance, recommends changes to Congress and the President, and offers mediation as an alternative to litigation. The office cannot order an agency to release records, cannot process requests or appeals, and does not accept disputes already in litigation.

The review function gives the office a vantage point across the whole system. By statute, the office reviews agency policies, procedures, and compliance with the Freedom of Information Act. A review of policies examines the written rules an agency applies to incoming requests, including categorization, fee practice, and referral practice. A review of procedures examines the sequence of steps through which a request travels, including intake, search assignment, consultation, and response. A review of compliance examines whether actual practice matches the obligations the statute imposes, including time limits and communication with requesters. Because the office sits outside the agencies under review, comparison becomes possible. A practice that appears inevitable inside one agency can appear anomalous when set beside the practice of other agencies handling comparable volume, and a recurring point of friction identified across several agencies can be named as a systemic problem rather than as a series of unrelated failures by individual requesters.

The recommendation function connects administrative experience to legislative and executive oversight. The office recommends policy changes to Congress and to the President, carrying findings from case level friction and agency review into the two branches positioned to alter law, allocate resources, or direct executive practice. A recommendation carries weight through the quality of the record behind it rather than through any power to command. Advisory opinions belong to the same family of tools. The office may issue advisory opinions at discretion, which allows the office to state a considered view on a question of practice or interpretation where guidance could prevent disputes or standardize expectations, without converting every question into a binding ruling. Discretion over when to issue such an opinion preserves flexibility, because the office can reserve formal statements for questions of general significance and handle narrower problems through direct assistance and mediation.

Mediation supplies the most direct service to individual requesters. The office offers mediation services to resolve disputes between requesters and agencies as a non exclusive alternative to litigation. Each element of that description carries meaning. Mediation addresses disputes, meaning concrete disagreements over matters such as the adequacy of a search, the application of an exemption, fee questions, or prolonged delay, rather than abstract questions about the act in general. The service is an alternative to litigation, meaning a requester and an agency can attempt a negotiated resolution without pleadings and judicial timetables. The alternative is non exclusive, meaning a requester who enters mediation does not surrender the right to pursue other avenues, including litigation, if mediation fails to produce agreement. That preservation of options lowers the risk of trying mediation first, since an unsuccessful attempt does not consume the legal remedies that existed before the attempt began. For a requester without counsel, mediation offers a structured conversation with a neutral participant, replacing the unequal exchange of form letters and unanswered calls that had characterized many stalled requests.

The limits on the office are as deliberate and as important as the functions, and plain statement of those limits prevents misunderstanding of the entire design. The office cannot compel or order an agency to release records. The office cannot process requests or appeals. Intake and adjudication remain agency responsibilities, and conversion of the office into a parallel processing center would duplicate agency work, confuse accountability for deadlines, and overwhelm a small office with the volume of the entire federal system. The office does not take disputes already in litigation. Once parties have placed a dispute before a court, judicial authority governs resolution, and parallel mediation under the office would risk conflicting direction and interference with proceedings under a judge. Authority of this kind is mediation rather than command, and description of the office as an enforcement office is an error. Enforcement, in the architecture of the act, belongs to the federal courts, which can order release, review records in camera, and award fees. The office was built to make resort to that enforcement less frequently necessary, not to replicate enforcement in a softer form.

That distinction explains both the promise and the boundary of the experiment Congress created in 2007. A mediator can clarify a misunderstanding that neither side recognized, can narrow a request by agreement so that an agency search becomes manageable, can prompt an agency to explain a delay and commit to a schedule, and can carry a requester concern into an agency at a level where attention is available. None of those results requires power to order release, and many become harder to obtain when the neutral party holds coercive power, because candor declines when statements can be used as admissions in a later order. At the same time, a requester facing a determined refusal gains information and communication from the office but gains no substitute for a judge. The statute accepted that trade deliberately, leaving the ultimate legal obligation with agencies in the first instance and with courts on review.

The 2007 act also settled a definitional dispute with direct consequences for who paid favorable fee rates, by writing a functional definition of a representative of the news media into the statute. Fee categories established in earlier rounds gave representatives of the news media duplication charges only, without search and review charges, on the reasoning that gathering information for dissemination serves the public understanding the act exists to promote. Disputes had arisen over which requesters qualified, particularly as publishing changed and as individuals without affiliation to an established newspaper or broadcaster gathered and distributed information. The statutory definition answered by looking to activity rather than to institutional form. A representative of the news media is a person or entity that gathers information of potential interest to a segment of the public, uses editorial skills to turn the raw materials into a distinct work, and distributes that work to an audience. Gathering, editorial transformation, and distribution form the three elements, and satisfaction of the elements turns on conduct and purpose rather than on ownership of a printing press, possession of a broadcast license, or employment by a legacy organization.

The reach of that definition to freelancers and to independent and online publishers followed from the functional test itself. A freelancer who gathers information of potential interest to a segment of the public, shapes that information through editorial skill into a distinct work, and distributes the work to an audience meets the definition through the work performed, even without a staff position. An independent publisher operating through a website, a newsletter, or another channel meets the definition on the same terms, provided the activity includes genuine gathering, genuine editorial transformation, and genuine distribution to an audience. The definition reaches persons who gather and intend to distribute. Intention matters because a person at the start of a project has no finished work to display, and a test confined to completed publication would deny favorable treatment at precisely the stage when records are being gathered and fees are being assessed. At the same time, the elements impose real limits. Mere collection of records for private curiosity, without editorial transformation and distribution to an audience, falls outside the definition, and the requirement of a distinct work prevents bare republication of raw material without editorial contribution from qualifying automatically.

Visibility into the progress of a request formed the next line of attack on delay. The act required agencies to assign an individualized tracking number to any request that would take longer than ten days, and to provide a telephone number or an internet service through which a requester could check the status of the request. The tracking number transformed a request from an item inside an opaque queue into an identified matter that both sides could reference with precision. Communication failures in a high volume system often arise from ambiguity about which request is under discussion, particularly where a requester has filed several requests or an agency has divided a request among offices. A unique number removes that ambiguity in every later conversation, letter, and mediation. The status channel transformed waiting from a passive condition into a monitored one. A requester able to check status could learn whether a request had been received, where processing stood, and whether further patience or escalation was warranted, without filing a new inquiry that itself consumed agency time.

The fee consequence for missed deadlines gave the time limits of the act a sanction that earlier versions had largely lacked. Under the 2007 provision, an agency that failed to comply with a statutory time limit generally could not assess search fees, and could not assess duplication fees for educational requesters, noncommercial scientific requesters, and representatives of the news media, subject to statutory exceptions for unusual and exceptional circumstances. Search fees represent agency labor, and removal of search fees after a missed deadline means the cost of agency delay falls on the agency rather than on the requester waiting for the response. Duplication protection for the favored categories preserves the fee policy of the act, under which requesters serving educational, scientific, and public dissemination purposes receive records at the lowest cost, and prevents an agency from recovering through duplication charges a portion of the amount surrendered through search charges. The qualifier that the bar applies generally, together with exceptions for unusual and exceptional circumstances, acknowledges that some requests present volume, complexity, or consultation burdens that make a deadline genuinely difficult, and that a fee sanction aimed at inertia should not operate as a penalty for circumstances beyond reasonable control. Prompt determination protected fee revenue, while delay carried a defined financial price, legible to agency managers in a way that exhortation had never achieved.

A quieter provision addressed the intelligibility of records after release. The act required that each redaction in a produced record indicate the exemption relied upon for that redaction. A produced record with deletions marked by exemption gives the requester a map of the agency reasoning at the level of the individual passage. Without such marking, a requester confronting a page of blacked out passages could not tell whether the agency had withheld a name under a privacy exemption, a technique under a law enforcement exemption, or deliberative material under the deliberative process protection, and could not frame an appeal or a lawsuit with precision. Marking each redaction by exemption allows targeted challenge. A requester can concede some deletions and contest others, can direct argument to the exemption actually invoked, and can identify patterns, such as reliance on an exemption in contexts where that exemption has narrow scope. The requirement imposed effort at the point of review, and the effort purchased clarity for appeal, mediation, and litigation alike.

The attorney fee clarification addressed the litigation practice problem at the heart of the round. Fee awards under the act depend on whether a requester has substantially prevailed, and agencies had argued that a requester whose suit ended without a court order, because the agency released records after suit was filed, had not prevailed at all. That argument, accepted, would have allowed an agency to defeat fee liability in case after case by withholding until litigation commenced and releasing after litigation had forced attention, leaving requesters to bear costs that agency delay had made necessary. The 2007 act clarified that a requester has substantially prevailed where relief was obtained through a judicial order, through an enforceable written agreement, or through a voluntary or unilateral change in agency position, provided the claim was not insubstantial. The voluntary or unilateral change category, often described as the catalyst theory, restores the connection between litigation and result. A suit that causes release has achieved the purpose of the act for that requester, and fee eligibility follows the achievement rather than the formality of an order. The proviso that the claim must not have been insubstantial guards the boundary, excluding fee recovery where a suit lacked a genuine basis and an agency change of position coincided with a claim that never carried weight.

Three supporting provisions completed the administrative scheme. The act addressed records held for an agency by a government contractor, closing a gap that outsourcing had opened. An agency that contracts out a function does not shed the disclosure obligations attached to the records of that function, and a rule attentive to contractor held records prevents the form of custody from deciding whether the act applies. As recordkeeping and program administration moved into contractor hands across the period leading to 2007, the question of custody acquired practical importance equal to questions of exemption, because a record placed beyond reach by contract structure is as unavailable as a record withheld under a legal claim. The act also addressed FOIA Public Liaisons, designated officials through whom a requester could raise concerns about the processing of a request and seek resolution without immediate resort to an appeal or a lawsuit. A liaison gives an agency queue a human point of contact with responsibility for communication, and gives the system an intermediate step between front line processing and the mediation offered by the Office of Government Information Services. Finally, the act addressed Chief FOIA Officer responsibilities, locating accountability for the act at a senior level inside each agency. A chief officer with defined responsibilities can press for resources, review performance data, and answer for the condition of the program in a way that dispersed processing offices cannot.

Read as a whole, the OPEN Government Act of 2007 redefined what reform of the Freedom of Information Act could mean. Earlier rounds had enlarged judicial power, narrowed or widened exemptions, restructured fees, and adapted the statute to electronic records. The 2007 round accepted the disclosure standard largely as settled for the moment and treated time as the contested resource. A tracked request, a fee sanction for a missed deadline, a marked redaction, a mediator without command power, a functional test for media status, a catalyst rule for fees, and named officials answerable for performance all serve the same proposition: a right to records that arrives after the need has expired is a diminished right, and a statute that ignores administration will be defeated by administration. The round also demonstrated restraint. Congress did not convert mediation into enforcement, did not federalize request processing, and did not disturb the exemption framework through which genuine confidentiality interests receive protection. Within the six round sequence of major revisions, the 2007 amendments therefore occupy a distinctive place, as the round that measured the act against the experience of waiting and rebuilt the procedures of the act around that measurement.

## The Final Round and the Codification of Openness

The Freedom of Information Act Improvement Act of 2016, Public Law 114-185, signed by President Obama on June 30, 2016, arrived in the fiftieth anniversary year of the original act and carried a different character from the rounds that preceded it. The round of 2016 answered a quieter problem, the fragility of a disclosure policy that rested entirely on executive direction. Much of the statute did not invent a new standard for withholding and release. Instead, the statute converted policy that the executive branch had applied by memorandum into text that only Congress could remove, tightened a set of procedural protections that requesters had received inconsistently, placed a time boundary around one of the most contested privileges in the act, and required forms of proactive and electronic access that treated repeated public demand and a fragmented request system as problems of administration rather than problems for each requester to solve alone.

The codification theme runs through the central provisions and explains their design. A memorandum from an Attorney General binds the Department of Justice and guides agencies across the executive branch, but a memorandum carries the lifespan of the administration that issued it and of the leadership that chose to honor it. A succeeding Attorney General could revoke the guidance, narrow its interpretation, or replace its presumption with a more deferential instruction to defend agency withholding decisions whenever a plausible legal basis existed. By placing the presumption of openness and the foreseeable harm standard into the text of the act, the 2016 round removed that particular form of reversibility. A future administration retained full authority over litigation strategy, guidance, training, and the vigor with which agencies searched for and reviewed records, but the administration could not by memorandum restore a regime in which the mere technical applicability of an exemption sufficed to justify withholding. Removal of the standard required Congress to amend the statute, with the deliberation, publicity, and political cost that an amendment entails.

The policy that Congress codified had a specific provenance. Since 2009, the Department of Justice had applied a presumption of openness under Attorney General Holder's FOIA memorandum, a policy directing agencies toward disclosure and toward withholding only under a more demanding justification than exemption coverage alone. For seven years, from 2009 to 2016, that standard operated as executive policy. Its operation during those years gave Congress an observed record to evaluate rather than a theory to test. Codification in 2016 therefore ratified a working practice and froze into law a standard whose administrative consequences were at least partly known. Congress did not impose an untried disclosure philosophy on a resistant bureaucracy in a single step. Congress observed an executive experiment, allowed the experiment to run across two presidential terms in part, and then decided that the experiment deserved permanence beyond the tenure of the officials who had launched it.

The presumption of openness states the orientation from which administration of the act begins. Records belong in the open unless a reason recognized by the statute supports withholding, and the burden of locating and articulating that reason rests on the agency holding the records. A presumption, in legal practice, does its work at the margins and in cases of doubt. Where coverage under an exemption was arguable, where harm was speculative, or where an agency faced a choice between a broad withholding and a narrower one, the presumption instructed the decision maker to resolve the uncertainty in favor of release. Codifying the presumption gave that instruction a source higher than internal guidance. An agency official deciding a close question after June 30, 2016, applied a statutory orientation, and a court reviewing the decision read the exemptions against a congressionally declared default rather than against an executive preference that the executive itself could withdraw.

### What does the foreseeable harm standard require?

The foreseeable harm standard requires an agency to withhold information only when the agency reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption, or when disclosure is prohibited by law, and to consider partial disclosure and segregate releasable material whenever full disclosure is not possible.

The precision of the standard deserves attention, because the standard changes the question an agency must answer. Under a reading focused only on exemption coverage, the inquiry ended once an agency placed information within the language of one of the nine exemptions. A record concerning deliberations, a record compiled for law enforcement purposes, or a record containing commercial information could be withheld because the category covered it, regardless of whether release of the particular record threatened the interest that had justified creating the category. The foreseeable harm standard added a second inquiry after the question of coverage. The agency had to reasonably foresee that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption. The agency did not have to prove that harm would occur. The agency did have to ground an expectation of harm in the content of the record and the circumstances of release, at a level a reviewing court could examine, rather than resting on the assertion that records of a given type were generally sensitive.

The second prong of the formulation addresses a different situation. An agency shall withhold information under the act only if the agency reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption, or disclosure is prohibited by law. The prohibition prong recognizes that some withholding decisions do not turn on predicted consequences at all. A statute, a court order, or another binding legal command may forbid release outright, and in such a case an inquiry into foreseeable harm adds nothing because the law has already answered the disclosure question. By stating the two prongs together, the 2016 text separated discretionary, harm based withholding from mandatory withholding and subjected only the first category to the harm inquiry. An exemption permits withholding under the conditions the act specifies. After 2016, those conditions included foreseeable harm, and an agency invoking an exemption had to articulate harm to the protected interest rather than treating permission as compulsion.

Partial disclosure forms the second half of the standard's discipline. Where full disclosure is not possible, the agency must consider partial disclosure and take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information. The duty matters because records rarely divide neatly into wholly exempt and wholly open files. A memorandum may contain a deliberative recommendation embedded in a recitation of facts, dates, participants, and actions already taken. A report may contain a confidential source reference on one page and releasable findings on twenty others. Without a segregability duty, an agency could withhold an entire record on the strength of a small exempt portion, converting a narrow protection into a broad one through the physical form of the file. The 2016 language required the agency to consider the intermediate option between full release and full withholding and to perform the work of separation: reviewing the record, identifying exempt portions, redacting or extracting those portions, and releasing the remainder. The statute's insistence on reasonable steps acknowledges limits of practicality while rejecting convenience as a ground for wholesale denial, and the resulting production gives the requester a visible map of the withholding decision and a concrete basis for challenging particular redactions on appeal.

The foreseeable harm standard has an express limit, and the limit clarifies the relationship between the 2016 act and Exemption 3. The standard does not override material prohibited by law under Exemption 3. Exemption 3 covers matters specifically exempted from disclosure by another statute meeting the conditions the act prescribes, a structure refined in the Government in the Sunshine Act round of 1976 after the Supreme Court decision in FAA v. Robertson. Where a qualifying statute prohibits disclosure, the prohibition governs, and an agency does not conduct a harm balancing that the other statute has foreclosed. The distinction reflects a judgment about institutional authority. The foreseeable harm standard governs the discretion Congress left to agencies under the act's own exemptions. Where Congress itself, in another statute, ordered material withheld, an agency applying a harm test to release the material anyway would displace a direct legislative command with an administrative prediction. The 2016 round therefore strengthened disclosure within the sphere of agency discretion while respecting the boundary at which Congress had spoken in prohibitory terms elsewhere.

The deliberative process provisions of the 2016 round addressed the privilege that sits within Exemption 5, the exemption covering inter agency or intra agency memorandums or letters that would not be available by law to a party in litigation with the agency. The deliberative process privilege protects predecisional, deliberative material: opinions, recommendations, drafts, proposals, and the give and take through which officials develop a decision. The 2016 act provided that the deliberative process privilege shall not apply to records created 25 years or more before the date on which the records were requested. The boundary operates on the age of the record measured from creation to the date of request, not from the date of any decision the record informed and not from the date an agency processed the request. A record created exactly at the boundary falls outside the privilege. A record created one day inside the boundary retains whatever protection the privilege otherwise provides, subject to the foreseeable harm analysis and to the other conditions governing Exemption 5.

Understanding the boundary requires taking the candor argument seriously, because the privilege rests on a claim about how officials behave under observation. The argument for the privilege holds that effective government decision making depends on frank internal exchange. Officials who expect every tentative position, rejected option, and blunt assessment to become public during the life of a controversy will temper what they write, shift discussion to oral channels that leave no record, avoid recording dissent, and frame memorandums for an external audience rather than for the decision maker who needs candid advice. The resulting record becomes thinner and less honest, decisions rest on less fully tested reasoning, and the public ultimately receives worse governance in exchange for a formal transparency that drove substance out of the written file. Defenders of the privilege also argue that release of predecisional material misleads the public by presenting proposals that an agency considered and rejected as though the proposals represented agency policy, confusing accountability for final decisions with exposure of every step that preceded them. On this view, accountability properly attaches to the decision, the reasons the agency gives for the decision, and the factual record supporting the decision, while the internal path toward the decision requires a measure of shelter to function at all.

The disclosure argument answers from the same concern for the quality and legitimacy of decisions. Deliberative material often contains the factual analysis, the identified risks, the dissenting assessments, and the rejected alternatives that explain why an agency acted as it did, and a rationale offered publicly after a decision may omit considerations that shaped the outcome. Withholding the deliberative record can conceal error, improper influence, disregard of evidence, or reasoning that the agency would prefer not to defend, and the privilege may cover material long after any plausible chilling effect on candor has faded. Critics of broad privilege claims also observe that the candor rationale proves too much if accepted without limit, because almost any internal record can be described as part of some decision process, and officials in a system built on eventual accountability may write more carefully, rather than less honestly, when a record will someday be read outside the building.

Congress in 2016 answered the duration question with age. The choice of a fixed period of 25 years reflects the structure of the privilege's own justification. Candor matters because a live decision process can be distorted by exposure while the process continues and while its participants remain engaged in related decisions under public scrutiny. As years pass, the participants leave the roles they held, the decision recedes into a settled historical record, successor policies replace the policy under formation, and the prospect that disclosure will alter behavior in the original process disappears entirely because the original process ended long before. A privilege justified by the needs of an ongoing process weakens as the process recedes, and at some point the justification expires even if the document retains the formal characteristics of deliberative material. Age serves as a proxy for that expiration. Rather than requiring courts and agencies to reconstruct, record by record, whether disclosure of a particular memorandum threatens candor in a decision process decades closed, the statute fixes a period after which the answer is conclusively no for purposes of the privilege. The proxy sacrifices precision in individual cases, but the proxy gains administrability, predictability, and an end to perpetual privilege claims over the historical record.

Proactive disclosure received a concrete rule in the same statute. Records that have been requested three or more times must be made available for public inspection in an electronic format. The provision, known as the rule of 3, converts repeated individual demand into a trigger for general publication. The logic begins with an observation about what repeated requests reveal. A single request may reflect an idiosyncratic interest, but three or more requests for the same records signal a broader public interest that an agency can identify without speculation. Once that signal exists, processing the same records repeatedly for successive requesters wastes agency resources and requester time alike, and each new requester pays the cost of a search and review that earlier requesters already funded. Posting the records for public inspection in electronic format ends the repetition. Any person can inspect the records without filing a request, waiting in a processing queue, or paying duplication charges, and the agency handles the records once rather than once per requester.

The single portal provision addressed fragmentation at the point of entry. Before a consolidated system, a requester faced a government of many doors: each agency maintained separate instructions, forms, addresses, and submission channels, and a request directed to the wrong component or framed under the wrong procedure could consume weeks before processing began. The 2016 act required the Director of the Office of Management and Budget, in consultation with the Attorney General, to ensure the operation of a single, consolidated online request portal through which the public can submit a request to any agency. The consolidated FOIA.gov portal that followed is the implementation of that statutory mandate. The provision's significance lies in the uniformity of the doorway rather than in any change to the law applied behind the doorway. Exemptions, deadlines, fees, and appeals operated as before for each agency, but the act of filing, historically a source of error and deterrence for requesters unfamiliar with a particular agency's internal organization, became a single standardized transaction through one address.

The procedural provisions of the 2016 round completed the statute by regulating the path a request travels after filing. A requester must be allowed a minimum of 90 days to file an administrative appeal. The floor addresses a simple fairness problem: an agency that set a short appeal window could extinguish the administrative remedy through delay in delivery, complexity in the production, or the requester's need to review a large release and identify the withholdings worth challenging. Ninety days gives the requester a realistic period to examine produced records, compare redactions against the act, consult counsel or assistance where needed, and frame an appeal that identifies specific errors rather than a general objection.

Notice requirements ensured that requesters learned of assistance that earlier rounds had created. Agencies must notify requesters of the right to seek dispute resolution services from the agency FOIA Public Liaison and from the Office of Government Information Services at the points the statute specifies. The FOIA Public Liaison, a role developed in the OPEN Government Act of 2007 round, serves as an official within the agency to whom a requester can turn with concerns about processing, and the Office of Government Information Services, created by the same 2007 act within the National Archives and Records Administration, offers mediation services as a non exclusive alternative to litigation. The pre 2016 structure left many requesters to discover liaison and mediation options through experience, advocacy organizations, or counsel, and rights that requesters do not know about provide little protection. A statutory notice duty converted that scattered knowledge into a standard communication delivered in the ordinary course of processing, at the moments when a requester confronting a denial, a delay, or a fee dispute would most benefit from knowing that an intermediate step existed between silent acceptance and a federal lawsuit.

The codification of the Chief FOIA Officers Council extended the statute's codification theme into institutional structure. The council, bringing together the Chief FOIA Officers charged with oversight of the act within their agencies, had operated as a coordinating body through executive practice. Writing the council into the statute gave the body a legal basis independent of the administration that convened it and recognized that consistent administration of a government wide statute requires a standing forum in which the responsible officials address common problems: backlogs, technology, training, standardization of practice, and the uneven application of standards across agencies of very different sizes and missions. The council's codification secured the forum itself, so that coordination on FOIA administration continued as a statutory function rather than as a discretionary initiative vulnerable to neglect.

Fee provisions supplied the enforcement edge for deadline discipline. The 2016 act tightened fee bars for missed deadlines, building on the structure the OPEN Government Act of 2007 had established, under which an agency that failed to comply with a statutory time limit generally could not assess search fees, and could not assess duplication fees for educational, noncommercial scientific, and news media requesters, subject to statutory exceptions. Tying fee eligibility to timeliness converts the deadline from an aspiration into a condition of revenue: an agency that processes a request late loses the ability to charge for work the statute expected the agency to perform on schedule. The tightening in 2016 narrowed the room agencies had to miss deadlines while retaining full fee authority, and the mechanism operates automatically through the fee rules rather than through litigation, which most requesters cannot afford to pursue over a delayed production alone.

The statute provided no additional appropriations. That absence frames the honest limit of the 2016 round. Congress imposed a more demanding withholding standard, a duty of partial disclosure, a proactive posting obligation triggered by demonstrated demand, a government wide portal, longer appeal windows, notice duties, and tightened fee consequences, and Congress funded none of the added work through the act itself. Agencies absorbed the obligations within existing budgets, in competition with the substantive missions for which those budgets were appropriated. The foreseeable harm analysis takes longer per record than a categorical exemption claim. Segregability review takes longer than wholesale withholding. In the short run, however, the structure of the 2016 round placed its ambitions and its constraint in the same text, and the backlogs that had prompted earlier rounds remained a function of resources as much as of law.

Taken together, the provisions of Public Law 114-185 addressed the durability of the governing standard itself, reasoning that a disclosure regime whose central rule could be rewritten by internal memorandum had not finished the work of legislation. The settlement remained incomplete by design and by budget, and later administrators retained wide influence over how energetically the statute was administered. The text that administrators applied, however, after June 30, 2016, stated the presumption, the harm test, and the limits in words that only Congress could change.

| First round, 1974 | Watergate and the narrowing construction in EPA v. Mink | Enforceable deadlines, fee limits, in camera review, narrower Exemptions 1 and 7, segregability, and fee shifting | Strengthened access |
| Second round, 1976 | The broadening construction in FAA v. Robertson | A two limb qualification test for statutes claimed under Exemption 3 | Strengthened access by narrowing Exemption 3 |
| Third round, 1986 | Law enforcement pressure inside drug legislation and long running fee disputes | Fee categories and a strengthened waiver alongside a broader Exemption 7, a lowered harm standard, and three exclusions | Mixed, with fees toward access and law enforcement provisions away from access |
| Fourth round, 1996 | Electronic recordkeeping and a determination deadline missed as a matter of routine | Coverage for electronic records, electronic search, requested formats, electronic reading rooms, expedited and multitrack processing | Strengthened access by preservation and proactive duties |
| Fifth round, 2007 | Documented delay, backlog, and litigation practice problems | Tracking numbers, a fee consequence for missed deadlines, mediation through the Office of Government Information Services, and clarified attorney fees | Strengthened access through procedure |
| Sixth round, 2016 | Dependence on executive policy for the openness standard and inconsistent procedure | A codified presumption of openness, the foreseeable harm standard, a deliberative process age limit, proactive posting, and a single portal mandate | Strengthened access by standard and limits |

Across six rounds, the amendment record shows Congress returning to the same statute for different reasons and with different instruments. The first round supplied enforcement power through deadlines, judicial inspection, segregability, and fee shifting. The second round supplied a qualification test for borrowed withholding statutes. The fourth round supplied continuity, translating coverage, search, production, and the reading room into electronic terms before a change in office practice could shrink the right by implication. The fifth and sixth rounds supplied procedure and durability, building mediation, tracking, notice, proactive posting, and a statutory harm standard around a substantive core that earlier rounds had established. A reader tracing [how the amendments operate in agency practice](/2022/01/15/foia-agency-implementation/) can see that sequence as a gradual shift in the object of reform, from the definition of the right toward the administration and permanence of the right.

The counter-reading deserves equal weight, because the arc of the six rounds did not move uniformly toward openness. The round of 1986 is the standing proof. In a single enactment carried inside drug enforcement legislation, Congress made requesting cheaper for journalists, scholars, and ordinary requesters while broadening Exemption 7, lowering the harm standard for most law enforcement withholding, and creating three exclusions under which an agency responded as if records did not exist. The deadline history supplies a second qualification. Congress in 1974 imposed a ten working day determination period, and Congress in 1996 doubled that period to twenty working days after the shorter period had been missed as a matter of routine. Each Congress answered the pressures assembled around a particular round, and enforcement or confidentiality pressure could move the statute toward withholding.

The transparency argument for the amendment record rests on verification rather than trust. Disclosure allows legislators, journalists, researchers, and citizens to compare official accounts against contemporaneous records, to detect error and improper influence, and to evaluate whether an agency followed the criteria that law assigned to a program. The strongest amendments strengthened verification directly. In camera review allowed a judge to test an exemption claim against the record. Segregability forced passage level justification instead of file level refusal. Redaction marking, annual reporting, the Exemption 3 statute listing, and proactive posting converted individual decisions into patterns that oversight could examine. On the transparency account, secrecy carries a burden of justification because concentrated control of information allows the institution that acted to control the evidence by which action gets judged, and the amendment history repeatedly reduced that control in defined and checkable ways.

The deliberative candor argument rests on a different institutional concern, presented with equal care because several amendments accepted part of that argument while rejecting a general version of it. Officials who expect tentative recommendations, rejected options, and blunt assessments to circulate outside the decision process during a live controversy may write less fully, shift discussion to oral channels, and frame memorandums for an external audience rather than for the decision maker. Genuine confidentiality interests also protect sources, investigations, personal privacy, and material whose release could damage national defense or foreign relations. The amendment record honored those concerns through exemptions, exclusions for narrow categories, and protection for deliberative material within a defined period. The record rejected the broader claim that candor or sensitivity justified unreviewable withholding, by pairing each protection with a standard, a harm test, judicial inspection, or an age limit that a court could apply.

Comparison across statutes sharpens the study value of the amendment record. Read beside [the companion post-Watergate reform statute](/2021/10/15/ethics-in-government-act-1978-guide/), the amendments of 1974 appear as one part of a broader legislative response to executive secrecy, directed at records rather than at financial disclosure and conflicts of interest. Read on internal evidence alone, the six rounds teach a method for reading any disclosure statute. A careful reader asks what prompt produced a provision, whether the provision changed substance or procedure, what standard a court can enforce, what consequence follows when an agency misses a duty, and whether a protection carries a boundary of age, harm, specificity, or category. Those questions expose the difference between a stated presumption and an enforceable right, because the amendment record shows presumption acquiring force only when deadlines, inspection powers, fee consequences, and harm standards gave the presumption operational meaning.

The amendment record also rewards organized study, because the evidence sits in layers that cross reference one another: public law numbers and Statutes at Large citations, vote tallies and veto messages, case names that supplied the prompt for a correction, exemption language that changed by a single deleted word, and deadline figures that doubled after a generation of missed compliance. A reader tracing those layers across five decades benefits from a single study record, and a durable way to maintain that record is to [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html). Held together in that form, the chronology preserves the central lesson of the six rounds. A disclosure statute is not a settlement reached once. The statute is an instrument that each generation of legislators, judges, agencies, and requesters tested against scandal, litigation, technology, backlog, and competing claims of candor, and that each major revision rewrote at exactly the point where testing had exposed a weakness.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which FOIA amendments passed over a veto?

The amendments of 1974 were the round enacted over a presidential veto. President Gerald R. Ford vetoed the bill on October 17, 1974, calling the amendments unconstitutional and unworkable and objecting to judicial review of classified material and to the deadlines. The House voted on November 20, 1974, by 371 to 31 to override, and the Senate voted on November 21, 1974, by 65 to 27, with enactment following on November 21, 1974 as Public Law 93-502. The other five major revision acts in the six round sequence were signed by the President in office at enactment rather than enacted over a veto. The override gave the 1974 deadlines, in camera review, fee limits, and exemption changes a legislative foundation independent of executive agreement.

### Q: What did the 1974 FOIA amendments add?

The amendments of 1974 added the enforcement machinery that the original statute lacked in precise form. Enforceable time limits required a determination within ten working days of receipt and a determination on administrative appeal within twenty working days. Fee charges were limited to reasonable standard charges for document search and duplication, with a public interest fee waiver added. Courts received authority to examine records in camera, including classified records. Exemption 1 was narrowed to material properly classified under Executive order criteria, Exemption 7 was rewritten around records or information with a six harm test, and reasonably segregable portions had to be released. The round also added attorney fees and litigation costs for requesters who substantially prevailed, annual agency reports, and disciplinary referral for arbitrary or capricious withholding.

### Q: What did the 1996 FOIA amendments do about electronic records?

The Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996 confirmed that the act reached records maintained in electronic format and that a search under the act included an electronic search. Agencies had to make reasonable efforts to provide records in the form or format requested where the record was readily reproducible in that form or format. Reading room records created on or after November 1, 1996 had to be made available electronically, and a new proactive category covered records released under the act that an agency determined had become or were likely to become the subject of subsequent requests for the same records, accompanied by an index. The round treated electronic recordkeeping as a change in the medium of agency records rather than as a reason to place records beyond a right written around paper files.

### Q: What is the foreseeable harm standard from the FOIA amendments?

The foreseeable harm standard, codified by the Freedom of Information Act Improvement Act of 2016, provides that an agency shall withhold information under the act only if the agency reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption, or disclosure is prohibited by law. The standard added a second inquiry after exemption coverage, requiring an expectation of harm grounded in the record rather than a claim that a category applied in the abstract. Where full disclosure was not possible, the agency had to consider partial disclosure and take reasonable steps to segregate and release nonexempt information. The standard codified the policy that the Department of Justice had applied by memorandum since 2009 under Attorney General Holder, and the standard does not override material prohibited by law under Exemption 3.

### Q: Did the FOIA amendments create an ombudsman?

The OPEN Government Act of 2007 created the Office of Government Information Services within the National Archives and Records Administration, and the office opened in September 2009. The office reviews agency FOIA policies, procedures, and compliance, recommends policy changes to Congress and the President, may issue advisory opinions, and offers mediation services to resolve disputes between requesters and agencies as a non exclusive alternative to litigation. The ombudsman function therefore rests on mediation rather than command. The answer to the ordering question is direct: the office mediates and cannot order release. The office also cannot process requests or appeals, and the office does not take disputes already in litigation, because enforcement authority under the act belongs to the federal courts.

### Q: Did the FOIA amendments put a sunset on deliberative withholding?

The amendments of 2016 placed an age limit on deliberative withholding rather than a general sunset on the exemption that contains the privilege. The Freedom of Information Act Improvement Act of 2016 provided that the deliberative process privilege within Exemption 5 shall not apply to records created 25 years or more before the date on which the records were requested. The word sunset can describe the result only with qualification, because no provision repealed the privilege and no provision set an expiration date for the statute. The limit operates record by record, measured from creation of the record to the date of request. A record created exactly at the 25 year boundary falls outside the privilege, while a record created inside the boundary retains whatever protection the privilege otherwise provides, subject to the conditions governing Exemption 5.

### Q: What did the 2007 FOIA amendments say about news media?

The OPEN Government Act of 2007 defined a representative of the news media in functional terms rather than by institutional affiliation. Under the statutory definition, a representative of the news media is a person or entity that gathers information of potential interest to a segment of the public, uses editorial skills to turn the raw materials into a distinct work, and distributes that work to an audience. Gathering, editorial transformation, and distribution form the three elements of the test. The definition reaches freelancers and independent and online publishers who gather information and intend to distribute a distinct work, even without a staff position at an established outlet. The definition matters in the fee system because representatives of the news media pay duplication charges only, without search and review charges, under the categories established in the round of 1986.

### Q: Why does each round of FOIA amendments follow a scandal?

The premise requires correction at the strength the record supports, because scandal explains only the first round fully. The amendments of 1974 followed the Watergate scandal and the narrowing judicial construction in EPA v. Mink. A judicial construction explains the second round, because the Government in the Sunshine Act of 1976 followed FAA v. Robertson, in which the Supreme Court had accepted broad discretion under Exemption 3. Administrative and technological pressure explains the later rounds more accurately than scandal. The round of 1986 followed law enforcement pressure and fee disputes, the round of 1996 followed electronic recordkeeping and a routinely missed deadline, the round of 2007 followed documented delay and backlog, and the round of 2016 codified an executive policy standard and addressed consistency across agencies.

### Q: What change did the 1976 amendments make to Exemption 3?

The Government in the Sunshine Act of 1976 amended Exemption 3 only, after FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, had held that a statute granting broad discretion to withhold material on objection qualified as a statute specifically exempting material from disclosure. After the amendment, an outside statute qualified under Exemption 3 only if the statute required that matters be withheld in such a manner as to leave no discretion on the issue, or the statute established particular criteria for withholding or referred to particular types of matters to be withheld. The Department of Justice Guide described the amendment as in effect overruling Robertson. Discretion alone therefore stopped serving as a basis for qualification, and a borrowed withholding statute had to carry either a command or a defined frame that a court could inspect.

### Q: How did the 1986 amendments structure FOIA fees by requester category?

The Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986 replaced a relatively flat fee arrangement with categories priced according to the identity and purpose of the requester. Commercial use requesters paid search, review, and duplication. Educational institutions and noncommercial scientific institutions paid duplication only, with the first 100 pages free. Representatives of the news media also paid duplication only, with the first 100 pages free. All other requesters paid search and duplication, with the first two hours of search time and the first 100 pages free, and no review charges. The round also clarified and strengthened the public interest fee waiver, under which disclosure had to be likely to contribute significantly to public understanding and not primarily in the commercial interest of the requester.

### Q: What were the three exclusions created by the 1986 amendments?

The amendments of 1986 created three exclusions at 5 U.S.C. 552(c), covering situations in which an agency treated records as not subject to the act and responded as if the records did not exist. The first exclusion covered records of a pending criminal investigation where the subject was unaware and revealing the existence of the records could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. The second covered informant records maintained by a criminal law enforcement agency where the informant identity had not been officially confirmed. The third covered Federal Bureau of Investigation records on foreign intelligence, counterintelligence, or international terrorism whose existence was classified. An exclusion differed from an exemption because no exemption was cited and no withheld material was described for appeal or judicial review.

### Q: How did the 1986 amendments change the Exemption 7 threshold and harm standard?

The amendments of 1986 widened Exemption 7 at the threshold and lowered the harm standard inside the exemption. At the threshold, the text deleted the word investigatory, leaving the exemption to cover records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes across the range of enforcement functions rather than material tied to an investigatory character. The six harm categories from the round of 1974 remained the structure of the test, covering interference with proceedings, fair trial, privacy, confidential sources, techniques, and safety. The standard for most categories changed from disclosure that would cause an enumerated harm to disclosure that could reasonably be expected to cause such a harm. The Department of Justice Guide recorded exceptions for Exemption 7(B) and part of Exemption 7(E), so the lowered standard governed most but not all of the six harms.

### Q: What did the 2007 amendments do about delay in FOIA processing?

The OPEN Government Act of 2007 attacked delay through visibility and consequence. Agencies had to assign an individualized tracking number to any request that would take longer than ten days and provide a telephone number or internet service through which a requester could check status. An agency that failed to comply with a statutory time limit generally could not assess search fees, and could not assess duplication fees for educational, noncommercial scientific, and news media requesters, subject to statutory exceptions for unusual and exceptional circumstances. The round also addressed attorney fees, clarifying that a requester had substantially prevailed where relief came through a judicial order, an enforceable written agreement, or a voluntary or unilateral change in agency position where the claim was not insubstantial, so a release produced by filing suit carried fee consequences.

### Q: What proactive posting duty did the 2016 amendments create?

The Freedom of Information Act Improvement Act of 2016 required that records requested three or more times be made available for public inspection in an electronic format. The provision, known as the rule of 3, converted repeated individual demand into a trigger for general publication. Three or more requests for the same records signaled a broader public interest that an agency could identify without speculation, and posting the records ended repeated searches and reviews for successive requesters seeking identical material. A later requester could inspect the posted records without filing a request, waiting in a processing queue, or paying duplication charges, and the agency handled the records once rather than once per requester. The duty extended a proactive disclosure idea that the round of 1996 had introduced for released records likely to attract subsequent requests.

### Q: How many FOIA amendments were there in total, and how does the Department of Justice count differ?

The six round sequence in the amendment history counts the major substantive revision acts: the amendments of 1974, the Government in the Sunshine Act of 1976, the Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986, the Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996, the OPEN Government Act of 2007, and the Freedom of Information Act Improvement Act of 2016. The Department of Justice Office of Information Policy counts seven congressional amendments in total, including narrower measures alongside the major revisions. The narrower measures in that count comprise a technical change in 1978, an enactment in 1984, a restriction concerning foreign requesters in 2002, and a rule concerning designation under Exemption 3 in 2009, the OPEN FOIA Act of 2009. The two counts answer different questions, one about major revision and one about every congressional amendment.

### Q: Why did the determination deadline change from ten to twenty working days?

The amendments of 1974 required a determination within ten working days of receipt, and the Electronic Freedom of Information Act Amendments of 1996 lengthened the determination period from ten working days to twenty working days. The occasion was practical failure rather than a change in the principle that requests deserved prompt decision. The ten working day period was routinely missed across agencies, and President Clinton, in signing the legislation of 1996, acknowledged the backlogs and limited resources under which agencies administered the act. A deadline missed as a matter of routine loses disciplinary force, because delay becomes the expected condition rather than an exception requiring explanation. Congress therefore traded nominal speed for a standard with a greater prospect of compliance, while a determination within twenty working days remained a decision on the request rather than permission to defer production without limit.

### Q: What did the first round of amendments do about judicial review of classified records?

The amendments of 1974 authorized judicial in camera examination of records, including classified records, and narrowed the exemption that a court applied during that examination. In camera examination means inspection by the judge in private, outside the presence of the parties, so the court can decide whether an exemption covers material without disclosing the material in the course of deciding. The authorization reversed the limitation that EPA v. Mink had placed on review under Exemption 1. Alongside inspection, Exemption 1 was narrowed so that material had to be properly classified under Executive order criteria, tying protection to compliance with the classification system rather than to sensitivity of subject matter in the abstract. An agency affidavit describing records in general terms therefore ceased to mark the outer limit of judicial inquiry in classification cases.

### Q: What role did EPA v. Mink play in the amendment history?

EPA v. Mink, 413 U.S. 493 (1973), supplied the judicial prompt for the first major revision, alongside the Watergate scandal. The Supreme Court barred in camera review of classified material under Exemption 1, leaving the executive classification determination largely conclusive, and read the obligation to release segregable portions narrowly, so material intertwined with exempt material could remain withheld as a whole. Judicial review is the enforcement mechanism of the act, and a rule preventing the reviewing judge from seeing disputed records reduced review to assessment of affidavits prepared by the party defending withholding. Congress answered in the amendments of 1974 by authorizing in camera examination expressly, narrowing Exemption 1 to material properly classified under Executive order criteria, and codifying and extending the duty to release reasonably segregable portions of records.

### Q: What single request portal did the 2016 amendments require?

The Freedom of Information Act Improvement Act of 2016 required the Director of the Office of Management and Budget, in consultation with the Attorney General, to ensure the operation of a single, consolidated online request portal through which the public can submit a request to any agency. The consolidated FOIA.gov portal that followed is the implementation of that statutory mandate. Before consolidation, each agency maintained separate instructions, forms, addresses, and submission channels, and a request directed to the wrong component or framed under the wrong procedure could consume weeks before processing began. The portal changed the point of entry rather than the law applied after entry. Exemptions, deadlines, fees, and appeals operated as before for each agency, while filing became a single standardized transaction through one address.

### Q: Which round of FOIA amendments narrowed access to records?

The round of 1986 narrowed access in the law enforcement area while lowering the cost of requesting, and that combination makes the round the countercurrent in the six round sequence. The Freedom of Information Reform Act of 1986 deleted the word investigatory from Exemption 7, broadened the threshold to records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes, and lowered most harm showings from would cause to could reasonably be expected to cause. The same round created three exclusions at 5 U.S.C. 552(c), under which an agency treated specified records as not subject to the act and responded as if the records did not exist. Against those changes, the fee categories and the strengthened public interest waiver moved toward access, so the accurate description of the round is mixed in direction and narrowing in substantive law enforcement coverage.
