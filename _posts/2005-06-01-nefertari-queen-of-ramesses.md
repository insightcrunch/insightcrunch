Nefertari is usually introduced as a love story, and that is the least reliable thing anyone says about her. What survives of Nefertari is not a record of feeling. It is a record of expenditure: a temple carved into a Nubian cliff and dedicated in her name, a facade on which her statues stand at the same height as her husband's, a string of titles that place her at the summit of the Egyptian court, a letter she sent to a foreign queen that was archived in a foreign capital, and a painted tomb in the Valley of the Queens whose surviving color is among the finest to come out of ancient Egypt. Those things can be measured. Affection cannot.

That distinction is the organizing argument of this guide, and it deserves a name so it can be tested: call it the honored-queen thesis. The case for Nefertari's importance does not rest on inferring what Ramesses II felt about her. It rests on the observable fact that he spent state resources honoring her at a level almost no other Egyptian queen received, and that the honors were public, monumental, and permanent. Resources are evidence. Sentiment is interpretation, and in this instance the interpretation has grown so much larger than the evidence that it now obscures it.

There is a second reason to be careful with her, which is that the popular account of Nefertari has absorbed a great deal of material that belongs to other people or to nobody at all. She is regularly confused with Nefertiti, who lived roughly a century earlier and belonged to an entirely different dynasty and religious world. She is credited with a documented biography that does not exist, since her parents, her birthplace, and her age at marriage are all unknown. She is described in terms of physical beauty on the basis of images that were produced according to fixed artistic conventions and were never intended as portraits. Clearing that material away leaves less than the legend promises and more than most readers expect, because what remains is a genuinely exceptional set of monuments and one of the very few pieces of surviving correspondence written in the name of an Egyptian queen.

## Why Nefertari Matters

Egyptian queens are, as a class, poorly served by the evidence. The Egyptian state recorded kings. Queens appear in the record as adjuncts, named in a husband's inscriptions, shown at his feet or beside his throne, given titles that describe their relationship to him rather than any office of their own. There are exceptions, and they are famous precisely because they are exceptions: Hatshepsut, who took the kingship itself; Nefertiti, whose unusual prominence in the Amarna period remains debated; Cleopatra VII, who ruled as a monarch in a Hellenistic system. Nefertari belongs to none of those categories. She never ruled and never claimed to.

What makes her worth a full study is that she is the best-documented example of the ordinary category taken to an extraordinary degree. She held the standard position of Great Royal Wife, and she held it with a level of monumental support that lets us see what that position could mean when a king chose to invest in it. Most Egyptian queens are a name and a title. Nefertari is a name, a title set, a temple, a tomb, a diplomatic letter, and appearances across several major building programs. She therefore functions as a case study in the actual reach of a queen's role, which is a question the broader treatment of [women and power in New Kingdom Egypt](/2003/11/01/women-and-power-in-new-kingdom/) takes up across the period as a whole. That article owns the general question. This one owns Nefertari.

### What kind of position did a Great Royal Wife hold?

The Great Royal Wife was the principal consort among several wives, the woman whose sons stood first in the succession, and a participant in state ritual with religious functions of her own. The position carried genuine standing at court and in the temple, but it conferred no independent political authority separate from the king.

The role had accumulated weight across the New Kingdom. Earlier queens of the Eighteenth Dynasty, including Ahmose-Nefertari and Tiye, had established precedents for queens who appeared prominently in monuments, corresponded with foreign courts, and received cult after death. Nefertari inherited a role that had already been enlarged, and the honors she received should be read as the continuation of a developing institution rather than as an invention of her husband's affection.

### The name and the confusion with Nefertiti

Her full name appears as Nefertari Merytmut, which combines a personal name with an epithet identifying her as beloved of the goddess Mut, consort of Amun and one of the principal deities of Thebes. The name element that both she and Nefertiti share carries the sense of beauty or goodness, which is why the two names look alike and why they are so persistently confused.

The two women have almost nothing else in common. Nefertiti was the wife of Akhenaten, lived in the Eighteenth Dynasty, and was central to the religious upheaval of the Amarna period, in which the traditional pantheon was displaced in favor of the sun disk. Nefertari lived in the Nineteenth Dynasty, roughly a century later, in a court that had systematically repudiated the Amarna experiment and restored the old gods. Their worlds were not merely different; the second was constructed in explicit opposition to the first. The separate account of [Nefertiti and her disputed prominence](/2004/06/01/nefertiti-egypt-famous-queen/) covers her case in full, and readers who arrived here after a name mix-up will find what they were looking for there.

## The Life and Its Context

Almost everything about Nefertari before her marriage is unknown, and it is worth stating that plainly before examining what can be said.

### Origins and the question of her parentage

No inscription names Nefertari's father or mother. This silence is itself informative, because Egyptian royal women who were the daughters of kings held and displayed the title King's Daughter, and Nefertari's extensive title set does not include it. The absence is not proof, since title selection was not wholly consistent, but it is a strong indication that she was not born to the royal house. The likeliest reading is that she came from the Egyptian nobility, a family of standing sufficient to place a daughter in a royal marriage without itself being royal.

A single object has generated a great deal of speculation about her background. Among the material recovered from her tomb was a knob or fragment bearing the cartouche of Ay, the short-reigned king who followed Tutankhamun at the close of the Eighteenth Dynasty. Some writers have taken this as evidence of a family connection linking Nefertari to the late Amarna court. The inference is weak. Objects bearing royal names circulated widely, were reused, were given as gifts, and turned up in contexts that had nothing to do with descent. An item with Ay's name in a tomb cut two generations later establishes that the item existed and reached that tomb. It does not establish a bloodline.

### The Ay fragment and what it does not prove

No. A fragment bearing the cartouche of King Ay was found among objects in her tomb, and some writers have built a family link on it, but objects carrying royal names circulated freely as gifts and heirlooms. Her title set omits King's Daughter, which points instead to noble rather than royal birth.

The honest position is that Nefertari enters the historical record already married and already elevated, and that her earlier life is not recoverable. Readers should be wary of accounts that supply a birthplace, a father, or an age, because those details are supplied by the writer rather than by any source.

### The marriage and the accession

Nefertari appears as Great Royal Wife from the beginning of Ramesses II's reign, which conventionally begins about 1279 BCE. The consistency of her appearance in early monuments indicates that the marriage predated the accession, contracted while Ramesses was crown prince under his father Seti I. This was standard practice. Egyptian princes married before inheriting, and a crown prince's principal wife became the Great Royal Wife on his accession without any further ceremony being required.

The timing matters for understanding her position. Nefertari was not selected by a reigning king from a field of candidates. She was already in place when the reign began, which means her elevation reflects decisions made in Seti I's court about a suitable match for the heir. Whatever the personal relationship became, it began as a dynastic arrangement of the ordinary kind.

She was not the only wife. Isetnofret held the position of a second principal queen and appears in the record alongside Nefertari through the earlier decades of the reign, and the relationship between the two women and their respective children turns out to matter considerably for what happened afterward. Beyond these two, Ramesses II accumulated a large secondary household over a reign of sixty-six years, including foreign princesses received through diplomatic marriage and, in the later decades, several of his own daughters elevated to queenly status in a practice whose meaning is debated among Egyptologists.

### Where she stood in the early reign

Through roughly the first two decades of the reign, Nefertari is the queen who appears in the major monuments. She is present in the Nubian building program, at Thebes, at Luxor, and in the ceremonial record. Her prominence in this period is not disputed and does not require any special explanation beyond the position she held.

The broader shape of the reign, its campaigns, its enormous construction program, and the administrative machinery that supported both, is set out in the overview of [Ramesside Egypt and the age of the Ramesses kings](/2005/01/01/ramesside-egypt-complete-guide/), and the arguments about the king himself belong to the profile of [Ramesses II and the case for calling him Egypt's greatest pharaoh](/2005/02/01/ramesses-ii-greatest-pharaoh/). Nefertari's biography sits inside both and is best read against them.

## The Titles and What They Actually Meant

Egyptian titles were not decorative. They were positional statements within a highly formalized system, and reading them carefully tells us more about Nefertari's standing than any amount of speculation about her marriage.

### The title set, read one by one

Her titles include Great Royal Wife, Lady of the Two Lands, Mistress of Upper and Lower Egypt, Hereditary Princess, Lady of All Lands, Great of Praises, Sweet of Love, and God's Wife. Each carried a specific institutional or ritual meaning, and the combination places her at the apex of the female hierarchy at court.

Taking the principal ones in turn clarifies the picture considerably.

Great Royal Wife, in Egyptian *hemet nesut weret*, identifies her as the senior consort. The qualifier matters: there were royal wives, and there was the great one. The title established precedence over every other woman in the household and marked her sons as the primary line of succession.

Lady of the Two Lands and Mistress of Upper and Lower Egypt are female counterparts to elements of royal titulary, associating her with the unified kingdom in the same formula used for the king. They do not confer rule. They assert that she belongs to the institution of kingship as its female component.

Hereditary Princess, in Egyptian *iryt-pat*, is a rank title from the wider Egyptian honorific system rather than a statement of royal birth. It denotes elevated standing and was held by high officials in its masculine form. Its presence in her titulary alongside the absence of King's Daughter is one of the reasons for concluding that she was noble rather than royal by birth.

God's Wife, in its fuller form God's Wife of Amun, is the significant one for understanding her religious role. It designated a priestly office in the cult of Amun at Thebes, associated with participation in temple ritual, and it carried real institutional weight. The office had been prominent earlier in the New Kingdom and would become extremely powerful much later, in the Third Intermediate Period, when it was held by royal daughters who functioned as effective rulers of Thebes. In Nefertari's hands it indicates a genuine ritual function rather than an ornamental designation.

Great of Praises and Sweet of Love belong to a different category. These are epithets rather than offices, conventional formulae applied to queens as a class, and they should not be read as personal characterizations. A queen described as sweet of love is being described in the standard vocabulary of her position, not being paid a compliment specific to her.

### The evidentiary value of a title set

The methodological point generalizes beyond Nefertari. When approaching any Egyptian royal woman, the title set is the most reliable single body of evidence available, because titles were regulated, conventional, and inscribed in durable materials by an administration that cared about getting them right. Images are conventionalized. Epithets are formulaic. Later literary accounts are usually much later. Titles are contemporary, official, and specific.

What Nefertari's title set establishes is a woman occupying the senior consort position with an active priestly role in the Amun cult and formal association with the kingship as an institution. That is a substantial standing. It is also, and this deserves emphasis, a standing several other New Kingdom queens shared. The titles alone do not make her exceptional. What makes her exceptional is the scale of the monuments attached to them.

## The Children and the Succession That Passed Them By

Nefertari bore Ramesses II a number of children, and their fate is one of the more instructive parts of her story because it demonstrates the limits of monumental honor.

### The sons and daughters attributed to her

She is credited with roughly four sons and two daughters, including the eldest royal son Amunherkhepeshef, Pareherwenemef, Meryatum who became High Priest of Re at Heliopolis, and Meryre, along with the daughters Meritamen and Henuttawy. The attributions rest on inscriptions and statue groups, and some assignments remain debated.

Amunherkhepeshef was the firstborn son and therefore the designated heir through the earlier decades of the reign. He appears in military scenes alongside his father and held the expected titles of a crown prince. Pareherwenemef likewise appears in campaign contexts. Meryatum's career took a priestly rather than a military direction, and he reached one of the senior religious offices in the country at Heliopolis. Meritamen, the daughter, would become significant after her mother's death in a way discussed below.

The problem for all of them was arithmetic. Ramesses II reigned for approximately sixty-six years and lived to something close to ninety, an extraordinary span that outlasted the natural lifetimes of his elder children. Amunherkhepeshef predeceased his father. So did a long series of subsequent heirs. The son who eventually succeeded, Merenptah, was the thirteenth son and was already an older man when he took the throne, and he was the son of Isetnofret rather than of Nefertari.

The consequence is worth stating clearly because it cuts against a sentimental reading. The most honored queen of the Nineteenth Dynasty did not found the line that continued it. The dynasty ran through her co-wife's descendants. Isetnofret's family, including Merenptah and later Merenptah's successors, held the throne, and Nefertari's sons are footnotes in the succession despite the temple, the tomb, and the titles.

This gap between honor and outcome is a useful corrective to the assumption that monumental prominence in Egypt tracked political power. It did not, at least not reliably. Nefertari received the honors. Isetnofret's line received the crown. Both facts are secure, and holding them together produces a more accurate picture of what a Great Royal Wife's position actually secured.

### Meritamen and what followed

After Nefertari's death, her daughter Meritamen assumed a prominent role, taking on the position of Great Royal Wife herself. Ramesses II elevated several of his daughters to queenly status over the course of the reign, a practice that recurs in Egyptian kingship and that modern readers find uncomfortable.

The interpretation of this practice is contested and the honest treatment is to present the disagreement rather than resolve it. One reading takes the elevations at face value as marriages in the ordinary sense. Another argues that the position of Great Royal Wife had ritual functions that required a female occupant, and that when a king outlived his wives a daughter could be installed to fill the ceremonial role without a marital relationship in the modern sense. The evidence does not decisively settle which reading is correct, and Egyptologists differ. What can be said is that the practice is documented, that it followed Nefertari's death in this instance, and that Meritamen appears afterward in contexts where her mother had previously appeared.

### The sons' burial and the long shadow of a sixty-six year reign

The fate of Nefertari's sons is documented in a way that most royal children's is not, because the burial place assigned to the sons of Ramesses II has been identified and investigated.

The tomb designated KV5, in the Valley of the Kings, was known to nineteenth-century explorers, who found its accessible portions choked with debris and moved on. Systematic re-investigation beginning in the late twentieth century, undertaken by an American mission mapping the Theban necropolis, established that the tomb was far larger than anyone had supposed. Clearance revealed a complex of chambers and corridors running back into the hillside on a scale unmatched by any other tomb in the valley, with a great many rooms and evidence for further extension. Inscriptional material recovered from it names sons of Ramesses II, including Amunherkhepeshef, which identifies the complex as a collective burial installation for the king's male children.

The design principle is unusual and tells us something about the problem the reign created. Egyptian royal tombs were normally single burials. A tomb built to receive a large number of the king's sons implies planning for a situation in which many sons would die before their father, and a sixty-six year reign guaranteed exactly that. Ramesses II outlived heir after heir. The tomb is an administrative response to the arithmetic of extreme longevity.

For Nefertari's biography, KV5 supplies the closing chapter on her children. Her firstborn, designated heir through the earlier decades, was interred there rather than succeeding. So were a series of his brothers. The line she had established as the primary succession, by virtue of being Great Royal Wife, ran out in a shared tomb while the king continued to reign.

The condition of KV5 is poor. Flood water has repeatedly entered the valley over the centuries, and the tomb sits where runoff collects, so its chambers filled with hardened debris that both damaged the decoration and preserved the layout. Recovery of inscriptional material has been slow and the work continues. What has been established is enough to identify the tomb's purpose and several of its occupants, and it has substantially altered the picture of how the reign handled its dynastic problem.

## The Small Temple at Abu Simbel

The single most substantial monument to Nefertari's standing is a temple, and it is the strongest evidence for the honored-queen thesis.

### A temple bearing a queen's name

Yes. The Small Temple at Abu Simbel in Nubia was dedicated jointly to the goddess Hathor of Ibshek and to Nefertari, making her one of very few Egyptian queens honored with a temple bearing her name during her lifetime. Its facade carries six standing colossi about ten meters tall, four of Ramesses II and two of Nefertari.

The full architectural treatment of the site, including the Great Temple, the solar alignment, and the twentieth-century relocation, belongs to the dedicated guide to [Abu Simbel and what Ramesses II built it to say](/2005/05/01/abu-simbel-temple-of-ramesses/). What concerns a biography of Nefertari is narrower: what the Small Temple establishes about her position.

### Equal scale in a system that never granted it

Egyptian art used relative size as a strict grammar of rank, so queens are normally rendered substantially smaller than kings. At Abu Simbel Nefertari's colossi match her husband's in height, a deliberate departure from a tightly regulated convention, and departures in such a system carry meaning rather than reflecting artistic whim.

This is the detail Egyptologists return to, and understanding why requires appreciating how disciplined Egyptian proportional convention was. Hierarchic scale operated with considerable consistency across two and a half millennia of Egyptian art. Kings tower over officials. Officials tower over servants. Queens stand at the king's shoulder or knee. The system was not a limitation of the artists' abilities; it was a coding scheme, and every viewer read it fluently.

Setting a queen's statue at the king's height inside that scheme is a statement that the intended audience could not miss. It does not claim she is king. The iconography is careful about that: her crown is Hathoric, with cow horns, sun disk, and tall plumes, marking divine association rather than royal authority, and the temple is dedicated to a goddess with the queen alongside rather than to the queen alone. What it claims is that she stands at a level the visual system does not normally permit.

The dedicatory inscriptions reinforce the point by naming her explicitly in connection with the foundation. The temple was not built for a queen in general. It was built for this queen, named.

### The interior and the Hathoric program

Inside, a hall carries six pillars whose capitals take the form of the head of Hathor, the sistrum-shaped column type associated with her cult. Reliefs show the royal couple presenting offerings to deities, and Nefertari appears performing ritual acts that in many other contexts are the prerogative of the king alone. Beyond the hall a vestibule leads to a sanctuary containing a representation of Hathor in her cow form emerging from the rock face.

The theological logic is coherent. Hathor was associated with love, music, motherhood, and the protection of the dead, and she was the goddess with whom queens were most naturally identified. A temple that identifies Nefertari with Hathor in a Nubian setting is doing for the queen a version of what the Great Temple next door does for the king, associating a living royal person with divinity in a province where Egyptian authority needed continual reassertion. The two temples are one program, and reading the Small Temple as a private gesture separated from politics misses how the pair works.

### What the temple does and does not prove

It proves expenditure. Carving a temple into a cliff, with ten-meter colossi and a decorated interior, consumed skilled labor and administrative attention over years, and no Egyptian king spent at that level casually. It proves public commitment, since a rock-cut temple is not a private gift but a permanent state monument visible to everyone passing on the river. It proves that the honor was sanctioned by the religious establishment, since a temple required priesthood, endowment, and cult.

It does not prove love. A king with a political need to display an idealized royal couple in a frontier province had reasons to build this temple that have nothing to do with private feeling, and a king who did love his wife would have built the same temple. The monument is compatible with both explanations and distinguishes between neither. This is the recurring problem with Nefertari, and the responsible response is to report what the monument establishes and decline to fill the gap.

## The Letter to Puduhepa

The most unusual item in the Nefertari file is not a monument. It is a piece of correspondence, and it survives not in Egypt but in the archives of a foreign capital.

### A queen inside the diplomatic apparatus

Yes. A letter sent in Nefertari's name to Puduhepa, queen of the Hittites, survives among the cuneiform tablets recovered from the Hittite capital, along with related correspondence and records of gift exchange. It followed the peace agreement between the two powers and shows an Egyptian queen participating in formal interstate contact.

The context is the settlement that ended decades of confrontation between Egypt and Hatti, the agreement examined in the account of [the world's first surviving peace treaty](/2005/04/01/worlds-first-peace-treaty-egypt/). Treaties in the Late Bronze Age were not concluded and then filed away. They were maintained by continuing correspondence, exchanges of gifts, and eventually by dynastic marriage, and the archives at the Hittite capital preserve a substantial body of that traffic because the tablets were baked hard when the palace burned.

Nefertari's letter belongs to that traffic. It is addressed queen to queen, uses the diplomatic conventions of the period including expressions of goodwill and inquiries after health, and accompanies the exchange of gifts. Puduhepa was herself an exceptionally prominent figure in the Hittite state, a queen who sealed documents in her own name and participated actively in governance, and the correspondence between the two courts included her directly.

Several things follow from this document, and it is worth being careful about which are secure.

What is secure is that letters were sent in the queen's name, that the foreign court received and archived them, and that the practice was considered appropriate by both parties. Queen-to-queen correspondence was a recognized channel in Late Bronze Age diplomacy, running in parallel to the king-to-king channel, and Nefertari's participation places her inside the formal apparatus of international relations.

What is not secure is the degree of personal involvement. Royal correspondence was drafted by scribes according to convention. Whether Nefertari dictated the substance, approved a draft, or merely lent her name to a document produced by the foreign office is not recoverable from the tablet. The same caution applies to every piece of royal correspondence from the period, including the kings' letters.

What can be said with confidence is that the position of Great Royal Wife carried a recognized external face. The queen was a party in interstate relations, not merely a figure at court, and the archives of a foreign power treated communications in her name as worth keeping. For a category of person whose surviving documentation is usually limited to a husband's monuments, that is a significant addition to the record.

## Where Else Nefertari Appears

Beyond Abu Simbel, Nefertari appears across the monumental record of the earlier reign, and cataloguing those appearances establishes how consistently she was present.

At Luxor Temple she appears in the additions Ramesses II made to the front of the building, in the court and pylon area where the king's building program is most visible. At Karnak she appears in reliefs. At Thebes she is present in the decoration associated with the royal mortuary establishment. In the Nubian program she appears beyond Abu Simbel in other temples of the chain. Statue groups show her beside the king at the reduced scale that was conventional outside Abu Simbel.

Her presence in ceremonial contexts is documented as well. Egyptian kingship generated a constant stream of ritual occasions, from temple foundations to festival processions to the reception of tribute, and the Great Royal Wife had a defined place in many of them. Reliefs showing her shaking a sistrum, the sacred rattle associated with Hathor, place her in an active ritual function rather than as an observer.

One detail of her presentation deserves separate notice because it is easy to overlook and carries real weight. Nefertari's name is written inside a cartouche, the elongated oval that encircles royal names in Egyptian writing. The cartouche was not available to everyone. Its use was restricted to the king and to a limited set of royal persons, and its application to a queen's name places her inside the royal writing system rather than beside it. Great Royal Wives of the New Kingdom generally received this treatment, so it is not unique to her, but it belongs in any inventory of the formal marks of her position and it reinforces the pattern the rest of the evidence shows.

The distribution of her images across different scales is also informative. At Abu Simbel she stands at the king's height. In most other contexts she appears at the conventional reduced scale, at his shoulder or beside his leg, exactly as Egyptian proportional convention dictated. The variation is the point: the artists and administrators responsible for her representation applied the normal rule almost everywhere and departed from it at one specific monument. A queen whose exceptional treatment was a general policy would appear at equal scale routinely. A queen whose exceptional treatment was concentrated in a single major foundation appears as Nefertari does, which suggests a deliberate decision about that foundation rather than a blanket elevation of her status across the reign.

What the distribution shows is systematic inclusion rather than occasional appearance. Nefertari was a standing element of the reign's public presentation through the period in which she lived, and her disappearance from that presentation after her death is correspondingly noticeable.

## The Tomb: QV66

The second great monument to Nefertari is her tomb, and it has done more than anything else to shape her modern reputation.

### The burial ground and its place in the necropolis

Nefertari was buried in the Valley of the Queens on the west bank at Thebes, in the tomb designated QV66. The valley served as the principal burial ground for royal women and some royal children during the Nineteenth and Twentieth Dynasties, lying south of the royal necropolis where the kings themselves were interred.

The Valley of the Queens sits in the same Theban landscape as the kings' burial ground, and the two necropolises were served by the same community of specialist craftsmen, the workers whose village and working practices are unusually well documented. The technical tradition behind QV66 is the tradition described in the guide to [the Valley of the Kings and how its tombs were cut and decorated](/2004/01/01/valley-of-the-kings-explained/). The same crews, the same tools, the same methods of laying out and painting a rock-cut burial produced both.

### Schiaparelli, 1904, and what was left

The Italian archaeologist Ernesto Schiaparelli located and entered QV66 in 1904 while directing the Italian Archaeological Mission in Egypt. The burial had been thoroughly robbed in antiquity, leaving little of the original funerary equipment, but the painted decoration on the walls had survived in extraordinary condition.

The contrast between the two findings is the defining fact about the tomb. Robbers had taken essentially everything portable. What they could not take was the decoration, and the decoration turned out to be the treasure.

The surviving objects were meager: fragments of a pink granite sarcophagus lid, shabti figures of the kind buried to perform labor for the deceased in the afterlife, fragments of wooden boxes, djed pillar amulets, and a pair of sandals. A set of mummified knees was recovered and has been the subject of subsequent examination, with the identification as Nefertari's remains considered possible rather than established. Anyone reading a confident statement that Nefertari's mummy has been identified should treat the confidence as running ahead of the evidence.

### The unresolved question of her remains

No securely identified mummy of Nefertari survives. The tomb was robbed in antiquity and the burial disturbed. A pair of mummified knees recovered from QV66 has been examined and is considered a possible but unconfirmed identification. Unlike many New Kingdom kings, she was not among the bodies gathered into the later royal caches.

The absence is not unusual for a queen. The royal mummy caches assembled by priests at the close of the New Kingdom, which preserved the bodies of many kings including Ramesses II himself, were organized around the kings. Queens' burials in the Valley of the Queens were plundered and, in most cases, their occupants were not recovered.

### The plan and the surfaces of QV66

QV66 descends through a stairway into a series of chambers cut from limestone, with an antechamber, a side room, a further stairway, and a pillared burial chamber. Because the local limestone was poor, the walls were covered in plaster and painted rather than carved in relief, and it is that painted plaster that carries the decoration.

The layout follows the developed pattern for high-status Theban burials of the period. A descending entrance stair leads into an antechamber with an adjoining side chamber, from which a second stair descends to the burial chamber proper, whose ceiling is carried on pillars and whose floor is sunk to receive the sarcophagus. Additional small chambers open off the burial chamber and served for storage of equipment.

The decision to plaster and paint rather than to carve was dictated by geology, not by budget, and it produced the tomb's defining quality. Relief carving requires stone of adequate quality. The limestone in this part of the valley is friable and interrupted by flint bands and shale seams, which makes fine carving impractical. Applying a plaster ground over the rough rock created a smooth, uniform surface, and painting on that surface allowed a range of color and a subtlety of line that carved relief does not easily achieve.

### What the painted program actually depicts

The walls carry scenes from the funerary literature Egyptologists call the Book of the Dead, showing Nefertari being received and guided by deities, making offerings, passing the gates and guardians of the underworld, and playing the board game senet. The ceilings are painted deep blue with golden stars.

The program is theological rather than biographical, and mistaking it for the latter is the most common error readers make about this tomb. Nothing painted in QV66 records events from Nefertari's life. The scenes depict her passage through the afterlife according to a standard body of funerary texts and images used across elite Egyptian burials of the period. She is shown in the company of Osiris, Isis, Hathor, Anubis, Thoth, Neith, Selket, Maat, and others, in compositions whose content was drawn from a shared repertoire.

That said, the execution is what distinguishes the tomb, and the distinguishing qualities can be described precisely.

The color survives with unusual intensity. Egyptian painters worked with a limited mineral palette: carbon black, calcium-based white, yellow and red ochres, green from copper compounds, and the manufactured blue that Egyptian workshops produced by firing a mixture of silica, lime, copper, and an alkali. In QV66 these colors retain saturation and tonal range to a degree that most surviving Egyptian painting does not, because the tomb was sealed, dark, and dry for most of its history.

The modeling is more sophisticated than the Egyptian convention usually permits. Painters in this tomb used graded washes and subtle shifts of tone to suggest volume in faces, limbs, and drapery, working within the conventional outline system rather than abandoning it. The result reads as fully conventional Egyptian art executed at the upper limit of what the convention allowed.

The linework is confident and fine. The drawn outlines that organize every Egyptian painted scene are, in QV66, laid down with a control that indicates painters at the top of the profession. These were the state's best craftsmen, and the tomb is the clearest surviving demonstration of what that workforce could do when given a project of the highest priority.

The senet scene deserves separate mention because it is widely reproduced and widely misunderstood. Senet was a board game played across Egyptian society, and its appearance in a funerary context is not a record of the queen's leisure preferences. By the New Kingdom the game had acquired a symbolic reading in which the passage of pieces across the board represented the passage of the deceased through the trials of the afterlife toward rebirth. Nefertari is shown playing against an unseen opponent, and she is winning. The scene is a statement about successful passage, not a domestic vignette.

### Why QV66 became the tomb everyone knows

QV66 is celebrated for the quality and preservation of its painted decoration rather than for its contents, which robbers stripped in antiquity. The combination of a large decorated area, a highly skilled painting workshop, and burial conditions that protected the pigment produced surfaces that rank among the finest surviving from ancient Egypt.

There is a further reason for its reputation that has nothing to do with the ancient world. QV66 became famous in the modern period partly because it was the subject of one of the most publicized conservation campaigns ever undertaken on an Egyptian monument, and that campaign generated photography, publication, and public attention on a scale that few tombs receive. Fame in archaeology is partly a function of documentation, and this tomb has been documented exceptionally well.

## The Conservation Problem

The condition in which Schiaparelli found the paintings did not last, and the reason is worth understanding because it applies far beyond this tomb.

The threat was salt. The limestone of the Theban hills contains soluble salts, principally sodium chloride and sulfates, and the shale layers interleaved with the limestone contain more. Moisture moving through the rock carries these salts in solution toward the surface. When the moisture evaporates at the interface between rock and plaster, the salts crystallize. Crystal growth exerts pressure, and the pressure lifts the plaster ground away from the rock behind it.

The result is detachment. Painted plaster separates from its support, blisters, cracks, and eventually falls, taking the painting with it. In QV66 the process had already caused losses, and areas of the decoration were at risk of falling within a foreseeable period. Water introduced by the flooding of the valley floor during rare storms, humidity carried in by visitors, and earlier well-meaning interventions using unsuitable materials all contributed.

A major conservation campaign addressed the problem, undertaken jointly by the Getty Conservation Institute and the Egyptian antiquities authority, running from the mid-1980s into the early 1990s. Its approach is instructive because it deliberately rejected the older model of restoration.

The campaign did not repaint. Where paint had been lost, the loss was left visible rather than filled in with a modern reconstruction, on the principle that a viewer should be able to distinguish ancient work from modern intervention. What the conservators did was stabilize: reattaching detached plaster to the rock, consolidating fragile paint layers, removing earlier inappropriate materials, cleaning surfaces obscured by dirt and by the residues of previous treatments, and grouting voids behind the plaster to prevent further separation. The work was preceded by extensive scientific analysis of the pigments, the plaster composition, the salt chemistry, and the microclimate of the tomb.

The campaign is widely cited as a model for painted-surface conservation, and its documentation is substantial. Its second contribution was to establish that stabilization alone would not preserve the tomb if the environment were not managed, which is why access to QV66 has since been restricted, with limited visitor numbers, controlled durations, and monitoring of humidity and temperature. A tomb that is fully open to unlimited visitors is a tomb whose microclimate is continually disturbed by the moisture and heat that human bodies introduce.

That restriction is itself a piece of heritage philosophy worth noticing. The decision accepts that the monument's survival and its accessibility are in tension, and resolves the tension in favor of survival. The same logic governs a number of other high-value painted tombs in Egypt and elsewhere.

## When Nefertari Died and What Changed

### Dating her death from the monumental record

Nefertari appears to have died around the twenty-fourth or twenty-fifth year of Ramesses II's reign, which places it near 1255 BCE, roughly a third of the way into a reign that would run about sixty-six years. She is absent from the monumental record thereafter, and her daughter Meritamen takes on the queenly role in her place.

The dating is inferential rather than recorded. No document states the year of her death. What the evidence shows is a pattern: Nefertari is consistently present in the monuments of the earlier reign and consistently absent from those of the later reign, and the transition falls in the middle of the third decade. Her presence at the dedication of Abu Simbel and the subsequent appearance of Meritamen in her position bracket the change.

Her age at death is unknown, since her birth year is unknown. Estimates that supply a figure are calculating from assumptions about age at marriage rather than from evidence.

The consequences of her death for the shape of the reign were considerable. Ramesses II lived roughly four more decades. Through those decades the queenly role was filled successively by others, including his daughters and, later, the Hittite princess received under the terms of the diplomatic settlement with Hatti. Isetnofret's family rose. Nefertari's sons died before their father. By the end of the reign, the court that had honored Nefertari at Abu Simbel had been substantially replaced by a different set of people.

This is the fact that most complicates the romantic reading, and it is not usually mentioned in popular accounts. The tomb and the temple belong to the earlier reign. They record the standing of a queen who then vanished from the record forty years before her husband did, whose children did not inherit, and whose position was filled by others in short order. The honors were real and they were also, in dynastic terms, a dead end.

## The Sources and How Far They Can Be Trusted

A biography is only as good as its sources, and Nefertari's are unusual in composition: heavy on monuments, extremely light on documents, with nothing personal at all.

### What we have

The monumental record is the bulk of it. Temple reliefs, statuary, and inscriptions at Abu Simbel, Luxor, Karnak, and elsewhere give her titles, her images, and her presence in ceremonial contexts. This evidence is contemporary and official, which makes it reliable for what it records and useless for anything it does not record. Egyptian monuments state positions and relationships. They do not narrate events in a queen's life.

The tomb is the second body of evidence. QV66 supplies her funerary treatment, her titles again, the theological program considered appropriate for her, and the quality of workmanship assigned to her burial. It supplies no biography.

The diplomatic correspondence is the third and the most unusual. The letter to Puduhepa and the associated gift exchange records place her in an interstate context and demonstrate that the position had an external dimension.

Archaeological material from the tomb is the fourth: the sarcophagus fragments, shabtis, amulets, sandals, and the disputed human remains. It is thin because the tomb was robbed.

### What we lack

There is no chronicle, no annal, and no narrative account of Nefertari's life written in antiquity. There is no letter written by her about anything personal. There is no record of her parentage, her birthplace, her age, her health, her opinions, or her relationships with anyone other than in the formulaic terms of monumental inscription. There is no contemporary description of her character, and the epithets applied to her are the standard epithets applied to queens.

There is also no ancient assessment of her. Later Egyptian tradition did not produce a body of stories about Nefertari as it did about certain kings, and classical authors, who preserved a good deal of Egyptian material in distorted form, do not discuss her.

### Why this matters for reading any modern account

The gap between the surviving evidence and the biographies that circulate is enormous, and it is filled by inference, convention, and invention. When a modern account describes Nefertari's feelings, her personality, her relationship with her husband, her reaction to events, or her appearance, that account is not drawing on a source. It is supplying material.

The methodological rule that follows is worth carrying beyond this article. For any figure from the ancient world, ask what kind of source a claim would have to rest on, and then ask whether such a source exists. For Nefertari, claims about position, titles, monuments, and formal role can rest on inscriptions that exist. Claims about interiority cannot rest on anything, because nothing of that kind survives.

## The Question of Her Appearance

Few questions about Nefertari are asked more often, and few have a less satisfying answer.

### Can we know what Nefertari looked like?

Nothing is known about Nefertari's actual appearance. Every surviving image of her follows Egyptian artistic convention, which rendered royal women according to a fixed idealized formula rather than as individual likenesses. The paintings in QV66 and the statuary at Abu Simbel show a type, not a face, and no securely identified remains permit reconstruction.

Understanding why requires understanding what Egyptian art was for. A royal image was not a record of how someone looked. It was a functional object: a body for the spirit to inhabit, a statement of status, a participant in ritual. Its job was to represent the person correctly according to the category they belonged to, and the categories had standard forms. A queen was rendered young, slender, with regular features, a particular treatment of the eye, and a conventional wig or crown, because that is what a queen looked like in the visual language of the period.

The convention was applied with such consistency that images of different women from the same period are often distinguishable only by the accompanying inscription. Remove the name and the identification frequently becomes impossible. This is not a failure of Egyptian art; it is a consequence of what Egyptian art was trying to do.

There are periods when this loosened. The Amarna period produced images with individualized features and unusual body proportions, and Middle Kingdom royal portraiture developed a distinctive treatment of aging faces. The Ramesside period was not one of those moments. Nineteenth Dynasty royal representation is smooth, idealized, and highly standardized, which is precisely the style visible in Nefertari's images.

The description of her as beautiful therefore rests on the beauty of the artworks rather than on any information about the woman. The paintings in QV66 are exquisite. What they show is an ideal, executed by superb craftsmen, and the ideal is the same ideal applied to other queens of the period.

The disputed remains do not help. The mummified knees recovered from the tomb have been examined and are consistent with an adult woman, and estimates of stature have been proposed from them, but the identification itself is unconfirmed and inferences about appearance built on an unconfirmed identification carry all the uncertainty of the identification plus their own.

## The Legend and What It Gets Wrong

Nefertari has attracted a body of popular material that runs well beyond the evidence, and separating the layers is a necessary part of any serious treatment.

### Does the record show affection or calculation?

The claim rests on inscriptions in which the king describes her in affectionate terms and on the extraordinary scale of the monuments dedicated to her, particularly the Small Temple at Abu Simbel. Whether those honors reflect private feeling or political and ritual calculation cannot be determined, since Egyptian royal inscriptions record formal statements rather than personal sentiment.

The inscriptions most often quoted in support of the romantic account are genuine, and they are also conventional. Egyptian royal texts operated within a repertoire of formulae, and expressions of a king's regard for his queen belong to that repertoire. A phrase describing a queen as the one for whom the sun rises is a formal statement of her position within a cosmological scheme in which the king is associated with the solar cycle. Reading it as a private declaration transplants a modern genre of expression into a context that had no such genre.

None of this means the relationship was cold. It means the texts are not evidence about it either way. A king who felt nothing would have used the same formulae, because the formulae were what one used.

### The persistent errors

The confusion with Nefertiti is the most widespread and the most easily corrected. Different woman, different dynasty, different century, different religious world.

The claim that Nefertari was a princess or a foreign royal is unsupported. Her titulary omits King's Daughter, and no source names her parents.

The claim that she ruled, co-ruled, or exercised political authority in her own right has no basis. She held a consort's position with religious functions. Nothing in the record suggests governing power, and the Egyptian system did not provide for it outside the exceptional cases where a woman took the kingship itself, as the treatment of [Hatshepsut and how a woman became pharaoh](/2003/06/01/hatshepsut-female-pharaoh-of-egypt/) sets out.

The claim that her tomb was found intact, or that treasures comparable to Tutankhamun's were recovered from it, is false. QV66 was robbed in antiquity, and the surviving objects amount to fragments.

The claim that her mummy has been identified is overstated. A possible identification exists and remains unconfirmed.

The claim that she died in childbirth, or of a specific illness, or at a specific age, appears in various accounts and rests on nothing. The year of her death is inferred from her disappearance from the monuments. The cause is unknown.

The claim that Abu Simbel's Small Temple was built as a memorial after her death is doubtful. The evidence indicates a dedication to a living queen, though whether the work was completed before or after she died is not firmly settled.

### Why the legend took hold

It is worth asking why Nefertari in particular attracted this material, because the answer illuminates something about how ancient figures acquire modern reputations.

Three factors combined. The first is the visual power of the tomb, whose paintings are genuinely beautiful and which photograph extremely well, giving her a modern presence that better-documented queens lack. The second is the temple, which is an unusual and quantifiable honor and which invites explanation, and the readiest available explanation in the modern imagination is romantic love. The third is the near-total absence of biographical information, which leaves a vacuum. Figures about whom a great deal is known accumulate qualifications. Figures about whom almost nothing is known accumulate stories.

The result is a reputation that is disproportionate to the evidence in content while being entirely proportionate to it in scale. Nefertari deserves attention. She does not deserve the specific attention she gets, which is directed at things the sources do not support and away from the things they do.

## Nefertari Among Egyptian Queens

Placing her in a comparative frame is the most useful way to assess the honored-queen thesis, because the claim is that her treatment was exceptional and exceptionality can only be judged against a field.

### Most honored, or most loved?

The evidence supports her as the most honored of his wives, which is a documented fact, rather than the most loved, which is not documented. She received a temple, equal-scale statuary, and an exceptional tomb, and no other wife of Ramesses II received comparable treatment. Isetnofret, his other principal queen, is far less prominent monumentally.

Comparison with Isetnofret is the sharpest available test, since the two women occupied similar positions at the same court. Isetnofret appears in the record, holds the appropriate titles, and is commemorated, but the volume and scale of her monumental presence do not approach Nefertari's. There is no temple for Isetnofret. There is no equal-height statuary. Her burial has not been identified with the certainty that attaches to QV66. On the measure of resources committed, Nefertari was treated differently.

The countervailing evidence, already noted, is that Isetnofret's son inherited the throne and her family supplied the subsequent dynasty. Whatever the basis of the difference in monumental treatment, it did not translate into dynastic outcome.

### Comparison across the New Kingdom

Set against queens of the wider period, Nefertari's honors are high but not unique in kind.

Ahmose-Nefertari, at the start of the Eighteenth Dynasty, received a posthumous cult that ran for centuries at Thebes, an honor Nefertari did not receive on the same scale. Tiye, principal wife of Amenhotep III, appears in colossal statuary at her husband's scale, corresponded with foreign courts, and had a temple dedicated to her in Nubia at Sedeinga, which makes her the closest structural parallel to Nefertari and arguably the precedent for her treatment. Nefertiti's prominence in Amarna art is of a different and disputed character, involving iconography that some scholars read as approaching royal status.

The comparison with Tiye is the important one and it should temper any claim of uniqueness. A Nubian temple for a Great Royal Wife had been done before, roughly a century earlier, by a king whose reign Ramesses II's builders knew well. Abu Simbel's Small Temple is the largest and most elaborate example of an established practice rather than an invention.

What survives better for Nefertari than for Tiye is the tomb, and that is partly an accident of preservation rather than a difference in original honor. Judging relative treatment across queens is complicated by the uneven survival of evidence, and honest comparison acknowledges that the queen with the best-preserved monument is not necessarily the queen who received the most.

### The verdict on exceptionality

The honored-queen thesis survives this comparison in a modified form. Nefertari received honors at the very top of the range available to an Egyptian queen, matching or exceeding the strongest precedents, and she received them across multiple categories at once: temple, statuary scale, tomb quality, diplomatic role, and ritual office. Few queens hold that full combination. The claim that she was uniquely honored is too strong. The claim that she was honored at the highest level the institution permitted is defensible and is what the evidence supports.

## Memory and Afterlife

What happened to Nefertari's memory after her death is a further test of how her honors are best understood.

Egyptian practice provided several mechanisms by which a person could be remembered: a mortuary cult with offerings, inclusion in later king lists or genealogies, a continuing presence in the decoration of monuments, and eventual absorption into local religious practice. Some royal figures received all of these, and a few, such as Ahmose-Nefertari and the deified architect Imhotep, became objects of popular veneration long after their deaths.

Nefertari received considerably less. Her name and image continued to be visible in the monuments where they had been carved, which is a passive form of survival rather than an active cult. There is no evidence of a substantial ongoing cult in her name comparable to those attached to certain earlier queens. Her daughter's assumption of the queenly role, and the subsequent rise of Isetnofret's family, meant that the court's institutional memory moved on within her husband's own lifetime.

This absence is a useful check on the temptation to read the Abu Simbel temple as evidence of an enduring devotion. The honors were concentrated in a period and did not generate a lasting tradition. What preserved Nefertari for the modern world was not Egyptian memory but Egyptian construction technique: a rock-cut temple that outlasted the culture that built it, and a sealed tomb whose plaster held its color in the dark for three thousand years until an Italian archaeologist opened the door.

## The Valley of the Queens and Where QV66 Sits Within It

Nefertari's tomb belongs to a necropolis with its own history, and understanding that setting clarifies both what her burial had in common with others and where it stood apart.

The Egyptians called the valley by a name meaning roughly the place of beauty, and it lies in a desert wadi on the Theban west bank, south of the larger valley used for royal male burials. Its use for high-status interments begins in the Eighteenth Dynasty, when it received burials of royal women and officials, and it becomes the principal burial ground for queens and royal children during the Nineteenth and Twentieth Dynasties. Several dozen tombs of varying scale and completeness have been recorded there, along with a considerable number of unfinished or undecorated shafts.

The valley's geology explains a good deal about its tombs. The limestone is of variable quality, and in many places it is interrupted by shale and marl layers that make sustained fine carving impractical. Tomb builders responded by plastering and painting, which is why the valley is known for painted rather than carved decoration. That solution had consequences for survival, since plaster is vulnerable to the salt movement described above in a way that carved stone is not.

Within the valley, QV66 stands out on several measures at once. Its decorated area is large. Its painting quality is at the highest level found there. Its plan is fully developed rather than truncated, with the complete sequence of descending stair, antechamber, side room, second stair, and pillared burial chamber. And its state of preservation, before conservation intervened, retained more original surface than most.

Other significant burials in the valley include those of Nineteenth and Twentieth Dynasty queens and of several sons of Ramesses III whose tombs carry distinctive decoration showing the king presenting the young princes to deities. Comparing QV66 against these establishes that Nefertari's tomb sits at the top of the range rather than in a category of its own, which is the same conclusion the temple comparison produced.

There is one further point about the valley worth registering. The distinction between the kings' valley and the queens' valley was not merely a matter of prestige. The two necropolises received different classes of funerary text and different decorative programs, reflecting a theological distinction between the king's afterlife, which involved a specific solar and Osirian trajectory reserved for the ruler, and the afterlife of everyone else, including queens, which followed the pattern set out in the funerary literature used by the elite generally. Nefertari's tomb is decorated with that general elite material, executed superbly. It is not decorated with royal texts, because those were not hers to use.

## How a Tomb Like QV66 Was Made

The process behind the paintings is worth reconstructing because it explains their quality and because it connects Nefertari's burial to a documented working community.

The workforce came from the specialist village on the Theban west bank whose inhabitants cut and decorated the royal tombs and whose written records survive in unusual quantity. These were state employees, housed together, organized into gangs with named foremen and scribes, and paid in rations delivered on a schedule. Their community produced an archive of ostraca and papyri covering work assignments, absences, disputes, supplies, and personal affairs, and it gives us a clearer picture of how Egyptian craftsmen actually lived than any other body of evidence from the ancient world.

The sequence of work began with cutting. Quarrymen advanced the tomb from the entrance inward, following a plan, removing chips in baskets, and shaping the chambers to their intended dimensions. This was the crude phase, and its output was a rough rock volume.

Preparation followed. Because the limestone was inadequate for carving, the walls received a plaster ground. The typical approach used a coarse underlayer to fill irregularities and level the surface, followed by a finer white layer to receive the paint. Getting this right mattered enormously, since the adhesion of the plaster to the rock determined how long the decoration would survive, and it was the failure of that adhesion three millennia later that necessitated the conservation campaign.

Layout came next. Draftsmen established the composition using grids and guidelines, working to the proportional canon that governed Egyptian figural art, in which the human body was laid out across a fixed number of squares with the placement of knee, waist, shoulder, and hairline determined by the system. This canon is why Egyptian figures are so consistent in proportion across centuries and across sites.

Outline drawing followed layout, and then painting. Colors were applied in a conventional order, with backgrounds and larger areas first and detail work last. Final outlining sharpened the forms after the color was down.

Each stage employed different specialists, and the archive from the workers' village shows the crews organized accordingly. The quality visible in QV66 reflects the assignment of the best available people to a high-priority project, and the fact that such an assignment could be made and sustained is itself evidence of the queen's standing.

## The Ramesside Court and the Position of Royal Women

Nefertari's career makes fuller sense against the structure of the court she belonged to.

The Nineteenth Dynasty inherited a royal household that had grown large and formally organized. A king maintained a principal wife, secondary wives, foreign wives received through diplomacy, and a substantial establishment of women attached to the palace with its own administration, staff, and economic resources. This establishment held land, produced textiles, and functioned as an economic unit as well as a residence.

Within that structure, the Great Royal Wife occupied a defined position with three components. The first was dynastic: her sons ranked first for the succession. The second was ritual: she participated in temple ceremony, held priestly titles, and functioned as the female counterpart in rites that required a divine pair. The third was representational: she appeared beside the king in official contexts as the visible female half of the institution of kingship.

None of these components amounted to political authority in the sense of issuing orders, controlling revenue, or commanding personnel outside her own establishment. The Egyptian state did not provide a queen with an office in the administration. What she had was proximity, standing, and ritual function, and the influence available through those channels was real but informal and therefore undocumented.

This is the frame within which the honored-queen thesis should be read. Nefertari's monuments do not indicate that she exercised unusual power, because the monuments record honor rather than authority, and Egyptian practice honored queens for occupying their position well rather than for governing. The question of what informal influence royal women actually exercised across the New Kingdom is a genuine historical problem that the specialist treatment of the subject takes up, and the honest answer is that the evidence rarely permits a confident conclusion for any individual woman.

There is a further consideration specific to the Ramesside period. The Nineteenth Dynasty came to power from military rather than royal origins, following the disruption of the Amarna period and the extinction of the Eighteenth Dynasty line. A family in that position had a legitimacy problem, and the standard Egyptian response to a legitimacy problem was display: monuments, titles, and the visible performance of proper kingship. Elevating the Great Royal Wife with conspicuous honors fits that program. A king demonstrating that his house was properly royal had reason to make his queen conspicuously queenly.

That reading does not diminish Nefertari. It relocates the explanation for her monuments from the private sphere to the political one, which is where the evidence for Egyptian royal behavior generally sits, and it accounts for the pattern without requiring any assumption about what anyone felt.

## The Honest Verdict

Nefertari is worth studying, and the reasons she is worth studying are not the reasons she is usually studied.

The case for her importance rests on four things that the evidence establishes. She held the senior consort position with a full set of titles including a genuine priestly office in the cult of Amun. She received monumental honors at the highest level available to an Egyptian queen, including a temple bearing her name in a province where Egyptian display was a state priority, and statuary at a scale the artistic convention did not normally permit. She participated in the formal apparatus of interstate diplomacy, with correspondence in her name preserved in a foreign archive. And she was buried in a tomb whose decoration represents the upper limit of what the Egyptian painting tradition achieved.

The case against overstating her rests on four further things. Her honors, though at the top of the range, follow precedents set for earlier queens and are not unique in kind. She exercised no documented political authority. Her sons did not inherit, and the dynasty continued through her co-wife's line. And she disappears from the record roughly forty years before her husband's death, after which the court honored others in her place.

Holding both sets together produces the accurate picture: a queen honored to the maximum degree her position allowed, in a system where that degree of honor did not translate into power or dynastic continuity, whose modern fame rests disproportionately on the accidental survival of a painted tomb.

The romance should be set aside, not because the relationship was necessarily cold but because there is no evidence about it in either direction, and a biography built on an unsupported claim is weaker than one built on a narrower foundation of things that can be shown. What can be shown is substantial. A queen with her own temple, her own diplomatic correspondence, her own priestly office, and one of the finest painted tombs in Egypt does not need a love story to justify the attention.

It is worth adding where Nefertari sits relative to the two Egyptian women whose names the general reader is likeliest to recognize. Cleopatra VII ruled a kingdom, commanded armies, conducted her own foreign policy, and left a body of Greek and Roman testimony about her actions and her speech. Hatshepsut took the kingship itself, adopted royal titulary and iconography, ran a major building program in her own name, and dispatched trading expeditions recorded on her own temple walls. Both were political actors whose decisions can be examined. Nefertari was not, and treating her as though she belonged in that category distorts what she was.

What she offers instead is a clear view of an institution. Cleopatra and Hatshepsut are exceptions who illuminate the limits of the Egyptian system by breaking them. Nefertari illuminates the system by occupying its ordinary female position at maximum extension, which shows what that position could carry when a reign chose to load it fully. For understanding how Egyptian kingship distributed honor, ritual function, and dynastic expectation between a king and his consort, the ordinary case pushed to its limit is more informative than the exception.

Her tomb was cut, plastered, painted, sealed, robbed, forgotten, opened, photographed, damaged by salt, stabilized by conservators, and closed again to all but a controlled trickle of visitors. Her temple was carved into a cliff, buried in sand, cleared, sawn into blocks, lifted up a hillside, and rebuilt inside a concrete shell. Neither object has had a quiet three thousand years, and neither was built to explain the woman whose name it carries. That both survive at all is the reason we can say anything about her, and the reason to say it carefully.

## The Funerary Program of QV66 and What It Assumes

The decoration of Nefertari's tomb is a coherent theological argument, and following it through clarifies both what the Egyptians expected of the afterlife and what they thought a queen needed in order to reach it.

The governing text is the compilation Egyptologists call the Book of the Dead, a body of spells that developed out of earlier funerary literature and that was copied onto papyri, coffins, and tomb walls throughout the New Kingdom. The compilation was not a fixed book. Individual copies drew selectively from a large repertoire, and the selection made for a particular burial reflects choices about what that person would need.

The program in QV66 tracks a journey. The deceased leaves the world of the living, encounters deities who receive and guide her, passes a sequence of gates each with its own guardian whose name must be known, undergoes the judgment in which the heart is weighed, and arrives at a state of continued existence in the presence of Osiris. Along the way she must be able to identify entities correctly, speak the right formulae, and demonstrate that she is who she claims to be.

Two features of this scheme are worth drawing out because they are frequently misunderstood.

The first is that knowledge, not virtue, does most of the work. The spells function as information: the names of gates, the identities of guardians, the correct declarations. A well-equipped burial supplied the deceased with what she needed to know. This is why the texts are written on the walls and why their accuracy mattered. The moral element exists, most visibly in the judgment scene where the heart is weighed against the feather representing right order, but it operates alongside a body of practical instruction rather than replacing it.

The second is that the images are functional rather than illustrative. A painted scene showing Nefertari receiving offerings was understood to make the offerings effective. The picture does not depict something that happened; it causes something to happen. This is why the quality of execution mattered enough to justify assigning the best available painters, and why damage to a figure was a serious problem rather than an aesthetic loss.

The senet composition fits this scheme precisely. The game's board and the passage of pieces across it had acquired an afterlife reading in which movement toward the final square represented successful transition. Showing the queen at the board, winning, is an assertion of successful passage rendered in the visual idiom of a familiar game. It belongs with the gates and the guardians rather than with any record of how she spent her time.

What the whole program assumes about Nefertari is that she required the same equipment as any high-status Egyptian. She is not given royal funerary texts, which were reserved for the king and which describe a different trajectory involving the solar cycle. She is given the elite repertoire, executed at the highest available standard. That combination is the exact statement her monuments make everywhere else: the ordinary position, filled to its outer limit.

## What a Careful Reader Should Take Away

Nefertari repays study as a methodological exercise as much as a biographical one, and the transferable lessons are worth stating directly.

Distinguish categories of evidence. Titles, monuments, and archived documents are contemporary and official. Images are evidence about convention. Later accounts and modern narratives are evidence about their own periods. Mixing these categories produces the confident biography that circulates about Nefertari and that the sources cannot support.

Ask what would have to survive. When a claim is made about an ancient figure, identify the type of source it would require and ask whether Egyptian society produced such sources and whether they would have lasted. Claims about a queen's feelings fail both tests.

Distinguish honor from power. Egyptian monumental prominence tracked ritual and dynastic position rather than governing authority, and the two frequently diverged. Nefertari received the honors while Isetnofret's line received the throne.

Watch for survival bias. The best-preserved monument is not the same as the greatest original honor, and rankings built on surviving evidence should be stated as rankings within the surviving sample.

Treat conventional language as conventional. Epithets, formulae, and standard phrases in royal inscriptions describe positions within a system. They are not personal testimony, and reading them as such imports a modern genre into a context that lacked it.

Applied together, these produce a Nefertari who is smaller than the legend and more interesting than the summary: a woman whose life is unrecoverable, whose position is well documented, whose monuments are exceptional, and whose modern reputation is a construction with a traceable history.

## The Court After Nefertari

What filled the space Nefertari left is part of her story, because it shows how quickly and how completely the position could be refilled.

Her daughter Meritamen took on the queenly role first, and statuary and inscriptions record her in that capacity. Bintanath, a daughter of Isetnofret, likewise appears with queenly status. The elevation of royal daughters after the death of the principal wives is documented across the later decades of the reign, and the debate over how to interpret it has already been noted.

The more consequential development arrived from abroad. Roughly a decade after the peace settlement with Hatti, the two courts concluded a dynastic marriage, and a Hittite princess traveled to Egypt to become a wife of Ramesses II. The event was commemorated on stelae set up at several sites including Abu Simbel, and the accompanying text describes the journey, the reception, and the king's satisfaction in the elaborate terms Egyptian royal inscriptions reserved for diplomatic triumphs. The princess received an Egyptian name and the status of a Great Royal Wife. A second Hittite marriage followed later in the reign.

The framing of these events in the Egyptian record is instructive about how the state presented foreign relations. The arrival of a Hittite princess is narrated as a foreign ruler submitting a daughter to Egypt, which converts a negotiated exchange between equals into a demonstration of Egyptian superiority. Hittite records of the same relationship, including the correspondence surrounding the arrangements, show a considerably more balanced negotiation involving discussion of dowry, status, and the treatment the princess could expect. The gap between the two accounts is a good illustration of why Egyptian monumental texts are reliable about what happened and unreliable about what it meant.

For Nefertari, the significance is straightforward. The position she had held was, within roughly a decade of her death, occupied by a foreign princess whose arrival was celebrated as a political achievement, and the honors that followed attached to the new circumstances rather than to her memory. The Abu Simbel stela commemorating the marriage stands at the site of the temple dedicated to her, which compresses the whole point into a single location.

There is no indication of any deliberate diminishment. Her temple was not defaced, her name was not erased, and her tomb was not disturbed by the state. What happened was ordinary institutional replacement: a role became vacant and was filled, repeatedly, over the four decades her husband continued to reign. The absence of any hostile action is worth noting precisely because Egyptian history contains plenty of examples where a predecessor's memory was actively attacked. Nefertari's was left alone and moved past.

## How to Weigh the Honored-Queen Thesis

A named claim is only useful if it can be tested, so it is worth setting out what would support the honored-queen thesis and what would count against it.

The thesis holds that Nefertari's importance is established by the measurable scale of monumental honor she received, and that this measurable record is a sounder foundation for assessing her than any inference about the private relationship between her and her husband.

Four kinds of evidence support it. The first is expenditure that can be quantified in kind if not in figures: a rock-cut temple with ten-meter colossi and a decorated interior represents years of skilled labor allocated by the state. The second is deviation from convention, since the equal-height statuary at Abu Simbel breaks a proportional rule that Egyptian art otherwise applied with great consistency, and deliberate rule-breaking in a regulated system is a strong signal. The third is breadth across categories, since she holds not one honor but a full set: temple, statuary, priestly office, tomb quality, and diplomatic participation. The fourth is contrast with a control case, since Isetnofret occupied a structurally similar position at the same court and received markedly less.

Three kinds of evidence would weaken it. Discovery of comparable monumental honors for other wives of Ramesses II would undercut the contrast. Evidence that the Small Temple was principally a Hathor foundation to which the queen was attached secondarily, rather than a joint dedication in which she is named as a principal, would reduce the force of the temple argument. And demonstration that equal-scale representation of queens was more common in this period than the surviving sample suggests would weaken the deviation argument. None of these has materialized, but each is the kind of finding that would require revising the claim.

One further consideration limits how strongly the thesis can be stated. Survival is uneven, and the queens whose monuments happened to be built in durable materials in dry places are overrepresented in the record. Nefertari's Nubian temple was cut into rock and her tomb was sealed in a desert valley. A queen honored equally in mudbrick in the delta would leave nothing. The thesis is therefore best stated as a claim about the top of the surviving range rather than about the top of the actual range, which is a distinction that applies to a great deal of Egyptian history and is worth carrying elsewhere.

Stated with those qualifications, the thesis holds. Nefertari received honors at or near the maximum the institution of Great Royal Wife could carry, distributed across every category in which such honor could be expressed, and that fact is documented rather than inferred. It is a better foundation for her reputation than a love story that no source records.

## Reading Egyptian Queens: The Evidence Problem in General

Nefertari is a good case for thinking about a wider difficulty, since the obstacles her biography runs into are the obstacles that block almost every attempt to write about an Egyptian woman.

The first obstacle is that Egyptian written output was overwhelmingly institutional. The texts that survive in quantity are monumental inscriptions, administrative records, funerary literature, and religious composition. Personal writing exists, most abundantly in the letters and notes from the craftsmen's community on the Theban west bank, but it survives by accident and concerns people well below royal rank. There was no Egyptian genre of biography in the modern sense, and no Egyptian equivalent of the memoir, the private diary, or the intimate letter between spouses that later periods supply.

The second obstacle is that the institutional record was produced by and about the state, and the state was organized around the king. A queen enters that record when she has a function within it: as consort, as mother of an heir, as participant in ritual, as a party in diplomatic exchange. She does not enter it as a person with a history. The result is that we can often say what a queen's position was and almost never say what she did with it.

The third obstacle is that the visual record is conventionalized to the point of anonymity. This has already been discussed for Nefertari's appearance, and it generalizes. Images tell us how a queen was supposed to be represented, which is information about the institution rather than about the individual.

The fourth obstacle is survival bias, and it is the one most often forgotten. What we have is what happened to last, and what lasted was disproportionately stone in dry places. A queen commemorated in a rock-cut Nubian temple has better odds than a queen commemorated in a mudbrick shrine in the delta, where the water table and the reuse of building material have destroyed nearly everything. Judging relative importance from relative survival is therefore treacherous, and any statement that one queen was more honored than another needs to be checked against the question of what would have survived in each case.

Applied to Nefertari, these four obstacles explain the exact shape of her file. We know her titles because titles were inscribed. We know her monuments because monuments were durable. We know about the letter because a foreign palace burned and baked its tablets. We know nothing about her life because the categories of evidence that would record such things were not produced in her society, and would not have survived if they had been.

The discipline this imposes is worth stating as a rule. For an Egyptian royal woman, treat title sets, monuments, and archival documents as evidence; treat images as evidence about convention; and treat every statement about personality, emotion, motive, or private relationship as an assertion requiring a source that, in nearly all cases, does not exist.

## The God's Wife of Amun and What the Office Meant

One of Nefertari's titles repays a closer look, because the office it names had a history before her and a much larger future after her.

The position of God's Wife of Amun appears in the early New Kingdom and was held by prominent royal women, including Ahmose-Nefertari at the start of the Eighteenth Dynasty and Hatshepsut before she took the kingship. It carried participation in the Theban cult of Amun, an endowment supporting the office, and a defined ritual role, and it was significant enough that its holders are recorded exercising it.

The theological content of the office rested on the doctrine of divine birth, in which the god Amun was understood to father the king through the queen. A woman designated as the god's wife occupied a position in that scheme, and the office therefore linked the royal household directly to the most powerful temple establishment in Egypt.

What happened to the office later illustrates how much institutional weight it could carry. During the Third Intermediate Period, when central authority fragmented and Thebes operated with substantial independence, the office of God's Wife was held by royal daughters who functioned as the effective rulers of Upper Egypt, controlled enormous temple estates, adopted successors to maintain the line, and were commemorated with the apparatus of rulership. That development lay centuries ahead of Nefertari and belongs to the account of [the Third Intermediate Period and Egypt's long fragmentation](/2005/11/01/third-intermediate-period-egypt/), but it demonstrates that the title she held was attached to a genuine institution rather than being an honorific.

For Nefertari specifically, the title indicates a real ritual function in the Amun cult during a period when that cult was the wealthiest religious establishment in the country. It does not indicate that she wielded the kind of power her distant successors would, since the political conditions that made the office powerful had not yet arrived. What it does indicate is that her position had a religious dimension independent of her marriage, exercised in an institution with its own resources and its own standing against the crown.

## Nefertari in the Modern Imagination

The Nefertari most readers encounter was assembled in the twentieth century, and tracing how gives some perspective on why the legend has the shape it does.

Before 1904 she was a name in inscriptions, known to specialists, associated with a Nubian temple that European travelers had visited since the early nineteenth century. Schiaparelli's opening of QV66 changed that by supplying images. The paintings were photographed, published, and reproduced, and they entered general circulation at a moment when Egyptology commanded broad public attention and when reproduction technology was making high-quality color illustration widely available.

The images did something specific to her reputation. Egyptian art usually reaches a general audience as stone: statues, reliefs, and monumental architecture, all of it hard, monochrome, and imposing. QV66 supplied color, delicacy, and a human-scaled intimacy that the stone monuments do not offer. A queen presented through those paintings reads differently from a queen presented through a colossus, and the difference shaped how she was written about.

The conservation campaign of the late twentieth century amplified the effect. It generated a large body of technical publication, extensive new photography, exhibitions, and public interest, and it framed the tomb as a treasure requiring rescue, which is a framing that reliably increases attention. The restricted access that followed added scarcity to the picture.

Meanwhile the romantic reading consolidated. Popular writing, documentary treatment, and the general appetite for a love story attached to an ancient monument settled on the Small Temple as proof of devotion, and the account propagated through repetition until it functioned as common knowledge. The claim was never argued from evidence; it was inferred from expenditure and then transmitted.

None of this is a criticism of anyone in particular, and the underlying material is genuine. The point is that the modern Nefertari is a construction with a traceable history, built from a real temple and a real tomb plus a layer of interpretation that arrived long afterward and that the ancient sources do not support. Distinguishing the layers is the whole task of reading her carefully.

## What Remains Genuinely Uncertain

Correcting errors is only half the job. Being clear about open questions is the other half, because a reader who leaves thinking everything is settled has been misled in a different direction.

Her origins are unknown. The omission of King's Daughter from her titulary points to non-royal birth, but her family, birthplace, and background are undocumented.

The exact number and identity of her children is debated. Attributions rest on inscriptions and statue groups that are not always unambiguous, and reference works differ on individual assignments.

The year of her death is inferred rather than recorded, and the cause is entirely unknown.

The identification of the human remains from QV66 is unconfirmed and may never be resolved.

Whether the Small Temple at Abu Simbel was completed before or after her death is not firmly settled, though the dedication appears to have been conceived while she held her position.

Her personal involvement in the correspondence sent in her name cannot be determined, and the same limitation applies to royal letters generally.

The interpretation of Ramesses II's later elevation of his daughters to queenly status, which followed Nefertari's death, remains contested among Egyptologists, with readings ranging from marriage in the ordinary sense to the filling of a required ritual position.

Holding these open is not a failure of scholarship. A reader who can state both what the evidence establishes about Nefertari and where it runs out understands her better than a reader who has memorized a longer list of assertions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Queen Nefertari?

Nefertari Merytmut was the Great Royal Wife of Ramesses II, holding the senior consort position from the beginning of his reign around 1279 BCE until her death roughly twenty-four or twenty-five years later. Her parentage is unknown and her titulary omits King's Daughter, which points to noble rather than royal birth. She held an extensive set of titles including Lady of the Two Lands, Hereditary Princess, and God's Wife, the last being a genuine priestly office in the cult of Amun at Thebes. She is best known for the Small Temple dedicated to her at Abu Simbel and for her painted tomb in the Valley of the Queens.

### Q: Why was Nefertari so beloved by Ramesses II?

The claim rests on affectionate phrasing in royal inscriptions and on the exceptional scale of monuments dedicated to her, above all the Small Temple at Abu Simbel. Both are genuine, and neither settles the question. Egyptian royal texts operated within a repertoire of conventional formulae, and expressions of a king's regard for his queen belong to that repertoire rather than constituting private declarations. A king with a political need to display an idealized royal couple in a frontier province had reasons to build the temple that have nothing to do with feeling. The honors are documented; the emotion behind them is not recoverable from any surviving source.

### Q: What is Nefertari's tomb like?

QV66 descends by a stairway into an antechamber with an adjoining side room, then by a second stairway into a pillared burial chamber with a sunken floor and additional storage chambers opening off it. Because the local limestone is friable and interrupted by shale seams, the walls were covered with a plaster ground and painted rather than carved in relief. The decoration covers a large area and shows Nefertari being received and guided by deities, making offerings, passing the gates of the underworld, and playing the board game senet. Ceilings are painted deep blue with golden stars.

### Q: Why is Nefertari's tomb famous?

Its reputation rests on the quality and preservation of its painting rather than on its contents, which robbers stripped in antiquity. Three factors combined to produce surfaces that rank among the finest surviving from ancient Egypt: a large decorated area, painters working at the top of the state workshop tradition with confident linework and unusually sophisticated tonal modeling, and burial conditions that were sealed, dark, and dry for most of three thousand years. A major conservation campaign in the late twentieth century added to its fame by generating extensive photography and publication, since archaeological renown is partly a function of documentation.

### Q: Was Nefertari the favorite wife of Ramesses II?

The evidence supports her as the most honored of his wives rather than the most loved, since the first is documented and the second is not. Isetnofret, his other principal queen, held comparable rank at the same court but received nothing approaching Nefertari's monumental treatment: no temple, no equal-height statuary, no comparably identified tomb. On the measure of resources committed, the difference is clear. The complication is that Isetnofret's son Merenptah inherited the throne and her family supplied the subsequent dynasty, so whatever produced the difference in honors did not translate into dynastic outcome.

### Q: What did Nefertari look like?

Nothing is known about her actual appearance. Every surviving image follows Egyptian artistic convention, which rendered royal women according to a fixed idealized formula rather than as individual likenesses, and Nineteenth Dynasty royal representation was particularly smooth and standardized. Images of different women from this period are frequently distinguishable only by the accompanying inscription. The description of Nefertari as beautiful therefore reflects the quality of the artworks rather than information about the woman. The disputed human remains from her tomb do not help, since the identification is unconfirmed and any inference about appearance inherits that uncertainty.

### Q: Did Nefertari have her own temple?

Yes. The Small Temple at Abu Simbel in Nubia was dedicated jointly to the goddess Hathor of Ibshek and to Nefertari, and dedicatory inscriptions name her explicitly in connection with the foundation. Its facade carries six standing colossi roughly ten meters tall, four representing Ramesses II and two representing the queen, with her figures wearing the Hathoric crown of cow horns, sun disk, and plumes. Inside, six pillars carry Hathor-headed capitals and the sanctuary holds the goddess in her cow form emerging from the rock. A Nubian temple for a Great Royal Wife had precedent under Amenhotep III, so the honor is at the top of the established range rather than unprecedented.

### Q: When did Nefertari die?

She appears to have died around the twenty-fourth or twenty-fifth year of Ramesses II's reign, placing it near 1255 BCE, roughly a third of the way into a reign of about sixty-six years. No document records the year, and the dating is inferred from a clear pattern: she is consistently present in monuments of the earlier reign and absent thereafter, with her daughter Meritamen taking on the queenly role in her place. Her age at death is unknown because her birth year is unknown, and the cause of death is likewise undocumented despite various confident claims in popular accounts.

### Q: Where is Nefertari buried?

Nefertari was buried in the Valley of the Queens on the Theban west bank, in the tomb designated QV66. The valley lies in a desert wadi south of the larger necropolis used for royal male burials and served as the principal burial ground for queens and royal children through the Nineteenth and Twentieth Dynasties. Its limestone is of variable quality and interrupted by shale layers, which is why tombs there are characteristically plastered and painted rather than carved. QV66 stands out within the valley for the size of its decorated area, the quality of its painting, and the completeness of its plan.

### Q: What titles did Nefertari hold?

Her titles include Great Royal Wife, Lady of the Two Lands, Mistress of Upper and Lower Egypt, Hereditary Princess, Lady of All Lands, Great of Praises, Sweet of Love, and God's Wife. Great Royal Wife identifies her as senior consort with precedence over all other women of the household. Lady of the Two Lands associates her with the unified kingdom using formulae paralleling royal titulary. Hereditary Princess denotes elevated rank rather than royal birth. God's Wife designates a genuine priestly office in the Amun cult at Thebes. Great of Praises and Sweet of Love are conventional epithets applied to queens as a class rather than personal characterizations.

### Q: Who were Nefertari's children?

She is credited with roughly four sons and two daughters, though some attributions remain debated. The sons include Amunherkhepeshef, the firstborn royal son and designated heir through the earlier decades of the reign, Pareherwenemef, Meryatum who became High Priest of Re at Heliopolis, and Meryre. The daughters include Meritamen, who assumed the position of Great Royal Wife after her mother's death, and Henuttawy. None of her sons succeeded to the throne. Ramesses II outlived them all across a reign of about sixty-six years, and the crown eventually passed to Merenptah, the thirteenth son, born to Isetnofret.

### Q: Was Nefertari related to Ay or the Amarna royal family?

No connection is established. A knob or fragment bearing the cartouche of Ay, the short-reigned king who followed Tutankhamun, was recovered among objects from her tomb, and some writers have built a family link on it. The inference is weak, since objects bearing royal names circulated widely as gifts, heirlooms, and reused items, and an object turning up in a tomb cut two generations later establishes only that the object reached that tomb. Her titulary omits King's Daughter, which points instead toward noble rather than royal birth, and no inscription names either of her parents.

### Q: How was Nefertari's tomb discovered?

The Italian archaeologist Ernesto Schiaparelli located and entered QV66 in 1904 while directing the Italian Archaeological Mission in Egypt. He found a burial that had been thoroughly robbed in antiquity, with essentially nothing portable remaining. What the robbers could not remove was the painted decoration, which had survived in extraordinary condition. The recovered objects amounted to fragments of a pink granite sarcophagus lid, shabti figures, djed pillar amulets, pieces of wooden boxes, a pair of sandals, and a set of mummified knees whose identification as the queen's remains is considered possible but has never been confirmed.

### Q: What do the paintings in Nefertari's tomb show?

The walls carry scenes drawn from the funerary literature known as the Book of the Dead, showing the queen received and guided by deities including Osiris, Isis, Hathor, Anubis, Thoth, Neith, and Selket, making offerings, and passing the gates and guardians of the underworld. Ceilings are deep blue with golden stars. The best-known composition shows Nefertari playing senet against an unseen opponent, which is not a domestic scene: by the New Kingdom the game carried a symbolic reading in which a piece's passage across the board represented the deceased's passage through the trials of the afterlife toward rebirth.

### Q: Why is Nefertari shown the same height as Ramesses II?

Egyptian art used hierarchic scale as a strict grammar of rank, applied with consistency across two and a half millennia, in which kings tower over officials and queens stand at the king's shoulder or knee. At Abu Simbel, Nefertari's colossi match her husband's in height. Within so disciplined a coding scheme, that is a deliberate statement rather than artistic license. It does not claim she was king, and the iconography is careful on this point: her crown is Hathoric, marking divine association rather than royal authority. What it claims is that she stood at a level the visual system did not normally permit.

### Q: Did Nefertari play any diplomatic role?

Yes. A letter sent in her name to Puduhepa, queen of the Hittites, survives among the cuneiform tablets recovered from the Hittite capital, together with related correspondence and records of gift exchange. It followed the peace settlement between Egypt and Hatti and uses the standard diplomatic conventions of the period. Queen-to-queen correspondence was a recognized channel in Late Bronze Age diplomacy running parallel to the king-to-king channel. What the tablets establish is that the position of Great Royal Wife carried a recognized external face; whether Nefertari set the content herself or lent her name to a scribal product cannot be determined.

### Q: What happened to Nefertari's mummy?

No securely identified mummy survives. QV66 was robbed in antiquity and the burial disturbed, and the human remains recovered amount to a pair of mummified knees whose identification as Nefertari is regarded as possible but unconfirmed. Unlike many New Kingdom kings, including Ramesses II himself, she was not among the bodies that priests gathered into the royal caches at the close of the New Kingdom, since those operations were organized around the kings. Accounts stating that her mummy has been identified are running ahead of the evidence, and inferences about her health, stature, or age built on that identification inherit its uncertainty.
