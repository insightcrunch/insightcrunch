The Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 answers a question that American law had fumbled for half a century: which paid efforts to influence the federal government must be disclosed to the public, and by whom. The statute's answer is built out of definitions rather than prohibitions. It forbids almost nothing. Instead it identifies a class of professional advocates, requires them to register with the two administrative officers of Congress, and obliges them to report, client by client, the issues they pressed, the institutions they approached, and the money the work involved. Everything in the regime depends on where the definitional lines fall, and the most consequential line is a proportion: an individual counts as a lobbyist only when lobbying activities reach at least a fifth of the time that individual spends serving a particular client. A reader who understands that threshold, and the registration and reporting machinery built around it, understands both what the disclosure system shows and what it structurally cannot show.

![The Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, the statute that defined federal lobbying registration by a time threshold and built the modern disclosure record](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The formal identity is exact. The short title is the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, Public Law 104-65, printed at 109 Stat. 691 and codified at title 2 of the United States Code, sections 1601 to 1614. The enacted bill was S. 1060 of the 104th Congress. President Bill Clinton signed it on December 19, 1995, and section 24 of the act set its effective date at January 1, 1996. The statute repealed the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946, the narrower registration law it replaced, and it was substantially amended once, by the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007, Public Law 110-81, which moved reporting to a quarterly calendar, added a semiannual report on political contributions, raised the penalties, and assigned the Government Accountability Office an annual audit of compliance. As a statute profile, this article carries the whole subject in one place: the predecessor and its judicial narrowing, the definitions and the threshold they create, the registration and reporting system, the amendments, the enforcement record, and the separate registration regime for agents of foreign principals with which this act is persistently confused. ## The Disclosure Problem the Statute Answered

Congress framed the problem behind the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 as a problem of visibility. In the account set out in section 2 of the statute, codified at title 2, United States Code, section 1601, the public lacked a reliable way to learn who had been paid to influence federal decisions, on whose behalf that paid work had been performed, and to what extent the effort had been carried on. The findings tied that absence of information to a judgment about confidence. Congress found that effective public disclosure of the identity and extent of efforts by paid lobbyists to influence the federal government would increase public confidence in the integrity of government. Paid influence operated through channels that the disclosure system failed to map in a dependable form, and the failure itself had consequences for trust in the institutions receiving that influence.

The perception described by Congress in 1995 took a specific shape. Congress treated paid lobbying as a lawful feature of representative government, since petitions, information, and advocacy directed toward legislators formed part of the process by which bills were considered. The difficulty identified in 1995 did not concern the existence of advocacy. The difficulty concerned its obscurity. A citizen reading the public record could follow a bill through introduction, committee action, floor debate, and enactment, yet that same record revealed far less about the paid representatives who had sought to shape the bill between those formal steps. Where paid efforts remained out of view, suspicion filled the space that records might otherwise have occupied, and even routine representation could be read as evidence of a hidden transaction.

That concern explains why the statute took disclosure, rather than prohibition, as its central remedy. The findings in section 2 did not declare paid lobbying improper. Instead, Congress reasoned from disclosure to confidence, holding that a system able to show the identity of paid lobbyists and the extent of their efforts would support public confidence in the integrity of government. The remedy presupposed that openness could accomplish work that a ban could not attempt without colliding with rights of petition and speech. A registrant under the scheme that followed had to name a client and report the issues pressed and the institutions contacted. Identity answered the question of who had been hired. The extent of the effort, measured through income, expenses, issues, and contacts, answered the question of scale.

Before a unified scheme could be built, Congress confronted a legal landscape that had grown by accumulation rather than by design. The Congressional Research Service counted at least six different statutes carrying lobbying registration or disclosure standards before 1995. Registration and disclosure duties did not reside in a single title administered under a single set of definitions. Separate enactments had attached separate standards to separate categories of activity over a span of decades, and a person seeking to comply, or a reader seeking to follow the filings, faced several regimes rather than one. The 1995 act replaced that accumulation with one uniform scheme, a structural choice that reflected the diagnosis in the findings. A patchwork could not supply clear guidance if each of its parts defined the regulated activity in a different way.

Fragmentation carried practical costs. A prospective registrant must decide whether an activity counts as lobbying, which office receives a filing, and what a report must contain. When several statutes speak to overlapping conduct with differing language, each judgment grows harder, and inconsistent filings follow even where no filer intends concealment. The Congressional Research Service count therefore described a system in which the location of a disclosure duty could be as uncertain as the content of the duty itself.

The patchwork also had a history of failed repair. A stronger reform bill passed the Senate in 1976, and that measure died in the House. The bare sequence matters to the story Congress inherited in 1995, because the 1976 episode demonstrated that recognition of the problem had predated the 1995 enactment by roughly two decades without producing a replacement statute. The Senate had approved a stronger approach, bicameral agreement had not followed, and the older framework had remained in force by default. Between 1976 and 1995, according to the chronology preserved in Congressional Research Service accounts, the existing statutes continued to govern registration and disclosure even as Congress had already recorded a judgment that the existing arrangement warranted strengthening. The LDA arrived, in that sense, as the completion of an interrupted legislative project.

Congress wrote its most direct assessment of the older arrangement into the text of the statute itself. Section 2 found that existing lobbying disclosure statutes had been ineffective, and the findings assigned that ineffectiveness to three causes. The first cause was unclear statutory language. The second was weak administrative and enforcement provisions. The third was an absence of clear guidance as to who had to register and what had to be disclosed. Each cause named a different failure point. Unclear language left the boundaries of the regulated class uncertain. Weak administration and enforcement left violations without dependable consequences and filings without dependable review. Missing guidance left prospective registrants to interpret ambiguous commands on their own, a condition that produced uneven compliance across similarly situated filers. By placing all three causes in the findings, Congress presented the predecessor system as flawed in drafting, in operation, and in communication at the same time.

The wording of the findings deserves attention because findings perform a particular function inside a statute. A finding does not itself impose a duty, set a deadline, or establish a penalty. A finding records the premises on which the operative provisions rest, and courts, administrators, and later legislators read operative language against those premises. In section 2, Congress used findings to state why an overhaul, rather than a modest amendment, was required. Had the sole defect been weak enforcement, a penalty provision might have answered the problem. Had the sole defect been missing guidance, an instruction to the filing officers might have sufficed. The findings instead described language, administration, enforcement, and guidance failing together, and that combined description supported the decision to repeal the 1946 act outright. The architecture of the disclosure law mirrored the diagnosis. Definitions occupied section 3, registration occupied section 4, reports occupied section 5, and administration and penalties occupied sections 6 and 7, with each operative part answering a named defect.

Public confidence, the final element of the findings, linked the technical repairs back to the perception with which the congressional case began. Congress did not promise that disclosure would reduce the volume of paid advocacy or alter the outcome of any legislative contest. The promise recorded in section 2 was narrower and institutional in character. Effective disclosure of identity and extent would increase public confidence in the integrity of government. Integrity in that sentence referred to the openness of the process to inspection. A government whose paid influencers filed public reports could be judged on a record, whereas a government whose paid influencers operated beyond the filing system invited judgment based on surmise.

## The Predecessor Statute and Its Narrowing

The statute that the LDA repealed had governed federal lobbying registration for just under five decades. The Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946 formed Title III of the Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946, Public Law 79-601, a broader measure concerned with the organization and procedures of Congress. Placement inside a reorganization act shaped the character of the 1946 lobbying title. Lobbying regulation appeared as one element of a larger postwar effort to regularize congressional operations, rather than as a freestanding code devoted entirely to disclosure. The title imposed registration and reporting duties, and the LDA repealed the title outright in 1995, closing a statutory life that had begun in 1946 and had been reshaped, three years short of a decade into that life, by the Supreme Court construction handed down in 1954.

The registration trigger of the 1946 act turned on money and purpose in combination. A person who solicited, collected, or received money or anything of value fell within the title where a principal purpose of the person, or of the money, was to influence the passage or defeat of legislation in Congress. Each element of that formulation narrowed the class of covered persons. The solicitation, collection, or receipt element tied coverage to the movement of funds toward an influence effort. The principal purpose element tied coverage to the aim behind those funds. A person who advocated a position without soliciting, collecting, or receiving money for that purpose stood in a different position from a person who raised funds for a campaign of influence, even if the arguments each delivered to Congress were identical. The trigger therefore measured the financing of advocacy as much as the advocacy itself, and that measurement decision defined the reach of the predecessor statute from its first day in force.

A person captured by the trigger owed two sets of obligations. Registration came first, filed with the Clerk of the House and the Secretary of the Senate, the same pair of legislative officers whom the 1995 act later charged with administering its filing system. Reporting followed on a quarterly cycle, through periodic reports of receipts and expenditures. The quarterly reports required the registrant to account for money received and money spent in connection with the covered effort, giving the filings a financial character. Where the disclosure law later demanded identification of specific issues, houses of Congress, and federal agencies contacted, the 1946 title concentrated on the flow of funds.

Even before any court construed the 1946 title, its covered class was narrow by construction. The title reached a person raising or receiving money for the purpose of influencing legislation, a description that fit the professional fund-supported advocate more readily than the salaried employee arguing an employer position or the citizen spending personal funds on travel to Washington. The statutory language did not purport to register every person who communicated with Congress or every campaign that sought to move public opinion toward a legislative result. Congress in 1946 had selected a money-centered trigger, and a money-centered trigger necessarily left forms of influence outside the register. The later judicial construction examined in the Harriss litigation narrowed that already limited class again, and the second narrowing proved more consequential than the first because the second narrowing carried constitutional authority.

### What did the Harriss decision actually hold?

Harriss upheld the 1946 act against a vagueness challenge only by narrowing it to direct lobbying: a person who solicited or received contributions, where a main purpose was influencing legislation, and who communicated directly with members of Congress about pending or proposed legislation.

The decision in United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612, was handed down on June 7, 1954, with Chief Justice Earl Warren writing for the Court. The case presented a challenge to the 1946 act on vagueness grounds, a challenge with destructive potential for the statute. A holding that the act failed to give fair notice of the conduct it regulated could have invalidated the registration and reporting scheme in full. The majority avoided that result through construction. Rather than strike the act down, the Court read its language narrowly, identified the limited class of persons and activities that the language could constitutionally reach, and upheld the act as so confined. The technique preserved a statute while reducing its domain, and the reduction, rather than the preservation, determined how federal lobbying disclosure functioned between 1954 and the effective date of the LDA on January 1, 1996.

The construction announced by the Warren majority rested on three prerequisites that had to coincide. First, the person must have solicited, collected, or received contributions. Second, one of the main purposes of the person or of the contributions must have been influencing the passage or defeat of legislation. Third, the influence must have amounted to lobbying in its commonly accepted sense, meaning direct communication with members of Congress on pending or proposed legislation. No single prerequisite sufficed on its own. A person who raised money for a main purpose of influencing legislation, yet channeled that influence through newspaper advertisements or public rallies, failed the third prerequisite. A person who spoke directly with a member of Congress, yet had neither solicited nor received contributions for the effort, failed the first. Coverage under the 1946 act after Harriss required the conjunction of fundraising, legislative purpose, and direct member contact, and the failure of any one element placed the activity beyond the registration duty.

Together, the three prerequisites supplied the definiteness that the vagueness challenge had asserted the statute lacked. The contributions prerequisite anchored the act to identifiable transactions. The main purpose prerequisite confined the act to money directed toward passage or defeat of legislation. The direct communication prerequisite confined the regulated conduct to a recognizable practice, the approach to a member of Congress about a specific pending or proposed measure. Conduct inside the conjunction was, in the majority view, described with sufficient clarity to support registration and reporting duties backed by law.

The practical exclusions followed from the prerequisites with logical economy. A person spending personal money fell outside the act, because no contributions had been solicited, collected, or received from another source. A person whose contacts ran to congressional staff rather than to members of Congress fell outside the act, because the third prerequisite specified direct communication with members themselves, and staff conversations, however influential in the drafting of legislation, did not satisfy that specification. Indirect influence fell outside the act as well. Grassroots campaigns designed to generate constituent pressure, publicity efforts designed to shift the climate around a bill, and appeals addressed to the public rather than to a member all aimed at legislation without involving direct communication with a member of Congress on pending or proposed legislation, and Harriss placed that entire category beyond the registration command. The exclusions were not loopholes discovered by ingenious counsel after the fact. Each exclusion traced to a prerequisite that the majority had adopted as the price of sustaining the statute.

Justices Douglas and Jackson dissented on vagueness grounds. Their position, as recorded in the case, held that the statutory language remained too indefinite to support the duties imposed, even under the construction the majority had given the act. The disagreement therefore concerned the sufficiency of definition rather than the desirability of disclosure. The majority treated the three prerequisites as a cure for indefiniteness, extracting from general language a bounded category of direct lobbying financed by contributions. The dissenters treated the underlying terms as incurably uncertain for a statute that attached legal consequences to a failure to register and report.

The decades between the Harriss decision in 1954 and the repeal of the 1946 act in 1995 displayed the consequences of the construction in operation. Registration under the 1946 act became, in the assessment recorded by the Congressional Research Service, effectively a matter of choice for most influence work. A Washington representative whose practice centered on staff contacts could organize an entire career outside the covered class. An organization financing a public campaign on a pending bill could spend substantial sums without approaching the register. Voluntary registration by some filers coexisted with lawful nonregistration by others engaged in work of comparable effect, and the resulting register could not claim to list the population engaged in paid influence. Congress confronted that record when the findings of 1995 described unclear language and missing guidance, since the uncertainty deplored in section 2 had a judicial source as well as a legislative one. Harriss had settled the meaning of the 1946 act, and the settled meaning was too narrow for the disclosure purposes that Congress articulated four decades after the decision.

## Passage in the One Hundred Fourth Congress

The legislative vehicle for the replacement was S. 1060 of the 104th Congress, introduced by Senator Carl Levin of Michigan on July 21, 1995. Introduction placed the proposal inside a Senate where the case for disclosure reform carried support broad enough to move with unusual speed. The Senate passed S. 1060 on July 25, 1995, four days after introduction, by a vote of 98 to 0. A unanimous recorded vote of that size performed communicative work beyond the formal act of passage. Prospective registrants, House members weighing a companion measure, and the executive branch considering a signature could each read the tally as evidence that disclosure reform in 1995 lacked an organized partisan opponent in the chamber of origin. A bill that cleared its first chamber within four days of introduction had plainly been prepared and negotiated before the introduction date itself.

Senate passage, however, settled only half of the constitutional sequence. A measure regulating the very process by which measures advance invites attention to the steps that remain after a first chamber acts, and accounts of [how a bill becomes law in practice](/2022/02/01/how-a-bill-becomes-law-in-practice/) trace those steps through second chamber consideration, possible amendment, and presentation to the President. The LDA regulates people who seek to influence that sequence at defined points, from committee drafting through floor consideration, and the history of S. 1060 illustrated the sequence in compressed form. The bill had a Senate origin, a House counterpart, a committee report in the second chamber, and a final House decision to pass the Senate text rather than to insist on a separate House product.

The House route ran through the Committee on the Judiciary and through a companion bill, H.R. 2564. Companion bills serve a familiar function in bicameral legislating. A member of the second chamber introduces a parallel text so that the committee of jurisdiction can examine the policy and report a vehicle suited to floor action, without waiting idle for the first chamber to finish. In the House, the Judiciary Committee reported the companion measure as House Report 104-339, Part 1. The existence of a reported House companion alongside a passed Senate bill gave the House two procedural paths. The House could advance H.R. 2564 and then reconcile any differences with the Senate text, or the House could take up S. 1060 itself and pass the Senate language without change. The second path avoided a conference and avoided the delay that reconciliation between differing texts can produce.

On November 29, 1995, the House passed the Senate bill. The choice of vehicle carried legal significance, because passage of S. 1060 in identical form by both chambers meant that the text approved by the Senate on July 25, 1995 became the text presented for signature, with no conference committee required to harmonize competing versions. House Report 104-339, Part 1 belonged to the interval before that vote, documenting Judiciary Committee consideration of the companion while the Senate bill awaited final action. The sequence from July 21 introduction through November 29 House passage occupied a little over four months, a compact timetable for a statute that repealed a predecessor of nearly fifty years.

Presentation to the President followed House passage, and President Bill Clinton signed S. 1060 on December 19, 1995. Signature converted the enacted bill into Public Law 104-65, published at 109 Stat. 691 and codified at title 2, United States Code, sections 1601 to 1614, in chapter 26 of that title. The codification location placed lobbying disclosure among the provisions governing Congress itself, a placement consistent with the origins of the filing system in the offices of the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House. Enactment in December also set up a delayed start that separated the date of signature from the date on which duties attached to regulated persons.

Section 24 of the LDA set the effective date as January 1, 1996. A New Year effective date aligned the new registration and reporting duties with a clean calendar boundary, allowing the first semiannual periods under the statute to run on a regular cycle from the opening of 1996. The interval between signature on December 19, 1995 and effectiveness on January 1, 1996 was brief, a matter of days rather than months. On January 1, 1996, the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946 ceased to supply the governing standard, the Harriss construction lost the statute to which the construction had attached, and the definitions, thresholds, and reporting commands of the 1995 act began to govern paid lobbying disclosure for the federal government.

## The Architecture of the Enacted Text

The Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, Public Law 104-65, 109 Stat. 691, was built as a disclosure system rather than as a prohibition on advocacy. Its architecture moves in a deliberate sequence. Opening provisions state a title, record congressional findings, and supply controlling terms. Registration and reporting provisions then impose the central duties on persons who meet the statutory tests. Administrative and penalty provisions assign the filing system to two legislative branch officers and attach a civil sanction to knowing noncompliance. Later provisions amend neighboring regimes, repeal earlier disclosure laws, govern identification and tax related reporting, record a statement of Senate sentiment, and set the date on which the new system began. The enacted text separates three questions that the predecessor regime had blurred. One question asks what activity counts. Another asks who must disclose that activity and in what detail. A third asks which institution receives, reviews, publishes, and refers the resulting filings.

Section 1 supplies the short title, the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995. The act announces itself as a disclosure measure, and the operative provisions that follow fit that description. The statute does not make advocacy unlawful, does not license advocates before contact may occur, and does not require advance permission from the Secretary of the Senate or the Clerk of the House. The duties instead arise from defined contacts made for a client and from monetary and time conditions stated elsewhere in the act. The title also distinguishes the 1995 enactment from the older Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946 and from the Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938, a separate regime administered by the Department of Justice. The LDA, a term used for the 1995 act, draws paid advocacy into a uniform filing system while leaving foreign principal representation under a different framework unless a specific exemption applies.

Section 2 records findings codified at title 2, United States Code, section 1601. Congress found that existing lobbying disclosure statutes had been ineffective because statutory language lacked clarity, administrative and enforcement provisions lacked strength, and guidance about who must register and what must be disclosed lacked precision. Congress also found that effective public disclosure of the identity and extent of the efforts of paid lobbyists would increase public confidence in the integrity of government. The filings identify who seeks to influence specified federal action, on whose behalf the effort occurs, and how extensive the effort is, allowing public inspection of relationships that might otherwise remain visible only to officials and participants.

Section 3 supplies the vocabulary through which the rest of the act operates, codified principally at section 1602 of title 2. The section defines client, covered executive branch official, covered legislative branch official, lobbying activities, lobbying contact, lobbyist, and lobbying firm, among other terms. Placement near the front of the statute gives those terms control over registration, reporting, exemptions, and penalties alike. A person does not register merely because persuasion occurred in ordinary language. Registration follows only when the contacts, actors, subjects, compensation conditions, and thresholds fit the enacted terms.

Section 4 governs registration, codified at section 1603 of title 2. The timing rule requires registration no later than 45 days after a lobbyist first makes a lobbying contact or is first employed or retained to make such a contact, whichever event occurs earlier. An organization with employee lobbyists files a single registration for each client, while a lobbying firm registers separately for each client. Registration goes to both the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House, reflecting the bicameral structure of the filing system and the fact that covered officials sit in both chambers as well as in the executive branch. Each office performs the review functions assigned by section 6.

The contents required by section 4 reveal the information Congress considered material. A registration states the name, address, and principal place of business of the registrant and of the client, together with a general description of the business or activities of each. Those items connect a filing to identifiable parties and place the advocacy in an organizational setting. The registration also identifies, for each lobbyist, the name of the individual and covered executive branch or legislative branch positions that the individual held in the period specified by the reporting system. That item links advocacy to prior public service disclosed on the filing, without itself imposing a post employment ban.

Section 4 also contains two forms of transparency that reach beyond the named client. The first concerns organizations that finance and direct advocacy without appearing as the client. A registration identifies an organization other than the client that contributes more than 10,000 dollars toward the lobbying activities of the registrant in the semiannual period and in whole or in major part plans, supervises, or controls those activities. Both money and control matter under the enacted formulation. The second item concerns foreign interests. A registration provides information about a foreign entity holding at least 20 percent equitable ownership in the client or in such a contributing organization when the foreign entity directly or indirectly plans, supervises, controls, finances, or subsidizes the lobbying. The architecture thereby treats hidden direction as a disclosure problem distinct from the identity of the immediate client.

Monetary exemptions qualify the registration duty in section 4. As enacted, registration was not required for a client when the total income of a lobbying firm for lobbying activities for that client did not exceed 5,000 dollars in the semiannual period, or when the total lobbying expenses of an organization did not exceed 20,000 dollars in that period. A firm measures income received for work for outside clients, while an organization with in house advocates measures its own expenses. The statute ordered adjustment for changes in the Consumer Price Index on January 1, 1997, and on January 1 of every fourth year after that date, rounded to the nearest 500 dollars. An exemption from registration differs from an exclusion from the underlying terms. The contact may fit the governing vocabulary, yet the act excuses the filing when the monetary condition remains below the enacted line.

Section 5 governs reports, codified at section 1604 of title 2. As enacted, reports were semiannual, filed for each six month period and covering each client. A registrant reports the specific issues lobbied, including bill numbers where relevant, the Houses of Congress and federal agencies contacted, and the lobbyists who acted for the client. Those items tie the abstract fact of retention to concrete governmental targets and subjects. A good faith estimate supplies the financial measure, stated as lobbying income for a firm or lobbying expenses for an organization. A registrant could report an amount under 5,000 dollars as less than 5,000 dollars, and House and Senate guidance directs rounding of income and expense figures to the nearest 10,000 dollars. Registration terminates when the registrant is no longer employed or retained for the client and expects no further lobbying activity for that client.

Section 6 assigns duties to the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House, codified at section 1605 of title 2. The two officers review registrations and reports, verify apparent accuracy, and make filings available for public inspection and copying. They notify a person who appears not to have complied with the act. Referral forms the bridge to enforcement, because the Secretary and the Clerk refer apparent violations to the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia. The institutional limit is as significant as the assigned duties. The two congressional officers administer the filing system, and prosecution belongs to the United States Attorney. Administrative review can identify defects and prompt correction, while litigation authority rests with the prosecutor designated by the act.

Section 7 supplies the penalty rule as enacted, codified at section 1606 of title 2. A person who knowingly failed to remedy a defective filing within 60 days after notice from the Secretary or the Clerk, or who otherwise knowingly failed to comply with the act, faced a civil fine of not more than 50,000 dollars. Liability turned on a preponderance of the evidence, with regard to the extent and gravity of the violation. The defective filing branch emphasizes notice and correction, because the knowing failure to remedy within the specified period supplies the culpable conduct. As enacted, the LDA carried no criminal penalty under this provision. That feature distinguishes the 1995 text from the operative law after the 2007 amendments, which raised the civil amount and added a criminal sanction for a knowing and corrupt failure to comply.

The middle provisions extend outward to statutes that regulate adjacent forms of influence. Section 9 amends the Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938. The amendment replaced the term political propaganda with informational materials in that statute. Section 9 also carried forward the exemption from registration under the Foreign Agents Registration Act for agents registered under the LDA, subject to the limits of that regime. The provision coordinates two disclosure systems without merging them. Section 10 addresses a different boundary by amending the Byrd Amendment at title 31, United States Code, section 1352. That separate restriction concerns the use of appropriated funds to influence federal contracts, grants, and loans. Section 10 removed obsolete reporting features from the Byrd Amendment, aligning an older funding restriction with the disclosure environment created in 1995 without converting the restriction itself into part of the LDA.

Repeals clear the ground occupied by earlier disclosure law. The 1995 act repealed the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946 outright. The 1946 law, Title III of the Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946, had required a person who solicited, collected, or received money or anything of value, where a principal purpose was to influence the passage or defeat of legislation in Congress, to register with the Clerk of the House and the Secretary of the Senate and to file periodic reports of receipts and expenditures. Judicial construction in United States v. Harriss in 1954 narrowed that language, and the findings in section 2 identify the uncertainty that followed. Repeal prevented parallel operation of the older registration test and the new definitional scheme. Sections 11 and 12 also repealed lobbying provisions in housing statutes. The repeals reflect a consolidating impulse, under which at least six statutes had carried lobbying registration or disclosure standards before 1995. One uniform scheme replaced a patchwork in which coverage could depend on the statute under which a filing obligation arose.

Later provisions handle identification, tax reporting, and a statement of policy without disturbing the central filing sequence. Section 14 sets an identification rule for oral contacts. A person making an oral lobbying contact must, if asked by the covered official, state whether the person is registered under the act, identify the client, and state whether any foreign entity is a client or holds the specified interest described by the statute. Section 15 addresses tax exempt organizations that track lobbying expenditures under the Internal Revenue Code. Such an organization may report the figures developed under that tax accounting framework instead of constructing a second accounting system solely for LDA reports. The accommodation reduces duplicate measurement while preserving the report itself. Section 23 records the sense of the Senate that lobbying expenses should remain nondeductible. The provision states Senate sentiment rather than amending the tax code. Separately, the Internal Revenue Code had denied a business deduction for lobbying expenditures since 1993, so section 23 aligns the disclosure act with an existing tax rule through expression rather than operative amendment.

Section 24 sets the effective date as January 1, 1996. President Bill Clinton signed the act on December 19, 1995, after the Senate passed S. 1060 on July 25, 1995, by 98 to 0 and the House passed the Senate bill on November 29, 1995. Codification placed the act at title 2, United States Code, sections 1601 to 1614, chapter 26. Three versions of the law require separation in careful analysis. The act as enacted is Public Law 104-65, read in the Statutes at Large with the section structure Congress passed in 1995. The codified text arranges the provisions in title 2 and reflects editorial placement within the Code. The operative law after amendment includes later changes, including clarifying amendments in 1998 and substantial filing, threshold, and penalty changes in 2007. A semiannual period or a 50,000 dollars civil fine may state the 1995 design accurately while failing to state the rule governing a later filing.

## The Definitions That Decide Coverage

Coverage under the disclosure law begins with language rather than with job titles. Section 3 of the 1995 act, codified at title 2, United States Code, section 1602, defines the actors, communications, and supporting work that bring the registration and reporting system into operation. The enacted vocabulary performs gatekeeping in both directions, reaching conduct that formal labels might conceal and excluding conduct outside specified subjects, recipients, relationships, or exceptions. The term lobbyist illustrates the combined effect. As enacted, the term covered an individual employed or retained by a client for financial or other compensation whose services included more than one lobbying contact, excluding an individual whose lobbying activities constituted less than 20 percent of the time spent providing services to that client over the semiannual measuring period. Amendments in 2007 changed the measuring period to a quarterly period of three months, but the enacted design already linked repeated contact, compensation, and a time threshold.

The phrase lobbying activities reaches farther than the moment of contact. Section 1602(7) defines the phrase to include lobbying contacts and efforts in support of those contacts, including preparation and planning activities, research and other background work intended, at the time the work is performed, for use in contacts, and coordination with the lobbying activities of others. That structure explains how the statute reaches work that never itself touches an official. A memorandum prepared for use in a meeting, research assembled to support a request, planning for a sequence of communications, and coordination among advocates can count as part of the activity surrounding a contact. The intention element limits the reach. Research becomes covered support when, at the time performed, the research is intended for use in contacts. General professional knowledge, unrelated analysis, or work created without that intended use lacks the same statutory connection.

Covered executive branch official defines the executive recipients whose receipt of a communication can produce a lobbying contact. Section 1602(3) includes the President and the Vice President, officers and employees of the Executive Office of the President, officials at levels I to V of the Executive Schedule, members of the Senior Executive Service and Schedule C appointees, and commissioned officers of the uniformed services at pay grade O-7 or above, among others. The list favors rank, appointment status, and institutional position over informal influence. A communication to a career employee outside the enumerated categories does not become a contact merely because the employee works for an agency affected by the subject. Conversely, a communication to a listed official can qualify even when the official lacks final authority over the underlying decision. The statute asks whether the recipient belongs to a covered class, not whether the recipient personally controls the outcome.

Covered legislative branch official performs the parallel function for Congress and its supporting institutions. Section 1602(4) includes a member of Congress, an elected officer of either House, employees of members, employees of committees, employees of leadership offices and working groups, and other designated legislative branch employees. The inclusion of staff reflects the practical path of legislative work, in which communications often go to aides who evaluate proposals, draft language, and arrange decisions for members. The predecessor statute after United States v. Harriss had been construed around direct communication with members of Congress on pending or proposed legislation, leaving staff contacts outside the narrowed construction. The 1995 act answers that gap expressly by naming categories of legislative employees. Coverage depends on designated status, and the analysis begins with the office held by the recipient.

Client and lobbying firm define the commercial and organizational relationships that organize disclosure. A client is the party on whose behalf lobbying contacts are made and by whom a lobbyist is employed or retained, so the client gives the activity its represented interest. The registration system attaches filings to that relationship, with a separate registration for each client in the case of a firm. A lobbying firm, under section 1602(11), is a firm, partnership, or similar entity whose employees make lobbying contacts for outside clients. The phrase outside clients separates a firm serving others from an organization advocating for itself through its own personnel, even though both forms may generate registration and reporting duties under different measures. A self employed individual is treated as a lobbying firm for purposes of the act. That treatment prevents organizational form from deciding disclosure. A solo practitioner retained by a client performs the filing role of a firm, registers for the client, and reports income under the firm framework rather than escaping classification because no partnership or company stands between the individual and the client.

### What counts as a lobbying contact?

A lobbying contact is an oral or written communication, including an electronic communication, made on behalf of a client to a covered executive or legislative branch official about federal legislation, rules, programs, policy, administration, or a nomination or confirmation subject to Senate confirmation.

Each element of that sentence carries a limiting function. Form does not control, because oral statements, letters, and electronic messages can all qualify. Agency matters, because the communication must be made on behalf of a client rather than as an expression of personal opinion untethered to representation. Recipient matters, because the communication must go to a covered executive branch official or a covered legislative branch official. Subject matters, because the communication must concern an enumerated federal matter. The subjects include the formulation, modification, or adoption of federal legislation. They include federal rules, regulations, and Executive orders, together with a federal program, policy, or position of the United States Government. They include the administration or execution of a federal program or policy, including contracts, grants, loans, permits, and licenses. They also include the nomination or confirmation of a person subject to Senate confirmation.

Worked contrasts show how the elements and exceptions interact. A request for the status of a matter is excluded from the term, while a communication urging adoption of a bill falls within the term when directed to a covered official on behalf of a client. A speech delivered through public channels and published writing fall within the exceptions described by the statute, while a private letter to a covered official on the same subject may qualify as a contact. The difference lies in audience and channel. Public expression addresses an open audience, whereas the private letter directs a client sponsored appeal to a defined governmental recipient. Testimony at a hearing is excluded, while a meeting arranged around the hearing may be covered when the participants engage in a communication that meets the statutory elements. The hearing statement enters a formal proceeding under an exception, but a separate private discussion aimed at an enumerated subject is not insulated merely because the discussion occurs near the proceeding in time or place.

The statutory exceptions in section 1602(8)(B) give the term its outer boundary. Excluded communications include communications compelled by subpoena or required by law, testimony and statements in proceedings, requests for the status of a matter, communications made through public media, speeches and published writings, requests for a meeting, and communications by public officials acting in an official capacity. The categories share a logic. Compelled communications arise from legal obligation rather than voluntary client advocacy. Testimony occurs inside formal processes with their own records. A status request seeks information without pressing a position, and a request for a meeting arranges access without itself making the substantive appeal, although the ensuing meeting must be assessed on its own facts. Media communications, speeches, and published writings address public audiences, while a public official acting officially performs governmental duties rather than representing a client.

Taken together, the included subjects and the excluded categories express a theory of regulated influence. The LDA targets voluntary, client sponsored communications directed to specified officials about specified federal action, together with the compensated support work built around those communications. It leaves room for compelled compliance, formal testimony, information seeking, public discourse, and official governmental communication. That division does not depend on whether speech is persuasive in a loose sense. A published editorial may persuade many readers, and testimony may persuade a committee, yet each falls under an exception stated by Congress. A short private message may reach one covered official and qualify because every element aligns. The statute therefore regulates a defined channel of influence for disclosure purposes rather than attempting to catalogue every act that might affect public policy.

## Who Must Register: The Three-Part Test

The definition of a lobbyist in section 3 of the 1995 act, codified at section 1602(10) of title 2 of the United States Code, operated as a cumulative test. An individual fell inside the definition only when three conditions coincided in relation to the same client. The individual had to be employed or retained by a client for financial or other compensation. The services under that employment or retention had to include more than one lobbying contact. The lobbying activities for that client also had to amount to at least 20 percent of the time spent providing services to that client, measured as enacted over a six month period. Failure on any one element placed the individual outside the definition and removed the registration duty that section 4 attached to lobbyist status. The structure answered the finding in section 2 that earlier disclosure statutes had suffered from unclear language and an absence of clear guidance about who must register.

The first element, employment or retention by a client for compensation, anchored the definition in a paid professional relationship. Employment described the individual who worked inside an organization and performed advocacy services as part of that service. Retention described the individual engaged from outside, whether through a lobbying firm or through a self employed arrangement that the statute treated as a lobbying firm. In both forms, the relationship ran to a client, the person or entity on whose behalf contacts were made. The statutory phrase covered financial or other compensation, a formulation broader than a salary or a contract fee. Passage through that gate did not by itself create a registration duty, since the contact and time elements remained to be satisfied, but failure to pass through the gate ended the inquiry.

That boundary excluded the unpaid volunteer by operation of the test itself. A citizen who visited a congressional office, wrote to a covered official, or urged fellow citizens to press a position, without employment or retention for compensation by a client, did not satisfy the opening element, and the remaining elements never came into play. The disclosure law was aimed, in the language of the section 2 findings, at effective public disclosure of the identity and extent of paid efforts to influence government. The compensation element confined the registration system to that paid sphere. The Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938, by contrast, imposed registration duties without a compensation requirement, because that regime addressed representation of foreign principals rather than domestic paid advocacy services. Within the statute, compensation was the threshold question, and only after a paid relationship was established did the definition ask about contacts and time.

The second element required services that included more than one lobbying contact. A lobbying contact, under section 1602(8), was an oral or written communication, including an electronic communication, to a covered executive branch official or a covered legislative branch official, made on behalf of a client, regarding subjects the statute enumerated. Those subjects included the formulation, modification, or adoption of federal legislation, federal rules, regulations, or Executive orders. The subjects extended to a federal program, policy, or position of the United States Government, to the administration or execution of a federal program or policy, including contracts, grants, loans, permits, and licenses, and to the nomination or confirmation of a person subject to Senate confirmation. The definition carried exceptions for categories such as communications compelled by subpoena or required by law, testimony in proceedings, requests for the status of a matter, communications through public media, speeches and published writings, requests for a meeting, and communications by public officials acting in an official capacity. A communication inside an exception did not count, so the element turned on communications that matched the statutory description after the exceptions had been applied.

The more than one formulation excluded the single courtesy contact. An individual employed or retained for compensation who made one qualifying communication to a covered official on behalf of a client, and no second qualifying communication, did not meet the second element. The single contact might have been an introductory meeting, a delivery of a position paper with an oral explanation, or a telephone conversation about pending legislation, and in each instance the contact counted once. Without a second contact, the definition was not satisfied for that client relationship. Isolated communication, even when paid and even when directed to a covered official about legislation, was treated as insufficient by itself to place an individual inside the registration system. Repetition converted contact work from an incident of a broader engagement into a pattern of advocacy services that Congress chose to make visible. Two qualifying contacts satisfied the element, but the element filtered out the lone approach at the lower boundary of activity.

The third element filtered the definition from a different direction. An individual whose lobbying activities constituted less than 20 percent of the time spent providing services to that client was expressly excluded from the definition, even when compensation was present and even when the services included more than one lobbying contact. The exclusion covered the employee whose contact work formed a small fraction of a broader brief. An organization employee whose duties centered on program administration, technical analysis, or member services, and who on occasion joined a meeting with legislative staff, stood outside the definition if the advocacy share remained below one fifth of the service time. The same reasoning applied to a retained consultant whose engagement centered on research or compliance and whose advocacy component occupied a minor share of the work. The statute posed a quantitative question, a comparison between lobbying activities and total services for that client, and the importance of the issue or the seniority of the official contacted played no part in that comparison.

The test applied client by client, and that unit of analysis shaped outcomes across a working life. The definition spoke of services to that client and of contacts made on behalf of a client, and each reference pointed the measurement toward a single relationship rather than toward the aggregate of an individual practice. An individual retained by several clients therefore faced several separate applications of the test. For one client, an engagement might have consisted principally of advocacy services, with contacts, preparation, and coordination consuming well over 20 percent of the time devoted to that client, and the individual qualified as a lobbyist in that relationship. For a second client, the same individual might have provided mostly technical or managerial services, with occasional contact work below the threshold, and the individual stood outside the definition in that relationship. Registration followed the relationship in which the definition was met. A heavy concentration of influence work for one client could not be averaged away against other services for other clients, because the denominator for each measurement was confined to services for the client whose registration was in question.

Satisfaction of the test produced registration under section 4, and the statute attached a deadline to that consequence. Registration was required no later than 45 days after a lobbyist first made a lobbying contact or was first employed or retained to make a lobbying contact, whichever event occurred earlier. The alternative trigger mattered because employment or retention could precede any actual communication. A firm engaged to begin advocacy services on a stated date, with the first meeting scheduled later, could not measure the 45 days from the meeting alone if the retention had started the clock earlier. Conversely, a contact made at the outset of a relationship started the period even if formal retention followed afterward. The whichever is earlier rule closed the gap between the two sequences by selecting the first event in time, while the 45 day interval gave the registrant a defined period in which to assemble the required information.

The filing mechanics reinforced the client centered structure. An organization with employee lobbyists filed a single registration for each client, so the organization appeared as registrant once in relation to that client even when several employees qualified as lobbyists for the client and were named in the filing. A lobbying firm registered separately for each client, so a firm serving multiple clients filed multiple registrations, one standing for each relationship. A self employed individual was treated as a lobbying firm under the definitions, which placed the sole practitioner under the firm pattern rather than the organization pattern. Every registration went to two destinations, the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House, the officers whom section 6 charged with reviewing filings, verifying apparent accuracy, and making filings available for public inspection and copying. Dual filing matched the bicameral character of the institution whose officials received many of the contacts the statute covered.

### How much time triggers the lobbyist definition?

The definition is triggered when lobbying activities reach at least 20 percent of the time an individual spends providing services to a particular client during a six month period, counting preparation, planning, research, background work, and coordination alongside direct contacts made with covered executive branch and legislative branch officials.

The share was measured against all services for that client, and not against the whole working life of the individual. A part time adviser who devoted few hours in a week to a single client, and who spent most of those hours on advocacy services for that client, could cross the 20 percent line within that relationship even though advocacy occupied a modest share of a full schedule in the abstract. A full time employee of a large organization presented the opposite configuration. That employee might have devoted more absolute hours to contact work than the part time adviser, yet a broad portfolio of program, administrative, and analytical duties could leave the advocacy share below one fifth. The statute measured proportion within a defined service relationship, not raw hours in isolation. Changes in the denominator therefore changed the result, since an engagement that expanded to include substantial other services could move an individual below the line without any reduction in contacts, and an engagement that narrowed to advocacy alone could move the same individual across the line without any increase in contacts.

The numerator of the fraction was broader than the contacts themselves, because the statute defined lobbying activities, in section 1602(7), to include lobbying contacts and efforts in support of those contacts. Preparation and planning activities counted toward the numerator. Research and other background work counted when the work was intended, at the time the work was performed, for use in contacts. Coordination with the lobbying activities of others counted as well. Research undertaken to inform a planned communication formed part of lobbying activities, while general study of a policy field, undertaken without an intention at that time to use the material in contacts, fell outside the numerator even if the material later proved useful. Strategy conferences, drafting of talking points, assembly of data for a presentation, and review of legislative text for a planned approach all formed part of the effort that produced a contact, and the disclosure law treated that supporting effort as part of the activity the time test measured.

Measurement over the six month period as enacted introduced estimation into a test that read, on its face, as arithmetic precision. The statute fixed the fraction, the measuring period, and the categories entering the numerator, yet the underlying record consisted of professional time allocated across meetings, drafting, reading, and conversation, much of it unsegmented in ordinary practice. The individual and the employing organization or firm therefore assessed the share through self assessment, tracking how time for a client divided between advocacy services and other services. Precision on paper coexisted with judgment calls in practice for reasons internal to the work itself. A single meeting might have mixed a status inquiry, which the contact definition excepted, with advocacy on pending legislation, which the definition covered, and a research project might have served both a business purpose and a planned contact. Allocation decisions in such mixed cases rested with the filer, exercised against the statutory categories, and description of that structure carries no imputation about any individual filer.

The six month measuring period also smoothed variation that a shorter window would have amplified. Advocacy work arrives in pulses tied to committee schedules, floor calendars, and regulatory comment periods, so a single intense week could dominate a monthly calculation while representing a small share of a half year engagement. Measurement across the semiannual period, the same period that organized the reporting cycle in section 5 as enacted and the monetary exemptions in section 4, allowed the definition to distinguish sustained advocacy responsibility from a brief concentration of effort. An individual whose contact work surged during one legislative episode and then subsided faced the test on the whole period, with the surge weighed against the full body of services rendered for that client. The fraction identified the intensity Congress treated as the mark of a lobbyist, and the period identified the span across which intensity was to be judged.

## The Monetary Exemptions Beneath the Test

Beneath the three part definition, section 4(a)(3) placed a monetary exemption that could remove a registration duty even in a relationship where an individual met the definition of lobbyist. As enacted, registration was not required for a client if a lobbying firm total income for lobbying activities for that client did not exceed 5,000 dollars during the semiannual period, or if an organization total lobbying expenses did not exceed 20,000 dollars during the semiannual period. The exemption operated at the level of the client relationship, consistent with the client by client architecture of the definition and of the filing system. A firm evaluated the income figure separately for each client, and an organization evaluated the expense figure separately for each client, rather than aggregating receipts or expenditures across an entire practice. The two figures were not interchangeable, because each attached to a different kind of registrant and measured a different kind of amount. Income described money received by a firm from a client for lobbying activities, and expenses described money spent by an organization on its own lobbying activities through its employees.

The relationship between the two thresholds reflected the different economics of the two organizational forms. A lobbying firm sold advocacy services, and income from a single client in a six month period gave a direct measure of the scale of the engagement for that client. An organization that lobbied through its own employees bore costs of a different character, including portions of employee salaries allocated to advocacy time, overhead associated with that time, and expenditures supporting the contacts its employees made. An expense figure built from allocated internal costs could reach a substantial total for activity that, measured as a purchased service, would have commanded a smaller fee. The statute accommodated that difference by setting the organization expense threshold at 20,000 dollars against the firm income threshold at 5,000 dollars. The exemption also operated in a defined sequence after the time test. The time test determined whether an individual counted as a lobbyist, and the exemption then asked a second question about the scale of the relationship in dollar terms, so a relationship could satisfy the definition and yet remain exempt because the dollars fell at or below the applicable figure.

Congress did not leave the two figures frozen at the values printed in the statute at enactment on December 19, 1995. Section 4(a)(3) ordered adjustment of the thresholds for changes in the Consumer Price Index, with the first adjustment directed on January 1, 1997, and further adjustments directed on January 1 of every fourth year after that date. Each adjusted figure was to be rounded to the nearest 500 dollars. Selection of the Consumer Price Index tied the thresholds to a general measure of price change across the economy, rather than to legal services prices or congressional salaries, so the real economic scale at which registration attached remained broadly constant as the general price level moved. The four year interval meant that the nominal figures changed in steps rather than in a continuous drift, giving filers stable numbers across each interval. Rounding to the nearest 500 dollars kept the adjusted thresholds in round increments filers could apply without fractional arithmetic, at the cost of small deviations from a precise index based calculation in any given cycle.

The first adjustment date, January 1, 1997, arrived one year after the effective date of the statute on January 1, 1996, and the sequence after that date settled into the four year rhythm the text prescribed. Placement of the first adjustment so close to enactment aligned the figures with price levels measured near the start of the system, rather than leaving the figures anchored to drafting period levels for a full cycle. The adjustment clause signaled that Congress drafted the exemption as a lasting structural feature with a built in maintenance device, not as a transitional concession that inflation would erode. Without an adjustment mechanism, a fixed nominal threshold loses real value as prices rise, and relationships of steadily smaller real scale cross the line into registration, expanding the registration population without any legislator voting for that expansion. The index clause prevented that silent expansion, holding the boundary at approximately the real scale the enacting Congress selected, subject to the step pattern and rounding the clause specified.

The function of the exemption within a disclosure statute differed from the function of an exemption in a tax or a prohibition. The LDA imposed disclosure, not a ban, so the cost of inclusion was principally the burden of registration and reporting and the public visibility filing created, while the cost of exclusion was a gap in the public record. A de minimis floor balanced those costs at the lower end of activity. Below the floor, the burden of preparing, filing, reviewing, and publishing a registration for a very small engagement bore a poor relationship to the informational value of the filing, and the offices of the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House would have faced a volume swollen by engagements of minimal scale. The floor kept the system aimed at professional scale activity and out of incidental contact. The public record therefore concentrated on relationships large enough to represent sustained, resourced efforts to influence government action, and the floor served the same purpose as the single contact exclusion and the 20 percent exclusion through a different instrument, with significance measured in dollars rather than in contacts or in time share.

The exemption carried limits that preserved the character of the scheme. The exemption removed a registration requirement for a client relationship under stated dollar conditions, and the exemption did not redefine the underlying activity or excuse a filing for a different client whose figures exceeded the threshold. The figures applied per client and per semiannual period as enacted, so a relationship that grew across successive periods could move from exempt status in one period to registrable status in a later period when income for that client crossed the applicable figure. The period structure matched the rhythm of the semiannual reports that section 5 required as enacted, which used the same six month frame for reporting income and expenses and which permitted a registrant to report an amount under 5,000 dollars as less than 5,000 dollars. Alignment of the exemption period, the definition measuring period, and the reporting period gave the statute a single temporal unit in its original form.

## What a Registration Statement Contains

A registration statement under section 4 was the founding document of a disclosed relationship, and its contents were prescribed so that the filing identified the parties, the business of each party, the subjects expected to occupy the advocacy, the outside organizations behind the effort, the foreign interests connected to the client, and the individuals expected to perform the work. The statement opened with identities and business descriptions. The filing stated the name, address, and principal place of business of the registrant, and the name, address, and principal place of business of the client, together with a general description of the business or activities of each. Identification of the registrant told a reader which firm or organization had assumed the filing duty, and identification of the client told a reader on whose behalf contacts would be made, a distinction of substance because the registrant acted as agent or employee and the client supplied the interest the advocacy advanced. The business descriptions added context that names alone could not supply, placing each party in a line of business or activity that a reader of the public file could understand without resort to outside sources.

Alongside the identities of the parties, the statement identified the general issue areas in which lobbying was expected. The registrant listed the general issue areas on which the lobbyist or lobbyists were expected to engage in lobbying activities on behalf of the client. The expectation based formulation matched the moment of registration, within 45 days of the first contact or of employment or retention, whichever came earlier, a moment at which the engagement had begun but the full course of advocacy could not be stated with the specificity a later report could achieve. General issue areas located the engagement on the map of federal policy, indicating whether the expected work concerned taxation, health programs, energy, transportation, financial regulation, or defense, without requiring bill numbers that might not exist at the outset. The reports required by section 5 later supplied the specific issues lobbied, including bill numbers where relevant, and the Houses of Congress and federal agencies contacted. Registration disclosed the expected field of action at the start, and the reports disclosed the realized field of action after the period closed.

The coalition provision reached past the named client to organizations standing behind the lobbying effort. The registration statement identified any organization, other than the client, that contributed more than 10,000 dollars toward the registrant lobbying activities during the semiannual period and that in whole or in major part planned, supervised, or controlled those activities. Two conditions had to coincide. The organization had to contribute more than 10,000 dollars in the period, a funding condition that confined the provision to organizations with a material financial stake in the effort. The organization also had to plan, supervise, or control the lobbying in whole or in major part, a control condition that confined the provision to organizations exercising direction rather than organizations making incidental contributions to a broader cause. An organization that funded an effort directed by others fell outside the provision as enacted, as did an organization that directed an effort without contributing funds above the stated figure. The conjunction of money and control identified the configuration in which an allied organization both financed an advocacy campaign at a material level and steered the campaign, while the named client appeared as the party on whose behalf contacts were made.

The reasoning behind the coalition provision lay in the structure of organized advocacy as Congress encountered the practice during the drafting of the disclosure law. Advocacy on a federal issue frequently proceeded through associations, coalitions, and alliances in which several organizations pooled resources under a shared name or through a lead organization. In such arrangements, the client named in a registration might have been a coalition formed for the campaign itself, and the organizations that created the coalition, funded the campaign, and set the strategy might otherwise have remained invisible in the filing. Identification of a qualifying organization corrected that invisibility at the registration stage, so a reader learned the name of an organization that stood behind the advocacy with both money and direction. The provision operated through disclosure alone. Identification carried no prohibition and no penalty for funding or controlling lobbying, and the identified organization acquired visibility rather than liability. That design matched the premise in the section 2 findings, that effective public disclosure of the identity and extent of paid efforts would increase public confidence in the integrity of government.

Foreign entity disclosures formed a second layer of information about interests behind a client. The registration statement carried information about a foreign entity that held at least 20 percent equitable ownership in the client, or in an organization identified under the coalition provision, and that directly or indirectly planned, supervised, controlled, directed, financed, or subsidized the lobbying activities, together with related information about foreign entities connected through ownership and through participation in the direction or financing of the effort. The ownership figure, 20 percent, marked the level at which Congress treated a foreign stake as substantial enough to require visibility in a domestic disclosure filing, and the accompanying verbs described the channels through which a foreign interest could stand behind advocacy, through direction of the effort or through financing of the effort. A separate statute, the Foreign Agents Registration Act, governed agents of foreign principals under a system administered by the Department of Justice, and section 9 of the 1995 act carried forward an exemption from that separate system for agents registered under the LDA, with the exemption unavailable by regulation where a foreign government or a foreign political party was the foreign principal or the principal beneficiary of the work. The foreign entity items allowed the LDA file itself to show a qualifying foreign connection in the same record that disclosed the client and the registrant.

The final category of contents named the individuals who would perform the advocacy. The registration statement gave the name of each individual expected to act as a lobbyist for the client, and expectation matched the timing of registration, since the filing preceded or accompanied the opening of the engagement. Naming individuals, rather than naming only the firm or organization, attached the disclosure to identifiable persons whose contacts with covered officials carried personal professional reputation. The statement also disclosed, for each such individual, the covered executive branch positions and covered legislative branch positions that the individual had previously held, and the reports required disclosure of previously held covered positions as a continuing element of the system. Covered positions encompassed senior territory in both branches, including service as a member of Congress, service in leadership, committee, and member offices in the legislative branch, and service at senior levels of the executive branch, including the Executive Schedule and the Senior Executive Service. A registration statement therefore showed whether an expected lobbyist arrived at the engagement from a position inside the institution the advocacy addressed.

Congress wanted former officials visible at the point of registration for reasons that followed from the premise of the statute. Movement between government service and paid advocacy was lawful, and the 1995 act placed no ban on lobbying by former officials, leaving post employment restrictions to separate provisions of the criminal code. Disclosure addressed the movement through visibility instead of prohibition. A covered official who received a contact from a former colleague, a former member, or a former senior aide could consult the public file and see the prior position recorded in the registration statement for the client on whose behalf the contact was made. Journalists, researchers, and citizens examining the file received the same information at the start of the engagement rather than in a later reconstruction. Prior position disclosure also gave the statement a biographical dimension that the other contents lacked, describing the path by which the individual advocate had arrived at the work. Placed together, the contents composed a compact map of a disclosed relationship, naming the agent, the client, the field of action, the organizations and foreign interests behind the client, and the persons, with government pasts stated, who were expected to make the contacts.

## The Registration Decision Tree

The table below states each element of the registration test, what the element requires, and the route by which a person engaged in influence activity falls outside the requirement. Each row is a separate exit from the system, and the exits operate independently: failing any single element ends the inquiry.

| Definitional element | What it requires | How a person falls outside it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Client relationship | Employment or retention by a client for financial or other compensation | The unpaid advocate and the volunteer are outside the definition, because compensation is an element of it |
| Lobbying contacts | More than one lobbying contact with a covered official for that client | A single contact, however consequential, does not make a statutory lobbyist |
| Time share | Lobbying activities of at least 20 percent of the time spent serving that client in the measuring period | The strategist who directs a campaign while personally making few contacts stays below the share and never registers |
| Covered official | The communication must reach an official the statute lists, in Congress or the senior executive branch | Communications to officials outside the covered categories, and to state or local officials, are not lobbying contacts |
| Covered subject | The communication must concern legislation, rules, programs, nominations, or comparable federal action | A communication on a subject outside the enumerated list is not a lobbying contact |
| Excepted communication | The communication must not fall within a statutory exception | Testimony, status requests, compelled communications, and media communications are excluded by definition |
| Monetary floor | The engagement must exceed the income or expense threshold for the period | A lobbying firm at or below the income threshold, or an organization at or below the expense threshold, need not register for that client |
| Grassroots appeal | The communication must be directed to a covered official | An appeal addressed to the public, urging citizens to contact their representatives, is not a lobbying contact at all |

## Reporting After Registration

Registration under the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 opened a continuing obligation rather than completing a single act of compliance. Section 5 of the 1995 act, codified at title 2, United States Code, section 1604, required semiannual reports, filed for each six month period and covering each client separately. The structure followed registration itself, because a lobbying firm registered separately for each client and an organization with employee lobbyists filed a single registration for each client. A registrant with several clients therefore produced several reports for a single period, each confined to the work done for the client named in the corresponding registration. The cadence was later changed by amendment, a change that belongs to a separate stage in the history of the LDA, but the act that took effect on January 1, 1996, rested on the six month report.

Each report had to identify the specific issues lobbied, including bill numbers where relevant, and the Houses of Congress and federal agencies contacted. A registrant could not satisfy section 5 by stating that work concerned taxation or appropriations in the abstract without tying the disclosure to the matters actually pursued during the period. Where a contact concerned a bill, the bill number gave the filing a concrete reference that a reader could trace to a text, a committee, and a legislative history. Where work concerned a rule, a regulation, an Executive order, a federal program, or a nomination, the report described the issue with enough specificity to connect the contact to the government action at stake. The institutional disclosure followed the definition of a lobbying contact, which reached communications to covered legislative branch officials and covered executive branch officials. A filing therefore recorded whether work was directed toward the Senate, toward the House of Representatives, toward a federal agency, or toward a combination of institutions, a distinction that mattered because a legislative proposal might move through one or both Houses while the administration of a federal program might require contact with an agency charged with contracts, grants, loans, permits, or licenses.

Each report also identified the individuals who acted as lobbyists for the client during the period. The names connected the registration, which listed lobbyists at the outset of an engagement, to the work actually performed across six months. Personnel could change between registration and reporting, and a person listed in an early filing might perform no work in a later period, while another individual in the same firm or organization might assume the contacts after a registration had been filed. The semiannual report reconciled the roster with the activity. A reader could determine which individuals had acted, compare that list with the lobbyists named in the registration, and relate the names to the disclosure in the registration concerning covered executive or legislative branch positions previously held by each lobbyist.

Money formed another element of the design, and the statute treated money through estimation rather than exact accounting. A lobbying firm reported a good faith estimate of lobbying income received from the client for the period, while an organization that employed in house lobbyists reported a good faith estimate of lobbying expenses incurred during the period. The distinction preserved the difference between a firm that sold lobbying services to outside clients and an organization that spent its own resources on employees who made lobbying contacts on its behalf. In each case the figure represented the lobbying portion of a larger relationship or budget, because fees might cover research, strategy, monitoring, and contacts, and salaries might cover many duties unrelated to lobbying contacts. The good faith standard acknowledged that allocation required judgment about lobbying activities, a term that included contacts and efforts in support of contacts such as preparation, planning, research, background work, and coordination with the lobbying activities of others. Two accommodations shaped every figure in the register. A registrant could report an amount under 5,000 dollars as less than 5,000 dollars, rather than stating a precise sum for a small engagement or a small amount of expense. Guidance from the House and the Senate directed that income and expense figures be rounded to the nearest 10,000 dollars. Rounding at that scale conveyed the size of an engagement, whether income or expense fell in the range of tens of thousands of dollars or reached hundreds of thousands of dollars or more, without presenting an estimate as an audited total.

A special rule addressed organizations exempt from tax that already computed lobbying expenditures under the Internal Revenue Code. Such an organization could report lobbying expenditures computed under the code method instead of constructing a second accounting system solely for the LDA. The accommodation reduced duplication between two federal regimes that asked related questions about lobbying expense, since tax law used expenditure figures for purposes connected to exempt status while the disclosure law used figures to inform the public record. The rule applied to the method of computation rather than to the obligation to file, so an exempt organization that met the registration requirements registered and reported issues, institutions, and individuals in the ordinary way. Reports could also be amended when a registrant discovered an error, an omission, or a figure that required revision under the good faith standard. Registration terminated when the registrant was no longer employed or retained for the client and expected no further lobbying activity for that client. Both conditions mattered, because the end of a formal engagement joined to an expectation of no further activity closed the registration, while the filings already made for earlier periods remained part of the public record.

### What does a lobbying report disclose?

A lobbying report under the 1995 act discloses the specific issues lobbied, the Houses of Congress and federal agencies contacted, the individuals who acted as lobbyists for the client, and a good faith estimate of income received or expenses incurred for the lobbying work during the reporting period.

The answer defines both the reach and the boundary of disclosure under the statute. A report discloses issues, institutions contacted, people, and money, but a report does not disclose the content of any communication made to a covered official. The advice given to a client, the arguments presented in a meeting, the documents prepared for a contact, and the response of the official who received the communication fall outside the categories section 5 required. Disclosure under the LDA is disclosure of the existence and scale of influence work rather than disclosure of its substance. A reader of the register could learn that a named individual contacted the Senate or a named agency about a numbered bill on behalf of a named client for an estimated amount of income or expense, and that reader could not learn from the filing what the individual said, what the client hoped to obtain in precise terms beyond the issue described, or whether the official agreed, resisted, or acted at all. No provision of section 5 required a transcript, a summary of argument, or an account of an official reaction.

The distinction between existence and substance shaped the uses to which the filings could fairly be put. A report could establish that an engagement existed during a period, that a client maintained representation on an issue while a bill moved through a committee, or that spending on an engagement rose or fell between one semiannual period and the next. A report could not establish persuasion, causation, or the reason a member voted in a particular way or an agency decided a matter in a particular form. Monetary disclosure observed the same boundary, because the good faith estimate disclosed scale without disclosing contracts, invoices, salary allocations, or fee arrangements in detail. Permission to state an amount under 5,000 dollars as less than 5,000 dollars and the direction to round figures to the nearest 10,000 dollars reinforced the principle that the public record concerned magnitude rather than private commercial terms. The filings illuminated the structure around influence work and left the conversations at the center of that work unrecorded.

## The Public Record the Filings Create

Section 6 of the 1995 act, codified at title 2, United States Code, section 1605, assigned custody of the filings to the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House and imposed duties that converted private submissions into a public record. The two officers reviewed registrations and reports, verified apparent accuracy, made the filings available for public inspection and copying, notified a person who appeared not to have complied with the statute, and referred apparent violations to the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia. Administration and access therefore rested in the same hands. The offices that received a registration also held the registration open to any person who wished to inspect the document or obtain a copy, and the offices that answered questions from filers also performed the first screening for defects that could lead to notice and referral. Prosecution remained outside the two legislative branch offices, because the Secretary and the Clerk administered the filing system and referred apparent violations rather than prosecuting violations.

Availability for inspection and copying carried a significance larger than the physical act of opening a file room. Before the LDA took effect on January 1, 1996, lobbying disclosure rested on the patchwork described at the opening of this article, and the principal predecessor statute from 1946 had been narrowed by judicial construction to a fraction of paid influence work. No single register maintained by Congress itself brought together, in one scheme, the identity of clients, the identity of lobbyists, the issues pursued, the institutions contacted, and the estimated money involved. The 1995 act created that register, a single body of information searchable in principle, organized by registrant and client and renewed period after period. A citizen, a journalist, a researcher, a competing interest, or a public official could approach the same set of documents and extract the same basic facts about representation before the federal government, without needing access to the client, the firm, or the official contacted.

Journalists drew on the filings as raw material for reporting on the business of representation, and research organizations built systematic accounts from the same documents. The Center for Responsive Politics tallied the filings to count registered lobbyists and to total reported spending, producing figures that entered public debate about the scale of lobbying in Washington. The Center counted a record 15,137 registered lobbyists in 2007, and the Center reported that reported federal lobbying spending totaled about 3.21 billion dollars in 2014. Each tally rested on the underlying reports filed under the LDA, aggregated client by client and period by period. Aggregation of that kind depended on the uniformity the 1995 act imposed, because a count built from filings made under six separate statutes with different triggers and different categories would not have supported a single total, while the uniform register allowed thousands of separate reports to be added into one measure of registered activity and one measure of reported expenditure. The tallies also carried the limits of the underlying documents, because a count of registered lobbyists counted persons who met the statutory definition and filed, and a total of reported spending added good faith estimates rounded to the nearest 10,000 dollars rather than audited expenditures.

Disclosure of the kind the LDA established operates as one method of regulation among several, and the contrast with prohibition clarifies what the statute did and did not attempt. A prohibitory rule forbids specified conduct, such as a ban on a gift or a restriction on post employment contact by a former official, and enforcement proceeds against a person who engages in the forbidden conduct. A disclosure rule permits the conduct and requires the actor to record the conduct in a public filing, leaving judgment about the propriety or wisdom of the activity to readers of the record, to voters, to journalists, and to officials who answer for decisions made after contacts occur. The LDA rested principally on the second method. Lobbying remained lawful, paid representation remained lawful, and the statute answered the activity with registration, reporting, inspection, and a civil penalty directed at failures to file or to remedy defective filings rather than at the act of lobbying itself.

The wider transparency framework of which the register formed one part rested on the same theory applied to different materials. Disclosure under the LDA operated alongside other transparency statutes, including [the Freedom of Information Act of 1966](/2021/10/01/freedom-of-information-act-1966-guide/), which governed access to records held by executive branch agencies, and alongside the campaign finance disclosure administered by the Federal Election Commission and the financial disclosure required of senior officials under separate law. Each regime opened a different set of documents to inspection. Agency records revealed the internal deliberations and decisions of the government itself, campaign finance filings revealed money raised and spent in elections, and LDA filings revealed the representation arrayed before the government from outside. Together the regimes allowed a reader to follow a public question from several directions, from the money spent to elect officials, to the representatives who contacted those officials, to the records the agencies produced in deciding the question. No single regime supplied the whole picture, and the LDA contribution remained the register of paid lobbying maintained by the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House.

## The Technical Amendments

Congress returned to the disclosure law within a little more than two years of the effective date and chose correction over reconstruction. The Lobbying Disclosure Technical Amendments Act of 1998, Public Law 105-166, 112 Stat. 38, was signed on April 8, 1998. The measure adjusted definitions and exemptions in the 1995 act and left the structure of the LDA intact. Registration proceeded client by client through the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House, reports followed the categories established in section 5, administration rested with the two congressional officers, and the penalty framework addressed failures to comply with filing duties. The 1998 act belongs to the class of legislation described by the word technical in a title, a measure whose purpose was to clarify language, repair inconsistencies, and refine the boundaries of defined terms after a new statutory scheme had begun to operate in practice. A filing system administered by two offices and used by thousands of registrants generated questions about the meaning of operative language at a pace that drafting alone could not anticipate, and guidance from the Secretary and the Clerk could interpret a text without revising the text. Where experience showed that the text itself produced uncertainty or an unintended boundary, amendment offered the durable remedy at the level of definitions and exemptions, without reopening the policy settlement reached in 1995 about disclosure as the governing approach to paid lobbying.

A definitions driven statute generates technical corrections for reasons inherent in the form. Nearly every consequence in the LDA flows from a defined term. Lobbying contact determines which communications count toward the status of a lobbyist and toward the duty to register. Lobbying activities determines which supporting work enters an estimate of income or expenses and which preparation falls inside the disclosure regime. Covered executive branch official and covered legislative branch official determine whose receipt of a communication can trigger coverage. Client, lobbying firm, and lobbyist allocate the duties to file among organizations, firms, and individuals. A small ambiguity in any one term therefore translates directly into a filing duty or into the absence of a filing duty for a class of persons. An uncertain exception in the definition of a lobbying contact can move a category of communications into the register or out of the register across every client in the system, and an uncertain phrase in the definition of a covered official can alter the coverage of contacts with an entire office. Precision in definitions carries consequences of a kind that precision in a prefatory finding does not, because findings explain a statute while definitions operate the statute. Every operative term carries registration consequences, so small ambiguities in who counts as a covered official or which communications are excepted translate directly into filing duties or into the absence of filing duties.

Exemptions present the same problem from the opposite direction. An exemption withdraws a person, a communication, or an amount from a duty that the general language would otherwise impose. The monetary thresholds in section 4 excused registration where a firm received no more than the stated amount of income for a client or an organization incurred no more than the stated amount of expenses during the period, and the exceptions listed in section 3 removed specified communications from the definition of a lobbying contact, including communications compelled by subpoena, testimony in proceedings, requests for the status of a matter, and communications through public media. Each exemption required a boundary, and each boundary invited a case at the edge. Drafting that placed the edge in a slightly different position than Congress intended produced either disclosure Congress had not meant to require or silence Congress had not meant to permit. Technical amendment allowed Congress to reposition an edge after filers and administrators had located the edge in practice, while leaving the center of the scheme, the register and the reports, exactly where the 1995 act had placed the center.

The 1998 measure also set a pattern for the later history of the disclosure law, in which the 1995 architecture was adjusted by amendment rather than replaced. Later Congresses that wished to alter the frequency of reports, the manner of filing, the content of disclosure, or the penalties for noncompliance worked through amendments to the LDA instead of displacing the LDA with a new statute on a new foundation. The pattern preserved continuity in the public record, because filings made under an amended version of the same statute could be compared with filings made under the original version, subject to the changes each amendment introduced. The technical amendments therefore hold a place in an account of the LDA out of proportion to the length of Public Law 105-166. The 1998 act confirmed that the definitions enacted in 1995 would govern real filing decisions and that Congress accepted responsibility for refining the definitions when practice exposed a defect, and the measure confirmed the stability of the larger design at the same time, because a Congress dissatisfied with disclosure as a method, with registration by client, or with administration by the Secretary and the Clerk could have used the occasion to alter any of those choices and instead confined the intervention to clarification.

## The Amendments of the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act

The amendments followed a scandal that turned lobbying disclosure from a subject of periodic complaint into a subject of criminal prosecution and legislative response. The lobbying scandal centered on Jack Abramoff, who pleaded guilty in January 2006 to federal conspiracy and fraud charges arising from lobbying work. That plea gave the reform effort a concrete anchor, because a prominent practitioner had admitted to conspiracy and fraud in connection with that work, and the admission damaged public confidence in a disclosure system that had operated since January 1, 1996. The response took the form of S. 1 of the 110th Congress. President George W. Bush signed the measure on September 14, 2007, and it became Public Law 110-81, the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007. Title II of the 2007 act contained the amendments to the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995, and those amendments took effect for filings from January 1, 2008. The Honest Leadership and Open Government Act did not repeal the 1995 act. HLOGA retained registration with the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House, retained disclosure of clients, issues, houses of Congress and agencies contacted, and income or expenses, and retained the basic definitions of lobbying contact, lobbying activities, covered officials, client, lobbying firm, and lobbyist. The amendments worked inside that structure and changed the tempo of disclosure, the medium of disclosure, the range of political money disclosed alongside lobbying activity, the identification of organizations that fund and direct lobbying from behind a named client, and the consequences of failure.

The most visible alteration was the move from semiannual activity reports to quarterly activity reports. Under the 1995 act as enacted, a registrant filed a report for each six month period, covering each client, describing the specific issues lobbied, the houses of Congress and federal agencies contacted, and the lobbyists who acted, together with a good faith estimate of lobbying income for a firm or lobbying expenses for an organization. A six month interval allowed a considerable volume of contact, preparation, and coordination to accumulate before any public filing described it. The amendments substituted a quarterly report, designated the LD-2, for the semiannual report. Four filings a year replaced two, and each filing covered three months rather than six. The change compressed the distance between activity and disclosure, so a contact made early in a quarter appeared in a filing for that quarter, and a reader who followed a particular issue, client, or firm could trace the sequence of quarters as a continuous record. Quarterly filing also changed compliance discipline, because records had to be assembled twice as often, and errors, omissions, and changes in personnel or clients surfaced at shorter intervals.

The second major change concerned the medium of filing and the form in which the public could reach the filings. The 2007 act made electronic filing mandatory, and it directed the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House to make reports available on the Internet without a fee in a searchable, sortable, and downloadable form. Mandatory electronic filing removed the option of a paper report that administering staff would have to handle, store, and render accessible through separate effort, and it standardized submission in a format that the receiving offices could process and publish as part of a single system. Access without a fee removed a price barrier between the filing as a public record and the citizen, journalist, or researcher who sought to read it. Searchability allowed a reader to locate filings by registrant, client, lobbyist, or issue rather than paging through a chronological accumulation. Sortability allowed a reader to order results and compare filings across registrants or periods, and downloadability allowed a reader to take the data away from the viewing interface, analyze it in bulk, and cross reference it against other records. Taken together, the provisions treated disclosure as a data obligation and not only as a document obligation, and the Internet database maintained by the Secretary and the Clerk was designed to close the gap between filing and usable public knowledge.

The third major change added an entirely new filing, distinct from the quarterly activity report in subject and in rhythm. The amendments required a semiannual report on political contributions, designated the LD-203, filed by each registrant and by each listed lobbyist. The LD-203 operated on the semiannual rhythm even as activity reporting moved to quarters, a separation that reflected the different subject matter. Activity reports described lobbying for clients, while contribution reports described political money connected to the registrant and to the individual lobbyists whom the activity reports named. The report covered contributions to federal candidates, to officeholders, to leadership political action committees, and to party committees. The report also covered payments for events honoring covered officials, payments to entities named for covered officials or controlled by covered officials, and donations to presidential libraries and to inaugural committees. Each additional category closed a route through which money could honor or assist a covered official without appearing as a conventional campaign contribution. A payment for an event honoring an official, a payment to an entity that bore an official name or sat under an official control, and a donation to a presidential library or an inaugural committee each carried a different legal form from a check to a campaign committee, yet each placed resources in a setting closely associated with a public official. The LD-203 also carried a certification that had no counterpart in the quarterly activity report. Together with the contribution disclosure, the filer certified that the filer had read the Senate and House gift and travel rules and had not knowingly violated them. A report states what was given or paid, while a certification states that the filer has read a governing standard and has not knowingly breached it, and a careless certification therefore implicates the credibility of the filer in a way that an arithmetic error does not.

The fourth change tightened disclosure of coalitions and affiliated organizations that stand behind a named client. Registration under the 1995 act had required identification of an organization, other than the client itself, that contributed toward the lobbying activities and participated in directing them, because a client name alone could conceal the real source of funding and direction. An association, coalition, or ad hoc grouping could appear as the client while other organizations supplied the money and exercised control, and a filing that named only the formal client disclosed the agent without disclosing the principals who made the effort possible. The original formulation set the contribution level at more than 10,000 dollars in a semiannual period and required that the organization plan, supervise, or control the lobbying activities in whole or in major part. The amendments lowered the contribution level, shortened the measuring period, and loosened the control test. After the amendments, an organization that contributed more than 5,000 dollars in a quarter and actively participated in the planning, supervision, or control of the lobbying activities had to be identified. Control in whole or in major part had demanded a dominant share of direction before disclosure attached, while active participation demanded a genuine part in direction, without requiring that the part amount to the whole or to a major part.

The fifth change tightened the treatment of gifts and travel. The amendments barred registered lobbyists, and entities that employ or retain them, from giving gifts or travel that the rules of the relevant chamber do not permit. The formulation borrowed its content from the Senate and House rules rather than creating a free standing statutory catalogue, and that choice tied the statutory duty directly to the standards that govern members and staff as recipients. A gift that a chamber rule forbade a member or an employee to accept also became a gift that a registered lobbyist or an employing entity was barred from offering. The LD-203 certification reinforced the same linkage, since each registrant and each listed lobbyist attested semiannually to having read the gift and travel rules and to having avoided a knowing violation.

The sixth change reached outside the Lobbying Disclosure Act itself and into campaign finance disclosure, because the same legislation addressed the practice of bundling campaign contributions. A registered lobbyist who collects contributions from multiple donors and delivers or forwards them to a candidate committee, a party committee, or a leadership political action committee can exercise influence through the aggregate amount and through recipient knowledge of who assembled it, even though each individual contribution belongs to a different donor. The amendments addressed that aggregate through disclosure by the recipient committee. Readers who follow the interaction between lobbying disclosure and [campaign finance law impact](/2021/12/15/campaign-finance-law-impact/) can trace how the two disclosure regimes meet at that point. The Honest Leadership and Open Government Act amended the Federal Election Campaign Act, in a provision codified at 52 U.S.C. 30104(i), so that recipient committees disclose contributions bundled by registered lobbyists once the aggregate exceeds a threshold. The statute set the threshold at 15,000 dollars and provided for adjustment for inflation, and the Federal Election Commission set the threshold for calendar year 2021 at 19,300 dollars. The mechanism placed the reporting duty on the committee that received the bundled contributions rather than on the lobbyist who assembled them, while the Lobbying Disclosure Act itself recorded who lobbied, for whom, on what issues, and with what political contributions reported on the LD-203.

### What changed when the filing calendar moved to quarters?

The filing calendar moved from two six month periods each year to four three month periods, and the quarterly period became the measuring unit for activity reports, for monetary registration thresholds, and for the twenty percent time test that determines whether an individual counts as a lobbyist for a client.

The deeper consequence lay in the way a single interval came to govern distinct questions that the statute asks. A registrant reports lobbying activity for the period, and after January 1, 2008 that period is a quarter, so the LD-2 describes three months of issues, contacts, houses and agencies approached, lobbyists engaged, and income or expenses estimated in good faith. The statute also excuses registration for a client below a monetary level, and after the move to quarters that excuse is measured within a quarter rather than within six months. The definition of lobbyist in section 3 excludes an individual whose lobbying activities constitute less than 20 percent of the time the individual spends providing services to that client, and after the amendments that percentage is calculated over the quarterly period of three months rather than over the semiannual period of six months. An individual who concentrates lobbying into a single intense quarter, during consideration of a bill or a confirmation or an appropriations measure, faces measurement across that quarter alone, and a burst of activity that might have been diluted across six months of other services can cross the 20 percent line when the denominator covers only three months. The quarterly test therefore ties status to the period in which the contacts actually occur. The adjusted monetary thresholds illustrate the same restatement in dollar terms. The statute provides for adjustment of the thresholds for changes in the Consumer Price Index at four year intervals, and the move to quarterly measurement required the thresholds to be expressed as quarterly amounts. For the period beginning January 1, 2021, the lobbying firm income threshold stood at 3,000 dollars per quarterly period. For the same period beginning January 1, 2021, the organization expense threshold stood at 14,000 dollars per quarterly period, and that figure had been raised from 13,000 dollars by the adjustment process.

## The Revolving Door Provisions Beside the Disclosure Amendments

Title II of the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act amended the Lobbying Disclosure Act, but Title I addressed a neighboring subject through a different body of law. Title I amended the post employment restrictions at 18 U.S.C. 207, the criminal provision that governs what a former federal official may do after leaving government service. That provision descends through the lineage associated with [the Ethics in Government Act of 1978](/2021/10/15/ethics-in-government-act-1978-guide/), and it operates by prohibition rather than by registration and reporting. An extension of a ban forbids conduct outright for a stated period, while an amendment to a disclosure statute changes what a person who engages in permitted conduct must reveal about it.

The central extension doubled the duration of the ban on lobbying contacts for two categories of former officials. Before the amendments, the ban for former senators ran for one year after service ended, and the 2007 act extended that ban to two years, so a former senator was barred from making lobbying contacts for two years after leaving the Senate. Title I applied the same extension, from one year to two years, to very senior executive branch personnel, the category of former officials at the highest levels of the executive branch whom section 207 singles out for the broader restriction. For those two categories, a single year placed too short a distance between service at the highest level and paid advocacy directed back toward the institution that the official had just left or toward the government that the official had just served at a senior rank, and two years lengthened the interval during which knowledge, relationships, and standing acquired in office could not be converted into lobbying contacts. Other categories received different treatment. Former members of the House of Representatives remained under a one year ban on lobbying contacts, and the 2007 act left the House duration at one year even as it doubled the Senate duration. For Senate officers and higher paid Senate staff, the amendments changed the scope of the one year ban rather than its duration, and the ban was extended to contacts with the entire Senate. Confining the restriction to a former office or a former employing member would have left a former senior aide free to lobby the rest of the chamber immediately after departure, and extension to the entire Senate closed that route.

The structural distinction between Title I and Title II deserves emphasis because the two regimes can apply to the same person at different moments and answer different questions. The post employment restrictions at 18 U.S.C. 207 are prohibitions on contact, enforced through the criminal code. A former official inside the ban may not make the covered lobbying contacts at all, and a violation exposes the former official to criminal liability under title 18 rather than to a filing penalty under title 2. The Lobbying Disclosure Act, by contrast, imposes disclosure duties on persons who may lobby. A lobbyist who is free of any post employment ban, or whose ban has expired, registers, files quarterly activity reports, files semiannual contribution reports, and certifies compliance with gift and travel rules. A prohibition operates before the contact, by forbidding it, while disclosure operates during and after the activity, by recording it for public inspection. Title I tightened the boundary that separates government service from lobbying by former officials, lengthening or widening the interval of forbidden contact, while Title II tightened the record that governs lobbying by everyone who registers. A former senator in the second year after service illustrates the boundary, because the disclosure amendments, standing alone, would have required that former senator, if engaged as a lobbyist, to register and report, while the post employment restriction, as extended, forbade the lobbying contacts themselves during that year.

## Stronger Penalties and Standing Oversight

The penalty and oversight amendments addressed a further weakness in the 1995 design, after cadence and visibility. A disclosure statute depends on voluntary compliance supported by a credible consequence for failure and by a mechanism that detects failure without waiting for a complaint. As enacted in 1995, the Lobbying Disclosure Act supplied a civil consequence and an administrative referral path, but it supplied no criminal penalty under the act itself, and it assigned no standing audit function that would test compliance across the filing population on a regular cycle. The Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House reviewed filings, notified persons who appeared not to have complied, and referred apparent violations to the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia, and the civil fine for a knowing failure capped at 50,000 dollars. The 2007 act retained that administrative structure and altered the scale of the civil consequence, added a criminal consequence of a different character, and created two standing oversight mechanisms.

The civil change was a fourfold increase in the maximum. The amendments raised the civil fine from 50,000 dollars to 200,000 dollars for a person who knowingly failed to remedy a defective filing after notice or otherwise knowingly failed to comply with the act. The earlier maximum had stood unchanged since 1995, and the new maximum quadrupled the exposure for a single violation. The exposure applied under a provision that reaches both a failure to correct a defective filing within the period after notice from the Secretary or the Clerk and a broader knowing failure to comply.

The criminal change added a tier that had not existed under the Lobbying Disclosure Act as enacted. The amendments provided that a person who knowingly and corruptly fails to comply with the act faces imprisonment for not more than five years, a fine under title 18, or both. Knowingly requires awareness, and corruptly requires a culpable quality beyond mere awareness, so the criminal tier reaches a narrower and more blameworthy class of failure than the civil tier reaches. A negligent filing error, a good faith dispute about coverage, or a late correction after notice belongs, if anywhere, in the civil and administrative system of notice, cure, referral, and fine, while a knowing and corrupt failure, in which a filer understands the obligation and acts with a corrupt disregard of it, belongs in the criminal system. The maximum term of five years placed the offense in the range of a serious federal felony, and the alternative of a fine under title 18, alone or combined with imprisonment, gave a sentencing court the standard federal tools for calibrating punishment. Civil exposure of 200,000 dollars addressed the economics of neglect and delay, while criminal exposure addressed deliberate corruption of the record.

The oversight amendments created detection and reporting duties that run on a fixed schedule. The 2007 act assigned the Comptroller General an annual compliance audit function, to be performed by random sampling of filings. Random sampling carries a significance that a review of referred cases cannot supply, because referrals identify filings that an administrator or a reader has already flagged as defective, while a random sample draws filings without regard to suspicion, tests them against the requirements of the act, and produces an estimate of compliance across the filing population, including failures that no observer flagged. Performed annually, the audit function creates a recurring public measure of how completely lobbyists file required reports and how reliably filers document income and expenses, and that measure gives the congressional oversight committees a basis for judging whether guidance, thresholds, or penalties require further adjustment. The assignment to the Comptroller General placed the function in the legislative branch audit institution that reports to Congress, rather than in the offices that administer the filings or the office that prosecutes violations. The second oversight mechanism concerned the prosecutor rather than the filings. The amendments required the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia to report semiannually to the congressional oversight committees on enforcement actions taken under the act. That office holds the referral stream from the Secretary and the Clerk, and before the amendments a referral could pass into that office without any regular accounting back to Congress about what followed. The semiannual report requirement closed that informational loop and required the prosecuting office to describe enforcement actions taken, so that the committees charged with oversight could examine the volume and disposition of referrals. Together with the annual audit by random sampling, the semiannual enforcement report gave Congress two independent streams of information, one measuring the filing population and the other measuring the enforcement response.

The package as a whole discloses the judgment that Congress reached in 2007 about the statute enacted in 1995. The amendments retained the concept of the 1995 act without qualification, because registration remained the gateway, disclosure remained the instrument, the Secretary and the Clerk remained the administrators, and public access remained the route to confidence in the integrity of government that section 2 had announced. What the amendments changed, in cadence, in visibility, and in consequences, corresponded to the three features of the earlier design that the intervening decade of operation had placed under the greatest pressure. On cadence, semiannual reports allowed half a year of lobbying to pass before disclosure, and the amendments answered with quarterly activity reports and a quarterly measuring period for thresholds and status. On visibility, a filing that a reader could not search, sort, or download disclosed less than the fact of filing suggested, and political money connected to lobbying had sat outside the lobbying record, and the amendments answered with mandatory electronic filing in a free Internet database, with the LD-203 contribution report and its gift and travel certification, with tightened coalition identification, and with bundling disclosure in the campaign finance record. On consequences, the earlier scheme had paired a civil maximum of 50,000 dollars with no criminal tier and no standing audit of the filings, and the amendments answered with a civil maximum of 200,000 dollars, a criminal penalty of imprisonment for not more than five years, annual random sample audits by the Comptroller General, and semiannual enforcement reports from the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia. The 2007 act therefore operated on the administration of the 1995 design rather than on its concept: Congress retained disclosure as the instrument and directed the changes at the speed of filing, the accessibility of the record, the coverage of connected money, and the sanctions and oversight attached to the filing duties.

## Enforcement by Referral Rather Than by an Agency

The enforcement design of the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 divided administration from prosecution and placed each function in different hands. Under sections 6 and 7 of the 1995 act, codified at 2 U.S.C. 1605 and 1606, the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House administered the register. Those two officers reviewed registrations and reports, verified apparent accuracy, made filings available for public inspection and copying, and notified any person who appeared not to have complied with the disclosure law. The same officers then referred apparent violations to the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia. Neither officer prosecuted a violation or conducted enforcement litigation in the name of a chamber.

The distinction corrects a common assumption about the LDA. The Senate and the House did not enforce the statute themselves through their filing offices, and the disclosure law created no enforcement agency inside its own structure to investigate and punish violations. Instead, the statute relied on a sequence that began with administrative review and ended, if correction did not follow, with referral to a federal prosecutor outside Congress, the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia.

The penalty provision as enacted reflected the same emphasis on correction before punishment. A civil fine of not more than 50,000 dollars applied to a person who knowingly failed to remedy a defective filing within 60 days after notice from the Secretary or the Clerk, or who otherwise knowingly failed to comply with the statute. The 60 day period functioned as a cure window embedded in the penalty itself. Notice from a filing officer identified an apparent defect, and the recipient had 60 days to remedy that defective filing before the knowing failure described in the provision was complete on that ground. Liability was judged by a preponderance of the evidence, with regard to the extent and gravity of the violation.

The referral record described during the 2007 reform debates exposed the practical weakness of a system that depended on action after referral. Senator Christopher Dodd told the Senate Committee on Rules and Administration during those debates that since 2003 the Senate Office of Public Records had referred more than 2,000 cases to the Department of Justice with no visible response. The statement, offered by Dodd to the committee, measured the distance between identification of apparent violations by the filing office and observable enforcement by the receiving department. Reporting by the Government Accountability Office supplied a related description of prosecutorial practice after referral. The United States Attorney's Office for the District of Columbia described its practice, in that reporting, as focused on bringing delinquent filers into compliance through notification and follow up rather than on prosecution, and civil settlements formed a small share of referrals. That practice explained why referral totals and penalty totals could diverge sharply, since notification and follow up could close a matter through filing or correction without a settlement or a fine.

## What the Audits Found

The Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007 required the Comptroller General to audit compliance annually by random sampling and to report to Congress, and the resulting audits by the Government Accountability Office supplied systematic evidence about how the filing system operated in practice. The Government Accountability Office's 2016 annual audit covered filings from 2015 and estimated that 88 percent of lobbyists filed the initial LD-2 reports required for new registrations. The same audit estimated that 93 percent of filers could provide documentation for reported income and expenses. For a separate contribution report, the audit estimated that 85 percent of lobbyists filed the year end 2014 LD-203 reports as required. Those estimates described the routine machinery of the LDA at a high level of operation for most sampled filers in each category.

The 2016 audit also identified a recurring defect in the content of filed activity reports. An estimated 21 percent of LD-2 reports may not have properly disclosed one or more previously held covered positions. The LD-2 report required disclosure of covered executive branch and legislative branch positions previously held by listed lobbyists, information that connected an advocate's client work with earlier service in a covered government position. The defect concerned completeness within a filed report, and the estimate placed that defect in roughly one fifth of the sampled reports, a share large enough to limit the value of the covered position disclosure even where filing had occurred.

Government Accountability Office report GAO-19-357, issued in March 2019, examined LD-2 and LD-203 reports filed in the third and fourth quarters of 2017 and the first and second quarters of 2018. The report estimated that 92 percent of lobbyists who filed new registrations also filed the LD-2 report required for that quarter, and that 97 percent of filers could provide documentation for lobbying income and expenses. Those estimates exceeded the corresponding estimates in the 2016 audit of 2015 filings, with initial filing rising from 88 percent to 92 percent and documentation rising from 93 percent to 97 percent across the two audits. GAO-19-357 nevertheless found a detail level defect of a different kind, because an estimated 20 percent of LD-2 reports were not properly rounded to the nearest 10,000 dollars as House and Senate filing guidance directs. Rounding governed the presentation of good faith estimates of income and expenses, and a report could therefore be filed, documented, and substantively identifiable while departing from the prescribed form for stating its financial figures.

The two audits shared a pattern that neither set of figures erased. The routine machinery of the disclosure law worked for most filers in most categories measured by the Government Accountability Office in both periods. The recurring problems sat at a different level of compliance, in the completeness of covered position disclosure in the 2016 audit and in adherence to rounding guidance in GAO-19-357. Both defects reduced the comparability and completeness of the public record without indicating that the filing system had failed for the typical registrant covered by the samples. A further limit defined both audits, because the Government Accountability Office audited reports and filers inside the registration system through random sampling. The audits measured filed reports rather than activity that never entered the system at all, and the percentages did not, by themselves, measure the share of all influence activity that the LDA captured.

## The Constitutional Litigation

Lobbying regulation in the United States stands on constitutional ground shaped by the First Amendment right to petition the government for a redress of grievances. A paid advocate who communicates with a member of Congress or an executive branch official on behalf of a client engages in petitioning activity, and a law that requires registration and disclosure regulates the conditions under which that activity is identified to the public and to the government. The constitutional question was therefore not whether petitioning could occur, but whether government could require the person petitioning for compensation to disclose the client, the subject matter, and related information without violating the First Amendment. The LDA answered that question through disclosure rather than through a prohibition on contacts or a ban on representation by any category of advocate.

United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612, decided in 1954, supplied the foundational decision for that framework through litigation over the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946, the predecessor statute that the 1995 act later repealed. Chief Justice Earl Warren wrote for the Supreme Court, and the Court upheld the 1946 act against a vagueness challenge only by construing the predecessor narrowly. The construction that saved the act is set out in full in the account of the predecessor statute earlier in this article: the Court confined the 1946 act to contributions solicited or received for the main purpose of influencing legislation through direct communication with members of Congress, and the exclusions that followed from that confinement defined the registration problem Congress legislated against in 1995. The doctrinal point carried into the later statute is the method. The Court preserved a disclosure law by narrowing it rather than by invalidating it, and no later decision disturbed that settlement.

No decision has held the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 unconstitutional, and Harriss remained the foundational construction case for the statutory lineage that produced the LDA. The later decision in National Association of Manufacturers v. Taylor, 582 F.3d 1, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit in 2009, addressed the tightened coalition disclosure requirement enacted by the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007. The 2007 amendments required identification of an organization that contributed more than 5,000 dollars in a quarterly period toward lobbying activities and actively participated in the planning, supervision, or control of those activities, replacing the earlier standard of more than 10,000 dollars in a semiannual period combined with planning, supervision, or control in whole or in major part. The District of Columbia Circuit upheld the tightened requirement against a First Amendment challenge and held the disclosure obligation permissible as applied to organizations that funded and actively participated in lobbying.

The transparency argument, at its strongest, rested on the findings Congress placed in section 2 of the 1995 act. Congress found that earlier lobbying disclosure statutes had been ineffective because of unclear statutory language, weak administrative and enforcement provisions, and an absence of clear guidance about who must register and what must be disclosed. Congress also found that effective public disclosure of the identity and extent of the efforts of paid lobbyists to influence the public decisionmaking process would increase public confidence in the integrity of government. In the form presented by Congress and sustained in the coalition disclosure litigation, disclosure allowed officials and members of the public to know who paid for advocacy, which client stood behind a contact, and which organizations financed and directed a campaign while remaining behind a named registrant.

The petition rights argument, at its strongest, proceeded from the proposition advanced by the National Association of Manufacturers as challenger in Taylor that compelled disclosure burdens the act of petitioning and exposes the associations behind advocacy to identification because of participation in collective efforts to influence government. In that argument, a coalition allows organizations with shared interests to combine resources and advance a common position, and a requirement to identify a funding organization that actively participates can alter the willingness of organizations to join, finance, or direct such an effort. The argument also drew force from Harriss, where the Supreme Court confined registration to a narrow class of direct lobbying supported by solicited or received contributions. Presented in full, the petition rights position treated disclosure as a condition imposed on protected advocacy rather than as a neutral recordkeeping rule. The District of Columbia Circuit resolved the challenge in Taylor by upholding the requirement as applied to organizations that funded lobbying and actively participated in its planning, supervision, or control, leaving the 1995 act and the 2007 coalition amendment in force.

## The Separate Regime for Foreign Principals

The Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938 imposed a separate registration regime for agents of foreign principals, and the differences between that regime and the LDA explain a recurring source of confusion in lobbying law. Congress enacted the Foreign Agents Registration Act on June 8, 1938, as Public Law 75-583, 52 Stat. 631, amid congressional concern about foreign propaganda in the United States before the Second World War. The act was codified at 22 U.S.C. 611 to 621. Administration belonged to the Department of Justice, acting through the FARA Unit of the National Security Division, rather than to the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House. A registrant under the foreign agents statute registered with the Attorney General, not with the congressional filing officers who administered the LDA register.

Coverage under the Foreign Agents Registration Act turned on agency for a foreign principal and on the character of the work performed for that principal. A person who agreed to act as an agent of a foreign principal and who engaged in political activities, acted as public relations counsel, acted as a publicity agent, acted as an information service employee, acted as a political consultant, solicited, collected, or dispensed money or anything of value, or represented the interests of the principal before an agency or official of the United States Government was required to register with the Attorney General within 10 days of agreeing to act. The registration duty attached at the point of agreement rather than after a period of activity had accumulated under a quarterly or semiannual measuring rule.

The absence of thresholds distinguished the 1938 statute most sharply from the disclosure law enacted in 1995. The Foreign Agents Registration Act contained no time threshold and no monetary threshold, and the Congressional Research Service described the act as reaching agents regardless of how small the time or money involved was. Compensation was not required for coverage. An agent could therefore fall within the foreign agents regime for a limited engagement, for unpaid work, or for activity involving a small sum, provided the statutory relationship to a foreign principal and a covered role existed. The LDA, by contrast, organized registration around paid lobbying contacts, monetary exemptions, and a definition of lobbyist that excluded an individual whose lobbying activities constituted less than 20 percent of the time spent providing services to a client during the measuring period.

Disclosure under the Foreign Agents Registration Act also included duties directed at the materials distributed for a foreign principal. Registrants were required to file copies of informational materials with the Attorney General and to label those materials. Section 9 of the 1995 act amended the foreign agents statute by replacing the term political propaganda with informational materials and by carrying forward an exemption from foreign agents registration for agents registered under the LDA. Criminal exposure reinforced those duties, because a willful violation of the Foreign Agents Registration Act was a federal crime under 22 U.S.C. 618. The criminal character of willful noncompliance under the 1938 statute contrasted with the LDA as enacted in 1995, which imposed a civil fine under its penalty provision and contained no criminal penalty before the amendments of 2007 added a criminal provision to the domestic disclosure regime.

Eight statutory exemptions from foreign agents registration appeared at 22 U.S.C. 613, and subsection 613(h) supplied the exemption most likely to be confused with a general transfer between the two systems. Subsection 613(h) exempted a person engaged in lobbying activities for a foreign principal who had registered under the LDA. Regulation 28 C.F.R. 5.307 limited that exemption in a decisive respect, because the exemption was never available where a foreign government or a foreign political party was the foreign principal or the principal beneficiary of the work. The exemption therefore routed private foreign commercial representation into the LDA system where the statutory conditions were met, while government directed representation remained inside the Foreign Agents Registration Act. The distinction between the regimes rested on the identity of the principal, the administrator of the register, the trigger for registration, the presence or absence of thresholds, and the consequences of willful violation. Conflation of the two regimes produced errors in each direction, by understating the reach of the Foreign Agents Registration Act where a foreign government stood behind the work and by overstating the reach of the LDA where a filing belonged in the foreign agents system.

## A Threshold That Can Be Dropped Below

The statute defines a lobbyist by a proportion of time rather than by function, so the disclosure regime can be exited by delegating contacts while retaining the strategy, and the resulting gap is a consequence of the definition rather than of evasion. Section 3 of the 1995 act, codified at 2 U.S.C. 1602(10), defined a lobbyist as an individual employed or retained by a client for financial or other compensation whose services included more than one lobbying contact, excluding an individual whose lobbying activities constituted less than 20 percent of the time the individual spent providing services to that client. As enacted, the measuring period was the semiannual period of six months. After the amendments of 2007, the measuring period became the quarterly period of three months. The percentage test therefore asked how an individual's time for a particular client was divided, not whether the individual devised the message, selected the targets, or supervised the campaign.

Lobbying activities, as defined at 2 U.S.C. 1602(7), included lobbying contacts and efforts in support of contacts, including preparation and planning activities, research and other background work intended at the time performed for use in contacts, and coordination with the lobbying activities of others. The breadth of that activity definition coexisted with a threshold that measured the individual's lobbying activities against all services provided to the same client. A senior adviser could spend the largest share of an engagement on strategy, preparation, supervision, or direction of a campaign and a smaller share on activities counted toward the threshold, particularly where direct contacts with covered officials were assigned to colleagues. Delegation altered the distribution of contacts without necessarily altering the distribution of influence over the campaign, and the person who designed the approach and directed the sequence of contacts could remain outside the definition of a lobbyist for that client if the statutory percentage was not crossed, while the person who made the contacts appeared in the register.

Research by Timothy M. LaPira of James Madison University and Herschel F. Thomas, discussed by the Congressional Research Service in report R44292, attempted to measure the scale of activity outside disclosure for 2012. LaPira and Thomas estimated that about 52 percent of the people engaged in policy advocacy in the sample examined had not disclosed lobbying activity. The same research estimated total spending on lobbying activity at about 6.7 billion dollars, against about 3.3 billion dollars disclosed under the LDA. The comparison placed disclosed spending at roughly half of estimated total spending on lobbying activity in 2012. The Congressional Research Service presented the estimates as research evidence about the possible scale of undisclosed activity rather than as an official count of violations, and activity below the statutory threshold or outside the definition of a lobbying contact could contribute to the gap without establishing a violation in any individual case.

Registration counts supplied a second line of evidence and a necessary caution. The Center for Responsive Politics count of registered lobbyists, which had reached the record level set out in the account of the public record earlier in this article, had fallen by 2014 to 11,761, the lowest total the Center had reported since 1998. The decline between 2007 and 2014 was consistent with a movement of some advocacy work outside the register. The Congressional Research Service cautioned, however, that the decline in registrations was possible but not conclusive evidence of growth in unregistered lobbying, because administration policy changes and economic conditions also moved the count.

The grassroots exclusion illustrated another boundary drawn by definition rather than by concealment. A lobbying contact under the LDA was an oral or written communication, including an electronic communication, to a covered executive branch official or a covered legislative branch official, made on behalf of a client regarding specified federal legislation, rules, regulations, Executive orders, programs, policies, administration, contracts, grants, loans, permits, licenses, or nominations. A communication to the public was not a lobbying contact at all under that definition. An organization could therefore conduct a campaign designed to move public opinion and generate pressure on officials through constituents without the public communication itself becoming a lobbying contact. The distinction carried forward a boundary recognized in Harriss, where indirect or grassroots influence fell outside the predecessor act as narrowly construed by the Supreme Court in 1954.

The counter reading required equal weight and rested on the record that registration produced where the definition applied. On that reading, the regime was incomplete rather than ineffective, because the registered system produced a substantial public record that did not exist before 1995 and was used constantly by journalists and researchers. Before the LDA replaced the pre-1995 patchwork, no single uniform scheme supplied the quarterly activity reports, contribution reports, searchable electronic filings, client identifications, issue descriptions, and covered position disclosures that the later system generated. Incompleteness identified the limits of the statutory definition, particularly the 20 percent test, the monetary exemptions, and the exclusion of public communications from the definition of a lobbying contact. Ineffectiveness would have described a system that failed to produce usable disclosure for the activity Congress placed inside the definition, a description contradicted by the high filing and documentation estimates in the audits of 2015 filings and of filings from 2017 and 2018. The threshold therefore performed two functions at once, giving a clear administrative line for determining which individuals had to be listed as lobbyists for a client, in line with the congressional finding in section 2 that earlier law had suffered from an absence of clear guidance about who must register, while also making function subordinate to allocation of time. The gap followed from the words of the definition, and classification of the gap as a definitional consequence preserved the distinction between lawful activity outside a statutory category and a knowing failure to comply by a person inside that category.

## The Statute's Operative Shape

The Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 survives as the framework statute of federal lobbying regulation because its central design choices have never been reversed. Congress in 1995 chose disclosure over prohibition, chose an activity-based definition over the intent-based definition that the Supreme Court had narrowed in 1954, and chose administration by the filing officers of the two chambers with enforcement referred outward to a prosecutor. The Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007 kept each of those choices and repaired the weaknesses a decade of operation had exposed: the filing calendar, the public accessibility of the record, the absence of contribution disclosure, and the modesty of the penalties. What no amendment has altered is the threshold at the center of the definition, and so the statute's permanent tension is the one built into it at enactment. A regime that registers advocates by the share of their time spent on contacts will always describe the contact layer of influence work with precision while leaving the strategy layer, where the contacts are planned and directed, substantially outside its field of vision. That incompleteness is a property of the definition, documented in the oversight literature and in the estimates of researchers who have measured the undisclosed remainder, and it coexists with the regime's documented product: a continuous, public, congressionally maintained record of paid influence work that had no equivalent before 1996 and that journalists, researchers, and the Government Accountability Office draw on as a matter of routine. The reader continuing into the cluster will find the study path assembled in [the United States legislation study guide](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/), which places this statute among the transparency and campaign finance measures it interacts with and sequences the primary sources for each.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who must register under the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

Registration is required when an individual meets the statute's three-part definition of a lobbyist for a particular client: the individual is employed or retained by the client for financial or other compensation, the services include more than one lobbying contact with a covered official, and lobbying activities amount to at least 20 percent of the time the individual spends serving that client during the measuring period. The registration itself is filed by the employer or the lobbying firm, not by the individual, within 45 days of the first contact or of the engagement, whichever comes earlier, and a separate registration covers each client. Small engagements fall out through a monetary exemption for minimal income or expenses.

### Q: What is the 20 percent threshold in the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The threshold is the time-share element of the lobbyist definition. An individual who is employed or retained by a client and who makes more than one lobbying contact is nevertheless excluded from the definition if lobbying activities constitute less than 20 percent of the time the individual spends providing services to that client. As enacted in 1995 the share was measured over a six-month period; the 2007 amendments substituted the quarterly period. Lobbying activities, the numerator of the fraction, include the contacts themselves plus preparation, planning, research, and coordination performed to support them, so the measure reaches more than the hours spent in meetings with officials.

### Q: What did the Lobbying Disclosure Act replace?

The 1995 act replaced the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946, which it repealed outright, together with lobbying registration provisions scattered through other statutes, including provisions in the housing laws. The 1946 statute had been Title III of the Legislative Reorganization Act of that year. It required registration by persons who solicited, collected, or received money where a principal purpose was influencing the passage or defeat of legislation. The replacement was not a repair of that scheme but a new architecture: a single uniform statute, an activity-based definition in place of a purpose-based one, coverage of executive branch officials as well as Congress, and systematic reporting of clients, issues, and money.

### Q: Why was the 1946 lobbying law unenforceable before the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The 1946 statute's language invited a narrow reading, and in United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612 (1954), the Supreme Court supplied one. To save the act from unconstitutional vagueness, the Court confined it to persons who solicited or received contributions, where a main purpose was influencing legislation, and only through direct communication with members of Congress on pending or proposed legislation. That construction excluded anyone who spent funds without soliciting or receiving contributions from others, anyone communicating with congressional staff rather than members, and all indirect or grassroots influence. Because so much professional influence work fell outside those three limits, registration became effectively a matter of choice for most of the activity the earlier statute had been written to reach, and Congress recorded in the findings of the 1995 act that the earlier statutes had been ineffective.

### Q: What did the 2007 amendments add to the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007, Public Law 110-81, kept the 1995 architecture and strengthened its operation. Activity reports moved from a semiannual to a quarterly calendar, filing became electronic, and the reports were placed on the Internet in searchable, sortable, and downloadable form. A new semiannual report, the LD-203, required registrants and listed lobbyists to disclose specified political contributions and to certify compliance with the chamber gift and travel rules. The coalition disclosure threshold was tightened, disclosure of contributions bundled by lobbyists was added through an amendment to the Federal Election Campaign Act, the civil fine rose from 50,000 dollars to 200,000 dollars, a criminal penalty for knowing and corrupt violations was created, and the Government Accountability Office received an annual audit mandate.

### Q: Is shadow lobbying outside the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

Activity described as shadow lobbying sits outside the registration system by operation of the definitions rather than by concealment. The statute registers individuals whose services for a client include more than one lobbying contact and whose lobbying activities reach at least 20 percent of their service time for that client. A person who advises on strategy, prepares the materials, directs the campaign, or supervises the registered lobbyists while personally making few or no contacts fails one of those elements and never enters the register. Researchers Timothy LaPira and Herschel Thomas estimated that in 2012 about 52 percent of the policy advocates in their sample had disclosed no lobbying activity, which measures the size of the space the definition leaves outside the filings.

### Q: Who enforces the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

Enforcement is divided, and no single agency owns it. The Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House administer the system: they issue guidance, receive and review registrations and reports, make the filings public, notify persons who appear not to have complied, and refer apparent violations to the United States Attorney for the District of Columbia. Prosecution and civil enforcement belong to that United States Attorney, not to the two chambers, which corrects the common assumption that Congress polices the register itself. The Government Accountability Office adds a standing check by auditing compliance annually through random sampling, and since 2007 the United States Attorney has owed the congressional oversight committees a semiannual account of enforcement actions taken.

### Q: How is FARA different from the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938 governs agents of foreign principals, and nearly every design choice differs. FARA is administered by the Department of Justice rather than by the congressional filing officers. Its trigger is an agency relationship with a foreign principal, not a share of working time, and it contains no time threshold and no monetary threshold; compensation is not required. Registration is due within 10 days of agreeing to act, informational materials must be filed and labeled, and willful violation is a federal crime. A person registered under the Lobbying Disclosure Act is exempt from FARA for that lobbying work, but the exemption is unavailable where a foreign government or a foreign political party is the principal or the principal beneficiary.

### Q: What is a lobbying contact under the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

A lobbying contact is an oral or written communication, including an electronic communication, made to a covered executive branch official or a covered legislative branch official on behalf of a client, concerning the formulation, modification, or adoption of federal legislation, rules, regulations, or Executive orders; a federal program, policy, or position of the United States Government; the administration or execution of a federal program or policy, including contracts, grants, loans, permits, and licenses; or the nomination or confirmation of a person subject to Senate confirmation. The definition then carves out a list of exceptions, among them testimony, communications compelled by law or subpoena, requests for the status of a matter, communications distributed through public media, and requests for a meeting, so the category reaches advocacy directed at officials rather than every interaction with government.

### Q: Who counts as a covered official under the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The statute lists two families of officials. Covered legislative branch officials include members of Congress, the elected officers of each House, and employees of members, committees, and leadership offices, together with other designated legislative branch staff. Covered executive branch officials include the President and the Vice President, officers and employees of the Executive Office of the President, officials serving at levels I to V of the Executive Schedule, members of the Senior Executive Service, Schedule C political appointees, and commissioned officers of the uniformed services at pay grade O-7 or above. The categories matter because a communication counts as a lobbying contact only when it is directed to an official inside them; approaches to state officials or to junior federal staff fall outside the definition.

### Q: What information does a registration statement under the Lobbying Disclosure Act contain?

A registration, filed on the form known as the LD-1, identifies the registrant and the client by name, address, and principal place of business, and gives a general description of the business or activities of each. It states the general issue areas in which the registrant expects to engage, or has already engaged, for that client. It names each individual expected to act as a lobbyist and discloses covered executive or legislative branch positions that individual previously held. It also requires disclosure behind the client: any organization other than the client that contributes more than the statutory amount toward the lobbying and participates in planning, supervising, or controlling it, and specified information about foreign entities with an ownership or directing interest in the client or in such an organization.

### Q: What does a quarterly lobbying report disclose under the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The quarterly activity report, the LD-2, is filed for each client and reports the specific issues on which lobbying occurred, including bill numbers where a bill was the subject, together with the Houses of Congress and the federal agencies that were contacted and the names of the lobbyists who acted. It carries a good faith estimate of the money involved: income from the client in the case of a lobbying firm, or total lobbying expenses in the case of an organization lobbying on its own behalf, with small amounts permitted to be reported as less than 5,000 dollars. The report discloses the existence, targets, subjects, and scale of the work. It does not disclose the content of any communication, the advice behind it, or the response of the official approached.

### Q: What monetary thresholds exempt small-scale lobbying from registration under the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

Alongside the time threshold, the act carries a de minimis monetary exemption. As enacted, registration was not required for a client if a lobbying firm's total income for lobbying activities for that client did not exceed 5,000 dollars in the semiannual period, or if an organization's total lobbying expenses did not exceed 20,000 dollars in that period. The statute ordered both figures adjusted for changes in the Consumer Price Index on January 1, 1997 and every fourth year after, rounded to the nearest 500 dollars. After the 2007 move to quarterly periods, the adjusted thresholds in force for the period beginning January 1, 2021 stood at 3,000 dollars of firm income per quarter and 14,000 dollars of organization expenses per quarter.

### Q: What counts as lobbying activities besides the contacts themselves under the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The statutory term lobbying activities is deliberately wider than the contacts it supports. It includes efforts performed in support of lobbying contacts, among them preparation and planning activities, research and other background work that is intended, at the time it is performed, for use in contacts, and coordination with the lobbying activities of others. The breadth matters in two places. It supplies the numerator of the 20 percent time test, so an individual whose week is filled with drafting, analysis, and strategy sessions for a client's campaign may cross the threshold even though the contacts themselves occupy little time. It also defines the expense base that organizations report, so the money figures in the filings capture the support structure around a contact and not merely the meeting.

### Q: What penalties apply for violating the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The penalty structure has two tiers, and both were set in their operative form by the 2007 amendments. A person who knowingly fails to remedy a defective filing within 60 days after notice from the Secretary of the Senate or the Clerk of the House, or otherwise knowingly fails to comply with the act, faces a civil fine of not more than 200,000 dollars, assessed on proof by a preponderance of the evidence with regard to the extent and gravity of the violation; before 2007 the ceiling was 50,000 dollars. A person who knowingly and corruptly fails to comply faces the criminal tier: imprisonment for not more than five years, a fine under title 18 of the United States Code, or both.

### Q: How are bundled contributions by lobbyists disclosed?

Bundling means collecting contributions from other donors and forwarding them, or receiving credit for them, as a package delivered to a candidate or committee. The 2007 amendments addressed it through the campaign finance statute rather than through the disclosure act itself: an amendment to the Federal Election Campaign Act, codified at 52 U.S.C. 30104(i), requires candidate committees, party committees, and leadership political action committees to report contributions bundled by registered lobbyists and by political action committees connected to registrants once the bundled total passes a threshold set at 15,000 dollars in the statute and adjusted for inflation. The Federal Election Commission set the threshold for calendar year 2021 at 19,300 dollars. The duty falls on the recipient committee, which is the party positioned to know what it received.

### Q: Does the Lobbying Disclosure Act cover grassroots lobbying campaigns?

No, and the reason is definitional. A lobbying contact must be a communication directed to a covered official. A campaign that addresses the public, urging citizens to telephone or write to their representatives, communicates with the public rather than with an official, so the campaign itself generates no lobbying contacts and no registration duty for the people who design or fund it as such. The expenditure side is narrower than a full exclusion: where an organization is registered and reports its lobbying expenses, the costs of its lobbying activities enter its filings under the rules governing those reports. But the statute contains no register of grassroots campaigns, and the volume of public-facing advocacy a client conducts is not a figure the disclosure system was built to capture.

### Q: What did the Government Accountability Office find about compliance with the Lobbying Disclosure Act?

The Government Accountability Office has audited compliance annually since the 2007 amendments assigned it that duty, working from random samples of filed reports. Its 2016 audit of 2015 filings estimated that 88 percent of lobbyists filed the initial activity reports required for new registrations and that 85 percent filed the year-end contribution reports for 2014 as required, while an estimated 21 percent of activity reports may not have properly disclosed previously held covered positions. Report GAO-19-357, issued in March 2019, estimated that 92 percent of lobbyists who filed new registrations also filed the activity report required for that quarter, that 97 percent of filers could document reported income and expenses, and that about 20 percent of reports were not rounded as the filing guidance directs.

### Q: How long must a former senator wait before lobbying Congress?

Two years. The restriction is not part of the Lobbying Disclosure Act itself but of the post-employment provisions at 18 U.S.C. 207, which the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007 amended alongside the disclosure changes. As amended, a former senator may not make lobbying contacts with members, officers, or employees of either House, or with employees of other legislative offices, during the two years after leaving office; before 2007 the Senate ban ran for one year. Former members of the House remained under a one-year ban, and the same 2007 act extended the two-year period to very senior executive branch personnel for contacts with their former departments and agencies. The distinction matters because section 207 prohibits the contact outright, while the disclosure act regulates transparency for contacts that are permitted.

### Q: What should a student remember about the Lobbying Disclosure Act for an exam?

The examinable core is the definition and the reason it is built the way it is. The act is Public Law 104-65, signed on December 19, 1995 and effective January 1, 1996, codified at 2 U.S.C. 1601 to 1614. It replaced the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946 after United States v. Harriss had narrowed that statute to direct communications with members by persons handling contributions. A lobbyist is an individual employed or retained by a client who makes more than one lobbying contact and whose lobbying activities reach 20 percent of service time for that client. Registration follows within 45 days, reports disclose clients, issues, institutions contacted, and money, and the 2007 amendments added quarterly filing, contribution reports, and higher penalties without changing the 20 percent proportion.
