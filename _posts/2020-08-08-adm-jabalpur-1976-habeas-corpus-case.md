The adm jabalpur 1976 case, decided as Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla on 28 April 1976, stands as the judgment in which the Supreme Court of India held, by four votes to one, that a person detained during the Emergency could not move a court for habeas corpus on the ground that the detention took away life or liberty without a fair legal basis, because enforcement of Article 21 stood suspended under a Presidential order issued under Article 359. The majority spoke through Chief Justice A. N. Ray and Justices M. H. Beg, Y. V. Chandrachud and P. N. Bhagwati. Justice H. R. Khanna dissented alone. The claim defended here is the one the record supports most completely: the verdict operationalised an institutional failure pattern with four linked features, namely acceptance of the Article 21 suspension, deference to the executive order as beyond questioning, a dissent answered within months by supersession, and a reversal path that ran through Maneka Gandhi in 1978, the Forty-fourth Amendment and the nine-judge bench in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India on 24 August 2017.

## The Night of 25 June 1975 and the Order That Followed

The sequence began with a judgment and a proclamation. On 12 June 1975 Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha of the Allahabad High Court set aside Indira Gandhi's 1971 election from Rae Bareli for corrupt practices under the Representation of the People Act, a ruling traced in [the Allahabad judgment of June 1975 and its aftermath](/2019/04/17/allahabad-judgment-june-1975-indira/). On the night of 25 June 1975 President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed signed a proclamation of Emergency under Article 352 on the ground of internal disturbance, and the machinery described in [the Emergency of 1975 to 1977 reanalysed as a political settlement](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/) moved within hours. Police arrested opposition leaders, trade unionists, journalists and students under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, known as MISA, and under the Defence of India Rules. A second Presidential order of 27 June 1975, issued under Article 359, suspended the right of any person to move a court for enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22 for the period of the Emergency. A further order of 8 January 1976 suspended enforcement of Article 19 as well.

Article 359 did one precise thing and the whole case turned on its exact reach. The Article empowers the President, during an Emergency, to suspend the right to move a court for enforcement of named fundamental rights. It does not erase the rights from the text. It bars the courtroom door to petitions founded on them. The government of Indira Gandhi treated that bar as total in matters of detention: if enforcement of Article 21 stood suspended, then a detainee held under MISA could not ask a High Court to test whether the detention rested on legal authority, whether the detaining officer had applied his mind, or whether the order was passed from malice. Detenus and their families filed habeas corpus petitions in High Courts across the country anyway, and the High Courts, to a remarkable extent, agreed to hear them.

### Which High Courts had ruled for the detainees before the appeal?

Nine High Courts, among them Allahabad, Bombay, Delhi, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Punjab and Rajasthan, held that habeas petitions remained maintainable despite the Article 359 order, reasoning that a detainee could still show the detention order broke the detention statute or issued from malice, because suspension of a right's enforcement did not license detention without legal authority.

That near-uniform answer from the High Courts set the stage for the appeal. The Union of India and state governments carried the question to the Supreme Court, where a Constitution Bench of five judges heard argument through the winter of 1975 and 1976. The appellants included the Additional District Magistrate of Jabalpur, whose detention order against Shivkant Shukla, a detainee held under MISA in Madhya Pradesh, had been tested before the Madhya Pradesh High Court. The case name that entered legal history, ADM Jabalpur, came from that Jabalpur appeal, though the bench decided the fate of petitions from many states in a single hearing.

The hearing carried a weight that the lawyers in the room understood plainly. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), placed the habeas corpus litigation at the centre of the Emergency chapters, treating the petitions as the last institutional channel left open after Parliament, the press and the opposition had each been subdued by arrests, censorship and constitutional amendment. The Thirty-eighth Amendment of 1975 had barred judicial review of the Emergency proclamation itself. The Thirty-ninth Amendment had placed the Prime Minister's election beyond court scrutiny, a clause the Supreme Court struck down in Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain in November 1975 while upholding the election on the amended law. Against that record, the habeas petitions asked a narrower and harder question: could a court ask the state to justify taking a named person's liberty, when the Constitution's own emergency chapter had been used to close the usual door?

## The Question Framed by the Bench

The bench framed the issue with care, and the framing shaped the answer. The question, as Chief Justice Ray stated it, asked whether, in view of the Presidential order under Article 359 suspending enforcement of Article 21, any person could move a writ petition under Article 226 before a High Court for habeas corpus or any other writ to challenge the legality of a detention order on the ground that the order was not under, or in compliance with, the detention Act, or was illegal, or was vitiated by mala fides. Every element of the failure pattern sits inside that framing. The petitioner's complaint was never only that Article 21 had been breached in the abstract. The complaint was concrete: this officer signed this order, on these grounds, against this man, and the statute or the facts do not carry it.

Attorney General Niren De, arguing for the Union, pressed the widest reading of Article 359. His submission, recorded in the judgment, held that the object of Article 359 was to take away, for the Emergency period, the locus standi of any person to complain of deprivation of life or liberty, and that the suspension covered even a complaint that a detention order was mala fide or beyond the statute. The exchange that fixed the case in public memory came when Justice Khanna pressed the Attorney General on where the argument ended. Asked whether there could be a remedy if a police officer, acting from personal enmity, killed a detainee, Niren De answered in terms the judgment records: consistently with his submissions, there would be no remedy by way of proceedings in a court of law, an answer he gave while saying that the conclusion shocked his conscience as it might shock the bench's. The government of the day did not soften the submission in later argument. Senior counsel for the states aligned with the Union's reading.

For the detenus, a battery of senior counsel including Soli Sorabjee, V. M. Tarkunde, C. K. Daphtary and Shanti Bhushan argued the opposite architecture. Their submission treated Article 21 as one expression of a deeper rule: the executive may take life or liberty only under authority of law, a principle that predates the Constitution, runs through the common law and binds governments whether or not a fundamental right can be enforced in a given season. On that reading, the Article 359 order suspended a remedy attached to named Articles; it did not create a power to detain without law, and it did not repeal the detention statutes or the courts' power to insist that an officer stay inside them. The nine High Court judgments, delivered through late 1975 and early 1976, had largely accepted versions of that argument.

### What did the Attorney General concede in the hearing's hardest exchange?

Niren De conceded the full reach of his own submission: if a police officer killed a detainee from personal enmity during the Emergency, no court proceeding could lie on that score while the Article 359 order stood, because the suspension took away every person's standing to complain of a deprivation of life or liberty in court.

The hearing closed in February 1976. Judgment came on 28 April 1976. By then the Emergency had run for ten months, MISA had been amended to bar disclosure of detention grounds to the detainee in many cases, and the Forty-first Amendment debates and the Swaran Singh Committee's work pointed toward deeper constitutional surgery. The five judges delivered five separate opinions, four for the majority and one in dissent, running together to a text that lawyers still read as the low watermark of the Indian Supreme Court.

## Feature One: Acceptance of the Article 21 Suspension

The first feature of the pattern is suspension acceptance, and its content can be stated exactly. The majority held that Article 21 was the sole repository of the right to life and personal liberty against the state, that the Presidential order under Article 359 had suspended enforcement of that Article, and that, in consequence, no person held standing to move a court complaining of deprivation of life or liberty, even on the ground that the deprivation came without authority of law or from a mala fide order. Each limb mattered. The sole-repository limb did the deepest work, because it converted a remedial suspension into something close to a suspension of the right's legal existence for detention purposes. If the right lives only inside Article 21, and Article 21 cannot be enforced, then the right, for a detainee in a cell, has no address at which a court can reach it.

Chief Justice Ray's opinion carried the formal logic furthest. Ray treated the Constitution's text as a complete code on the point: the framers had placed life and liberty in Article 21, had qualified them by procedure established by law, and had provided in Article 359 for suspension of enforcement during an Emergency. Liberty, in the Chief Justice's treatment, was the gift of the Constitution and the law, and what the text gave on conditions the text could withdraw from the courtroom on other conditions. Justice Beg's opinion added an administrative trust note: the majority could assume, Beg wrote, that the state and its officers would act with a care for the detainee's welfare approaching a parent's care for a child, a passage that Austin's 1999 history quotes as evidence of how far deference travelled inside the majority's reasoning. The sentence matters because it replaced scrutiny with confidence at precisely the point where the petitions alleged abuse.

Justice Chandrachud reasoned from institutional consequence. If habeas petitions stayed open on grounds such as mala fides, he held, the Article 359 order would lose its purpose; courts would re-enter detention review through a side door the proclamation had shut. Justice Bhagwati, the junior judge on the bench, wrote in praise of the Emergency's discipline in passages he later regretted in public, and joined the holding that the petitions could not be entertained. The four opinions differ in route and in rhetoric. They converge on the operative direction: the appeals were allowed, the High Court judgments were set aside, and the habeas petitions failed at the threshold, without any inquiry into the merits of the detentions themselves.

The doctrinal setting explains why the holding landed with such force. In [the basic structure doctrine's establishment at Kesavananda Bharati in 1973](/2020/08/02/kesavananda-bharati-1973-reanalysed/), a thirteen-judge bench had held that Parliament's amending power stopped at the Constitution's basic structure, and Justice Khanna's opinion there had supplied the decisive formulation. Three years later, in ADM Jabalpur, the question was not amendment but suspension, and the majority treated the emergency provisions as self-contained: Article 359 meant what it said, and what it said ended the detainee's day in court. The contrast between the two benches frames the disagreement that scholars have argued since. T. R. Andhyarujina, in The Kesavananda Bharati Case (2011), traced how the personnel and the politics of the post-1973 court, including the 1973 supersession that made Ray Chief Justice over three senior colleagues, formed the institutional setting in which the 1976 bench sat.

Suspension acceptance also carried a factual premise that the majority did not test. The premise held that detention during the Emergency proceeded under law, so the loss was of a remedy rather than of legality itself. The Shah Commission, appointed after March 1977 under Justice J. C. Shah, documented detention orders signed without application of mind, grounds copied across files, and arrests made for reasons the record could not support. Those findings came later and belong to the reversal story. In April 1976 the majority decided the threshold question against the detenus while the detentions continued, and the decision itself became a fact inside the Emergency's machinery: state governments cited the judgment to resist every pending petition, and High Courts that had protected detenus through 1975 closed their files.

## Feature Two: Deference Dressed as Interpretation

The second feature is deference, and it operated as method rather than as a single holding. The majority declined, step by step, every route by which a court might have limited the Article 359 order's reach. A narrower reading was available on the text: suspension of the right to move a court for enforcement of Article 21 could bar petitions pleading Article 21 while leaving untouched petitions pleading that a detention order broke the parent statute, since the statute, not the Article, supplied the ground. Nine High Courts had taken versions of that route. The majority rejected it by treating any detention complaint as, in substance, a complaint about liberty, and therefore as a complaint inside Article 21, and therefore as barred. The move is worth pausing on, because it shows deference working through classification: by assigning every grievance to the suspended Article, the bench made the suspension coextensive with detention itself.

A second route ran through the rule of law as a limit on executive action independent of Part III. The detenus' counsel invoked the long common-law line under which an officer who imprisons without legal authority answers to a court, a line running from Entick v. Carrington in eighteenth-century England through the Indian courts' own pre-Constitution practice. The majority answered that, after the Constitution, the right against the state on life and liberty had to be found in the Constitution's own provisions, and Article 21 was that provision. A third route ran through Article 226's wide text, which empowers High Courts to issue writs for enforcement of fundamental rights and for any other purpose. Petitions framed under the other-purpose limb were held barred as well, because their real purpose was held to be enforcement of the suspended right.

Deference also governed the bench's treatment of the Emergency itself. The majority did not ask whether an Emergency proclaimed in June 1975 still answered to internal disturbance in April 1976, and the Thirty-eighth Amendment had in any case placed the proclamation beyond review. Nor did the bench examine the MISA amendments that withheld grounds from detenus, amendments that made the habeas remedy the only factual check on detention at the very moment the judgment removed it. In [the most consequential constitutional doctrine framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), the series treats judicial review as the settlement's load-bearing wall; ADM Jabalpur shows the wall unloaded by the court's own hand, through interpretation rather than through any surrender announced in those terms.

The scholarly split over this feature is the disagreement the article must adjudicate, and each side deserves its strongest form. The legal-doctrinal reading, stated carefully by its holders, says the text genuinely ran one way: Article 359 suspends enforcement, Article 21 is where the right sits, the 1950 text gave emergency government wide room, and a court that invented limits would have acted against the document it served. The Emergency-failure reading, stated in Austin's history and in Soli Sorabjee's essays, says the bench failed the moment: nine High Courts had found a lawful path to hearing the petitions, the Attorney General's concession showed where the government's logic ended, and the majority still chose the government's path. The architectural reading defended here absorbs both and adds what each misses. The doctrinal reading cannot explain the classification move or the refusal of the statutory route, which were choices, not commands of the text. The failure reading, framed as collapse under pressure, cannot explain the pattern's institutional completeness: acceptance, deference, punishment of the dissenter and repair by amendment and overruling form a sequence that a moment-of-weakness frame leaves as four accidents.

Granville Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution (1999) remains the fullest statement of the middle ground. Austin documented the government's court-focused strategy during the Emergency, the transfer of sixteen High Court judges in 1976 who had decided against the state, and the atmosphere in which the bench worked, while also recording that the majority's legal materials were real materials, handled badly under pressure. Sorabjee, who had argued for detenus as Additional Solicitor General's opponent in the case and wrote on it for four decades, held in his 2017 essays that the judgment stood as a warning about what courts owe precisely when deference feels safest. His phrase for the majority's work, in the essays collected after the Puttaswamy judgment, treated the 1976 opinions as the product of judges who had persuaded themselves that abstention was neutrality.

## Feature Three: The Khanna Dissent Stated by Its Holding

Justice H. R. Khanna's dissent answered the majority at the level of first principle, and its holding can be stated in four propositions drawn from the opinion's own text. First, Article 21 is not the sole repository of the right to life and liberty: the right predates the Constitution, which recognised and guaranteed a right that human beings hold under any ordered legal system. Second, even in the absence of Article 21, the state holds no power to deprive a person of life or liberty without authority of law; a detention without legal authority is an act the courts can strike at, because the officer who signs it acts outside the only source of power he has. Third, the Article 359 order suspends enforcement of named fundamental rights and does not touch this underlying position, since the petitioner's complaint of detention without authority of law is not, at root, a complaint under Article 21. Fourth, the rule of law, which Khanna treated as the Constitution's animating discipline, cannot coexist with a power in the executive to imprison at pleasure for the length of an emergency.

Khanna wrote the opinion in language pitched beyond the case. The Constitution and the laws of India, he held, do not permit life and liberty to sit at the mercy of absolute power, and he warned that a dissent is an appeal to the brooding spirit of the law and to the intelligence of a future day, borrowing Charles Evans Hughes's line from the American tradition. He closed by recording that the majority's view would leave the detainee without remedy for the Emergency's duration. The opinion engaged the majority respectfully and by name, answered the classification move directly, and grounded itself in authorities the majority also accepted: the pre-Constitution case law on official action without authority, the text of Article 359 read against Article 21 rather than as its repeal, and the framers' debates in which, as [the constitutional rights framework set at the founding](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/) shows, personal liberty was argued over as a commitment older than the document itself.

### How did the dissent treat the argument that liberty predates the text?

Khanna treated the age of the right as the point of law itself: because the right to life and liberty existed before the Constitution recognised it, suspension of one Article's enforcement could not extinguish the courts' power to strike at a detention made without legal authority, and the executive gained no fresh power from the Presidential order.

The dissent's architecture drew on the basic structure settlement Khanna had helped build. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973) his opinion had fixed the line between amending power and constitutional identity. In the 1976 dissent the same instinct appears at ground level: some content sits too deep for a proclamation to reach, not because a clause forbids the reach in terms, but because the document read as a whole assigns the executive no such power. Scholars who resist the architectural reading sometimes call this natural law in judicial dress. Khanna's text answers them on its face: his authorities are statutes, precedents and the structure of Part III and Part XVIII read together, and his central claim, that detention needs authority of law, is a claim any positivist can state, as the nine High Court judgments had already shown by reaching similar results without his philosophy.

The dissent also carried a biographical stake that its author understood before he wrote it. Khanna was the senior-most puisne judge, next in line for Chief Justice when Ray retired. T. R. Andhyarujina's account of the post-1973 court records that Khanna knew the price a dissent against the government could carry after the 1973 supersession of Justices Shelat, Hegde and Grover, who resigned when Ray was elevated over them. Khanna had been the government's favoured judge in no sense; his Kesavananda opinion had limited the amending power the government wanted widened. In April 1976 he signed the only opinion among five that kept the courtroom open to the detainee. Austin records that Khanna told his sister he had prepared himself to pay the cost, a family account Austin prints in the Emergency chapters of the 1999 history. The remark, widely repeated since, fits the documentary record of what followed within nine months.

## Feature Four: Supersession and the Resignation of January 1977

The fourth feature converted an internal judicial event into the pattern's enforcement mechanism. Chief Justice Ray retired on 28 January 1977. By the seniority convention, observed without break from 1950 until the 1973 supersession and restored in practice after it, Justice Khanna, the senior-most judge, would have become Chief Justice of India. On 28 January 1977 the government of Indira Gandhi appointed Justice M. H. Beg, a member of the ADM Jabalpur majority, as Chief Justice. Khanna resigned from the Supreme Court on the day the supersession took effect, ending a judicial career that had begun in the district courts of Punjab and run through the Punjab and Delhi High Courts to the top court in 1971.

The supersession's meaning was legible to every reader of the 1976 judgment, and the government made no serious effort to disguise it. Law Minister H. R. Gokhale's public defence of the 1973 supersession had already stated the doctrine that a government may pass over seniority for a judge whose philosophy it judges suitable, and the 1977 appointment applied that doctrine to the one judge who had dissented in the habeas corpus case. The sequence matters for the architectural reading. A failure pattern that ends at a bad judgment is a doctrinal event. A pattern that continues into the punishment of the judge who refused the judgment is an institutional system, complete with an incentive directed at every judge who would sit on the next emergency bench. The sixteen High Court judges transferred in 1976, several of whom had ruled for detenus, supplied the same signal at the level below.

Khanna's resignation correspondence and his later statements, preserved in part at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library and in family collections, show a judge who framed his exit as institutional defence rather than personal grievance. He declined, in the statements Austin and later writers cite, to attack Beg personally, and he directed his stated regret at the convention's breach. Within weeks the political ground shifted: Indira Gandhi called elections in January 1977, the Emergency was withdrawn in March 1977, and [the Janata verdict of March 1977](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/) brought Morarji Desai's coalition to office on a mandate read across the country as a referendum on the Emergency itself. Khanna's public standing rose as the majority's fell. The Janata government appointed him, in time, to chair no commission, but the Law Commission and public life kept his counsel; in 1979 he became a minister in Charan Singh's short government, and in 1982 opposition parties fielded him as their candidate for President of India, a nomination that recorded, in electoral form, the country's verdict on the dissent.

The majority judges' later accounts belong in the record at their strongest. Justice Bhagwati, who became Chief Justice in 1985, stated publicly more than once, including in interviews in the 2010s, that his ADM Jabalpur opinion was a mistake born of his early years on the bench, and that he apologised for it; he asked, in one recorded formulation, to be forgiven for the judgment. Justice Chandrachud, Chief Justice from 1978 to 1985, did not apologise in terms, though writers have noted his son's role, four decades later, in formally overruling the case. Justice Beg retired as Chief Justice in February 1978 without revisiting the opinion. Ray never recanted. These positions are stated here as their holders left them; the architectural reading needs no caricature of any of the four, because its claim is about what the institution did, in sequence, with texts and dates attached.

Read as a map, the four features answer the framing question the Emergency-failure account leaves open: how did the failure work? It worked by acceptance at the threshold, by classification inside the reasoning, by appointment after the judgment, and it was undone, piece by piece and in reverse order, by expansion of the same Article, by constitutional amendment, and by a larger bench naming the 1976 case and burying it. The map also disciplines the article's claim. Where a feature lacks a dated mechanism, the architectural reading would fail; each feature here carries one, and the sources sit in the reported judgment, the amendment text and the 2017 opinions rather than in recollection.

## The Reversal Trajectory: Maneka, the Forty-fourth Amendment, Puttaswamy

The repair began within two years and proceeded on three tracks, a sequence recorded in the constitutional histories of the period. The first track was doctrinal. On 25 January 1978 a seven-judge bench decided Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, holding that a procedure depriving a person of liberty under Article 21 must be just, fair and reasonable, not arbitrary or oppressive, and that Articles 14, 19 and 21 read together as a single code of fairness. Justice Bhagwati wrote the leading opinion, two years after joining the ADM Jabalpur majority, and the Maneka text all but disowned the 1976 reading of Article 21 as a bare licence to any enacted procedure. The second track was textual. The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978, passed by the Janata Parliament, rewrote Article 359 so that a Presidential order cannot suspend enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 at all. Khanna's proposition, rejected by four colleagues in 1976, entered the constitutional text in 1978: during any future emergency, the rights to protection against ex post facto criminal law, double jeopardy and self-incrimination under Article 20, and to life and liberty under Article 21, keep their courtroom door open by the document's own command.

The third track took forty years. Benches of the Supreme Court chipped at the 1976 precedent in later decades, and judges writing in other cases called it wrong, yet ADM Jabalpur stood formally on the books until a case about privacy supplied the bench large enough to remove it. On 24 August 2017 a nine-judge bench in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India held unanimously that privacy is a fundamental right. Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, writing for himself and three colleagues, and the son of Justice Y. V. Chandrachud of the 1976 majority, addressed the habeas corpus case directly. His opinion held that the judgments of the four majority judges were seriously flawed, that life and personal liberty are inalienable rights, and it concluded in words that admit no ambiguity: the ruling in ADM Jabalpur was accordingly overruled, and the view taken by Justice Khanna was affirmed as the correct view. Justice S. K. Kaul, in a separate concurrence, likewise recorded that ADM Jabalpur stood overruled, treating Khanna's dissent as the position the Constitution had always required.

The Puttaswamy language rewards close reading, because the 2017 bench did more than reach a different result. It characterised the majority's error as one about the nature of rights: the 1976 opinions had treated liberty as a concession from the state, while the Constitution, the 2017 plurality held, proceeds on the basis that life and liberty are natural rights recognised, not granted, by the text. That is Khanna's first proposition restated as the law of the land, forty-one years after he wrote it alone. Soli Sorabjee's essays of 2017 and after, written by a lawyer who had stood on the losing side of the 1976 hearing as counsel in the habeas matters and had campaigned against the judgment in print for decades, greeted Puttaswamy as the dissent's vindication and pressed the further point that overruling a precedent does not by itself rebuild the habits that produced it. The complication named in the brief is real and the article meets it head-on in the adjudication below: if Puttaswamy closed the failure, a pattern drawn from 1976 may describe a finished episode rather than a standing risk.

## Adjudicating the Three Readings

Three readings compete for the case, and the evidence assembled here decides among them on points each reading itself must accept. The legal-doctrinal reading holds that the bench applied a hard text faithfully: Article 359 suspends enforcement; the right sits in Article 21; the petitions therefore failed. Its strength is textual economy. Its weakness is the record of choices the economy hides: the classification of statutory complaints as Article 21 complaints, the refusal of the Article 226 other-purpose route, and the rejection of the common-law authority-of-law principle were each contestable on materials before the bench, and nine High Courts had contested them the other way within the same legal system and the same months. A reading that cannot register those choices as choices understates the case it explains.

The Emergency-failure reading holds that the court collapsed under political pressure: arrests, censorship, judicial transfers, a superseded Chief Justiceship in living memory and another supersession to come. Its strength is moral clarity, and Austin's documentation gives it a factual spine. Its weakness is that collapse language explains everything and nothing; it cannot say why the repair took the precise forms it took, amendment of Article 359 and a nine-judge overruling aimed at the sole-repository proposition, unless the failure had a structure the repairers could target. The failure reading also flattens the majority's reasoning into fear, where the texts show argument: bad argument, in this article's judgment, but argument that later courts had to answer as law, which is why Puttaswamy answers it as law, proposition by proposition.

The institutional-architectural reading defended here holds that the verdict operationalised a pattern with four features, each fixed to a dated mechanism, and that the pattern, not the episode, is what the reversal trajectory reversed. It explains the classification move as deference working as method. It explains the supersession as the pattern's enforcement arm rather than as gossip after the event. It explains why the Forty-fourth Amendment's drafters chose Articles 20 and 21 for their shield: they were legislating against the sole-repository holding, not against fear. It explains why Puttaswamy's plurality spent pages on a 1976 detention case inside a 2017 privacy reference: the bench was dismantling a structure, and structures are dismantled at their joints. On the complication, the article concedes the correction and denies the closure. Puttaswamy completes the reversal trajectory; the pattern's analytical content survives the completion, because the map's use is diagnostic: it tells a reader of any later emergency, in any country with a suspension clause, which four places to look first.

This adjudication sits inside the series' wider frame. The founding settlement, as [the Constituent Assembly's three tensions](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) sets them out, left liberty, authority and unity in permanent negotiation, and [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) tracks their later renegotiations; ADM Jabalpur is the moment the liberty tension was resolved, for twenty-one months, wholly in authority's favour, by the institution charged with holding the negotiation open. The directive principles and fundamental rights settlement discussed in [the founding argument over rights and principles](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/) had imagined courts as the forum where the state's reasons get tested. In April 1976 the forum declined the test, and the decline, not the Emergency alone, is the article's subject.

## The Petitions Behind the Doctrine

Doctrine can make the case sound bloodless, so the record of the petitions deserves its own section. The men and women who filed them were held under MISA, a preventive detention statute under which the state need not charge, try or produce evidence against a detainee in open court. Detention orders ran for the Emergency's duration, were renewable, and after the 1975 MISA amendments could rest on grounds the government declined to disclose even to the detainee. Among the detained were Jayaprakash Narayan, Morarji Desai, Atal Bihari Vajpayee, L. K. Advani, George Fernandes (held after a period underground), journalists such as Kuldip Nayar, and thousands of party workers, trade unionists and students whose names fill the Shah Commission's annexures rather than the law reports. Wives, parents and lawyers carried their petitions to the High Courts, which is why so many of the reported habeas judgments bear family names as petitioners.

The High Courts that heard those petitions through 1975 did work the Supreme Court's judgment later erased from practical effect. The Allahabad High Court's reasoning, representative of the nine, held that the Article 359 order suspended enforcement of the named Articles and nothing beyond them: a detainee who said his detention broke MISA itself, or issued from an officer's malice, invoked the statute and the general law, and the court's writ under Article 226 answered that invocation. Judges in Bombay, Delhi, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Punjab, Rajasthan and Andhra Pradesh reached convergent results by routes that differed in detail. The convergence matters to the architectural reading, because it shows the legal system generating, at its middle level, a working answer that kept both the proclamation and the courtroom alive. The Supreme Court's reversal was therefore not the system's only possible output. It was a choice made at the top of the institution against the grain of nine of its own High Courts.

The petitioners' counsel in Delhi understood the stakes in institutional terms and argued them that way. Shanti Bhushan, appearing for detenus, warned the bench that the government's submission asked the court to write a blank page on which the executive could record any detention it pleased for as long as an emergency lasted. V. M. Tarkunde, a former Bombay High Court judge appearing for petitioners, pressed the authority-of-law line from the common law. The bench's questions during argument, preserved in accounts by counsel and summarised in Austin's history, show judges testing both sides hard; the hearing was long, serious and fully argued. That record is one reason the failure reading in its crude form, of a court simply refusing to think, cannot stand. The court thought, at length, and decided for the state on every contested point of classification, remedy and principle. An architectural account honours the record better: it examines the structure of the deciding, not the courage of the deciders in the abstract.

One more feature of the petitions belongs in the record: their geography. The appeals reached Delhi from state capitals across the Union, and the bench's single ruling governed them all. Federal spread is the quiet fact inside the case name. A district officer in Jabalpur signed an order; a national bench converted the challenge to that order into the governing rule for every detention in India. The same vertical reach that makes Indian constitutional adjudication powerful made the 1976 error total in a way a single High Court's error could never have been. When the correction came, it needed the same reach: a constitutional amendment binding every state, and a nine-judge bench whose overruling no smaller bench could be asked to perform.

## The Majority Opinions Read One by One

Chief Justice Ray's opinion is the judgment's formal spine. Ray read Article 21 and Article 359 as a matched pair: the Constitution grants the right in qualified form, procedure established by law, and provides the suspension machinery for emergencies in the same document. His opinion treated the pre-Constitution authorities on liberty as spent, on the ground that a written constitution displaces the general law on the subjects it covers. It treated the detainee's statutory arguments as Article 21 arguments in disguise. And it framed the court's role in emergency as one of fidelity to the emergency text, a fidelity that reads generously toward the suspending power and strictly against the suspended right. Later critics, including the Puttaswamy plurality, fastened on this opinion's premise that liberty under the Constitution is a grant. The premise, once accepted, decides the case before argument begins, which is why the 2017 bench spent its longest passages refusing it.

Justice Beg's opinion travelled furthest toward the executive's factual case. Where Ray argued from text, Beg argued from trust: the detainee's welfare, he wrote, was a matter of such care to the state that the court could proceed on the footing of that care, in a passage comparing the state's solicitude to a parent's. The passage has been quoted against the judgment ever since, and fairly, because it performs the second feature of the pattern inside a single paragraph: review replaced by confidence in the reviewed. Beg also joined the holding on standing and classification. His elevation to Chief Justice nine months later, over the dissent's author, welded his opinion to the pattern's fourth feature in the public record, whatever the appointing government's stated reasons.

### What separated Justice Chandrachud's reasoning from the Chief Justice's?

Chandrachud reasoned from consequence where Ray reasoned from text: admitting petitions framed on malice or statutory breach, he held, would empty the Article 359 order of content, since every detention challenge could be dressed in those terms, so the petitions had to fail as a class if the order was to mean anything at all.

Justice Bhagwati's opinion is the one its author spent later decades answering for. The junior judge on the bench, appointed in 1973, he wrote with an enthusiasm for the Emergency's stated aims that the other majority opinions did not match, praising the discipline of the period and the leadership that proclaimed it in terms quoted in every later history. His legal route joined the majority's: Article 21 as sole repository, suspension as bar, petitions unmaintainable. The interest of the opinion for this article lies in its author's sequel. Bhagwati's Maneka Gandhi opinion in 1978 rebuilt Article 21 on fairness; his public apologies in later years named ADM Jabalpur as an error of his early judicial life; and his career after 1977, as an architect of public interest litigation, ran in the opposite direction from his 1976 vote. The architectural reading files that sequel where it belongs: as evidence that the pattern was institutional, not personal, since the same judge, inside a repaired institution, produced the repair's first doctrine.

## The Institution Around the Bench

A bench does not sit in a vacuum, and the architectural reading earns its name by mapping the institution around the five judges in 1975 and 1976. The executive held instruments over the judiciary that no party before the court needed to mention. Appointment to the Chief Justiceship had been broken from seniority once, in April 1973, when Ray was elevated over Shelat, Hegde and Grover after the Kesavananda verdict limited the amending power; the three superseded judges resigned. T. R. Andhyarujina's The Kesavananda Bharati Case (2011) documents the lesson the 1973 event taught the institution: a verdict against the government's constitutional programme could be followed by a reordering of the court's headship. The Law Minister's stated doctrine of a judiciary committed to the government's philosophy, defended in Parliament by H. R. Gokhale and by Mohan Kumaramangalam before his death in 1973, supplied the theory. Transfers supplied the practice at the High Court level: in 1976 the government transferred sixteen High Court judges, an exercise of the Article 222 power without the judges' consent in several contested instances, and the transfer of Justice Sankalchand Sheth from Gujarat produced litigation that reached the Supreme Court in 1977 in Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth, where the court hedged the transfer power with consultation requirements after the Emergency had ended.

Parliament, meanwhile, was remaking the document the bench was asked to read. The Forty-second Amendment, passed in late 1976 after the judgment, cut at judicial review across a wide front: it curtailed the High Courts' writ power, inserted tribunal provisions, extended the Lok Sabha's life and declared the amending power unlimited, the last clause struck down in Minerva Mills v. Union of India in 1980. The sequence matters for causation. The majority in April 1976 cannot be blamed for an amendment passed in November, and this article does not blame it. The sequence matters instead as pattern evidence: acceptance by the court and expansion by the legislature fed each other through 1976, each institution reading the other's compliance as licence. Austin's history calls the period the judiciary's darkest hour, a phrase Sorabjee also used; the architectural account translates the metaphor into mechanisms a reader can check.

The press, the fourth institution in the story, worked under pre-censorship imposed in June 1975 and could report the judgment only within limits the censors allowed. The Indian Express and The Statesman had resisted censorship in ways their readers recognised, blank editorials among them, but the habeas judgment's practical meaning, that no court would test a detention, spread through the legal profession and the detained families faster than through print. Lawyers in Delhi and the state capitals advised clients' families that the road closed in April 1976 stayed closed. That advice, multiplied across thousands of files, is the judgment's lived content, and it is why the article treats a threshold ruling on maintainability as a substantive event in the Emergency's history rather than a procedural footnote. A ruling on who may be heard is, for the person outside the door, the whole of the law that reaches him, and the detenus of 1976 experienced the Constitution exactly at that door, in the weeks after April, when petitions their families had carried for months were returned unheard.

## The Long Correction in the Courts

Between Maneka Gandhi in January 1978 and Puttaswamy in August 2017, the Supreme Court lived with a precedent its members increasingly disowned without formally removing. The Forty-fourth Amendment had already denied future emergencies the 1976 case's subject matter, since Articles 20 and 21 could no longer be suspended, yet the judgment survived as authority on the nature of rights, citable for the proposition that Part III is the exhaustive home of liberty claims against the state. Benches writing on Article 21's expansion, through the decades of the right-to-livelihood, right-to-health and right-to-education cases, built on Maneka's fairness code and routed around 1976. In the 2010s, judges began saying the quiet part in open text. In cases touching the Emergency's legacy, opinions described ADM Jabalpur as a judgment the court regretted, and the 2011 and 2012 benches in other matters recorded its moral standing in terms that left only the formal act of overruling undone. The formal act needed nine judges, because a Constitution Bench of five can be overruled only by a larger bench, and no reference assembled nine until the privacy case did.

Puttaswamy's route to the point deserves precision. The reference asked whether privacy is a fundamental right. The Union, defending the Aadhaar programme's data architecture, relied in part on the logic that rights are textual grants with textual limits, and the 1976 precedent stood behind that logic as its strongest historical instance. The nine judges therefore had to clear the ground the majority of 1976 had occupied. The plurality opinion of Justice D. Y. Chandrachud performed the clearing at length, reconstructing the 1976 hearing, the Attorney General's concession and Khanna's answer, before recording the overruling. Justice Kaul's concurrence added the institutional note that a court which cannot correct its gravest error forfeits the authority that correction confers. Unanimity across nine judges, spanning the court's ideological range in 2017, gave the overruling a weight no five-judge reappraisal could have carried.

Scholarly verdicts had converged long before the court caught up. Austin's 1999 history fixed the scholarly frame: the judgment as the judiciary's contribution to the Emergency's constitutional damage, balanced against the same institution's Kesavananda achievement three years earlier. Sorabjee's essays, from the Emergency's immediate aftermath to his 2017 and later retrospectives, pressed the professional point: the Bar had argued the case fully, the materials for the right answer sat on the bench's table, and the failure was therefore one of judicial will organised by institutional pressure, a formulation close to the architectural reading though framed in the language of duty. Andhyarujina's work on the Kesavananda era added the personnel record, the supersessions of 1973 and 1977 read as one story about headship as an instrument. H. M. Seervai's Constitutional Law of India, in its later editions, treated the 1976 majority's reasoning as untenable on the text itself, the strongest form of the doctrinal counter: even granting the majority its textual method, Seervai argued, the authority-of-law principle survived Article 359, as the High Courts had seen.

The political afterlife ran on a separate track and should not be confused with the legal one. The Janata government's White Paper and the Shah Commission's reports of 1978 assigned responsibility for Emergency abuses to named ministers and officials, and the Forty-fourth Amendment wrote the episode's lessons into Articles 352, 358 and 359, tightening the proclamation's grounds, requiring written cabinet advice and parliamentary approval within a month, and shielding Articles 20 and 21. Khanna's dissent entered civic memory through a channel no commission controlled: the New York Times's editorial salute to him in 1977, widely reprinted in India after censorship lifted, which told its readers that if India ever found its way back to freedom, someone would erect a monument to Justice Khanna, a prophecy the Puttaswamy plurality in effect fulfilled in prose forty years on.

## What the Pattern Teaches a Reader of Later Emergencies

The map's diagnostic use is the article's closing claim, and it can be stated without reaching beyond the record. Where a constitution permits suspension of remedies in an emergency, the ADM Jabalpur sequence identifies the four observations that decide whether the suspension stays a legal event or becomes a governing system. Observe whether the reviewing court treats the suspended right as a grant that vanishes with its remedy, or as a recognition that survives it. Observe whether the court classifies every grievance into the suspended category, or holds the statutory and general-law doors that the suspension text did not close. Observe what happens to the judges who dissent, since appointment and transfer decisions after a judgment are part of the judgment's institutional meaning. And observe the repair when it comes: amendment aimed at the holding's exact proposition, and overruling by a bench large enough to end argument, are the signatures of a system that has understood its own failure in structural terms.

India's text, after 1978, answers the first two observations in advance for Articles 20 and 21, which is the Forty-fourth Amendment's quiet triumph and the reason no later Indian emergency, had one been proclaimed, could reproduce April 1976 on the same ground. The third and fourth observations remain live disciplines rather than settled text, since appointments and bench strength sit in convention and practice. That residue is the honest limit of the architectural reading, and the article states it rather than claiming a closure the record does not supply. The pattern is a map of a finished failure and a standing checklist; the two uses do not compete.

## Khanna Before and After the Dissent

Hans Raj Khanna's career gives the dissent a context that hagiography would flatten. Born in 1912 in Amritsar, trained in law at Lahore, he rose through the Punjab judiciary, served as Chief Justice of Delhi and Himachal Pradesh's shared High Court arrangement's successor court in Delhi, and joined the Supreme Court in September 1971. His Kesavananda opinion in April 1973, limiting the amending power while upholding wide amendment within the basic structure, was the judgment that made him, in the government's eyes, a judge whose philosophy could not be presumed friendly; his ADM Jabalpur dissent confirmed the file. Between the two, he wrote opinions across tax, service and criminal law that colleagues cited for craft rather than drama. The dissent of April 1976 was thus not a departure in method. It applied, to the emergency clause, the same structural reading he had applied to the amending clause: the document's parts limit each other, and no single provision, read in isolation, licenses the destruction of the settlement's core.

His conduct after January 1977 modelled the institutionalism his opinion preached. He resigned without a public attack on his successor, declined to convert his grievance into a campaign, and let the resignation letter and later measured statements carry the point that the seniority convention existed to insulate headship from reward and punishment. The Nehru Memorial Museum and Library's holdings of his correspondence from the period, and the statements collected by his family, show correspondents across the political spectrum treating the resignation as the Emergency's clearest institutional protest by an officeholder who stayed inside constitutional forms throughout. He served briefly as Law Minister in 1979 under Charan Singh, chaired public inquiries' worth of committees in retirement, and died in 2008, having lived to read Puttaswamy's precursors though not the judgment itself, which came nine years after his death. Students preparing on these events often organise the record visually, and a reader assembling the four features into slides for study may find a [presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) a practical way to keep the map, the chronology and the five opinions in one file while working through the reported text.

The federal dimension of the story, easily missed in a Delhi-centred telling, deserves its closing note. The detentions were ordered by district and state officers under a central statute; the resistance came from state High Courts; the reversal came from the national bench; the repair came from a national amendment that no state could dilute. Readers tracing that vertical movement can set it beside [the federal design of the Indian Constitution](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/), where the founding text distributed power in the expectation that the levels would check one another. In 1975 and 1976 the check ran upward only as far as the High Courts, and failed at the summit. The Forty-fourth Amendment's shield for Articles 20 and 21 can be read as the federation's levels agreeing, after the fact, to remove the summit's power to fail in that particular way again.

## The Anatomy of Article 359 and the Order of 27 June 1975

Precision about the instrument matters, because the majority's holding and Khanna's answer both turn on what the order of 27 June 1975 actually did. Article 359 sits in Part XVIII of the Constitution, the emergency provisions, beside Article 358, which automatically suspends Article 19 during an emergency proclaimed on war or external aggression. Article 359 works differently. It empowers the President to declare, by order, that the right to move any court for enforcement of such fundamental rights as the order names shall remain suspended for the emergency's duration or a shorter stated period. The order of 27 June 1975 named Articles 14, 21 and 22. Article 14 carries equality before the law. Article 22 carries the safeguards on arrest and preventive detention, including the right to consult a lawyer and to be produced before a magistrate within twenty-four hours. Article 21 carries life and personal liberty. The selection was the detention system in constitutional form: equality review closed, arrest safeguards closed, liberty review closed.

The text of Article 359 also carried, from the start, a distinction the detenus' counsel pressed and the majority set aside. Suspending the right to move a court for enforcement of a right is not the same act as suspending the right, and neither act amends the detention statutes or enlarges the powers they grant. An officer detaining under MISA still needed MISA's authority, exercised as MISA directed, by the officer MISA named, on satisfaction MISA required. The High Courts' judgments built on exactly this seam. The Allahabad court's reasoning treated the seam as the case: the proclamation and the order could close Part III's door while the statute's door, and the writ court's power to police the statute, remained. The majority closed the seam by characterising the substance of every such complaint as liberty, hence as Article 21, hence as barred. Khanna held the seam open by the same characterisation run the other way: a complaint of detention without authority of law is in substance a complaint that the officer acted without power, a complaint no emergency clause had ever needed Part III to sustain.

Article 352's proclamation and Article 359's order also differ in their political life, and the difference fed the deference feature. The proclamation of 25 June 1975 rested on internal disturbance, a ground the Forty-fourth Amendment later replaced with armed rebellion, and required, after 1978, written advice of the Union Cabinet and prompt parliamentary approval. In 1975 the proclamation issued on the Prime Minister's advice conveyed to the President at night, with the Cabinet informed the next morning, a sequence the Shah Commission later recorded with disapproval. A bench inclined to test the emergency's instruments would have found the record tender at several points. The ADM Jabalpur majority tested none of them, and its silence on the proclamation's making is part of the pattern: acceptance at the threshold applied to the emergency's founding documents as fully as to its detention orders.

## The Shah Commission and the Detention Record

The evidentiary base for judging the majority's factual premise arrived after March 1977, when the Janata government appointed the Shah Commission under Justice J. C. Shah, a former Chief Justice, to inquire into Emergency excesses. The Commission's reports of 1978 examined detention files across states and recorded patterns that bear directly on the 1976 hearing's unexamined assumption that detention proceeded under law. District officers had signed detention orders in batches. Grounds were withheld, copied or framed in formulas. Some orders rested on the detainee's political identity rather than on any act the statute could reach. The Commission named the misuse of MISA a systematic feature of the period rather than a scattering of errors, and its volumes printed case after case in which the detention file could not have survived the scrutiny the High Courts had offered in 1975 and the Supreme Court withdrew in 1976.

That record reframes Justice Beg's parental-care passage with documentary cruelty, though the passage was vulnerable on the day it was written, since the petitions before the bench alleged precisely the mala fides his confidence assumed away. It also reframes the Attorney General's concession. Niren De's submission was not a debating extremity; it described the legal position the government required for the detention system the Shah Commission later documented, in which an officer's enmity, error or obedience to an oral instruction could not be questioned anywhere. The architectural reading draws the two records together: the failure pattern's first two features, acceptance and deference, were the legal conditions under which the documented detention practice operated without a forum. The judgment did not create the practice, which predated it by ten months. The judgment removed the last institutional obstacle to its continuation, and the Commission's findings measure what that removal cost in individual files.

The Shah Commission also supplies the counterfactual discipline the adjudication needs. Critics of the architectural reading sometimes treat the nine High Court judgments as brave aberrations that a sterner age would have reversed anyway. The Commission's record suggests the opposite valuation: the High Courts' statutory route was working as designed, testing files, releasing some detenus, and forcing officers to assemble grounds, until the apex court unified the law the other way. Institutional failure, on this evidence, was not the judiciary's uniform condition in 1975 and 1976. It was a decision taken at one level of the institution against the demonstrated practice of another level, which is why the pattern's features are stated as operations performed by identifiable actors on identifiable dates rather than as a spirit of the age.

## Why the Formal Overruling Took Forty-one Years

The interval between moral consensus and formal overruling, 1976 to 2017, is itself evidence about how constitutional courts store their errors. A five-judge bench's precedent binds smaller benches; only a larger bench can displace it. For most of the forty-one years, no litigant's case required a nine-judge bench to assemble, and the doctrinal work of Article 21 proceeded on Maneka Gandhi's foundation without needing the 1976 case reopened. Lawyers cited ADM Jabalpur rarely and defensively, usually for the state in detention matters, and courts distinguished or ignored it. The precedent survived, in the phrase lawyers use, as bad law seldom invoked: too weak to build on, too senior to brush aside. Its survival carried a cost beyond doctrine. Every year it stood, the official record of the Republic included an unreversed holding that a detainee without remedy was a constitutional possibility, and foreign courts and scholars citing Indian emergency law cited it as the system's stated position.

Three conditions finally aligned in 2017. A bench of nine had assembled for the privacy reference, the largest constitution of the court in a generation. The Union's argument in defence of Aadhaar's data regime leaned, at the level of principle, on the grants theory of rights that the 1976 majority had carried furthest. And the bench included, in Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, a judge whose family history made the reckoning unavoidable and whose plurality opinion performed it as legal analysis rather than as filial gesture, reconstructing the 1976 record from the reported text. The overruling's language, examined earlier in this article, did the two things a complete correction requires: it named the error's proposition, that liberty is the state's grant, and it installed the opposite proposition, that the Constitution recognises rights persons hold, as the premise from which later cases would reason.

The interval also explains a feature of the scholarly record that the Emergency-failure reading handles awkwardly. If the profession had judged the case a collapse under pressure, the forty-one years look like cowardice compounded. Read architecturally, they look like the ordinary, grinding mechanics of precedent: a correction waiting for a bench of sufficient size and a case that forced the question. Austin, writing in 1999, already anticipated the form the end would take, treating Khanna's dissent as the opinion a future court would adopt when the institution could afford its own history. Sorabjee's essays pressed for exactly that adoption and named the vehicle when it arrived. The reversal trajectory's last feature is therefore dated 24 August 2017 not because opinion changed then, but because the institution's machinery finally matched its judgment.

## Civic Memory: The Dissent Outside the Law Reports

Khanna's dissent acquired a second life outside doctrine, and that life forms part of the evidence for the pattern's fourth feature, since a supersession meant to discipline a court instead canonised the disciplined judge. After the March 1977 election, Indian newspapers free of censorship retold the January supersession as the Emergency's emblematic act of institutional revenge. The New York Times editorial of 1977, saluting Khanna and predicting a monument, was reprinted and quoted in India for decades. Portraiture followed: Khanna's portrait hangs in the Supreme Court's corridors in the place custom reserves for honoured judges, and his statue and named lectures multiplied in the years after his death in 2008. When the Puttaswamy plurality adopted his view in 2017, newspaper coverage treated the event as a family and national story at once, the son's bench burying the father's judgment by adopting the dissenter's, a framing the plurality's own text invited by its care in reconstructing the 1976 hearing.

Civic memory can distort as well as preserve, and the article's evenhandedness obligation applies here too. The monument story risks shrinking a five-judge case into a one-judge morality play, in which four villains and one hero replace analysis. The record resists the shrinkage. The majority's opinions were argued positions on a genuinely hard text, held by judges two of whom, Chandrachud and Bhagwati, went on to build much of the rights jurisprudence the monument story celebrates. The government that superseded Khanna acted under a doctrine of judicial appointment it had announced in 1973 and defended in Parliament, not in a midnight improvisation. And the dissent's author, for his part, refused the hero's script in his own statements, returning always to the convention and the text. The architectural reading keeps the monument and discards the play: Khanna's stature in memory is data about how the institution's punishment misfired, not a substitute for reading the four opinions that outvoted him.

One further memory deserves record because it belongs to the detenus rather than the judges. Families who had filed the petitions of 1975 remembered April 1976, in accounts given to the Shah Commission and to journalists after 1977, as the month the law ended. That recollection, repeated across states and languages, is the human scale of a threshold ruling on maintainability, and it disciplines any account, including this one, that treats the case as a contest of doctrines. Doctrines were indeed contested, and this article has adjudicated them. They were contested over the liberty of named persons in named cells, and the pattern's four features are, in the end, a description of how those persons lost their forum, how the judge who would have kept it lost his office, and how the forum and the office's honour were, in different measures and at different speeds, restored.

## The Hearing Revisited: Counsel, Questions and the Record

The reported judgment compresses months of argument into five opinions, and recovering the hearing's shape from counsel's accounts and Austin's reconstruction sharpens what the bench chose. The detenus' side opened with the statutory seam: MISA authorises detention on a named officer's satisfaction, recorded in an order, on grounds the statute recognises; a petitioner who denies the satisfaction, the officer's authority or the order's fidelity to the statute invokes the statute, and Article 359, which names Articles of Part III, has nothing to say to him. Counsel then widened the base. The right to move for habeas corpus, they argued, had lived in Indian courts since the nineteenth century under the Criminal Procedure Code and the charter courts' powers, continued under Article 226's other-purpose limb, and rested on a principle older than fundamental rights: an imprisonment without legal warrant is a trespass the courts exist to correct. The Attorney General's answer, delivered with a candour the bench's questions kept testing, was that the emergency chapter had re-founded the whole subject for its duration: the Constitution itself, through Article 359, had authorised the withdrawal of the remedy, and a court honouring the document must honour the withdrawal at its full width.

The bench's interventions, as reported by participants, show the majority forming early around the characterisation question. When counsel for detenus framed a complaint as statutory, the recurring question from the bench asked whether the complaint was not, in truth, that the petitioner's liberty had been taken, and whether that was not Article 21's ground. Khanna's questions ran the other way, toward the limits of the state's logic, culminating in the exchange with Niren De on the officer who kills from enmity. That exchange deserves its fame for a precise reason: it extracted, from the government's own counsel, the admission that the submission's endpoint was a detention regime without any judicial remedy for any abuse, including a killing. A bench that adopted the submission after that admission cannot be described as uninformed about what it adopted. The architectural reading rests heavily on this point. Acceptance, the pattern's first feature, was acceptance with the consequences stated aloud in the courtroom by the state's own law officer.

Argument also ranged over comparative ground that the opinions touch lightly. Counsel cited the British habeas tradition and the wartime cases, including Liversidge v. Anderson and Lord Atkin's famous dissent of 1942, in which Atkin accused the majority of being more executive-minded than the executive; the parallel with Khanna's position was available to every reader in 1976 and was drawn in print within the decade. American materials on the suspension clause and Ex parte Milligan entered through the detenus' written submissions. The majority's opinions engaged these materials sparingly, preferring the domestic text. Khanna engaged the common-law line directly, treating it as evidence of the legal system's settled premise rather than as ornament. The contrast in method is the hearing's deepest lesson: the same library sat open before all five judges, and the case turned on which shelf each judge treated as load-bearing.

## Emergency Clauses Beyond India

A brief comparative pass, kept strictly to the record, shows why scholars treat ADM Jabalpur as an outlier among courts of its generation rather than as the era's norm. The Indian text was not unusually weak. Many constitutions permit emergency derogation; the Indian peculiarity after June 1975 was the combination of a remedy-suspension clause, a detention statute withholding grounds, and a court willing to classify every challenge into the suspended category. Britain's wartime detention regime under Regulation 18B produced Liversidge's deference in 1942, yet the British courts of the same war decided other detention cases for the subject on statutory construction, and Parliament's regime carried a statutory advisory process the Indian detenus lacked after the MISA amendments. The United States' suspension clause, invoked in the Civil War and tested in the Second World War's Japanese-American cases, produced its own judicial disgraces, Korematsu among them, later repudiated in terms as solemn as Puttaswamy's; the American repudiation, like the Indian one, arrived through a later bench's dictum rather than through the clause's own machinery.

The comparison disciplines two temptations. Against the temptation to treat 1976 as what any court would have done, the record shows courts in comparable systems finding statutory seams under comparable pressure, as India's own High Courts did in 1975. Against the temptation to treat India's text as uniquely treacherous, the record shows the text's seam was real and was used by nine High Courts before the apex closed it. The failure, comparatively stated, sits where this article has placed it throughout: in operations performed on a workable text by the institution at its summit, within a political system that then punished the one judge who declined the operations. That is a narrower and more useful finding than either national self-flagellation or textual fatalism, and it is the finding the four-feature map exists to carry.

## Amendments Around the Case: The Thirty-eighth, Thirty-ninth and Forty-second

The judgment sat inside a moving constitutional text, and three amendments frame its meaning. The Thirty-eighth Amendment of August 1975, weeks after the proclamation, inserted clauses barring court review of emergency proclamations and of Article 359 orders, and made the President's satisfaction final. The Thirty-ninth Amendment of the same month placed the elections of the President, Vice-President, Prime Minister and Speaker beyond the ordinary courts; the Supreme Court struck the clause shielding the Prime Minister's election in Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain in November 1975, applying Kesavananda's basic structure doctrine to void part of a constitutional amendment for the first time. That November assertiveness matters to the ADM Jabalpur story, because it proves the same court, in the same Emergency, could and did strike at the government's constitutional engineering when the question was framed as amendment beyond power. Five months later, on the detention question framed as suspension within power, it would not. The contrast is the cleanest internal evidence that 1976 was a choice among available judicial postures, not the single posture the times allowed.

The Forty-second Amendment of December 1976, passed eight months after the judgment by a Parliament whose opposition benches sat partly in detention, then attempted to settle the emergency settlement permanently: judicial review trimmed, the Lok Sabha's term extended, Article 368 declared beyond basic-structure limits, and a new chapter of fundamental duties added. Its judicial-review clauses were dismantled in stages after 1977, and Minerva Mills (1980) voided the Article 368 clauses. Read beside the judgment, the amendment shows the pattern's legislative echo: an apex court that had accepted suspension in April faced, by December, a constituent body emboldened to constitutionalise far wider executive supremacy. Causation should not be overstated; the amendment's programme had its own authors and timetable. Yet the sequence is part of the record Austin and Andhyarujina both stress: institutions read one another's compliance, and the habeas judgment was read in Parliament as the judiciary's terms of coexistence with the Emergency, terms the Forty-second Amendment then proceeded to improve upon from the executive's side.

## Reading the Judgment as a Whole Text

A reader approaching the reported judgment entire, rather than through summaries, encounters features that summaries flatten and that this article has tried to restore. The text is long, five full opinions with separate architectures, and the majority is not a single voice but four: Ray's textual formalism, Beg's administrative trust, Chandrachud's consequentialism about the order's purpose, Bhagwati's endorsement of the period's stated aims. Later law treats the four as one holding, and for authority they are one. For understanding, the differences matter, because the reversal trajectory answered them separately: Maneka answered Bhagwati's and Ray's thin Article 21, the Forty-fourth Amendment answered the suspension holding all four shared, and Puttaswamy's plurality answered the grants theory that Ray stated most purely and Beg assumed most comfortably. A correction that arrives in matched pieces confirms, from the repair side, the architecture the failure side displayed.

The dissent, read entire, is equally distinct from its legend. It is not a speech. It is a structured opinion that takes the government's strongest textual point, the width of Article 359, concedes the suspension of the named remedies, and then locates the petitioner's surviving ground in the law of official power: the detaining officer is a creature of a statute, his act beyond the statute is a private trespass dressed in office, and no proclamation cited to the bench had purported to make officers into legislators of their own authority. That is why the dissent could be, and in 2017 was, adopted without embarrassment by a court that prides itself on craft. It also explains the dissent's durability in teaching: generations of Indian law students meet the case as a set text, majority and dissent in one volume, and examiners' questions return to the same seam the hearing turned on. The case teaches because both sides argued law; it warns because the side that argued law better, on the later court's own verdict, lost by four to one in the year it mattered most.

## What the Case Decided and What It Did Not

Delimiting the holding protects the article's claim from the overreach the brief warns against. ADM Jabalpur decided that, while the Article 359 order of June 1975 stood, no court could entertain a habeas corpus petition, or any proceeding in substance seeking release, on grounds that a detention violated Article 21, was mala fide, or departed from the detention statute. It did not decide that detentions were in fact lawful; it refused the inquiry. It did not hold the Emergency proclamation valid against challenge, a question the Thirty-eighth Amendment had separately fenced and the bench did not reach. It did not construe MISA's merits provisions, because its threshold holding made construction unnecessary. And it did not survive, as living law, any of its three operative propositions: the sole-repository premise fell to Maneka's integrated reading and Puttaswamy's natural-rights framing; the suspension consequence fell to the Forty-fourth Amendment; the classification bar fell with the premise that fed it.

The delimitation also clarifies the dissent's scope. Khanna did not hold that Article 21 could never be suspended in enforcement; the text said otherwise in 1976, and he did not pretend it unsaid. He held that the suspension did not reach the distinct complaint of detention without authority of law. Later shorthand that turns the dissent into a claim that fundamental rights are unsuspendable misstates it, and the misstatement matters because the Forty-fourth Amendment's drafters, legislating Khanna's practical result, chose the accurate route: they changed the suspension clause itself for Articles 20 and 21 rather than declaring a judicial philosophy. Precision about what each actor did, bench, dissenter, amenders and the 2017 court, is the article's method from its first section, and the failure pattern survives the precision, which is the test the pattern's critics are entitled to set.

## The Bar in the Emergency Courtroom

No account of the case is complete without the profession that argued it, since the architectural reading assigns the Bar a diagnostic role: the quality of the losing argument is the measure of the bench's choice. The detenus' counsel in the Supreme Court and the High Courts included lawyers who spanned the profession's politics, from Soli Sorabjee and V. M. Tarkunde to Shanti Bhushan and C. K. Daphtary, men who agreed on little else in 1976 and agreed on the authority-of-law submission. Their coordination across nine High Court jurisdictions, in a season of censorship and arrests of lawyers themselves in several states, kept the statutory seam argued consistently until the appeals consolidated in Delhi. The government's side was argued with equal professionalism by the Attorney General and the Advocates General of the appealing states; Niren De's candour in the fatal exchange was a law officer's honesty about his brief's logic, and later commentary that treats him as the case's villain misreads a record in which he stated, on instruction tested in open court, exactly what the government's position entailed and let the bench see the end of the road before it chose to walk it.

The profession's institutional sequel mirrors the court's. The Bar Council and bar associations debated the judgment under censorship's limits in 1976, honoured Khanna after 1977, and built the case into the profession's teaching canon, where it functions as the standard against which Indian advocates measure courtroom duty in hard seasons. Sorabjee's four decades of essays, written from inside that profession, carry its verdict in its own idiom: counsel had done the work, the materials had been laid, and the failure was the bench's. The architectural reading adopts that verdict with one amendment of scope. Professions, like courts, are structures of incentive; the supersession taught watching lawyers what a government could do to a judge, and the lesson's audience included every future bench. Remembering the Bar's part keeps the story honest about who stood where when standing was priced.

## Counterarguments Answered in Full

The strongest counter to this article's reading, named in its brief, holds that Puttaswamy closed the pattern: a failure fully corrected by amendment and overruling is a finished episode, and extending its framework risks treating a repaired institution as permanently suspect. The answer given earlier can be stated with its full reasoning here. A map of a finished failure retains two uses that closure does not touch. The first is evidentiary: the four features explain the historical record, including the repair's own shape, better than the rival readings, and an explanation does not lapse when its subject ends. The second is diagnostic: suspension clauses survive in the world's constitutions, including India's for rights other than Articles 20 and 21, and the features tell observers where to look while a crisis is live, when the knowledge can still matter. Against both uses, the closure objection proves too much: it would retire every framework drawn from a resolved crisis, including the Forty-fourth Amendment itself, whose clauses are nothing but the pattern of 1975 to 1977 written as prevention.

A second counter holds that the architectural reading is the Emergency-failure reading in technical dress, renaming moral collapse as structure. The distinction is testable. The failure reading predicts that, pressure removed, the same institution returns to health by default; the architectural reading predicts that repair requires aimed acts at the failure's joints, and the record shows aimed acts: an amendment targeted at the suspension proposition for two named Articles, an overruling that reconstructed the 1976 hearing and answered its premise about the nature of rights, and a doctrinal rebuild in Maneka aimed at the procedure clause the majority had read thin. Repairs of that aim are not a convalescent's rest. They are engineering, and engineering implies a structure that had failed in identifiable places, which is the article's claim and not the failure reading's.

A third counter, from the doctrinal side, holds that the majority read a genuinely hard text as well as it could be read, and that the nine High Courts were simply wrong together. This counter deserves the respect of a direct answer, because H. M. Seervai's contrary textual case, the strongest available, must be met on the text. Seervai's argument runs: Article 359 suspends the right to move a court for enforcement of the named rights; the detenus' petitions that survived the High Courts did not seek enforcement of those rights but enforcement of a statute and of the common law of trespass; nothing in Article 359 suspends statutes. The majority's answer, that substance defeats form, is a real canon of construction, yet it cuts both ways, since in substance a suspended remedy for a subsisting wrong is exactly what the High Courts identified and what the clause's words, remedy-suspension rather than right-extinction, describe. On the texts alone, the case was arguable both ways, which is precisely why the doctrinal reading cannot carry the whole explanation: where a text argues both ways, the direction a court takes it is an institutional fact, and institutional facts are this article's subject. The four features name the direction and its machinery, and the counters, answered in sequence, leave the map standing as the reading that explains the most of the record with the least remainder.

## The Verdict's Place in the Series' Argument

Within this series, ADM Jabalpur is the negative pole of the judiciary pillar, the case against which the pillar's affirmative doctrines are measured. The basic structure doctrine, established at Kesavananda in 1973 and treated in this series as the founding settlement's self-defence, had announced that some content lies beyond constituent power. ADM Jabalpur announced, three years later, that the most basic content of all, the detainee's access to a court against imprisonment without law, lay within a proclamation's reach. The two propositions cannot both describe one constitution comfortably, and the Republic's subsequent law chose between them: Maneka, the Forty-fourth Amendment and Puttaswamy each moved the system toward the Kesavananda pole and away from the Jabalpur pole, until in 2017 the earlier of the two great benches stood vindicated and the later stood overruled by name. A reader who follows the pillar's arc from 1973 to 2017 watches an institution argue with itself across forty-four years and settle, at the end, on the side of its own best day.

That arc is also the answer to the reader's opening question: what did the verdict operationalise institutionally? It operationalised the discovery that a constitutional court can disable the settlement it guards without amending a word of it, by classification, by deference and by acceptance at the threshold, and that the disabling can be answered only by the full machinery of correction, amendment, enlargement of doctrine and a bench of nine. The four-feature map is the case's portable residue. Courts elsewhere, and this court in some future season the text cannot foresee, will meet suspension clauses again. The Jabalpur record tells them, and tells the citizens watching them, exactly what the first four signs of failure look like, in what order they tend to arrive, and what a correction complete enough to end the argument has historically required.

## A Note on Sources and the Reported Text

This article rests on the reported judgment in Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla, (1976) 2 SCC 521, read in its five opinions entire, and on the two Presidential instruments of June 1975 that the judgment construes. The correction record rests on Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, (1978) 1 SCC 248, on the text of the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978, and on K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1, in particular the plurality opinion of Chandrachud J. and the concurrence of Kaul J. The institutional narrative follows Granville Austin, Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), in its Emergency chapters; Soli Sorabjee's essays on the case, including his 2017 retrospectives on the overruling; and T. R. Andhyarujina, The Kesavananda Bharati Case (2011), for the appointment history of 1973 and its sequel in 1977. The detention record follows the reports of the Shah Commission of Inquiry (1978). Where these sources divide, the article has said so and given each its strongest form, in line with the charter under which the series is written: settled facts plain, disputed points marked as disputed, and every position anchored to a named holder, a named work and a date.

One feature of the sources themselves deserves remark, because it shapes what can responsibly be claimed. The 1976 judgment is a public text, and every proposition this article attributes to Ray, Beg, Chandrachud, Bhagwati or Khanna can be checked against it line by line. The supersession's inner history is less open: the government of the day left no published statement of reasons beyond the appointment notification and the general doctrine its ministers had defended since 1973, and Khanna's resignation correspondence survives in partial archival form. The article has therefore stated the supersession by its dated, public facts, the seniority, the appointment, the resignation, and has attributed motive only as the period's participants and historians stated it, Austin and Andhyarujina among them, rather than as a fact the record proves by itself. That restraint is not a hedge on the pattern's fourth feature. A punishment mechanism in a constitutional system works through its public facts: every judge in India in January 1977 could read the sequence without access to any file, and it is the sequence, public and dated, that the map records.

The same discipline governs the article's treatment of the majority judges as persons. Each of the four is represented here by his opinion and by his documented later statements, Bhagwati's apologies and Maneka authorship, Chandrachud's later Chief Justiceship and silence on the case, Beg's retirement without reconsideration, Ray's absence of any recantation. Nothing in the four-feature map requires a theory of any man's character, and the article offers none. Institutions fail through officers acting inside roles, incentives and doctrines; the map's four features are stated at that level throughout, and the persons enter as the dated holders of the roles, which is where the record puts them and where a fair reading leaves them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is ADM Jabalpur case?

ADM Jabalpur is the short name for Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla, decided by a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court of India on 28 April 1976. The case decided whether courts could hear habeas corpus petitions from persons detained under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act during the Emergency, after a Presidential order under Article 359 had suspended enforcement of Article 21. By four votes to one the court held the petitions could not be entertained. Justice H. R. Khanna dissented. The case is remembered as the Supreme Court's gravest Emergency-era judgment and was formally overruled in 2017.

### Q: What did the 1976 verdict say?

The verdict held that Article 21 was the sole repository of the right to life and personal liberty, that the Presidential order of 27 June 1975 under Article 359 had suspended the right to move any court for enforcement of Article 21, and that consequently no detainee had standing to challenge a detention, even by alleging that the detention order was mala fide, illegal or outside the detention statute. The four majority judges, Chief Justice A. N. Ray and Justices M. H. Beg, Y. V. Chandrachud and P. N. Bhagwati, allowed the government's appeals and set aside the High Court judgments that had admitted the petitions.

### Q: Who was Justice H.R. Khanna?

Hans Raj Khanna (1912 to 2008) was a judge of the Supreme Court of India from 1971 to 1977 and the senior-most puisne judge when the ADM Jabalpur case was decided. Born in Amritsar and trained at Lahore, he served in the Punjab judiciary and as Chief Justice at Delhi before his elevation. His opinion in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) supplied the decisive formulation of the basic structure doctrine. In ADM Jabalpur he wrote the lone dissent. In January 1977 the government superseded him for the Chief Justiceship in favour of Justice M. H. Beg, and Khanna resigned from the court the same day.

### Q: What was Khanna's dissent?

Khanna's dissent held that Article 21 is not the sole repository of the right to life and liberty, because that right predates the Constitution, which recognised rather than created it. He held that even without Article 21 the state has no power to deprive a person of life or liberty without authority of law, so a detention lacking legal authority could still be struck down by a court, and that the Article 359 order, which suspends enforcement of named fundamental rights, did not erase that position. He warned that the Constitution does not permit life and liberty to rest at the mercy of absolute executive power.

### Q: Why was Khanna superseded?

Khanna was superseded in January 1977 because the government of Indira Gandhi appointed Justice M. H. Beg, a member of the ADM Jabalpur majority, as Chief Justice over Khanna, the senior-most judge entitled to the office under the seniority convention when Chief Justice Ray retired. The government defended departures from seniority by a doctrine that headship could go to a judge whose outlook the government judged suitable, a doctrine first applied in 1973. The sequence, a dissent against the government in April 1976 followed within nine months by the dissenter's supersession, was read across the legal profession as the dissent's price, and Khanna resigned.

### Q: When was ADM Jabalpur overruled?

ADM Jabalpur was overruled on 24 August 2017 by the nine-judge bench in K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, the privacy case. Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, writing for a plurality of four, held that the judgments of the four majority judges in 1976 were seriously flawed, that life and personal liberty are inalienable, and recorded that ADM Jabalpur stood overruled and that the view taken by Justice Khanna was the correct view. Justice S. K. Kaul's concurrence likewise treated the 1976 case as overruled. Earlier, Maneka Gandhi (1978) had rebuilt Article 21 and the Forty-fourth Amendment (1978) had barred suspension of Articles 20 and 21.

### Q: Can Article 21 be suspended?

After the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978, enforcement of Article 21 cannot be suspended even during an Emergency. The amendment rewrote Article 359 to provide that a Presidential order suspending enforcement of fundamental rights cannot extend to Articles 20 and 21. Before that amendment, the text allowed suspension of enforcement, and ADM Jabalpur (1976) read that power at its widest. The distinction the whole episode turns on is between the right itself and the remedy: the amendment removes the remedy question for life and liberty altogether, by keeping the courtroom open for Articles 20 and 21 in every emergency.

### Q: Which High Courts decided habeas petitions in favour of detainees?

Nine High Courts, including Allahabad, Bombay, Delhi, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Punjab and Rajasthan, decided habeas corpus petitions in favour of detainees during the Emergency, holding the petitions maintainable despite the Article 359 order. Their shared reasoning was that suspension of enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22 did not bar a detainee from showing that a detention order broke the detention statute itself or issued from malice, because the complaint then rested on the statute and the general law rather than on the suspended Articles. The Union appealed those rulings, and the Constitution Bench in ADM Jabalpur set them aside on 28 April 1976.

### Q: What argument did Niren De present as Attorney General?

Niren De, the Attorney General arguing for the Union, submitted that Article 359's object was to withdraw, for the Emergency period, every person's standing to complain in court of a deprivation of life or liberty, and that the suspension covered complaints that a detention was mala fide or beyond the statute. Pressed by Justice Khanna on whether any remedy would exist if a police officer killed a detainee from personal enmity, De answered, in the exchange the judgment records, that consistently with his submissions no proceedings could be taken in a court of law on that score, while conceding that the conclusion shocked his conscience.

### Q: How did Justice Beg reason in his majority opinion?

Justice M. H. Beg joined the majority holding that Article 21 was the sole repository of the right to life and liberty and that its suspended enforcement barred the habeas petitions. His opinion added a distinctive note of administrative trust, reasoning that the court could proceed on the footing that the state and its officers cared for the detainee's welfare with something approaching parental solicitude, so the absence of judicial review carried less danger than the petitioners alleged. Later histories, including Granville Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), quote that passage as the clearest instance of deference inside the majority's reasoning. Beg became Chief Justice in January 1977.

### Q: How did Justice Bhagwati reason in his majority opinion?

Justice P. N. Bhagwati, the junior judge on the bench, joined the holding that the petitions were barred and wrote in terms warmly supportive of the Emergency's stated purposes, praising the discipline of the period in passages that later accounts quote against the judgment. His sequel distinguishes his record: in Maneka Gandhi (1978) he wrote the leading opinion rebuilding Article 21 around fairness, and in later public statements and interviews, including in the 2010s, he described his ADM Jabalpur opinion as a mistake of his early years on the bench and apologised for it, asking to be forgiven for the judgment.

### Q: How did Justice Chandrachud reason in his majority opinion?

Justice Y. V. Chandrachud reasoned from institutional consequence. He held that if detainees could challenge detentions on grounds of mala fides or statutory non-compliance, the Article 359 order would be emptied of purpose, since nearly every detention challenge could be framed in those terms; the petitions therefore had to fail as a class if the Presidential order was to operate. He joined the sole-repository reading of Article 21 and the dismissal of the petitions. Chandrachud served as Chief Justice from 1978 to 1985. His son, Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, wrote the Puttaswamy plurality in 2017 that formally overruled ADM Jabalpur.

### Q: What happened to habeas petitions pending after the judgment?

After 28 April 1976 the habeas petitions pending in the High Courts failed at the threshold. The Supreme Court's ruling bound every court, so petitions that nine High Courts had entertained and in many instances allowed were dismissed as unmaintainable, and detainees remained held under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act without a judicial forum able to test their detention orders for legality or malice. Lawyers advised families that the road had closed. Detention review revived only with the Emergency's end in March 1977, the release of detenus by the incoming Janata government, and the Shah Commission's examination of detention records in 1977 and 1978.

### Q: What did the Forty-fourth Amendment change for Articles 20 and 21?

The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978, enacted by the Janata Parliament, amended Article 359 so that a Presidential order during an Emergency cannot suspend the right to move a court for enforcement of Articles 20 and 21. Article 20 protects against ex post facto criminal laws, double jeopardy and compelled self-incrimination; Article 21 protects life and personal liberty except by procedure established by law. The amendment wrote into the text the practical core of Justice Khanna's 1976 dissent: in any future emergency, the courtroom stays open for life, liberty and the Article 20 protections, whatever proclamation is in force.

### Q: What did Maneka Gandhi decide about procedure under Article 21 in 1978?

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, decided on 25 January 1978 by a seven-judge bench, held that a procedure depriving a person of life or liberty under Article 21 must be just, fair and reasonable, not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive, and that Articles 14, 19 and 21 form an integrated code rather than sealed compartments. The case concerned the impounding of Maneka Gandhi's passport, and Justice Bhagwati's leading opinion rebuilt Article 21 two years after he had joined the ADM Jabalpur majority. The decision displaced the 1976 reading of Article 21 as satisfied by any enacted procedure, though it did not formally overrule the earlier case.

### Q: What language did Puttaswamy use to overrule ADM Jabalpur?

In K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (24 August 2017), Justice D. Y. Chandrachud's plurality opinion held that the judgments rendered by the four majority judges in ADM Jabalpur were seriously flawed, treated life and personal liberty as inalienable rights that the Constitution recognises rather than grants, and recorded the conclusion that ADM Jabalpur must be and was accordingly overruled, with the view taken by Justice Khanna affirmed as the correct view. Justice S. K. Kaul, concurring, also recorded the 1976 case as overruled. The nine-judge bench's unanimity on privacy gave the overruling of the Emergency precedent its formal finality.

### Q: How did Granville Austin assess the verdict in Working a Democratic Constitution?

Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), placed the habeas corpus litigation at the centre of his Emergency chapters and treated the 1976 judgment as the judiciary's deepest failure of the period, while documenting the pressure system around the bench: the 1973 supersession precedent, the transfers of High Court judges in 1976, censorship and the government's stated preference for a judiciary aligned with its philosophy. Austin also recorded that the majority worked with real legal materials handled under pressure, and he gave Khanna's dissent and resignation a central place in the story of the Constitution's survival and repair after 1977.

### Q: What position did Soli Sorabjee take on the verdict in his essays?

Soli Sorabjee, who had appeared in the Emergency-era habeas litigation and wrote on the case across four decades, including essays of 2017 and after the Puttaswamy judgment, held ADM Jabalpur to be a standing warning about judicial duty under pressure. His position was that the Bar had argued the case fully and the materials for the correct answer were before the bench, so the failure lay in the majority's choice, not in the law's silence. He greeted the 2017 overruling as the vindication of Khanna's dissent, while warning that overruling a precedent does not by itself rebuild the habits that produced it.

### Q: What did Justice Khanna do after Justice Beg became Chief Justice?

When Justice M. H. Beg was appointed Chief Justice on Chief Justice Ray's retirement in January 1977, Justice Khanna, the senior-most judge passed over, resigned from the Supreme Court on the day the supersession took effect. He made no public attack on his successor and framed his objection through the act itself and later measured statements: the seniority convention existed to keep the court's headship outside reward and punishment for judgments. His correspondence from the period is preserved in part at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library. He later served briefly as Law Minister in 1979 and died in 2008.

### Q: Who was Shivkant Shukla in the case title?

Shivkant Shukla was a detainee held under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act in Madhya Pradesh, whose detention order, signed by the Additional District Magistrate of Jabalpur, was challenged by habeas corpus petition before the Madhya Pradesh High Court. The High Court's handling of the challenge formed one of the appeals the Constitution Bench heard together, and the appeal's caption, Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla, supplied the name by which the ruling is universally cited. The bench's answer in his case governed every detention challenge in the country, which is why a Jabalpur detention order names a national precedent.
