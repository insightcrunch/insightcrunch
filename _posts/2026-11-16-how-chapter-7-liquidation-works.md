## A Filing That Ends a Company Instead of Saving It

Chapter 7 bankruptcy for a company works as a controlled shutdown under United States federal bankruptcy law. A petition filed under Title 11 of the United States Code creates a bankruptcy estate, a trustee takes control of the firm, an automatic stay halts collection, the trustee sells the assets for cash, creditors file claims, and the proceeds are paid down a ranking fixed by sections 507 and 726 of the Code. Secured lenders are paid from their collateral first, costs and priority claims follow, general unsecured creditors share what remains, and shareholders stand last in line.

![Liquidation Sale of a Failed Company and the Queue of Claims - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-64.webp)

Chapter 7 liquidation is not the sale of a business but the conversion of a firm into a ranked list of claims: once a trustee takes control, every asset becomes cash for a statutory queue, and the order of that queue, fixed before any sale begins, decides almost everything that follows. That sentence describes the whole machine. The filing does not ask whether the company can be repaired, refinanced or returned to its owners. It asks a narrower and colder question, namely what the firm owns, what it owes, and in which order the law allows the second list to be paid from the first.

The contrast with reorganization explains the choice of chapter. A Chapter 11 case keeps the enterprise alive while debts are restructured under a confirmed plan, and management often stays in place as debtor in possession. A Chapter 7 case has no plan, no confirmation hearing aimed at survival and no future trading company at the end. The corporation or partnership that files receives no discharge either, because section 727(a)(1) limits a Chapter 7 discharge to individuals. The entity is wound down, its property is distributed, and the shell that remains has no assets left to operate with and no legal release worth having.

A board reaches that door when the arithmetic of rescue fails. Lenders refuse further advances, suppliers demand payment before shipment, and the cash on hand covers weeks rather than quarters of payroll and rent. Selling the company as a going concern may have been tried and may have produced no buyer willing to assume the debts attached to the enterprise. In that setting liquidation is not an admission that the assets are worthless. Machinery, inventory, receivables, patents and customer lists can all carry real value. The admission is narrower: the value sits in the pieces and in the claims against them, and no capital structure resting on top of those pieces can be made to work.

The filing itself is a formal act with immediate legal consequences. A voluntary petition under section 301 commences the case and constitutes an order for relief, which means the court does not first hold a trial on whether the company deserves protection. From that moment the property of the estate is gathered under section 541, collection activity stops under section 362, and authority over the business passes away from the directors who signed the petition.

Creditors experience the filing as a change of forum and of rules. A supplier owed money for delivered goods no longer pursues the company in an ordinary collection suit. A landlord no longer changes the locks on the strength of a lease default alone. Each claimant instead files a proof of claim, waits for the trustee to convert property into money, and takes the dividend that the statutory ranking assigns to that class. Douglas Baird made the underlying point in The Elements of Bankruptcy in 1992: bankruptcy replaces a scramble among individual creditors with a collective proceeding, and the collective rules, rather than speed or aggression, determine who recovers and how much.

## What a Dying Company Actually Owns

The estate is broader than the balance sheet suggests. Section 541 of the Code sweeps in all legal or equitable interests of the debtor in property wherever located and by whomever held at the commencement of the case. That language reaches the obvious items first: cash in bank accounts, inventory in warehouses, machinery on a factory floor, vehicles, office equipment and real property owned outright. It also reaches less visible property, including accounts receivable owed by customers, deposits held by utilities and landlords, tax refunds in process, insurance proceeds connected to estate property and causes of action the company could have brought against others on the filing date.

Inventory deserves close attention because its book value and its sale value rarely agree. Raw materials that a manufacturer could have turned into finished goods may sell only as commodity stock once production stops. Finished goods may sell well through ordinary channels for a short period, or poorly through auction if the brand loses support. Work in progress often sits at the bottom of the scale, since a half assembled product fits the production line of the failed firm and few others. The trustee therefore inventories by category, tests the market for each category separately and records why a chosen sale method fits the property offered.

Receivables form a second class with a similar gap between face value and recovery. A customer that owes money for goods already received may pay in full, may assert that the goods were defective, or may claim a setoff based on its own dealings with the debtor. Some receivables have already been pledged to a lender as collateral, which means the cash collected belongs first to the secured claim rather than to the general fund.

Intangible property often decides whether a liquidation pays a meaningful dividend or a token one. Patents, trademarks, trade names, copyrights, software, domain names and customer data can all be property of the estate, and in many service and technology firms they outweigh the physical plant. Elizabeth Warren and Jay Lawrence Westbrook, in The Law of Debtors and Creditors, first published in 1986, describe liquidation practice as an exercise in identifying value that accounting records present poorly, and intangibles are the clearest instance. A trademark detached from the business that built its reputation may fetch little, while a patent portfolio with licensing history can attract bidders from an entire industry.

Claims belonging to the company are property too. A preference action under section 547, a fraudulent transfer action under section 548 and a strong arm claim under section 544 all arise or pass to the estate, and the trustee pursues them as assets rather than as grievances. Contract rights matter in the same way: a favorable supply contract, a below market lease or a pending purchase order can hold value that a buyer will pay for, even though the failed firm can no longer perform. Charles Jordan Tabb, in The Law of Bankruptcy in 1997, treats the estate as a snapshot taken at filing plus defined additions, and that snapshot discipline governs disputes about property acquired later.

Some property stays outside the estate or passes through it quickly. Goods held in trust for another party, property in which the debtor holds only bare legal title, and collateral in which the estate has no equity beyond a secured debt can all limit what the trustee can distribute. Property of the estate is also distinct from property abandoned under section 554, where a trustee gives up an asset that is burdensome or of inconsequential value. The map of ownership at filing therefore sets the outer boundary of the liquidation. Everything inside the boundary is administered for creditors in rank order, and everything outside it belongs to someone else from the start.

## The Unit Economics of a Firm That Has Stopped Trading

A trading company converts inputs into revenue in a repeating cycle, and liquidation begins when that cycle can no longer fund itself. The firm buys materials, pays labour, ships product and collects payment weeks after shipment, so cash leaves long before cash returns. While lenders and suppliers finance the gap, the model holds together. When either group withdraws, the same cycle that produced revenue produces a cash crisis, and a business with full order books can still run out of money.

Fixed costs explain why a short interruption becomes fatal. Rent under a commercial lease, insurance premiums, equipment finance payments, salaried supervision, utilities and compliance costs continue whether the plant ships product or sits idle. Variable costs fall with output, but the fixed layer does not, and that layer was sized for a level of trade the firm no longer reaches. Each week of reduced output spreads the fixed layer over fewer units, raising the cost of each unit and weakening the price the firm can offer against healthier rivals. The result is a spiral in which lower volume raises unit cost, higher unit cost loses orders, and lost orders lower volume again.

Margins in a failing firm often look better on paper than in cash. Revenue is recorded at shipment, while warranty claims, returns, rebates and doubtful accounts arrive later. A debtor that has been stretching payments may also carry payables far beyond agreed terms, which flatters cash in the short term while destroying the supplier credit the next production cycle depends on. By the filing date the honest measure is not reported profit for a past quarter but the cash conversion of the remaining assets: how much inventory can be sold for, how many receivables will actually be collected and how quickly, minus the cost of holding and selling everything.

The cost of stopping is itself a line item. Securing premises, preserving insurance, maintaining essential systems, guarding against theft and deterioration, and keeping records accessible all consume money from the estate before any creditor in the general pool receives a dollar. Those costs rank as administrative expenses when they meet section 503, and their priority under section 507 means the estate pays for its own winding down ahead of older trade debts. A trustee therefore weighs every week of delay against the value preserved by that delay. Holding inventory for a better selling season may raise gross proceeds, or the carrying costs and price decay may consume the gain entirely.

Working capital trapped inside the firm is the prize liquidation releases. Cash tied up in slow moving stock, in receivables from slow paying customers and in deposits scattered across landlords and utilities returns to a single fund under the control of one office holder. The release comes at a discount, because forced sellers rarely obtain the price an unhurried owner would accept, and because buyers price in the risk that records are incomplete and warranties from the seller are worthless. The discount is the central economic fact of Chapter 7. The queue decides how proceeds are shared, but the discount decides how large the fund for sharing will be, and both questions run through every decision a trustee takes on timing, method and expense.

Historical practice reflects that tension. Under the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, the first permanent federal bankruptcy law in the United States, receivers and trustees sold assets with far thinner market infrastructure than a modern trustee commands, and going concern sales were harder to arrange. The modern Code, effective October 1, 1979 under the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978, gives a trustee broader sale powers under section 363 and clearer authority to run a business for a limited period under section 721. 

## Where the Remaining Value Sits in the Chain

A company in liquidation rarely holds its remaining value in one place. Value is scattered along the chain of relationships that kept the firm trading: stock bought from suppliers and not yet paid for, deposits lodged with landlords and utilities, goods held by carriers and warehouses, amounts owed by customers, and rights under contracts that other parties still want performed. Each link in that chain is also a claim or a defense in the case, so mapping where value sits and mapping who can reach it are the same exercise performed from opposite ends.

Suppliers stand at the start of the chain and often hold the sharpest grievances. Goods delivered on credit shortly before filing may support a reclamation demand under section 546(c) if the statutory conditions are met, or an administrative claim under section 503(b)(9) for goods received by the debtor within twenty days before the petition in the ordinary course. Goods still in transit raise stoppage questions under commercial law outside the Code. A supplier that retained title, held a purchase money security interest or shipped on consignment may reach the goods themselves rather than a dividend, and each arrangement turns on documents signed while the relationship was healthy and uncontroversial.

Customers hold value at the other end. A customer that prepaid for goods never delivered becomes a creditor, sometimes with a limited priority under section 507(a)(7) for deposits toward personal, family or household purchases. A customer that received goods and has not paid is a source of estate funds, and the trustee collects with the authority of the court behind the demand. Between those positions sit warranty obligations, service contracts and unfinished orders, each of which a buyer of the business assets may or may not assume. The price a buyer offers for a customer list depends heavily on whether the customers regard the transfer as continuity or as abandonment.

Landlords, lenders and taxing authorities occupy fixed points in the chain with rights defined by documents and statutes rather than by trade custom. A landlord holds a claim for unpaid rent and for damage from rejection of the lease, capped by section 502(b)(6), and may also hold a deposit that offsets part of the loss. A lender with a perfected security interest holds rights in defined collateral under section 506. A taxing authority holds priority for certain taxes under section 507(a)(8) and a general claim for others.

Employees connect every link, because the work of selling, collecting and accounting runs through people who know where stock is stored, which customers pay slowly and which records can be trusted. A trustee who can retain a small number of key staff for a short period often recovers more than a trustee who locks the doors on the first day. Wages for work after filing that benefits the estate rank as administrative expenses, while wages earned before filing fall into the priority scheme of section 507(a)(4) within its limits. The chain therefore has a human segment, and its value decays fastest of all: staff who leave take operational memory with them, and no sale process can recover memory once dispersed.

Seeing the firm as a chain also explains why liquidation outcomes vary so widely between cases with similar debts. A debtor whose value sits in unencumbered, easily sold assets produces a fund. A debtor whose value sits in pledged collateral, disputed receivables and expiring contracts produces litigation and a thin residue. The debts on the two balance sheets may match almost exactly, yet the first estate pays a substantial dividend and the second pays almost nothing to general creditors. Position in the chain, perfection of interests and quality of records decide the difference long before any auctioneer is engaged.

## The Petition, the Order for Relief and the Estate

A voluntary corporate case begins when an authorized officer or board files a petition with the bankruptcy court under section 301, accompanied by schedules of assets and liabilities, a statement of financial affairs and lists of creditors and contracts. The filing must be authorized under the governance documents of the entity and state law, and a disputed authorization can shadow the opening weeks of a case. The petition date fixes the legal landscape: the estate is measured at commencement, the stay takes effect, lookback periods for avoidance actions are counted backward from that date, and deadlines throughout the case run from the order for relief that the voluntary filing itself constitutes.

An involuntary case follows a different path. Under section 303, qualifying creditors may file a petition against a company, and the company may contest it. Until the court enters an order for relief, the debtor generally continues to operate, and creditors bear the risk that a failed involuntary petition brings damages under section 303(i). Involuntary filings are uncommon against operating companies because the petitioning creditors must prove that the debtor is generally not paying debts as they become due, unless a custodian has taken charge of the property. The device exists as a backstop against a debtor that would rather let favored creditors strip the firm in private than face a collective proceeding in public.

The order for relief converts a filing into an administration. The United States Trustee, an office within the Department of Justice created nationally by the Bankruptcy Judges, United States Trustees, and Family Farmer Bankruptcy Act of 1986, appoints an interim trustee under section 701 from a panel of private trustees. The estate under section 541 comes into being as a separate legal entity for many purposes, with its own tax identity and its own bank accounts opened by the trustee.

History supplies a plain instance of the sequence. The Oregon iron flow battery maker ESS Tech ceased operations and filed a voluntary Chapter 7 petition in the District of Delaware on November 13, 2026, stating in its Form 8-K that holders of its common stock were unlikely to receive any distribution. The disclosure captures the standard order of events in compressed form: operations end by board decision, a petition follows in a chosen federal district, and the expectations of shareholders are addressed at the outset because the ranking that governs distribution places equity behind every class of debt.

Schedules filed under penalty of perjury give the case its working map. Schedule A and B list real and personal property, Schedule D lists secured claims, Schedule E and F list priority and general unsecured claims, Schedule G lists executory contracts and unexpired leases, and the statement of financial affairs traces transfers, payments, lawsuits and closed accounts over defined periods before filing. Creditors and the trustee test those disclosures against bank records and tax returns, and gaps between the schedules and the records often point to recoverable transfers or missing assets. 

Venue and district shape the practical conduct of a case without changing the governing statute. A corporation may file where it is incorporated, where its principal place of business sits, or where an affiliate case is pending, and large cases have often been filed in the District of Delaware or the Southern District of New York for the depth of their benches and bars. Lynn LoPucki criticized forum competition among bankruptcy courts in Courting Failure in 2005, arguing that courts competing for large cases may bend practice toward the parties who choose the forum. Whatever the district, the Code sections that build the estate and rank the claims remain identical, and a small manufacturer and a public company pass through the same statutory doorway.

## The Automatic Stay and the Race It Stops

The automatic stay in section 362 is the first substantive protection a filing creates, and it arises by operation of law the moment a petition is filed, without a separate court order or a hearing. The stay halts the commencement or continuation of judicial and administrative actions against the debtor, enforcement of judgments, acts to obtain possession of estate property, acts to create, perfect or enforce liens, and most collection calls, letters and setoffs.

The race the stay stops is easy to picture. Without a collective proceeding, each creditor improves its position by moving first: suing quickly, obtaining a judgment, levying on bank accounts, seizing equipment or demanding cash before shipment while other creditors wait. Douglas Baird and Thomas Jackson, in their work on corporate reorganizations beginning in the 1980s, framed bankruptcy as a response to this common pool problem, in which individual collection destroys value that collective restraint would preserve. A bank account emptied by the fastest creditor cannot fund the sale process that might have paid every creditor something, and equipment seized piecemeal cannot be sold as a working line.

The stay is broad but never total. Section 362(b) lists exceptions, including criminal proceedings, certain domestic support and family law matters, and defined tax and regulatory actions. Governmental units enforcing police and regulatory power may continue many proceedings, although the stay still blocks enforcement of a money judgment against estate property in most settings. A landlord with a judgment of possession obtained before filing, a lender whose collateral is not estate property, or a party to a terminated contract may each stand partly outside the wall. The trustee and creditors read the exceptions closely, because an act wrongly treated as stayed can stall the administration, and an act wrongly treated as excepted can strip the estate.

Duration matters as much as scope. The stay continues as to estate property until that property is no longer property of the estate, and as to other acts until the case is closed, dismissed or converted. In a corporate Chapter 7 case there is no discharge to mark an endpoint, so closing and dismissal do the work. A secured creditor unwilling to wait seeks relief under section 362(d), and the court balances the value of the collateral, the equity cushion above the debt and the need for the property in the administration.

Setoff and bank practice create a quiet corner of stay law with large practical effects. A bank holding a debtor deposit may place an administrative freeze on an account while seeking stay relief to exercise setoff under section 553, a practice addressed by the Supreme Court in Citizens Bank of Maryland v. Strumpf in 1995. Cash in a frozen account can be the difference between an estate that funds an orderly sale and an estate that cannot pay for insurance or security in its first week. Trustees therefore move early to identify accounts, redirect receipts into estate accounts and negotiate releases, and the speed of that housekeeping often decides whether the stay protects a functioning administration or merely a locked room.

For employees, suppliers and customers the stay is experienced as silence. Invoices go unpaid, delivery obligations pause and phone calls meet a recorded message directing callers to the court docket. That silence is not a judgment that the claims lack merit. It is the sound of individual remedies being replaced by a single queue, in which each claim will be examined, classified and paid according to rank when the fund exists. The frustration the stay causes is real, and the Code accepts it as the price of preventing a disorderly seizure contest in which the strongest claimant takes property that the ranking rules assign to others.

## The Trustee Takes the Keys

Control of the company passes to a trustee whose authority comes from sections 701 to 704 of the Code. The United States Trustee appoints an interim trustee from a standing panel of private individuals screened for the role, and creditors may elect a different trustee at the meeting of creditors under section 702 if enough voting claims participate. In practice the interim trustee usually serves for the whole case. The office is fiduciary, owed to the estate as a whole, and supervised by the court and by the United States Trustee Program.

### Who runs the company after a Chapter 7 filing?

A panel trustee appointed under section 701 takes control. The trustee secures premises, bank accounts and records, directs remaining staff, and answers to the bankruptcy court and the United States Trustee. Directors and officers lose authority over estate property, although the court may authorize limited operations under section 721 in rare cases.

The first duties are physical. The trustee changes locks or confirms security, takes possession of keys, alarm codes, books, servers and banking credentials, and verifies insurance on buildings, vehicles and inventory. Bank accounts in the name of the debtor are closed or controlled, and new estate accounts receive incoming receipts. Former officers are directed to turn over property and records under section 542, and a refusal can be met with a turnover order enforced by the court.

Section 704 lists the duties that structure the administration: collect and reduce estate property to money, account for all property received, examine proofs of claim, oppose an improper discharge where relevant, furnish information to parties in interest, and file a final report and account with the court and the United States Trustee. Charles Jordan Tabb, in The Law of Bankruptcy in 1997, presents the trustee as the central investigative officer of a liquidation, because no other participant holds both the records and the incentive to follow them wherever they lead.

Operating the business is the exception rather than the rule. Section 721 allows the court to authorize the trustee to operate the debtor business for a limited period when operation serves the estate, for example to complete work in progress, run a seasonal sale or keep a license alive until a buyer closes. Operation creates administrative obligations, including wages, taxes and insurance, and a trustee who trades at a loss spends money that belongs to creditors. Most business cases therefore involve a short, controlled wind down rather than continued trade: perishable stock is sold, open orders are completed or rejected, and premises are emptied on a timetable set by rent accruing against the estate.

The trustee also inherits the disputes of the firm. Insiders may have received payments, assets may have been transferred to affiliates, and books may have been kept in a manner that flatters the final trading period. Examination under Rule 2004 of the Federal Rules of Bankruptcy Procedure allows the trustee to compel testimony and documents from officers, accountants and counterparties. Settlements reached by the trustee require court approval under Rule 9019 after notice to creditors, which keeps private bargains inside public view and gives any creditor a chance to object before estate property is compromised away.

Compensation aligns the office with recovery rather than with delay. A trustee receives a commission under section 326 calculated on moneys disbursed, capped on a sliding scale that falls as distributions grow, plus a small statutory payment in a case with no assets. The structure rewards turning property into distributions and penalises nothing so much as an estate left idle. It also explains a hard feature of small cases: where the likely recovery is tiny, the economic incentive to pursue doubtful claims weakens, and the trustee may report a case as one with no assets available for distribution after diligent inquiry.

## Secured Debt, Collateral and the Limits of the Stay

A secured claim rests on collateral, and section 506(a) divides it into a secured portion equal to the value of the creditor interest in the property and an unsecured portion for any balance owed above that value. A lender owed more than its collateral is worth therefore stands in two places at once: first against the property itself, and later in the general unsecured pool for the deficiency. Valuation drives everything, and valuation depends on the proposed use or disposition of the property. Equipment valued as part of a working plant carries one figure, the same equipment valued for auction carries another, and litigation over which standard applies is common in cases where the collateral nearly covers the debt.

### Can a secured lender take its collateral during a Chapter 7 case?

A secured lender cannot simply seize collateral after filing, because section 362 stays foreclosure and repossession. The lender may seek relief from the stay under section 362(d), showing lack of adequate protection or no equity in the property, and the court decides after notice and a hearing.

Adequate protection is the doctrine that fills the waiting period. While the stay holds a lender away from collateral that is falling in value, section 361 allows the court to order cash payments, replacement liens or other protection that keeps the secured position from eroding. If protection fails, relief from the stay follows, and the lender enforces its rights under state law outside the collective process. Where the property has no equity above the lien and is not needed for an effective administration, section 362(d)(2) supports relief as well, although in a liquidation the need argument usually turns on whether a trustee sale would realise more than a foreclosure sale for the benefit of junior claimants.

Perfection decides whether a claimed security interest survives contact with the trustee. A lien that was never perfected under the applicable state filing system, often Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code for personal property or the land records for real property, can be avoided by the trustee using the strong arm power in section 544(a), which gives the trustee the rights of a hypothetical judgment lien creditor at filing. An avoided lien leaves the creditor general and unsecured, and the collateral value flows into the estate for distribution by rank. Searches of filing records therefore rank among the earliest tasks in any case with significant secured debt, because one defective filing can change the outcome for every other creditor.

Surcharge and carve out arrangements qualify the picture at the edges. Under section 506(c), a trustee may recover from collateral the reasonable and necessary costs of preserving or disposing of that property to the extent of the benefit to the secured creditor, so a lender can bear the cost of the sale that pays it. In negotiated carve outs, a secured lender agrees that part of the collateral proceeds funds the administration, usually because an orderly trustee sale promises a better return than an immediate foreclosure. 

Interest and fees follow the equity in the collateral. An oversecured creditor may recover post petition interest and reasonable fees provided for in its agreement under section 506(b), paid from the cushion of value above the debt. An undersecured creditor receives no such interest, a rule confirmed for Chapter 7 practice by the Supreme Court in United Savings Association of Texas v. Timbers of Inwood Forest Associates in 1988. The distinction rewards lenders that lent against genuine value and prevents a claim for the time value of money from consuming an estate in which the lender was never fully covered. For unsecured creditors watching the fund, the Timbers rule preserves whatever residue the collateral leaves behind.

## Turning Assets Into Cash

Sale authority comes from section 363, which allows a trustee, after notice and a hearing, to sell property of the estate outside the ordinary course of business, and within the ordinary course in defined circumstances. A sale may be public or private, by auction or by negotiated agreement, and the trustee must show that the method chosen serves the estate. Notice goes to creditors, who may object to price, terms or procedure, and the court resolves objections before the sale closes. A buyer that purchases in good faith receives protection under section 363(m), which generally preserves the sale against reversal on appeal unless the sale was stayed, and that protection is what allows buyers to pay full value without fearing that the transaction will unravel later.

Free and clear sales under section 363(f) do much of the heavy lifting in business liquidations. Where the statutory conditions are met, property can be sold free of liens and other interests, with those interests attaching to the proceeds instead. A buyer acquires clean title, lenders keep their rank against the cash, and disputes about value move from the asset to the fund, where they can be resolved without blocking the transaction. A trustee may sell a whole division as a unit rather than piecemeal, facing the same perimeter questions examined in [how corporate separations divide assets and debt](/2026/11/06/how-corporate-spin-offs-work/), because deciding which assets, contracts and liabilities travel together determines what a buyer will bid. Selling the unit intact can preserve going concern value in the equipment, workforce arrangements and customer relationships that separate auctions would scatter.

Method follows asset type. Inventory suited to retail channels may be sold through a store closing sale conducted by a specialist agent under court approved terms, with the agent fee weighed against the higher recovery the format produces. Industrial equipment goes to auction, by catalog and increasingly by timed online bidding, with reserve prices set from appraisals. Real property is marketed through brokers, exposed to the market for a tested period and sold subject to higher bids at a hearing. Receivables may be collected by the trustee, sold at a discount to a factor, or compromised claim by claim where defenses make full collection unlikely. Each method carries its own cost structure, and the trustee records the comparison that justified the choice.

Appraisal discipline protects the process from its own optimism. Trustees rely on appraisers who value property on the basis the sale will actually use: orderly liquidation value for a marketed sale, forced liquidation value for a rapid auction, fair market value for a negotiated transaction with time to test the market. Warren and Westbrook, first published in 1986, emphasize that liquidation value is a function of method and time rather than a fixed property of the asset. A trustee given ninety days and a marketing budget recovers a different sum from the same warehouse of goods than a trustee forced to sell in nine days, and creditors judging a result fairly must compare the price with the conditions under which it was obtained.

Liens, taxes and sale costs come off the top of particular funds before distribution. A sale of encumbered property pays the costs properly chargeable to that property, satisfies the lien to the extent of the value, and releases any surplus into the general estate. Exemptions play no part for a corporate debtor, since exemption rights under section 522 belong to individuals. Abandonment under section 554 removes property that cannot be sold for more than its burdens, including contaminated land or fully encumbered equipment, returning it to the debtor or leaving it to the lienholder. The residue after every sale, settlement and abandonment is a sum of money and a list of claims, and at that point the liquidation has completed its conversion task and distribution can begin.

## Claims, Proof and the Bar Date

A claim exists in bankruptcy whether or not its holder files anything, because section 101 defines a claim broadly as a right to payment, disputed or undisputed, contingent or fixed. Proof and allowance are separate questions from existence. Under section 501, a creditor files a proof of claim using the official form, attaching documents that support the debt, and a properly filed claim is deemed allowed under section 502(a) unless a party in interest objects. The trustee reviews claims against the schedules and the books of the debtor, and objections resolve duplicate filings, claims for amounts never owed, penalty claims dressed as damages and claims filed in the wrong class.

The bar date gives the process its closing edge. In a Chapter 7 case, Rule 3002 of the Federal Rules of Bankruptcy Procedure sets the deadline for filing proofs of claim, generally ninety days after the first date set for the meeting of creditors in a case with assets, with longer periods for governmental units. A notice of no dividend tells creditors in an assetless case not to file at all, and a later discovery of assets produces a fresh notice and a fresh filing period. A late claim is not always worthless, but section 726 subordinates tardily filed claims within the distribution scheme, paying them only after timely claims of the same class have been satisfied, which in a thin estate usually means nothing at all.

Classification begins at filing and follows the documents. A creditor claiming secured status identifies the collateral and the perfection behind the lien. A creditor claiming priority identifies the section of 507 that grants it, whether wages under section 507(a)(4), taxes under section 507(a)(8) or another listed category. A creditor that checks the wrong box does not lose the underlying debt, but the trustee objection process sorts the claim into its proper rank, and rank rather than the amount claimed is usually where the money is decided. Large claims attract scrutiny of interest calculations, fee provisions and default charges, each tested against the agreement and against section 502(b), which disallows claims for unmatured interest and caps certain lease and employment damages.

The meeting of creditors under section 341 is where claims meet the record in public. Convened by the United States Trustee and presided over by the case trustee rather than a judge, the meeting places a representative of the debtor under oath to answer questions about assets, transfers, debts and records. Creditors may attend and ask questions, although many cases draw none, and the trustee uses the occasion to test the schedules against live answers. Statements made there shape the investigation that follows: an unexplained transfer mentioned in passing can become an avoidance action, and a missing asset can become a turnover demand.

Estimation and allowance disputes handle claims that resist a fixed number. A contingent claim, such as a guarantee that has not been called, or an unliquidated claim, such as a lawsuit still pending at filing, may be estimated by the court under section 502(c) where waiting for liquidation of the claim would unduly delay distribution. The estimate binds the distribution without deciding the underlying dispute for all purposes. Setoff rights preserved by section 553 reduce a claim by amounts the creditor owes the debtor, subject to limits and to the stay. When the claims register is finally settled, the trustee holds the denominator of the dividend calculation: the total allowed claims in each class, against which the fund built from sales is measured class by class in strict rank order.

## The Queue Itself: Priority Under the Code

Distribution in a corporate Chapter 7 case under Title 11 of the United States Code follows rank rather than bargaining strength, timing of a lawsuit, or volume of complaint. Section 726 of the Code supplies the order for property of the estate after secured claims have been satisfied from their collateral under section 506, and section 507 supplies the ranking inside the priority tier itself. A claim either fits a defined class or it does not, and classification happens on the papers filed in the case, tested against the statute, long before any dividend is calculated. Charles Jordan Tabb, in The Law of Bankruptcy (1997), treats that classification exercise as the center of liquidation practice, because a creditor placed one tier too low may receive a fraction of what a correctly placed creditor receives from the same fund. The trustee therefore reads every proof of claim against the Code first and against fairness arguments second, since the statute leaves little room for equitable rearrangement between classes.

The table below settles which class is paid from what source, and in which order, when a trustee distributes the proceeds of a liquidation.

| Rank | Claim class | Statutory basis | How it is paid |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Secured claims to the value of collateral | section 506 | Paid from the proceeds of the collateral itself up to the value of that collateral |
| 2 | Administrative expenses | section 507 and section 726 | Paid in full from unencumbered estate funds before any lower class receives a distribution |
| 3 | Priority unsecured claims | section 507 and section 726 | Paid in the internal order set by section 507 after administrative expenses are satisfied |
| 4 | General unsecured claims | section 726 | Paid pro rata from whatever funds remain after every higher class has been paid in full |
| 5 | Subordinated and penalty claims | section 510 and section 726 | Paid only after general unsecured claims are satisfied in full |
| 6 | Interest on claims | section 726 | Paid at the legal rate from any surplus left after all claim classes are satisfied |
| 7 | Shareholders | section 726 | Receive any residue after every claim and interest obligation has been paid in full |

Reading the table vertically explains most liquidation outcomes. A class is paid in full before the next class receives anything, a rule lawyers call absolute priority in distribution, and within a single class that cannot be paid in full, creditors share pro rata in proportion to their allowed claims. A secured creditor whose collateral sells for less than the debt holds two positions at once: a secured claim to the value of the collateral under section 506(a), and a general unsecured claim for the deficiency, which waits with trade creditors and bondholders far down the line. A lender that holds middle-market loans and stands in the queue as a creditor illustrates the split clearly, since the portion covered by a perfected lien on equipment or receivables is satisfied from that property, while any shortfall depends entirely on the unencumbered fund, a position described in accounts of [how business development companies lend to private firms](/2026/11/12/how-business-development-companies-work/). Oversecured creditors stand apart again, because section 506(b) allows interest and reasonable fees against the collateral cushion where the agreement provides for them and the value exceeds the debt.

Priority inside the unsecured tiers is equally mechanical. Section 507(a) lists categories in a fixed internal sequence, among them domestic support obligations in individual cases, administrative expenses cross-referenced from section 507(a)(2), wage claims within statutory caps, employee benefit contributions, certain tax claims, and claims for death or personal injury arising from unlawful operation of a motor vehicle. In a corporate case the wage, benefit and tax categories do most of the practical work. Subordination under section 510 can push a claim below general unsecured creditors where a contract agreed to subordination, where a court orders equitable subordination for misconduct, or where the claim arises from the purchase or sale of a security of the debtor. Penalty claims that are not compensation for actual pecuniary loss sit low under section 726(a)(4). Interest under section 726(a)(5) and any surplus return to the debtor under section 726(a)(6) complete the ladder, and in a corporate liquidation that final rung usually means the shareholders, whose interest is an ownership position rather than a claim at all.

## Paying for the Winding Down Itself

A liquidation consumes money before it distributes any. The trustee must secure premises, insure assets, hire auctioneers and brokers, retain accountants to reconstruct records, and pay counsel to pursue or defend litigation that belongs to the estate. Section 503(b) of the Code defines administrative expenses to include the actual and necessary costs of preserving the estate, compensation for services rendered after the petition, certain taxes incurred by the estate, and fees awarded under section 330 to trustees, examiners and professional persons employed under section 327. Section 507(a)(2) then grants those expenses second priority among unsecured claims, and section 726(a)(1) places them at the head of the distribution from unencumbered funds. The logic is functional rather than generous: without a promise that the people who wind a company down will be paid ahead of older debts, few professionals would accept the work, and assets would sit uninsured and unsold while their value leaked away.

### How is a Chapter 7 trustee paid?

A Chapter 7 trustee is paid from the estate itself, through a commission on moneys disbursed to parties other than the debtor, calculated under section 326 on a sliding percentage scale with statutory caps, plus reimbursement of reasonable expenses, and the award requires court approval after notice under section 330 before any payment is made.

Fees in a liquidation are therefore supervised rather than contractual in the ordinary sense. A professional files an application describing the work performed, the time spent, the rates charged and the benefit to the estate, and creditors and the United States Trustee may object before the court allows the fee. Warren and Westbrook (first published 1986), describe fee review as one of the few continuous controls creditors retain over a process otherwise run by the trustee, because every dollar allowed as an administrative expense reduces the dividend available below it in the queue. Courts test applications against the factors in section 330, including whether the services were necessary and beneficial at the time they were rendered, and they reduce awards where work duplicated another professional's effort or where a sale produced too little value to justify the cost of obtaining it.

Administrative insolvency is the hard edge of the system. Where unencumbered assets cannot cover the cost of administering the estate, the case yields no dividend to unsecured creditors at all, and a trustee may abandon burdensome property under section 554 rather than spend estate funds converting an asset whose sale would benefit only the administration itself. Secured creditors sometimes fund a short administration through a negotiated carve-out from collateral proceeds, agreeing that a defined sum may be used for trustee and professional costs in exchange for an orderly sale of the collateral that is expected to return more than a hurried foreclosure. Where no such agreement exists, section 506(c) permits recovery from collateral only to the extent of the reasonable and necessary costs of preserving or disposing of that property, and only to the benefit of the holder of the claim secured by it. The queue therefore pays for its own operation first, and the size of that bill is one of the earliest and most reliable predictors of what general creditors will eventually receive.

## Clawback: Preferences and Fraudulent Transfers

The estate a trustee distributes is not limited to the property a company holds on the petition date. Sections 544, 547 and 548 of the Code give the trustee avoidance powers, often called clawback powers, that pull certain pre-petition transfers back into the estate so that section 550 can recover the property or its value for distribution through the queue. The policy reaches back to the Statute of Bankrupts of 1542 and to the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, both of which treated secret or favoring transfers by a failing debtor as a fraud on the general body of creditors. Douglas Baird, in The Elements of Bankruptcy (1992), frames avoidance as the enforcement mechanism behind equality of distribution: a ranking system means little if a debtor can empty the estate in favor of chosen creditors in the weeks before a filing renders the ranking irrelevant.

Preference law under section 547 is the most frequently used branch. A trustee may avoid a transfer of the debtor's property to or for the benefit of a creditor, made on account of an antecedent debt, while the debtor was insolvent, within ninety days before the petition for ordinary creditors and within one year for insiders, where the transfer lets the creditor receive more than it would receive in a Chapter 7 distribution if the transfer had never occurred. Each element is litigated on documents: payment records, invoices, ledger entries and correspondence showing whether a debt predated the payment. Defenses in section 547(c) protect transfers made in the ordinary course of business between the parties, payments that were substantially contemporaneous exchanges for new value, and transfers followed by further unsecured credit that replenished the estate. A supplier paid on ordinary thirty-day terms in the ordinary pattern of dealing therefore often keeps the payment, while a lender that extracted an unusual lump sum against an old balance shortly before the filing faces recovery.

Fraudulent transfer law reaches further back and asks a different question. Section 548 covers transfers made within two years before the petition where the debtor acted with actual intent to hinder, delay or defraud a creditor, and also transfers where the debtor received less than reasonably equivalent value while insolvent, undercapitalized or unable to pay debts as they matured. Section 544(b) lets the trustee borrow the longer reach-back period of applicable state law, commonly the Uniform Voidable Transactions Act adopted in most states, which extends the window to four years in many jurisdictions. Classic targets include property transferred to relatives or affiliates for nominal consideration, excessive payments to departing executives, and leveraged transactions that loaded a company with debt while value passed to sellers or shareholders. The recipient of an avoided transfer may assert a good faith defense under section 548(c) where value was given without knowledge of voidability, and initial transferees face stricter liability under section 550 than later recipients who took in good faith. Every recovery enlarges the same fund, and a successful avoidance action changes no creditor's rank; it increases the amount that rank is paid from, which is why trustees evaluate litigation as an asset of the estate like any other, weighing cost, collectability and proof before filing suit.

## Contracts and Leases the Estate Does Not Want

A company enters liquidation carrying promises as well as property. Supply agreements, equipment leases, property leases, service contracts and license arrangements may each be executory, meaning material performance remains due on both sides, and section 365 of the Code governs whether the estate keeps or sheds them. In a Chapter 7 case the default leans toward shedding: under section 365(d)(1), an executory contract or unexpired lease that the trustee does not assume within sixty days after the order for relief, or within any extension the court grants, is deemed rejected. The trustee assumes only a contract that promises net value to the estate, for example a below-market lease that can be assigned for a payment, or a supply arrangement needed to complete saleable inventory, and assumption requires cure of defaults and adequate assurance of future performance under section 365(b).

Rejection is not rescission. Section 365(g) treats rejection as a breach of the contract immediately before the petition date, which converts the counterparty's expectation of future performance into a pre-petition claim for damages that files with the general unsecured creditors. The landlord of a rejected property lease receives a further statutory limit: section 502(b)(6) caps a lessor's claim at the rent reserved for the greater of one year or fifteen percent of the remaining term, not to exceed three years, plus unpaid rent already due. A counterparty that supplied goods or services after the petition under a contract the estate used before rejection may instead hold an administrative claim for the value actually conferred, which is one reason trustees move quickly to state which contracts a skeleton operation still needs. Ipso facto clauses, contract terms that purport to terminate an agreement because of insolvency or a bankruptcy filing, are generally unenforceable under section 365(e), so a counterparty cannot defeat the trustee's choice merely by pointing to such a clause.

Certain categories receive special treatment because Congress judged that a counterparty or a market needed protection beyond the general rule. Personal service contracts and contracts that applicable law excuses a party from accepting performance from a different entity cannot be assumed or assigned under section 365(c). Financial contracts, including swaps, forward contracts and securities contracts, carry safe harbours in sections 555, 556 and 560 that permit termination and netting despite the automatic stay. Collective bargaining agreements follow the procedures of section 1113 in reorganization cases, while a liquidation more commonly ends the employment relationship altogether, leaving wage and benefit claims to the priority system. Charles Jordan Tabb, in The Law of Bankruptcy (1997), notes that section 365 in a liquidation functions less as a planning tool than as a sorting mechanism: valuable contracts become assets to be sold or assigned, burdensome contracts become claims to be ranked, and nothing in between survives, because a company that will not trade again has no use for performance extending into a future it will not have.

Non-residential real property leases carry an additional discipline under section 365(d)(4), which sets an outer period for assumption or rejection and obliges the trustee to perform lease obligations arising after the order for relief until the decision is made, so rent during administration can itself become an administrative charge against the estate rather than a mere rejected claim.

## Wages, Benefits and the Workforce at Shutdown

Employees of a liquidated company hold claims for work already performed, and the Code places part of those claims ahead of ordinary trade debt while leaving the remainder to share with general creditors. Section 507(a)(4) grants priority to allowed unsecured claims for wages, salaries and commissions, including vacation, severance and sick leave pay, earned within one hundred eighty days before the petition date or before the business ceased, whichever occurred first, subject to a per-employee cap that is adjusted periodically under section 104. Section 507(a)(5) grants a parallel priority for contributions to employee benefit plans arising from services rendered within the same one hundred eighty day window, reduced by amounts paid under the wage priority and subject to its own formula. Amounts earned outside the window, or above the cap, remain valid claims, but they file as general unsecured claims and receive only the dividend that class obtains.

### Do employees get paid before other creditors in a liquidation?

Employees are paid before general unsecured creditors for a defined slice of what they are owed, because section 507 grants priority to wages earned within one hundred eighty days before the filing, up to a statutory cap per employee, while amounts above the cap or outside the period rank as ordinary unsecured claims.

The distinction between a priority slice and full payment matters in practice, because a workforce claim is often the largest single human cost in a shutdown. A machinist owed back pay, accrued vacation pay and a final commission may see the priority portion paid in full from a modest estate while the balance returns pennies, and a salaried manager owed a large severance sum may find most of that sum above the cap. Warren and Westbrook (first published 1986), observe that the wage priority reflects a judgment that employees extend involuntary credit to a failing employer, since labour is delivered before payday and cannot be repossessed, unlike goods a supplier might reclaim under section 546(c) or section 503(b)(9), which grants administrative priority to the value of goods received by the debtor within twenty days before the petition in the ordinary course of business.

Shutdown obligations extend beyond the bankruptcy claims process itself. The federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act of 1988 requires covered employers to give sixty days of notice before a plant closing or mass layoff, subject to exceptions that include a faltering company provision applied narrowly, and a trustee who continues operating a business for a period after the petition can incur obligations under that statute as an employer. Pension plans raise separate questions governed by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 and the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, whose claims for unfunded benefit liabilities can be substantial and whose lien and priority treatment has generated extensive litigation. Health coverage continuation rights under federal law depend on a plan continuing to exist, and a liquidation that terminates every plan can end that route altogether. None of those regimes changes the queue inside the bankruptcy case; each adds a body of law around the edges of the queue, determining which obligations become claims, which become administrative costs of a brief continued operation, and which fall on insurance or guarantee funds outside the estate entirely.

## Taxes and the Government as Creditor

Governments appear in a liquidation in several capacities at once: as taxing authorities holding priority claims, as regulators whose police powers the automatic stay does not block under section 362(b)(4), and occasionally as ordinary contract creditors. Section 507(a)(8) grants priority to specified tax claims, including income taxes for a taxable year ending on or before the petition date where a return was last due within three years before the filing, property taxes incurred before the petition and last payable without penalty within one year, employment taxes on wages paid before the petition, and certain excise taxes, each with its own measuring period. Taxes outside those categories, and penalties that are not compensation for actual pecuniary loss, fall lower: a tax penalty unrelated to a priority tax claim ranks with the subordinated and penalty tier under section 726(a)(4), while a penalty tied to a priority tax generally follows that tax.

The estate itself becomes a taxpayer during administration. A trustee who sells appreciated property, collects receivables or operates a business briefly generates tax obligations for the estate, and section 503(b)(1)(B) treats taxes incurred by the estate as administrative expenses, which places them ahead of pre-petition priority claims in the distribution order. Section 505 allows the court to determine the amount or legality of a tax where the amount has not been contested and adjudicated before the petition, giving the trustee a forum to resolve disputed liabilities that would otherwise block a final distribution. Section 362(a)(8) stays certain tax proceedings, yet assessment and audit activity receive tailored treatment elsewhere in section 362, reflecting a balance between orderly administration and revenue collection that Congress has adjusted repeatedly since the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978 created the modern Code effective October 1, 1979.

Priority for taxes rests on a rationale different from the wage priority. A taxing authority is an involuntary creditor that cannot screen a taxpayer in advance, cannot demand security from every business that begins trading, and collects revenue on which public services depend, so the Code protects a defined band of recent taxes rather than every liability however old. Older income taxes can lose priority where a return was filed late or where the measuring periods in section 507(a)(8) have run, and such claims then share as general unsecured claims despite the sovereign identity of the holder. Secured tax liens stand on a different footing again, because a properly perfected federal tax lien under the Internal Revenue Code attaches to property and is satisfied from that property subject to the rules governing lien validity against a trustee exercising the strong-arm power in section 544. Douglas Baird and Thomas Jackson, in their work on corporate reorganizations published during the nineteen eighties, used tax and wage priorities as test cases for a broader question that liquidation practice still confronts in every case: each priority granted to one class is paid for by the class below it, and a queue with too many favored tiers leaves the general creditors, whose claims the collective process was built to protect, dividing a residue that repeated exceptions have already consumed.

## Customers, Deposits and Unfinished Orders

Customers occupy an unusual position in a liquidation because many of them are creditors without ever having thought of themselves as lenders. A buyer who prepaid for goods never delivered, a subscriber who paid for a service period that will never run, and a client whose materials sit inside a closed workshop each hold a claim against the estate, and most such claims are general unsecured claims unless a specific provision lifts them higher. Section 507(a)(7) grants priority to allowed unsecured claims of individuals, up to a periodically adjusted cap, arising from deposits made in connection with the purchase, lease or rental of property, or the purchase of services, for personal, family or household use that were never delivered or provided. The provision protects household consumers rather than commercial buyers, and a business customer that prepaid a supplier ordinarily files with the trade creditors regardless of how sympathetic its position appears.

Property that merely sits in the debtor's hands raises a prior question, because not everything on the premises belongs to the estate. Goods held in trust, customer property held for repair, and items subject to a valid bailment may fall outside property of the estate under section 541(d) where the debtor held only legal title and no equitable interest, and the owner seeks return of the property itself rather than a dividend. Consigned goods turn on the Uniform Commercial Code and on whether the consignor perfected its interest against the debtor's creditors, a filing question that decides whether inventory on the shelves is an estate asset or a third party's property. Gift cards and stored value balances sit at the harder end of the spectrum: the holder paid value in advance, the obligation is unsecured, and treatment depends on the terms of issue and on consumer protection law in the issuing jurisdiction, while the trustee's duty is to schedule the liability honestly and let the claims process rank it.

Unfinished orders force a practical decision before any legal ranking occurs. A trustee may complete work in progress where the remaining cost is small and the finished goods sell for materially more than the parts, an exercise of the power to operate the business for a limited period under section 721 with court authorization, or may sell the unfinished inventory as it stands to a buyer in the same trade. Where completion would consume estate funds for the benefit of one customer alone, the trustee declines, the contract is rejected, and the customer's damages claim joins the queue. Charles Jordan Tabb, in The Law of Bankruptcy (1997), places customer claims among the clearest illustrations of the gap between commercial expectation and statutory design: a deposit feels like money held safely apart, yet unless a trust, a lien or section 507(a)(7) intervenes, the deposit was legally an unsecured loan to the company from the moment it was paid, and liquidation merely reveals a character the payment held all along.

Forwarding addresses, warranty records and service histories therefore matter to the trustee as claim administration tools, since each document either supports a customer's proof of claim, limits it to the correct amount, or shows that the obligation belonged to another entity entirely from the start.

## Patents, Brands, Licenses and Data on the Sale Table

Intangible property often carries the most portable value in a liquidation, because a patent, a trade mark, a domain name or a customer database can be sold to a buyer anywhere while a factory lease and heavy plant anchor value to one site. Section 541 brings intellectual property into the estate as property, and section 363 permits the trustee to sell it free and clear of interests under the conditions of section 363(f), with those interests attaching to the proceeds instead. A patent portfolio is valued on the remaining term of each patent, the breadth of its claims, the cost of enforcement and the existence of a licensing history that proves a market. A brand is valued on recognition that survives the failure of its owner, which is why names associated with a product category can sell for substantial sums while names associated only with the failed firm's own service record attract little interest beyond the value of redirected web traffic.

Licenses cut in both directions. Where the debtor is a licensee, section 365(c) and the case law on personal and non-exclusive licenses may prevent assumption and assignment of a patent or copyright license without the licensor's consent, which can destroy the very value a buyer came to purchase. Where the debtor is a licensor, section 365(n) protects a licensee of intellectual property as defined in section 101, allowing the licensee to retain its rights under the license if the trustee rejects the agreement, in exchange for continued royalty payments. Trade marks fall outside that statutory definition, a gap the Supreme Court addressed in Mission Product Holdings v. Tempnology in 2019 by holding that rejection is a breach rather than a rescission, so a trade mark licensee's rights survive rejection under the general breach principle. Software, source code escrow arrangements and franchise agreements each add their own assignment restrictions, and a trustee inventories them early because a sale deadline missed while consent is negotiated can leave an asset unsaleable.

Data requires separate handling because value and legal restriction travel together. Customer lists and transaction records can be estate assets, yet the Federal Trade Commission Act, state privacy statutes and the debtor's own published privacy policy may limit a sale, and section 332 provides for appointment of a consumer privacy ombudsman in cases where a sale of personally identifiable information would violate the debtor's stated policy. The ombudsman reports on the potential losses and gains of privacy from a proposed transfer, and courts weigh that report before approving a sale under section 363(b). Lynn LoPucki, in Courting Failure (2005), warned that intangible and going-concern value is the value most easily lost to haste, forum competition and insider advantage in large case practice, and the warning applies with equal force to a modest liquidation: a brand sold in a rushed auction to the only bidder who knew the sale was happening returns a price that the queue then treats as the asset's worth, and every class below the secured tier pays for the shortfall.

## The Meeting of Creditors and the Paper Trail

A liquidation runs on documents, and the meeting of creditors is the one occasion where the paper process becomes a spoken examination. Section 341 of the Code requires the United States Trustee to convene a meeting of creditors in every case, and section 343 requires the debtor, which in a corporate case means its officers or designated representatives, to appear and submit to examination under oath. Creditors, the panel trustee and the United States Trustee may question the representative about the schedules, the statement of financial affairs, the location of books and records, transfers made before the filing, and the whereabouts of assets that the schedules describe imperfectly. In most corporate liquidations few creditors attend, because the cost of attendance exceeds any realistic effect on a dividend, yet the examination still serves the trustee, who obtains admissions and leads on the record that later support avoidance actions, objections to claims and reports to the court.

The paper trail begins earlier and lasts longer than the meeting. The petition, schedules of assets and liabilities, and the statement of financial affairs required by section 521 place the debtor under a duty of complete disclosure signed under penalty of perjury, and the trustee compares those filings against bank records, tax returns, insurance documents and the debtor's own ledgers for the years before the filing. Rule 2004 of the Federal Rules of Bankruptcy Procedure permits a broader examination of any entity concerning the debtor's acts, conduct, property, liabilities and financial condition, a scope courts describe as a legitimate fishing expedition where the estate's records are incomplete. Claims are filed on Official Form 410 with supporting documents, the clerk maintains the claims register, and section 502 governs allowance, with a properly filed proof of claim deemed allowed unless a party in interest objects and sustains the objection.

Administration closes through accounting as much as through sale. The trustee files a final report and account under section 704(a)(9), showing every receipt and disbursement, the fees and expenses claimed, the claims allowed in each class and the dividend each class will receive, and the United States Trustee reviews that report before the court approves distribution. The United States Trustee Program, created nationally by the Bankruptcy Judges, United States Trustees, and Family Farmer Bankruptcy Act of 1986, supplies the standing oversight behind the panel trustee system: it appoints and supervises panel trustees, reviews fee applications, monitors cases for abuse and convenes the section 341 meeting, while Alabama and North Carolina perform parallel functions through bankruptcy administrators. Warren and Westbrook (first published 1986), treat that supervisory layer as a structural answer to a recurring problem in liquidation history since the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, namely that a process run entirely by interested parties drifts toward the interests of the parties who appear most often. The record a case leaves behind, from petition to final decree, is therefore not bureaucratic residue; the record is the mechanism by which an absent creditor, who never attended a meeting and never hired counsel, can verify that the queue was honoured.

## Conversion, Dismissal and the Case That Never Pays Out

A Chapter 7 case does not always travel in a straight line from petition to distribution. Section 706 of Title 11 gives a debtor a right, subject to limits, to convert a liquidation case to a case under Chapter 11, Chapter 12 or Chapter 13, provided the case began under Chapter 7 and the debtor meets the eligibility rules of the destination chapter. A corporation that discovers a buyer for the enterprise as a whole, or a creditor body willing to fund a plan, may therefore leave liquidation behind and attempt reorganization instead. The conversion right reflects a judgment written into the structure of the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978: liquidation is a default for a firm without a workable future, and a firm that can demonstrate a workable future belongs in a chapter built around a plan rather than a sale calendar.

Conversion also moves in the opposite direction, and that movement matters more in practice. A case that begins under Chapter 11 can be converted to Chapter 7 under section 1112 when a court finds cause, including substantial or continuing loss to the estate, an inability to effectuate a plan, or unreasonable delay that harms creditors. Charles Jordan Tabb, in The Law of Bankruptcy (1997), treats conversion as the point where the law stops subsidizing hope: a reorganization earns its protection while it holds a plausible route to payment, and it loses that protection when the estate shrinks month by month with no route in sight. At conversion, a Chapter 7 trustee replaces the debtor in possession, the sale machinery takes over, and claims that were once expected to be restructured are instead ranked for distribution from whatever value remains.

Dismissal ends a case by a different route. Under section 707, a court may dismiss a Chapter 7 case for cause after notice and a hearing, and cause includes unreasonable delay by the debtor that prejudices creditors, nonpayment of required fees, and failure to file required information. Dismissal restores the parties, as far as the Code permits, to the positions they held before the petition: the automatic stay under section 362 dissolves, creditors resume collection under nonbankruptcy law, and property revests according to the terms of the dismissal order. A dismissed case produces no queue discipline at all, because the collective proceeding that replaced the individual race has been withdrawn and the race resumes.

A third ending pays nobody, and it is the most common ending in smaller cases. In a no-asset case, the trustee investigates the schedules, finds no unencumbered property worth selling after liens and exemptions and the costs of administration are counted, and files a report of no distribution. Creditors receive a notice telling them not to file proofs of claim unless assets are later found. The estate closes with the machinery of sections 701 to 704 having run in full and the waterfall of section 726 having nothing to pour through it. Such a case still performs work: it produces an orderly examination under oath, a public record of assets and liabilities, and a bar against piecemeal seizure, even though the dividend to general unsecured creditors is zero and the company itself simply ceases to exist.

## No Discharge at the End of the Road

### Does a company receive a discharge in Chapter 7?

No. Section 727(a)(1) of Title 11 denies a discharge to a debtor that is not an individual, so a corporation or partnership that completes a Chapter 7 liquidation receives no discharge at all. The debts are not forgiven; the company ends because its assets have been sold and distributed.

That denial surprises readers who transfer the logic of individual bankruptcy to companies. An individual who completes a Chapter 7 case receives a discharge under section 727, a court order that releases personal liability for discharged debts and operates as an injunction against collection of those debts as personal obligations. The fresh start for individuals rests on a human rationale: a person continues to live and work after bankruptcy, and a discharge allows that person to do so without the permanent weight of prepetition debt. A corporation has no comparable continuing life to protect. Its charter, its contracts and its workforce do not require rehabilitation in the same sense, because the entity can be dissolved and its owners can form a new entity without carrying the old debts as personal burdens, subject always to guarantees and to liability imposed by other law.

Warren and Westbrook explain the distinction as a deliberate allocation of the fresh start to persons rather than to organizational shells. The corporate form already limits shareholder liability; a shareholder loses the investment in the failed firm, and limited liability stops the loss there in the ordinary case. Adding a corporate discharge on top of limited liability would give owners a second layer of protection while giving creditors nothing in return, because a discharged but assetless corporation holds no value for anyone. Section 727(a)(1) therefore closes the door cleanly: the estate is administered, the proceeds are distributed under section 726, and the remaining unpaid claims have no solvent defendant left to pursue.

The practical consequence shapes behavior long before a petition is filed. Because no discharge awaits a corporation, a Chapter 7 filing offers owners no personal cleansing of company debts and no injunction they can invoke for the entity afterward. Creditors whose claims remain unpaid after distribution may pursue any party who remains liable outside the estate, including a guarantor under a separate guaranty agreement, an officer liable under a specific statute, or a successor that assumed the debt by contract. Claims against the defunct corporation itself become claims against an empty shell, collectible in theory and worthless in practice once the charter is dissolved under state law.

Dissolution and bankruptcy remain separate operations, and confusing them produces errors. Dissolution is a creature of state corporate law, governing how directors wind up affairs, give notice to claimants and distribute remaining assets to shareholders. Chapter 7 is federal law under Title 11, governing the estate, the stay, the trustee and the order of payment. A corporation may dissolve without filing for bankruptcy, and a corporation may complete a Chapter 7 case and still require formal dissolution afterward to extinguish the charter. The absence of a discharge ties the two together in one respect: neither process forgives debts in the corporate setting, and each process instead settles who gets paid from a finite pool before the entity disappears.

## Liquidation Against Reorganization: Two Different Machines

Chapter 7 and Chapter 11 answer different questions, and treating one as a faster version of the other misreads both. Chapter 7 asks a distributive question: given a firm that will stop operating, which claims does the law pay first from the proceeds of a sale? Chapter 11 asks a restructuring question: given a firm whose operations hold value beyond a breakup, how should claims and interests be rewritten so the firm can continue? The first machine liquidates and ends the company. The second machine, when it succeeds, confirms a plan under section 1129, discharges the reorganized debtor under section 1141 and leaves a functioning enterprise in place of the old capital structure.

Control separates the machines at the outset. In Chapter 7, an interim trustee takes charge under section 701, creditors may elect a trustee under section 702, and the duties in section 704 place collection, sale and distribution in the trustee's hands while management steps aside. In Chapter 11, the debtor ordinarily remains in possession under sections 1107 and 1108, management keeps running the business, and a trustee appears only for cause under section 1104, such as fraud, dishonesty, incompetence or gross mismanagement. That difference in control reflects a difference in trust: liquidation assumes the firm's stewards have reached the end of a viable enterprise, while reorganization assumes the enterprise retains value that its stewards, supervised by the court and by creditors' committees, can preserve.

The treatment of value separates the machines a second time. A Chapter 7 sale under section 363 converts assets to cash in discrete transactions, free and clear of interests where section 363(f) permits, and the proceeds enter a single ranked distribution. A Chapter 11 plan classifies claims under section 1122, solicits votes under section 1126 and distributes cash, new debt or new equity according to the plan's terms, often over years. The contrast with negotiated restructuring sharpens the point: a filing that arrives with creditor support already arranged, of the kind described in [a guide to prepackaged bankruptcy and negotiated Chapter 11 filings](/2026/10/22/how-prepackaged-bankruptcy-works/), uses Chapter 11 to ratify a bargain struck before the petition, while Chapter 7 contains no bargain to ratify and no plan to confirm.

Other legal systems draw the same boundary with different instruments. India's codified process, examined in [a guide to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016 and its resolution process](/2012/04/01/insolvency-bankruptcy-code-2016-guide/), places a corporate debtor into a resolution period first and moves to liquidation when resolution fails or creditors choose it, so liquidation there functions as the fallback after a rescue attempt. United States law under Title 11 keeps the two tracks in separate chapters from the start and allows conversion between them, which places the choice of machine at the center of the case from the petition date onward.

Cost and time separate the machines a third time. A Chapter 7 case carries trustee commissions under section 326, sale costs and professional fees, yet its administrative structure is comparatively lean because no plan, disclosure statement or confirmation hearing is required. A Chapter 11 case carries the heavier administrative burden of a going concern: operating reports, committee professionals, valuation litigation and plan negotiation, all paid as administrative expenses while the business continues to spend. Douglas Baird, in The Elements of Bankruptcy (1992), frames the choice as a comparison between the value a firm holds as a collection of assets and the value it holds as an operating whole, net of the costs each process consumes. Where the operating whole holds no premium, the leaner machine preserves more for the queue, and Chapter 7 earns its place as the honest ending for a firm whose parts are worth more than its future.

## A Worked Example: One Manufacturer Through the Waterfall

A hypothetical manufacturer of metal components supplies the figures, and the figures stay fixed through every step. The trustee sells the plant, the equipment, the inventory and the receivables for gross sale proceeds of 4,200,000 dollars. One lender holds a secured claim of 2,600,000 dollars, fully covered by collateral sold for 2,600,000 dollars. Administrative expenses total 310,000 dollars. Priority wage and tax claims total 290,000 dollars. General unsecured claims total 6,000,000 dollars, filed by suppliers, landlords, trade creditors and holders of unsecured notes. Shareholders hold the common stock of a company that has stopped trading.

The priority table set out earlier in the discussion of the queue supplies the map for the money, and the example follows its rows in order. That table settles which class is paid from what, in which order: secured claims to the value of collateral first from the collateral itself, then administrative expenses, then priority unsecured claims, then general unsecured claims, then subordinated and penalty claims, then interest on claims, and shareholders last of all. The worked example tests the table against arithmetic. A table of ranks means little until a finite fund meets it, and the fund here is finite in a way that leaves the lower rows empty.

The secured lender leaves the general contest at the first step. Because the collateral sold for 2,600,000 dollars and the secured claim is 2,600,000 dollars, section 506 gives the lender an allowed secured claim equal to the full amount, and the collateral proceeds pay that claim in full. Those proceeds never enter the fund available to other creditors. Subtracting them from the gross sale proceeds of 4,200,000 dollars leaves unencumbered proceeds of 1,600,000 dollars. The lender has no deficiency claim in the example, because the collateral covered the debt exactly, so the lender does not reappear among the general unsecured creditors to share in the remaining fund.

Administration takes the next slice, because a liquidation that nobody pays to run produces no distribution for anyone. The 310,000 dollars of administrative expenses cover the trustee's commission under section 326, auction and storage costs, and the professional fees allowed under section 330 for work that preserved and sold the estate. Section 507(a)(2) grants those expenses priority, and section 726 pays them before the general classes. Subtracting 310,000 dollars from 1,600,000 dollars leaves 1,290,000 dollars. Priority wage and tax claims of 290,000 dollars follow under section 507, covering employee wage claims within the statutory cap and period in section 507(a)(4) and tax claims of the kinds listed in section 507(a)(8). Subtracting 290,000 dollars leaves a remaining fund for general unsecured creditors of 1,000,000 dollars.

Against that fund stand general unsecured claims of 6,000,000 dollars. Section 726(a)(2) distributes the fund to those claims pro rata, each creditor receiving the same fraction of its allowed claim. One million dollars divided across six million dollars of claims yields a dividend of about 16.7 percent: a supplier owed 60,000 dollars receives about 10,000 dollars, and a noteholder owed 600,000 dollars receives about 100,000 dollars, subject to rounding and to the allowance of each claim. Subordinated and penalty claims receive nothing because the fund is exhausted at the general unsecured row, interest on claims receives nothing for the same reason, and shareholders receive nothing. The order fixed before the first sale decided the outcome: the same gross proceeds, ranked differently, would have paid a different set of creditors, and no argument at the distribution hearing can move a claim from one row of the table to another.

## From the Statute of Bankrupts to the Modern Code

English law supplied the original template, and the template was punitive. The Statute of Bankrupts of 1542, enacted under Henry VIII, treated bankruptcy as a creditor remedy against traders who absconded, kept to their houses or otherwise evaded payment. Commissioners could seize the trader's property, sell it and divide the proceeds among creditors. The statute contained no discharge, no fresh start and no concern for the debtor's rehabilitation; it existed to defeat flight and concealment and to replace a scramble for assets with an administered division. That core move, substituting a collective division for individual seizure, survives in every later system, including the ranked distribution of section 726 under Title 11.

Federal bankruptcy law in the United States arrived in bursts before it arrived permanently. Congress enacted a Bankruptcy Act in 1800 after a financial panic, repealed it in 1803, returned with an act in 1841 that introduced voluntary bankruptcy, repealed that act in 1843, tried again in 1867 after the Civil War and repealed that statute in 1878. Each repeal reflected a political judgment that a federal system invited abuse or favored debtors at the expense of creditors. The Bankruptcy Act of 1898 ended the cycle and became the first permanent federal bankruptcy law, supplying a durable framework of referees, trustees and discharges that governed American liquidation for eighty years and normalized the idea that a failed firm's assets would be gathered and divided under federal supervision.

Reform in the twentieth century professionalized the machinery. The Chandler Act of 1938 strengthened trustees, expanded the role of the Securities and Exchange Commission in large corporate cases and tightened the administration of estates against insider control. Mid-century practice exposed other weaknesses: referees who both administered and adjudicated cases, uneven trustee quality across districts, and a patchwork of amendments that left the 1898 Act difficult to navigate. The Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978 answered with a comprehensive code. Enacted as Title 11 of the United States Code and effective October 1, 1979, the Code created the modern structure: a unified definition of property of the estate in section 541, an automatic stay in section 362, priorities in section 507, distribution in section 726 and a dedicated liquidation chapter in Chapter 7.

Institutional supervision completed the design a few years later. The Bankruptcy Judges, United States Trustees, and Family Farmer Bankruptcy Act of 1986 created the United States Trustee Program nationally, placing appointment of panel trustees, convening of the section 341 meeting of creditors and monitoring of cases in an executive branch office separate from the bankruptcy judges who decide disputes. Alabama and North Carolina operate through bankruptcy administrators instead, a reminder that even a national code accommodates local institutional history. Charles Jordan Tabb, in The Law of Bankruptcy (1997), presents the resulting system as a settlement among competing aims: speed for creditors, honesty from debtors, and a public record strong enough that parties who never enter the courtroom can still trust the division of a failed firm's remains.

## How Companies Arrive at the Liquidation Door

Companies rarely arrive at Chapter 7 through a single failure. The typical path runs through narrowing options, each stage closing a door a healthier firm could have used. A manufacturer loses a major customer and covers the gap with a revolving credit line. A retailer signs leases priced for sales volumes that a shift in consumer habits removes. A technology firm spends against revenue a delayed product cannot deliver. In each pattern, the balance sheet deteriorates before the income statement confesses the damage, because debt taken on during the optimistic period remains at full face value while the assets it financed lose value in a thin resale market.

Cash decides the timing more often than balance sheet insolvency. A firm can hold assets worth more than its debts on paper and still miss payroll, default on a loan covenant and trigger cross-default clauses across its borrowing agreements. Lenders respond in stages: a forbearance agreement that buys weeks in exchange for fees and reporting, a tightening of the borrowing base that reduces available credit, and finally a refusal to fund further draws. Suppliers shorten terms, demand payment on delivery and file liens where state law permits. Each creditor acting alone behaves rationally, and the collective effect is a liquidity collapse that a court filing then freezes under the automatic stay in section 362.

Directors face a distinct legal landscape as the door approaches. In solvency, directors owe fiduciary duties to the corporation and its shareholders. In insolvency, Delaware case law, including the Delaware Supreme Court decision in North American Catholic Educational Programming Foundation v. Gheewalla (2007), holds that creditors gain standing to bring derivative claims for breach of fiduciary duty, while the duties themselves continue to run to the corporation. That shift changes the audience for board decisions without changing the standard into a direct duty to any single creditor. A board that sells the last valuable division to pay a favored lender, or that transfers assets to insiders for less than fair value, creates the raw material for avoidance actions under sections 547 and 548 after a filing, which is one reason careful boards document valuation and process with particular care during decline.

The choice of Chapter 7 over an out-of-court wind-down often turns on protection and finality. A state law dissolution leaves directors to gather assets, pay claims in an order state law prescribes and defend against creditors who dispute the treatment they received, all without a stay and without a trustee's power to recover preferential transfers for the benefit of the whole creditor body. An assignment for the benefit of creditors under state law supplies an assignee and a claims process, yet it lacks the federal stay, the avoidance powers of sections 544, 547 and 548, and the uniform priority scheme of section 507. Chapter 7 offers a single forum, a neutral trustee and a distribution order that binds dissenting creditors. For a firm with disputed claims, insider transactions in its past or creditors in many states, that binding quality explains the filing better than any hope of a better price for the assets.

Some arrivals are involuntary in form as well as in fact. Section 303 permits qualifying creditors holding unsecured claims above a statutory threshold to file an involuntary petition, and a court enters an order for relief if the debtor is generally not paying debts as they become due, subject to defenses for bona fide disputes. Involuntary cases remain rare, because petitioning creditors bear costs and risks, including possible damages under section 303(i) if the petition is dismissed. Their existence still disciplines the path to the door: a board that delays while insiders extract value knows creditors hold a statutory key of their own, and a trustee appointed after an involuntary filing examines the prepetition period with the same avoidance tools as in any other case.

## The Strongest Objection: Value the Queue Cannot See

The strongest objection to the account given here deserves its full force before any answer is attempted. The objection holds that a ranked queue, however fair its order, distributes only what a sale produces, and a piecemeal liquidation sale systematically produces less than the firm was worth as an operating whole. A plant sold apart from its workforce, its customer relationships, its supplier terms and its accumulated know-how fetches the price of used equipment. The same plant sold as a going concern, or preserved through reorganization, could pay creditors more in total even if each creditor's rank stayed identical. On that view, the queue is a precise instrument applied to a diminished fund, and precision in distribution cannot compensate for destruction in collection.

Douglas Baird supplies one foundation for the objection. In The Elements of Bankruptcy (1992), and in work with Thomas Jackson on corporate reorganizations, Baird defends market sales as a disciplined way to move assets to higher value uses and warns against reorganization processes that preserve firms for the benefit of managers and junior claimants at creditor expense. Read fairly, that defense cuts in an uncomfortable direction for a simple celebration of liquidation: an auction realises value only when bidders are numerous, informed and able to value the assets on offer. Where assets are specialized, information is concentrated in insiders, or credit for bidding is scarce, an auction can transfer a firm to the bidder with the best access rather than the best use, and the fund entering the queue understates the value the firm held the day before the petition.

Lynn LoPucki supplies the second foundation, aimed at the sale process itself. In Courting Failure (2005), LoPucki examines how large cases gravitate toward courts and procedures that favor speed and sale, and he warns that quick sales of whole businesses can entrench secured lenders and insiders who shaped the process before other creditors could organize. Applied to liquidation, the warning generalises: the party that controls timing, information and financing around a sale influences the price the sale achieves, and a low price harms general unsecured creditors first because every higher rank is paid before the loss reaches them. A queue can be perfectly lawful and still distribute the proceeds of an imperfect contest.

The objection also has an evidentiary edge. Going concern value is easiest to assert and hardest to prove after the doors have closed, the workforce has dispersed and customers have placed orders elsewhere. Each week of delay in a failing firm consumes the very value the objection seeks to protect, through rent, insurance, security, deterioration and the departure of employees whose skills formed part of the enterprise. A reorganization attempted on a firm without that value does not preserve a premium; it spends the liquidation fund on administrative expenses and returns creditors to Chapter 7 later, poorer, through conversion under section 1112.

The thesis therefore narrows rather than collapses. The order of the queue, fixed by sections 507 and 726 before any sale begins, decides distribution: given a fund, the ranks determine who receives payment and who receives nothing, and the worked example of the manufacturer shows that determination operating with arithmetic certainty. The sale method decides a prior question: how large a fund the queue receives. Trustee judgment under section 363, court supervision of sale procedures, competitive bidding and the choice between piecemeal and whole-unit sales all bear on that prior question, and a liquidation conducted carelessly can fail creditors even while the distribution that follows is flawless. A complete account holds both halves together: fairness in the order of payment, and vigilance about the process that sets the amount available to be ordered.

## Reading a Liquidation Like a Court Does

A court reads a liquidation through documents, dates and ranks rather than through the story a failed company tells about itself. The petition and schedules open the record: the schedules of assets and liabilities filed under section 521 list property, secured claims, unsecured claims, executory contracts and leases, and the statement of financial affairs reconstructs transfers and payments during the period before filing. Inconsistencies between those papers and tax returns, loan applications or audited statements attract a trustee's attention first, because a liquidation runs on paper before it runs on auctions, and the paper either reconciles or it does not.

The claims register supplies the second layer of reading. Each proof of claim filed under section 501 asserts an amount, a classification and a basis, and the trustee objects under section 502 where a claim lacks documentation, duplicates another claim or asserts a priority the Code does not grant. Priority receives particular scrutiny: a creditor claiming treatment under section 507 must fit a listed category, within a listed cap and period where the statute imposes one, and a claim dressed as priority that belongs among general unsecured claims changes every dividend beneath it if allowed to stand. Reading like a court means treating classification as a legal conclusion to be tested, never as a label a claimant chooses.

Valuation supplies the third layer. A secured claim under section 506 extends only to the value of the collateral, with any balance treated as unsecured, so appraisals, sale results and market evidence decide the boundary between a creditor paid in full and a creditor sharing pro rata with the trade. Where a trustee sells collateral, the realized price settles arguments that competing appraisals could sustain for months, which is one reason sale hearings focus on procedure: notice to interested parties, opportunity to bid, and evidence that the market had a genuine chance to speak. A price produced by an open process carries authority that a price asserted by an interested party cannot match.

If you read a distribution report, read it as the end of that chain of testing. The trustee's final report accounts for every receipt and disbursement, lists each allowed claim by class, states the dividend each class receives and identifies property abandoned or exempted from the estate. Objections to the report test arithmetic, classification and allowance together, and approval of the report closes the evidentiary loop that the petition opened. You can test the report against the queue in section 726 without specialist training: follow the money down the ranks, confirm that no lower class received payment while a higher class stood unpaid, and treat any departure from that sequence as a question the record must answer. The same discipline extends to the meeting of creditors under section 341, where the trustee questions the debtor's representatives under oath, creditors may examine the record, and answers given there can be measured against the schedules, the claims register and the sale results that follow later in the case. The same habit of keeping the order visible applies to any notes kept beside the file, including [a companion reference](https://vaultbook.net).

## What the Process Settles and What It Leaves Behind

A completed Chapter 7 case settles a defined set of questions with finality. It settles who controlled the assets after the petition, replacing directors and officers with a trustee answerable to the court and to the United States Trustee. It settles which transfers before filing stand and which return to the estate under sections 544, 547, 548 and 550. It settles the amount and rank of each allowed claim, the price each asset commanded through a supervised sale, and the distribution of the proceeds under section 726. Orders allowing claims, approving sales and approving final distribution bind parties who received notice and an opportunity to object, and that binding quality allows buyers to take assets, creditors to close books and former employees to treat a chapter of working life as ended.

The process leaves other questions untouched, and naming them prevents a category error about what liquidation is for. It does not settle whether the business model failed because of mismanagement, misfortune, fraud or a market that moved against an honest firm; a trustee investigates transfers and claims, not the whole moral history of an enterprise, although examinations under oath and avoidance litigation can expose facts that other proceedings later use. It does not settle claims against parties outside the estate, including guarantors, insurers under their policies, or officers facing liability under securities, tax or employment statutes. It does not restore jobs, honour the spirit of contracts the estate rejected, or compensate customers for relationships and expectations that held value to them and no market price at auction.

Shareholders stand at the center of what remains unsettled in economic terms. Section 726(a)(6) places any surplus after all claims and interest in the debtor's hands, which for a corporation means value returning toward the owners only after every creditor class has been paid in full. In the ordinary insolvent case no surplus exists, and the investment ends at zero without any finding that the owners acted badly. Limited liability, as Warren and Westbrook describe in The Law of Debtors and Creditors (first published 1986), caps the loss at the investment; the queue determines that the loss reaches the cap. Founders, families who held stock in a private firm and public investors alike discover the same arithmetic from the same direction: equity is a claim on whatever remains, and liquidation is the process that measures the remainder.

Restated in different language, the central claim holds. Liquidation under Chapter 7 does not merely sell a business piece by piece; it translates an enterprise into an ordered set of entitlements, hands every asset to a trustee for conversion into money, and lets a ranking settled by statute in advance govern nearly all of what follows. The ranking explains why a secured lender can leave whole while a loyal supplier receives a fraction, why the costs of the winding down itself rank ahead of old debts, and why the absence of a corporate discharge under section 727(a)(1) surprises nobody who has followed the money to the end of the queue. What the ranking cannot do is create value that the firm no longer holds, and a plain record of a case, kept beside [a separate reference](https://reportmedic.org), shows both the power and the limit of the order the statute imposes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Chapter 7 bankruptcy for a business?

Chapter 7 bankruptcy for a business is a liquidation proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code in which a trustee converts company property into cash and distributes the proceeds to creditors in a statutory order. Filing a petition creates a bankruptcy estate under section 541 and triggers an order for relief that ends ordinary trading rather than preserving it. The trustee collects assets, sells them, reviews claims, and pays each class under section 726 until the fund is exhausted. A corporation or partnership receives no discharge under section 727(a)(1), because the entity does not continue as a going concern after its assets are sold. Secured creditors look first to their collateral, administrative and priority claims rank ahead of general unsecured claims, and shareholders stand last in the queue.

### Q: How long does a corporate Chapter 7 case take from filing to closing?

A corporate Chapter 7 case lasts from several months for a small estate with few assets to several years for an estate holding litigation claims, real property, or disputed transfers. The trustee must gather records, examine officers at the meeting of creditors under section 341, market and sell property, resolve objections to claims, and file a final report before the court closes the case. A no asset case with nothing to distribute can close comparatively quickly once the trustee reports that no property remains for administration. Cases involving preference actions, fraudulent transfer suits under section 548, tax disputes, or contested sales take longer because each matter requires notice, hearing, and a court order. The court closes the case under section 350 after the estate is fully administered and the trustee is discharged.

### Q: Who chooses the trustee in a Chapter 7 case?

The United States Trustee chooses the interim trustee in a Chapter 7 case, selecting a private panel trustee under section 701 shortly after the petition is filed. Panel trustees are vetted members of a standing panel maintained for the district, and assignment follows a rotation designed to keep selection neutral. At the meeting of creditors held under section 341, eligible creditors may elect a different trustee under section 702 if a qualified candidate receives the required vote and meets statutory conditions. Election is uncommon in business cases, so the interim trustee ordinarily serves for the entire case. The trustee operates under the supervision of the United States Trustee and the bankruptcy court, posts a bond under section 322, and owes fiduciary duties to the estate and to creditors as a group rather than to any single claimant.

### Q: What property becomes part of the bankruptcy estate?

Property of the bankruptcy estate under section 541 includes nearly every legal or equitable interest the company holds on the petition date, wherever the property is located and whoever holds it. Inventory, equipment, accounts receivable, cash, real property, causes of action, contract rights, and intellectual property all enter the estate, as do proceeds and profits derived from estate property. Certain transfers recovered by the trustee, such as preferences under section 547 and fraudulent transfers under section 548, also return to the estate for distribution. Property the debtor holds only in trust for another party, and interests excluded by specific statutory provisions, fall outside the estate. Secured liens survive the filing and attach to collateral inside the estate, so inclusion determines administration and sale authority rather than erasing a lienholder's rights against the asset itself.

### Q: What does the automatic stay stop after a liquidation filing?

The automatic stay under section 362 stops most collection activity against the company and its property the moment a liquidation petition is filed. Lawsuits pause, judgments cannot be enforced, liens cannot be created or perfected through ordinary steps, setoff is frozen, and creditors cannot seize inventory, equipment, or bank funds outside the bankruptcy process. The stay replaces a race among individual creditors with a single collective proceeding administered by the trustee for the benefit of all claimants. A creditor seeking to reach collateral may ask the court for relief from the stay under section 362(d), showing cause such as a lack of adequate protection or an absence of equity in the property. Certain governmental and tax proceedings fall within exceptions in section 362(b), and the stay ends for property once the estate no longer holds an interest in it.

### Q: How does a creditor file a proof of claim in a liquidation?

A creditor files a proof of claim on the official form, stating the amount owed, the basis for the debt, and the documents that support it, and files the form with the bankruptcy court before the bar date set in the case. Section 501 permits a creditor or an indenture trustee to file, and section 502 treats a properly filed claim as allowed unless a party in interest objects. In a case with assets to distribute, the court sets a deadline and gives notice so that claims can be assembled into a single register for the trustee to review. A secured creditor describes the collateral and the value attributed to it, because classification affects payment under section 506. Late, incomplete, or undocumented claims face objection and possible disallowance, which removes them from any distribution from the estate.

### Q: What is a preference payment and why can it be recovered?

A preference is a transfer of the debtor's property to a creditor on account of an earlier debt, made while the debtor was insolvent during the ninety days before filing, or during one year for an insider, that lets the creditor receive more than a liquidation distribution would provide. Section 547 permits the trustee to avoid such a transfer and recover the payment for the estate. The policy is equality of distribution: a payment that rewards one creditor for pressing harder shortly before bankruptcy would otherwise shrink the fund shared by the whole class. Creditors may defend under section 547(c), for example by showing a contemporaneous exchange for new value, a payment made in the ordinary course of business, or subsequent new value given after the transfer. Recovered funds return to the general pool and are distributed according to the statutory priority scheme.

### Q: What is an undersecured creditor in a liquidation?

An undersecured creditor holds a claim larger than the value of the collateral securing it, so the lien covers only part of the debt. Section 506(a) divides such a claim into a secured portion equal to the collateral value and an unsecured portion equal to the remaining balance. During a liquidation sale, the creditor receives the collateral or its value up to the secured portion, and the deficiency joins the pool of general unsecured claims sharing pro rata in any remaining fund. An undersecured creditor generally receives no postpetition interest under section 506(b), because that allowance depends on collateral value exceeding the claim. Valuation therefore decides a large part of the creditor's recovery, and disputes over appraisal method, sale price, and the condition of equipment or real property often dominate negotiations between the trustee and the lienholder.

### Q: Why do shareholders usually receive nothing when a company liquidates?

Shareholders receive nothing in most company liquidations because equity interests rank below every class of claim in the distribution scheme of section 726 and the priority rules of section 507. Sale proceeds first satisfy liens against collateral, then pay the costs of administration, then meet priority claims such as certain wages and taxes, and then divide any remainder among general unsecured creditors. A company entering Chapter 7 ordinarily owes more than its assets can fetch at liquidation prices, so the fund is exhausted long before the equity tier is reached. Shareholders hold a residual interest, meaning a right only to value left after debts are paid in full with any required interest. Payment to shareholders becomes possible only in a surplus case, which requires full payment of all allowed claims, a condition that insolvent liquidating companies rarely meet.

### Q: Can a Chapter 7 case be converted into a Chapter 11 case?

A Chapter 7 case can be converted into a Chapter 11 case under section 706, provided the company is eligible for Chapter 11 and the case has not already been converted from another chapter. The debtor may seek conversion, and creditors, the trustee, or another party in interest may also ask the court to convert where reorganization offers a better outcome than liquidation. Conversion requires a court order after notice and a hearing, and the court weighs feasibility, the condition of the business, and whether a plan could plausibly be confirmed. A converted case changes course from sale and closure toward a plan that restructures debts or sells the enterprise as a going concern under court supervision. Conversion does not erase acts already taken in the liquidation, and claims, transfers, and orders entered before conversion retain effect unless the court directs otherwise.

### Q: What is the difference between liquidation value and going concern value?

Liquidation value measures what assets fetch when sold apart, often under time pressure, after trading has stopped and the assembled enterprise no longer functions as a unit. Going concern value measures the worth of the business as an operating whole, including trained staff, customer relationships, contracts in place, and the coordination among assets that piecemeal sale destroys. The gap between the two values explains why creditors sometimes prefer a sale of an entire division to an auction of separate machines and inventory. Douglas Baird, in The Elements of Bankruptcy (1992), treated the choice between sale forms as central to whether a bankruptcy system preserves value or merely divides loss. In a Chapter 7 case the trustee tests both measures through marketing and bidding, because the estate's duty is to obtain the highest return available for creditors within a practical timetable.

### Q: What happens to customer deposits when a retailer liquidates?

Customer deposits for goods or services not yet delivered become claims against the estate when a retailer liquidates, and a limited statutory priority protects part of each deposit. Section 507(a)(7) grants priority to individuals who gave money toward the purchase, lease, or rental of property or services for personal, family, or household use, up to an amount adjusted periodically under the Code. Any deposit amount above the priority cap ranks as a general unsecured claim and shares pro rata with other unsecured creditors. A trustee may also seek court approval to honor certain deposits or complete orders where doing so preserves value, for example by finishing a sale that brings more into the estate than it costs. Deposits held in genuine trust or escrow arrangements may fall outside estate property under section 541, depending on the governing documents and applicable nonbankruptcy law.

### Q: What happens to pension and benefit plans when a company liquidates?

Pension and benefit plans are administered under their own governing documents and federal law when a company liquidates, and plan assets held in trust generally do not become property of the bankruptcy estate. A defined benefit pension plan may be terminated through the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, the federal insurer created under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, which then pays guaranteed benefits within statutory limits. Unpaid employer contributions owed to a plan can become claims against the estate, with a limited priority under section 507(a)(5) for contributions to employee benefit plans arising from services rendered within the statutory period. Health and welfare benefits funded from general corporate assets stand on weaker ground, because no separate fund exists to pay them. Retiree benefits receive specific treatment under section 1114 in Chapter 11, a protection that has no direct counterpart in a liquidation case.

### Q: What is a no-asset Chapter 7 case?

A no asset Chapter 7 case is a liquidation in which the trustee determines, after reviewing schedules and examining the debtor, that no nonexempt property with value beyond liens and administration costs is available for distribution to unsecured creditors. The trustee files a report of no distribution, creditors are told not to file proofs of claim unless notice later changes, and the court closes the case once administration ends. The label describes an economic result rather than a separate legal category: assets may exist on paper yet be fully encumbered, abandoned under section 554 as burdensome, or worth less than the cost of selling them. If the trustee later discovers a recoverable transfer, a tax refund, or overlooked property, the case can be reopened under section 350(b) so the asset can be administered for creditors.

### Q: How are the assets of a liquidating company sold?

A trustee sells estate assets through private sale or public auction under section 363, after giving notice to creditors and parties in interest and obtaining court approval where the sale falls outside the ordinary course of business. Marketing may involve brokers, auctioneers, sealed bids, or a stalking horse bidder whose opening offer sets a floor and invites higher competing bids at a hearing. Sales are commonly made free and clear of liens under section 363(f) when statutory conditions are met, with liens attaching instead to the sale proceeds in the same order of priority. A buyer at a sale conducted in good faith receives protection under section 363(m) against reversal on appeal where no stay was obtained. The method chosen for each asset, from a single machine to an entire plant, depends on which approach produces the greatest net return after sale costs.

### Q: What does a trustee investigate before selling a company's assets?

Before selling assets, a trustee investigates the company's financial affairs under section 704, reviewing books, bank records, tax returns, schedules, and statements for gaps between recorded property and property actually present. The trustee traces recent transfers for preferences under section 547 and fraudulent transfers under section 548, checks lien perfection through public records, and verifies insurance, storage, and environmental risks attached to physical assets. Officers and accountants are questioned at the meeting of creditors under section 341 and, where records are incomplete, through examinations under Rule 2004 of the Federal Rules of Bankruptcy Procedure. Charles Jordan Tabb, in The Law of Bankruptcy (1997), described the trustee's investigation as the mechanism that turns a debtor's own incomplete account into a verified inventory for creditors. Only after ownership, liens, and recoverable transfers are mapped can a sale convey clear rights and produce a defensible price.

### Q: How can a claim be objected to in a Chapter 7 case?

A claim can be objected to by the trustee or another party in interest filing a written objection stating the ground for disallowance under section 502(b), such as an unenforceable agreement, an amount exceeding a statutory cap, or a duplicate filing. The claimant receives notice and an opportunity for a hearing, and may respond with documents showing the debt, its amount, and any security interest. A properly filed proof of claim is treated as prima facie evidence of validity under Rule 3001 of the Federal Rules of Bankruptcy Procedure, so the objecting party must first produce evidence sufficient to rebut that showing before the burden returns to the claimant. The court may allow, reduce, reclassify, or disallow the claim entirely. Objections matter because each allowed claim dilutes the share available to every other creditor in the same class during distribution.

### Q: What happens to a company's tax obligations in a liquidation?

Tax obligations survive as claims against the estate in a liquidation, and many carry priority status under section 507(a)(8) that places them ahead of general unsecured claims. The trustee files tax returns for the estate under section 6012 of the Internal Revenue Code and pays taxes incurred by the estate during administration as administrative expenses. A governmental unit files proofs of claim for prepetition income, employment, and excise taxes, and certain tax liens secured by property follow the collateral into a sale. Section 505 permits the bankruptcy court to determine the amount or legality of a tax where the issue has not already been decided by another tribunal. Because a corporation receives no discharge in Chapter 7, unpaid tax liabilities remain obligations of the defunct entity, although collection against an empty shell ordinarily yields nothing beyond the distribution the estate already made.

### Q: What happens to debts that remain unpaid after a liquidation ends?

Debts that remain unpaid after a corporate liquidation ends stay legally attached to the company, because section 727(a)(1) denies a discharge to a corporation or partnership in a Chapter 7 case. The practical result differs from the legal one: the company has no assets, no operations, and ordinarily no continuing existence from which a creditor could collect, so unpaid balances are written off by creditors as losses. Claims against other parties are unaffected by the liquidation, so a guarantor, a co obligor, or an insurer responsible for a debt remains liable under the governing contract and applicable law. A creditor that received a distribution keeps it, subject to the final decree, and the trustee's closing of the case under section 350 ends administration of the estate. Fraud based claims and successor liability questions are governed by separate doctrines outside the distribution itself.

### Q: How does Chapter 7 differ from dissolving a company under state law?

Chapter 7 differs from state law dissolution in forum, control, and effect: liquidation under Title 11 is a federal proceeding in bankruptcy court supervised by a trustee, while dissolution is a corporate act under state statute administered by the company's own directors or shareholders. Filing under Chapter 7 triggers the automatic stay of section 362, centralizes all claims in one court, and gives the trustee avoidance powers under sections 547 and 548 that no dissolution process provides. Dissolution leaves creditors to pursue individual remedies in separate courts, subject to state rules on winding up, notice to claimants, and the survival period for suits against the dissolved entity. Warren and Westbrook (first published 1986), contrasted collective federal proceedings with individual state collection for exactly this reason. A company may dissolve after a bankruptcy closes, but dissolution alone never creates a stay, a claims bar date, or a statutory distribution queue.
