## The Verdict

The Japanese Instrument of Surrender, signed aboard the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay at 9:04 AM on September 2 1945, is the shortest foundational document of the Second World War and the most crowded. Two pages, five paragraphs, and twelve signatures closed a conflict that had consumed four years of American effort and more than a decade of Japanese imperial expansion. The document is brief because its job was narrow: to record that Japan accepted the terms of the Potsdam Declaration, that its armed forces surrendered unconditionally wherever they stood, and that the Emperor and the Japanese government would henceforth exercise authority subject to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers. Nothing in it settles reparations, nothing in it draws borders, and nothing in it pronounces judgment. It is an instrument, in the legal sense, a tool for transferring the fact of defeat into the machinery of occupation.

The judgment of this close read is that the Instrument matters precisely because of what it compresses. Where the German surrenders at Reims and Berlin required detailed military-technical language about who stopped shooting when and under whose orders, the Japanese document folds the entire Pacific war into the Potsdam Declaration by reference and then spends its decisive fourth paragraph on the single political question that had nearly prevented surrender for weeks: the future of the Emperor. That paragraph, negotiated indirectly through the Byrnes Note of August 11 1945, preserved the imperial institution while subordinating it to Allied authority, and the compromise it recorded made possible the seven year occupation that followed. The rest of the document is architecture around that load-bearing sentence.

The ceremony that surrounded the signing matters too, and it matters in ways that reward attention to staging. General Douglas MacArthur, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, conducted a twenty three minute ceremony that combined military display, five Allied admirals of the line on deck, four hundred aircraft overhead, with language of reconciliation so deliberate that it startled observers who had expected a theater of vengeance. The Japanese delegation, Foreign Minister Mamoru Shigemitsu in formal morning coat and General Yoshijiro Umezu in field uniform, signed for the Emperor, the government, and the Imperial General Headquarters in that order, and the sequence of signatures carried constitutional meaning. Representatives of nine nations signed for the Allied Powers, and one of them, the Canadian representative, signed on the wrong line, an error that Japanese officials corrected by hand and that has since become the document's most human footnote. A close read of the text, the signing, and the ceremony shows how a committee-architecture coalition staged the formal end of the largest war in history: through procedure, through precision, and through a carefully chosen vocabulary of restoration rather than revenge.

### What is the Japanese Instrument of Surrender?

The Japanese Instrument of Surrender is the two page document signed on September 2 1945 aboard the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, by which Japan accepted the Potsdam Declaration, surrendered all its armed forces unconditionally, and placed the Emperor and the government under the authority of the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers.

### Who signed the Instrument of Surrender?

Two Japanese delegates signed, Foreign Minister Mamoru Shigemitsu and Army Chief of Staff Yoshijiro Umezu, followed by nine Allied representatives for nine nations, led by General Douglas MacArthur for the Allied Powers and Admiral Chester Nimitz for the United States.

### How long did the surrender ceremony last?

The ceremony aboard the Missouri lasted approximately twenty three minutes, from MacArthur's opening remarks at about 9:04 AM to his closing prayer, and it was watched by several hundred Allied officers, sailors, and correspondents on deck. The brevity was deliberate: a short, precise ritual left no room for ambiguity about what had just happened.

### Why did the Japanese delegation include both a civilian and a military signatory?

The two signatories reflected the dual structure of Japanese authority: Shigemitsu signed for the Emperor and the civilian government, while Umezu signed for the Imperial General Headquarters, so that both the political and military chains of command were bound by the same document.

### What was the Canadian signing error?

Canadian representative Lawrence Cosgrave signed his name on the line below the one reserved for Canada, beneath the French signature, and Japanese officials later drew a connecting line to correct the placement, a correction that remains visible on the document.

## The Moment: September 2 1945, Tokyo Bay

The morning of September 2 1945 dawned overcast in Tokyo Bay, with the gray bulk of the American fleet riding at anchor in waters that had been, until three weeks earlier, the protected heart of a hostile empire. The battleship USS Missouri, at fifty eight thousand tons the most powerful warship afloat, had been dressed for the occasion with her decks cleared and her crew in clean uniforms. Sometime before nine o'clock, a Japanese destroyer came alongside and put ashore an eleven man delegation led by two figures who would dominate every photograph of the day: Foreign Minister Mamoru Shigemitsu, limping on an artificial leg in a top hat and morning coat, and General Yoshijiro Umezu, chief of staff of the Imperial Japanese Army, in field uniform with his sword at his side. The delegation climbed the Missouri's gangway past ranks of American sailors, and the contrast that every correspondent noted, the civilian's formal dress beside the soldier's battle uniform, the conquered ascending to meet the conquerors on a deck crowded with admirals, was itself the first text of the ceremony. Nothing had been left to improvisation. MacArthur's staff had choreographed the morning down to the placement of the signing tables and the order in which the flags would fly.

The ceremony mattered because it was the visible performance of an ending that had already happened in less visible forms. The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, the Soviet entry into the Pacific war, the Imperial conference of August 14 at which Hirohito had broken the cabinet deadlock, the August 15 broadcast in which the Emperor's voice, never before heard by most of his subjects, had announced acceptance of Allied terms: all of these had brought the war to an end before a single pen touched paper in Tokyo Bay. The Missouri ceremony did not decide the surrender. It displayed it. And display was not a trivial function. A war fought by coalitions across six years and four continents needed a single legible moment of conclusion, a scene that could be photographed, filmed, broadcast, and remembered, and the ceremony was engineered to supply exactly that. The twenty three minutes on the Missouri gave the Pacific war the one thing it had lacked: a definitive full stop that everyone could see.

## A Two Page Document: The Physical Object

The Instrument itself is, as a physical object, almost aggressively modest. Two pages, five paragraphs, printed in English with a Japanese translation appended, and at the bottom the signatures that gave it force. There is no preamble recounting the war's causes, no statement of principles, no map of the postwar world, nothing of the declaratory architecture that filled the Atlantic Charter or the Yalta Protocol. The document's brevity was not an accident of haste. It was a deliberate choice, shaped by the Potsdam Declaration of July 26, which had already stated the Allies' terms in thirteen articles, and by the American decision that the surrender instrument should do one job, the legal transfer of Japan's submission, and do it without opening new negotiations. MacArthur's legal staff drafted the text in consultation with Washington, and the Japanese government was presented with it as a finished object, not as a draft open to amendment. The ceremony's function was signature, not negotiation.

That modesty of form is itself worth a reader's attention, because it tells a truth about how the war ended in the Pacific that a longer document would have obscured. The Allies had not bargained Japan into surrender; they had compelled it, and the Instrument records compulsion rather than agreement. Where armistice documents in earlier wars ran to dozens of pages of military detail, the Japanese Instrument assumes the Potsdam Declaration as its foundation and adds only what that declaration lacked: the mechanics of surrender and the status of the Emperor. A historian handling the document notices immediately how much weight rests on the fourth paragraph, the only paragraph that does genuine political work, and how the other four paragraphs are essentially the legal scaffolding required to make the fourth one operational. The two page format is thus not a curiosity. It is the argument.

## Paragraph One: Accepting Potsdam by Reference

The Instrument's opening paragraph runs as a single long sentence: "We, acting by command of and in behalf of the Emperor of Japan, the Japanese Government and the Japanese Imperial General Headquarters, hereby accept the provisions set forth in the declaration issued by the heads of the Governments of the United States, China, and Great Britain on 26 July 1945, at Potsdam, and subsequently adhered to by the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, which four powers are hereafter referred to as the Allied Powers." Every clause of that sentence carries a decision. The signatories act "by command of and in behalf of" three sources of authority, the Emperor, the government, and the Imperial General Headquarters, a tripartite formulation that binds the political, civilian-administrative, and military instruments of the Japanese state in a single act. The acceptance is of the Potsdam Declaration's "provisions," which means the entire thirteen article ultimatum, threats, terms, and postwar framework together, is imported into the Instrument by reference. And the definition of "the Allied Powers" that closes the paragraph, the United States, China, Great Britain, plus the Soviet Union through its subsequent adherence, establishes the legal coalition in whose name the surrender is received.

The reference technique was legally elegant and politically loaded at once. By accepting Potsdam "provisions" rather than restating them, the Instrument avoided any possibility that the Japanese government could claim a different reading of the terms at the moment of signing; the terms were the Potsdam terms, already published, already answered, and now simply incorporated. The phrasing also resolved, without discussion, the question of Soviet standing: the Soviet Union had not signed the Potsdam Declaration in July because it was not yet at war with Japan, but by August 8 it had declared war and its representative would sign the Instrument, and the paragraph's "subsequently adhered to" clause folded Moscow into the legal framework with a minimum of fuss. Readers who want the full context of the document being incorporated here will find it in [the series' close read of the Potsdam Declaration](/2004/11/26/potsdam-declaration-july-1945/), which walks through the thirteen articles, the emperor's silence, and the mokusatsu controversy in detail.

## Paragraph Two: Unconditional Surrender, Wherever Situated

The second paragraph contains the Instrument's operative core: "We hereby proclaim the unconditional surrender to the Allied Powers of the Japanese Imperial General Headquarters and of all Japanese armed forces and all armed forces under Japanese control wherever situated." The phrase "unconditional surrender" is the doctrinal inheritance of the [Casablanca announcement of January 1943](/2004/12/13/casablanca-unconditional-surrender-announcement/), where Franklin Roosevelt had committed the Allies to accept nothing less from Germany, Italy, and Japan. By September 1945 that doctrine had governed every Allied surrender discussion for two and a half years, and its appearance in the Instrument is the doctrinal circle closing: the formula announced as a press conference flourish at Casablanca is now the legal substance of the war's final document. The drafters kept the Casablanca language deliberately, because abandoning it at the moment of victory would have raised the question of what, exactly, Japan was being asked to do.

The geographic clause, "wherever situated," did more work than it appears to. In September 1945, Japanese forces remained under arms across an enormous arc: Manchuria and Korea facing the Soviet advance, China proper facing Chiang Kai-shek's armies, Burma and Malaya and the Netherlands East Indies facing British and Commonwealth forces, the scattered Pacific islands facing American commanders, and the home islands themselves. A surrender that covered only the home islands would have required separate negotiations in every theater, with all the opportunities for delay, evasion, and continued bloodshed that fragmentation would have invited. "Wherever situated" bound every Japanese commander, in every theater, to the same act, and it authorized MacArthur, as Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, to direct the subsequent local surrenders through his general orders. The Instrument thus functioned as a master switch, and the local ceremonies that followed through September and October, at Singapore, at Saigon, at Rabaul, were all implementations of this single sentence.

## Paragraph Three: Cessation of Hostilities

The third paragraph is the Instrument's most purely military clause, and it reads like the orders it was meant to generate: "We hereby command all Japanese forces wherever situated and the Japanese people to cease hostilities forthwith, to preserve and save from damage all ships, aircraft, and military and civil property and to comply with all requirements which may be imposed by the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers or by agencies of the Japanese Government at his direction." The paragraph does two things at once. It orders the Japanese military to stop fighting, using the imperative voice of a command, and it orders the preservation of the material instruments of war, ships, aircraft, property, so that they could be inventoried, seized, or destroyed under Allied direction rather than sabotaged by defeated units.

The preservation order had a bitter precedent from only four months earlier behind it. In the German surrender, the Allies had been acutely concerned with scuttling, the deliberate destruction of the German fleet and of military assets that defeated forces preferred to deny their conquerors. The Instrument's drafters, many of whom had watched the German naval and industrial demolition problems of May 1945, wrote the preservation requirement directly into the surrender act so that no Japanese commander could later claim that destruction of his own equipment was a legitimate act of war. The paragraph also contains the Instrument's first mention of the mechanism by which occupation would actually work: "agencies of the Japanese Government at his direction." The Allies did not intend to administer Japan directly through military government in the German style. They intended to govern through the existing Japanese state, issuing orders to it through the Supreme Commander, and the third paragraph quietly establishes that indirect structure. The Japanese government would remain standing, but it would stand at MacArthur's direction.

## Paragraph Four: The Emperor's Authority, Subordinated

The fourth paragraph is the sentence on which the Pacific war's ending turned: "The authority of the Emperor and the Japanese Government to rule the state shall be subject to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers who will take such steps as he deems proper to effectuate these terms of surrender." In a document of five short paragraphs, this is the one that does politics. It preserves the imperial institution, the Emperor remains the Emperor, and the Japanese government continues to exist, while making both subordinate to Allied authority. The formulation was the product of a month of agonized indirect negotiation in August, conducted through Swiss and Swedish diplomatic channels, and its wording descends directly from the Byrnes Note of August 11, in which the American Secretary of State had replied to Japan's conditional acceptance of Potsdam with language that kept the Emperor in place while reserving Allied control over "the ultimate form of government of Japan" to "the freely expressed will of the Japanese people."

The paragraph's significance can only be grasped against the alternative that haunted every Japanese cabinet meeting of August 1945. For the Japanese government's hardliners, the preservation of the kokutai, the national polity centered on the Emperor, was the non negotiable condition of any surrender; for the American government, an explicit guarantee of the Emperor's position had been stripped from the Potsdam Declaration at Secretary Byrnes's insistence, precisely because such a guarantee would have weakened the unconditional surrender framework. The fourth paragraph threads that needle by doing what both sides needed without saying what neither side could say: it names the Emperor, it keeps him, and it subordinates him. The Emperor's authority is not abolished, which satisfied Tokyo's minimum demand, and it is not guaranteed, which preserved Washington's doctrinal position. That the most consequential political sentence of the Pacific settlement is also its most artfully ambiguous is not a flaw in the Instrument. It is the Instrument.

## Paragraph Five: Good Faith and the Carrying Out

The fifth and final paragraph closes the document with an undertaking rather than a command: "We hereby undertake for the Emperor, the Japanese Government and their successors to carry out the provisions of this Instrument in good faith, and to take whatever action may be required by the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers or by any other designated representative of the Allied Powers for the purpose of giving effect to this Instrument." The phrase "and their successors" is the paragraph's most important legal device. It binds not only the men who signed but any future Japanese government, closing the door on the argument that a change of cabinet, a change of ministry, or even a change of constitutional order could release Japan from the surrender's obligations. The undertaking is to "carry out the provisions of this Instrument in good faith," language that looks forward to the occupation years and the compliance disputes they would bring, and it gives the Supreme Commander a blank check of authority, "whatever action may be required," to enforce the terms.

Readers sometimes pass over the fifth paragraph as boilerplate, but it is worth pausing on the legal architecture it completes. The first paragraph incorporates Potsdam. The second surrenders the armed forces. The third orders the ceasefire and preserves the assets. The fourth settles the Emperor. The fifth binds the future. Each paragraph answers a question that would otherwise have remained open, and together they form a document with no loose ends, which is precisely what a surrender instrument requires. The war had been ended by the most destructive weapons ever used and the most sweeping military defeats ever inflicted; the Instrument's job was to make that ending legally permanent, and the fifth paragraph's "successors" clause is the mechanism of permanence.

## Shigemitsu: The Civilian Signatory

Mamoru Shigemitsu, who signed first for Japan, was in many respects the most experienced diplomat in the Japanese government, and his presence at the head of the delegation was a political statement about who was surrendering. Born in 1887, Shigemitsu had served as ambassador to the Soviet Union, to Britain, and to the collaborationist government at Nanking; he had lost his right leg to a bomb thrown by a Korean independence activist in Shanghai in 1932; and he had become foreign minister in the final months of the war, in which capacity he had participated in the August cabinet deliberations that produced the surrender decision. His signature on the Instrument bound "the Emperor of Japan and the Japanese Government," the civilian and political authority of the state, and the choice of a diplomat rather than a general to carry that authority signaled that the surrender was an act of the state, not merely of the defeated army. The morning coat and top hat that made him the most visually distinctive figure on the Missouri's deck were not costume. They were the uniform of the Japanese civilian government, presented in its full formal dignity at the moment of its subordination.

Shigemitsu's personal history gave the ceremony an irony that the Allied correspondents were quick to note. This was a man who had been a victim of political violence, who had spent his career in the foreign ministry's most difficult postings, and who had argued within the August cabinet for acceptance of the Potsdam terms when the army's hardliners were still demanding a final decisive battle on the home islands. His signature was thus not the signature of a man broken by defeat so much as the signature of a man who had helped to produce the decision to surrender and was now executing it. The dignity of his bearing on the Missouri, widely remarked at the time, served the Japanese government's interest in presenting surrender as a sovereign act rather than a collapse, and it served MacArthur's interest too: the occupation that followed would work through the Japanese government, and a government that had surrendered with dignity was easier to work through than one that had been humiliated into incoherence.

## Umezu: The Military Signatory

General Yoshijiro Umezu signed second, for "the Japanese Imperial General Headquarters," and his signature bound the other half of the Japanese state: the military chain of command. Umezu was the Army's chief of staff, a hardliner who had opposed surrender to the end and who had been persuaded to sign only by the Emperor's direct intervention in the August 14 imperial conference. His presence in field uniform, sword at his side, was the military's formal acknowledgment that the war was over, and it was essential to the Instrument's function. A surrender signed only by the civilian government would have left open the argument, which Japanese army hardliners had made before and might have made again, that the military had never been defeated and was not bound by the politicians' decision. Umezu's signature closed that argument. The Imperial General Headquarters, the body that had directed every campaign of the Pacific war, was surrendering itself, in the person of its chief.

The contrast between the two Japanese signatories, the limping diplomat in morning dress and the stiff general in uniform, encapsulated the constitutional reality that the Instrument was designed to capture. Japan in 1945 was a state in which civilian and military authority ran in parallel lines to the throne, and the Instrument's drafters had understood that a surrender that bound only one line would be a surrender that could be repudiated by the other. The "by command of and in behalf of" formula in the first paragraph, with its threefold invocation of Emperor, government, and headquarters, was the textual expression of this understanding, and the two signatures were its physical expression. Umezu would later be convicted as a war criminal at the Tokyo trials and sentenced to life imprisonment. Shigemitsu would be convicted as well, sentenced to seven years, and eventually pardoned and returned to serve again as foreign minister in the 1950s. Their signatures on September 2 bound them both to a document that outlived the war and followed each man into the postwar reckoning.

## MacArthur: The Man Who Held the Pen

Douglas MacArthur, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, signed first for the victors, and everything about the ceremony was an expression of his conception of the role. MacArthur had been designated Supreme Commander by President Truman on August 15, over the objections of some in Washington who preferred a more conventional military governor, and he had spent the two weeks before the ceremony establishing the framework of the occupation he would direct for the next six years. His opening remarks at the ceremony, his closing prayer, his choice of the Missouri as the venue, his arrangement of the flags and the signing tables, even the decision to use five pens for his own signature so that he could distribute them as souvenirs, all of these were MacArthur's, and they reflected a commander who understood that the end of the war was also the beginning of a political performance that would shape the peace. The ceremony was, among other things, MacArthur's first act of occupation, and it set the tone for everything that followed: authority exercised through ceremony, through language, and through the careful management of symbols.

MacArthur's remarks repay the attention this close read gives them. He opened by declaring that the issues of the war, "involving divergent ideals and ideologies, have been determined on the battlefields of the world and hence are not for our discussion or debate." The sentence performed a remarkable act of rhetorical closure: the war's causes were not to be litigated at the ceremony, not because they were unimportant but because they had been settled by force and the ceremony's business was the future. He went on to commit both victors and vanquished to "that higher dignity which alone befits the sacred purposes we are about to serve," language that framed the occupation not as punishment but as a shared undertaking, and he closed the proceedings with a prayer "that peace be now restored to the world and that God will preserve it always." The religious register was deliberate. MacArthur, the son of a Civil War general and a lifelong student of history, was staging the war's end as a moral event, and the prayer's inclusion in a military ceremony gave the surrender a sacral quality that photographs and newsreels carried around the world. The rhetoric of reconciliation was not mere decoration. It was the operating philosophy of the occupation MacArthur was about to begin.

## Nimitz for the United States

Admiral Chester W. Nimitz signed for the United States, and his presence at the table was the Navy's claim on a victory that the Army's theater commander was presiding over. Nimitz had commanded the Pacific Fleet through the entire American counteroffensive, from the shattered remnants of December 1941 through Midway, the island campaigns, and the final approach to the home islands, and his signature represented the service that had carried the heaviest sustained burden of the Pacific war. The inter service politics of the signature were real but muted on the day: MacArthur signed for the Allied Powers as a whole, which gave him precedence, and Nimitz signed for the United States specifically, which acknowledged that the American national signature was distinct from the coalition signature. The arrangement reflected the command structure of the Pacific war's final phase, in which MacArthur's Supreme Commander designation had been a political appointment layered over the existing service commands, and Nimitz's separate signature preserved the Navy's institutional dignity within that arrangement.

Nimitz's signature also carried a particular historical resonance that the correspondents on deck understood. The Pacific war had begun, for the United States Navy, with the wreckage at Pearl Harbor, and Nimitz had taken command of that wreckage ten days after the attack. His journey from the ruins of Battleship Row to the deck of the Missouri, signing the document that ended the war the Japanese had started at Pearl Harbor, was the Navy's narrative arc made visible, and the ceremony's staging gave it full weight. The Missouri herself was a battleship, the Navy's chosen symbol of power, and the fleet at anchor in Tokyo Bay was Nimitz's fleet. MacArthur might have been the presiding figure, but the setting was the Navy's, and Nimitz's signature on the American line was the service's formal receipt of the victory it had spent four years earning.

## The Other Allied Signatories: Nine Nations at One Table

Beyond MacArthur and Nimitz, the Instrument was signed by representatives of eight more nations, and the roster is a map of the Pacific coalition. Hsu Yung-chang signed for the Republic of China, the ally that had been fighting Japan since 1937, longer than any other power, and whose presence at the table acknowledged a war effort that had tied down more than a million Japanese soldiers. Bruce Fraser, commander of the British Pacific Fleet, signed for the United Kingdom. Kuzma Derevyanko signed for the Soviet Union, whose August entry into the Pacific war had been one of the final pressures on the Japanese cabinet. Thomas Blamey signed for Australia, whose forces had fought the Japanese from New Guinea to Borneo. Lawrence Moore Cosgrave signed for Canada. Jacques Leclerc, the liberator of Paris, signed for the Provisional Government of the French Republic. Conrad Helfrich signed for the Netherlands, whose East Indies colony had been occupied by Japan for three and a half years. Leonard Isitt signed for New Zealand. Nine nations, nine Allied signatories plus MacArthur for the Allied Powers collectively, and the list's composition tells the story of a coalition that extended well beyond the Big Three.

The inclusion of France and the Netherlands is particularly instructive. Neither had been a major Pacific belligerent in the war's final phase; France's Pacific presence was minimal and the Netherlands' colonial army had been destroyed in 1942. Their signatures were political rather than military, acknowledgments that the Pacific settlement would restore the prewar colonial order, at least formally, and that the European colonial powers expected a seat at the table where that restoration was ratified. The Chinese signature, by contrast, was the recognition of a genuine and enormous war effort, and the Soviet signature was the recognition of a decisive late intervention. The roster thus encodes two different logics of coalition membership, the logic of contribution and the logic of status, and the Instrument's drafters had room for both. The nine nation framework would matter again in the months that followed, when the Allied Council for Japan and the Far Eastern Commission gave institutional form to the coalition that the signatures had assembled on the Missouri's deck.

## The Signing Order: What the Sequence Said

The order of signatures on the Instrument was not alphabetical and not random. It was a hierarchy, and it was read as one by everyone present. MacArthur signed first, for the Allied Powers, establishing the coalition's collective authority and his own primacy within it. Then came the national signatures in an order that reflected the drafters' assessment of each power's weight in the Pacific war: the United States, China, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, Australia, Canada, France, the Netherlands, New Zealand. The Japanese signatures had preceded them all, Shigemitsu and then Umezu, because the surrendered party signs first, acknowledging submission before the victors record their acceptance. The sequence is the ceremony's constitutional diagram: defeat first, then the coalition in order of consequence, with the Supreme Commander's signature standing above and apart as the embodiment of the Allied Powers collectively.

Protocol historians have noted that the signing order also solved a problem that had bedeviled Allied summitry throughout the war: the problem of precedence among sovereign equals. At Tehran, at Yalta, at Potsdam, the Big Three had managed precedence through careful choreography and the fiction of perfect equality. The Missouri ceremony could not use that fiction, because the Instrument was not a conference communique but a legal act, and legal acts require a definite order. The solution, MacArthur first as the embodiment of the whole, then the nations in descending order of Pacific weight, was elegant enough that no delegation objected, and it established a precedent that the subsequent local surrender ceremonies followed. The signature order is one of those details that looks like mere etiquette and turns out to be law.

## The Cosgrave Line: A Signing Error and Its Correction

The most human moment of the ceremony belonged to Colonel Lawrence Moore Cosgrave, the Canadian representative, who signed his name on the wrong line. The Instrument was prepared in two copies, one for the Allies and one for Japan, and on the Japanese copy the lines for the national signatures were closely spaced. Cosgrave, signing for Canada, placed his signature on the line below the Canadian line, beneath the French signature, and the error was not discovered until the ceremony was over. Japanese officials, with a punctiliousness that the Allied correspondents found remarkable under the circumstances, corrected the document by drawing a line from Cosgrave's misplaced signature to the correct Canadian designation and annotating the correction. The corrected copy, with its visible amendment, is the one preserved in the Japanese archives, and the error has become the standard anecdote of the ceremony.

The incident is worth more than anecdote, because it reveals something about the nature of the document and the seriousness with which both sides treated it. A surrender instrument is a legal act, and a legal act with a misplaced signature is a defective legal act; the Japanese correction was not fussiness but the defeated party's insistence that the document binding it be formally correct. There is a further irony that historians have not missed: the Japanese government, which had just surrendered unconditionally, devoted its first post signature act to ensuring that the Allies' own document was in order. The Cosgrave line, as it is sometimes called, is the Instrument's reminder that even at the moment of maximum humiliation, procedure mattered, and that the victors' carelessness was corrected by the vanquished's precision. The error also humanizes a ceremony that was otherwise so perfectly staged as to feel almost inhuman, and it is no accident that the Cosgrave story is the detail most often repeated in accounts of the day.

## The Battleship as Stage: Why the Missouri

The choice of the USS Missouri as the venue was MacArthur's, and it was overdetermined in the way that the best symbolic choices always are. The Missouri was the newest and most powerful battleship in the American fleet, commissioned in June 1944, and her selection announced that the surrender would take place under the guns of American naval power. She was named for President Truman's home state, a courtesy to the commander in chief that cost nothing and flattered the Missourian in the White House. She was large enough to accommodate the delegations, the press, and the hundreds of sailors who would witness the ceremony, and her quarterdeck provided a natural stage. And she carried, though few on deck that morning knew it, a piece of historical symmetry that MacArthur had quietly arranged: the Missouri had been launched at the New York Navy Yard within sight of the very slipway where her sister ship, the ship on whose deck the Japanese delegation might otherwise have signed, had been built. The battleship was not a neutral venue. It was an argument.

The battleship setting also solved a practical problem that the ceremony's planners had faced. A surrender of this magnitude needed to be both intimate enough to be legally precise and grand enough to be historically legible, and a warship's deck provided both: the signing tables were close enough for the cameras to capture every pen stroke, while the fleet at anchor and the aircraft overhead supplied the grandeur. The alternative venues that had been considered, a shore installation in Tokyo, the deck of an aircraft carrier, were rejected for reasons of symbolism and security alike. Tokyo was still a ruin, and a shore ceremony would have required the Japanese delegation to travel through a devastated city under Allied guard, a spectacle of humiliation that MacArthur, with his eye on the occupation to come, wanted to avoid. The Missouri, riding at anchor in the bay, was sovereign American territory that happened to float, and it allowed the ceremony to be both a display of power and a gesture of restraint.

## The Tables, the Flags, the Pens: The Ceremony's Material Details

The material details of the ceremony were arranged with a precision that bordered on the theatrical, and they deserve a close reader's attention because they were doing political work. The signing tables were ordinary mess tables, covered with green baize, placed on the Missouri's quarterdeck. Behind them flew the American flag that had flown over the Capitol on December 7 1941, brought to the ceremony at MacArthur's direction as a silent rebuke and a silent promise: the flag that had been flying when the war began would fly when it ended. The Japanese delegation faced the Allied representatives across the tables, and the press and the ship's company watched from the decks above and the superstructure. Every photograph of the ceremony shows the same composition: the two Japanese signatories seated, the Allied representatives standing behind, the flag above them all, the battleship's guns in the background.

MacArthur's use of five pens for his own signature is the best known of these details, and it is worth pausing on because it reveals the commander's understanding of the ceremony as an act of history-making. He signed "MacArthur" with one pen, then switched pens for each subsequent part of his name and title, so that he could present the pens as souvenirs to West Point, to his wife, to his aides. The gesture was pure MacArthur, self conscious, theatrical, and politically astute: it distributed the ceremony's relics among the institutions and people who would sustain his legend. The Japanese signatories, by contrast, used a single pen each, and Shigemitsu's difficulty in signing, his artificial leg made the low table awkward, and he had to remove his top hat and lean forward to reach the document, was noted by every observer as a moment of visible human strain within the ceremony's formal perfection. The material details are not decoration. They are the ceremony's second text.

## The Flyover: Four Hundred Aircraft as Punctuation

As MacArthur pronounced the ceremony closed, several hundred American aircraft, the accounts say four hundred, though the exact number varies in the records, flew over the Missouri in formation, and the roar of their engines provided the ceremony's final punctuation. The flyover had been planned as carefully as everything else. It was, in the most literal sense, a demonstration: the air power that had burned Japan's cities and blockaded its home islands, passing in review over the deck where the surrender was signed. The aircraft came from the carriers of the fleet at anchor, fighters and bombers in long precise formations, and their passage took several minutes, during which the delegations stood and watched. The Japanese delegates, who had lived through the firebombing of Tokyo and the atomic bombings, needed no explanation of what the formations signified.

The flyover's meaning was double, and the doubleness was intentional. To the Allied audience, it was a celebration, the visible proof that the war was won and that the instruments of victory were intact and disciplined. To the Japanese audience, it was a warning, a reminder that the power that had compelled the surrender remained present and could be used again. MacArthur's staff understood both meanings and intended both. The ceremony as a whole balanced reconciliation and intimidation with extraordinary care: MacArthur's words spoke of dignity and peace, while the battleship's guns and the aircraft overhead spoke of the consequences of renewed resistance. The flyover was the ceremony's final sentence, written in the sky, and it ensured that no one present would mistake the morning's courtesies for weakness.

## The German Parallel: Two Surrenders, Two Documents

The Japanese Instrument invites comparison with the German surrenders of May 1945, and the comparison illuminates both documents. Germany surrendered twice: first at Reims on May 7, in a document signed by General Jodl that the Soviets rejected as insufficiently formal, and then at Berlin Karlshorst on May 8, in a fuller ceremony presided over by Marshal Zhukov. The German instruments were military-technical documents, dense with provisions about the cessation of operations, the disarmament of forces, and the authority of the Allied commanders, and they reflected the circumstances of Germany's end: a state whose government had collapsed, whose leader was dead, and whose armed forces were surrendering piecemeal across a continent. The [series' account of the German surrender at Reims and Berlin](/2005/12/27/german-surrender-may-1945-reims-berlin/) walks through those documents and their double signing in detail.

The Japanese Instrument differs in almost every dimension that matters. Where the German documents were negotiated with a collapsing regime's military representatives, the Japanese document was presented to a functioning government as a finished text. Where the German surrenders required detailed military provisions because German forces were still fighting in isolated pockets, the Japanese Instrument could rely on the Potsdam Declaration for its terms and on the Emperor's authority for its enforcement. Where the German ceremonies were hasty and, at Reims, almost furtive, the Japanese ceremony was choreographed over two weeks and staged for maximum historical legibility. And where the German surrenders left the question of Germany's political future entirely open, to be settled by occupation authorities improvising in the ruins, the Japanese Instrument settled the single most important political question, the Emperor's status, in its fourth paragraph. The two surrenders thus represent two different theories of how wars end: the German theory, in which the defeated state disappears and the victors build from the rubble, and the Japanese theory, in which the defeated state survives, subordinated, and becomes the instrument of its own transformation.

## The Occupation Framework: Governing Through the Defeated State

The Instrument's third and fifth paragraphs, with their references to "agencies of the Japanese Government at his direction" and "whatever action may be required by the Supreme Commander," established the legal basis for the occupation that followed, and that occupation's character was the direct consequence of the Instrument's design. Unlike in Germany, where the Allies established direct military government through the Allied Control Council, in Japan MacArthur governed through the existing Japanese state, issuing directives to the Japanese government, which implemented them through its own ministries. The arrangement was possible because the Instrument had preserved the Emperor and the government as functioning authorities, subordinated but intact, and it was desirable, from the American perspective, because it economized on occupation manpower and preserved the administrative capacity needed to feed, house, and govern seventy million people. The [series' account of the Japanese surrender process](/2005/12/11/japanese-surrender-september-2-1945-uss-missouri/) traces the August decisions that made this framework possible.

The indirect occupation was one of the most consequential institutional choices of the postwar period, and its roots lie in the Instrument's fourth paragraph. By preserving the imperial institution, the Allies retained the single source of legitimacy that the Japanese population recognized, and MacArthur's subsequent use of the Emperor, most famously in the New Year's Day 1946 rescript in which Hirohito renounced his divinity, channeled that legitimacy toward the occupation's reform program. The arrangement had its critics, then and since: it shielded the Emperor from the war crimes prosecution that many in Washington and among the Allies demanded, it preserved elements of the prewar bureaucracy that had administered the war, and it created the appearance, which some historians have argued was also the reality, that Japan's transformation was less thorough than Germany's. But the arrangement also produced a stable, functioning occupation that ended in 1952 with a democratic constitution, a demilitarized state, and an alliance that endures. The Instrument's quiet paragraphs about governing through the Japanese government were the legal seed of that outcome.

## The Dates Question: August 14, August 15, September 2

No close read of the Instrument can avoid the question of dates, because the war's ending has three legitimate candidates and the Instrument is only one of them. On August 14 1945, the Japanese cabinet, following the Emperor's intervention at the imperial conference, communicated its acceptance of the Potsdam terms through Swiss and Swedish diplomatic channels; this is the date of the political decision, the moment the war was lost in the only forum that mattered. On August 15, the Emperor's broadcast announced the acceptance to the Japanese people, in a radio address that most of his subjects heard with confusion and grief; this is the date of the public ending, the moment the war stopped being a fact of daily life. And on September 2, the Instrument was signed aboard the Missouri; this is the date of the formal, legal, internationally witnessed conclusion. Each date is defensible, each marks a real transition, and the choice among them depends on what the chooser means by "the end."

This article holds September 2 as the formal conclusion, not because the other dates are wrong but because the Instrument is the document that made the ending legible to the world beyond Japan. The August 14 decision was communicated through diplomatic channels and understood only by governments; the August 15 broadcast was heard, in large part, only by the Japanese; the September 2 ceremony was photographed, filmed, and reported globally, and it is the date that entered the international historical record as V-J Day in the United States. The complication deserves to be stated plainly rather than resolved by fiat: wars end in layers, decision, announcement, and formalization, and the layers do not always coincide. The Instrument's significance is that it supplied the final layer, the one that could be archived, cited, and displayed, and in doing so it gave the war's ending a date that the whole world could share.

## Immediate Reception: Tokyo, Washington, and the World

The ceremony's reception was as carefully managed as the ceremony itself, and it varied sharply by audience. In Washington, the signing was reported as the definitive end of the war, the closing of the account that had opened at Pearl Harbor, and the press coverage emphasized the ceremony's dignity and MacArthur's statesmanship. In Tokyo, the reaction was more complex: the Japanese press, operating under the new realities of defeat, reported the ceremony factually, while the population absorbed the news of formal surrender with the same mixture of relief and grief that had greeted the August 15 broadcast. The Emperor's court, which had orchestrated the surrender decision, watched the ceremony's treatment of the imperial institution with acute attention, and the fourth paragraph's preservation of the Emperor was received in Tokyo as the vindication of the strategy that had made surrender possible. Among the Allied capitals, the ceremony was welcomed as the formal ratification of a victory that each had helped to win, and the nine nation signing roster ensured that each could claim a share of the moment.

The newsreel footage of the ceremony, shot by military cameramen and distributed worldwide, did as much as the printed text to fix the event in public memory. The images, Shigemitsu's top hat, Umezu's sword, MacArthur's five pens, the flag from December 7 1941, the aircraft overhead, became the visual vocabulary of the war's end, reproduced in newspapers, magazines, and eventually textbooks. The ceremony was, in this sense, the first fully mediated ending of a major war, an event designed from the start for photographic and cinematic reproduction, and its success as media is part of its historical significance. The Instrument's two pages would be read by lawyers and historians; the ceremony's twenty three minutes would be seen by everyone.

## The Medical Dimension: Casualties, Care, and the Human Cost

The Instrument ended a war that had killed, by the most careful estimates, between seventy and eighty five million people, and the medical dimension of that toll is the necessary background to any reading of the surrender document. In the Pacific theater alone, the final year of the war had produced casualties on a scale that strained every medical system involved: the firebombing of Tokyo in March 1945 killed an estimated one hundred thousand people in a single night, overwhelming the city's hospitals and fire services; the battle of Okinawa, from April to June, produced more than twelve thousand American dead and more than one hundred thousand Japanese military and civilian dead, with medical evacuation chains stretched across the Pacific; and the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki introduced injuries, burns, radiation sickness, and long term oncological consequences that military medicine had never before confronted. The surrender that the Instrument recorded stopped this accumulation of suffering, and the cessation of hostilities in its third paragraph was, among other things, a public health measure on a continental scale.

The occupation that the Instrument authorized also had a medical dimension that is often overlooked in diplomatic histories of the surrender. Japan in September 1945 faced imminent famine, with the rice harvest disrupted, the transportation network destroyed, and the urban population dependent on food imports that the war had cut off; American occupation authorities, working through the Japanese government under the Instrument's framework, organized emergency food shipments that averted mass starvation in the winter of 1945 to 1946. Military medical units conducted vaccination campaigns, rebuilt sanitation systems, and treated the epidemics, typhus, cholera, tuberculosis, that flourished in the ruins. Readers interested in how military medicine shaped and was shaped by the Pacific war's end can explore the subject further through [this history of battlefield medicine](https://reportmedic.org/tools/wwii-battlefield-medicine.html), which traces the medical services' development across the conflict. The Instrument's spare legal language contains no mention of any of this, but the document's true subject, the transfer of seventy million people from war to peace, was in large part a medical event.

## Named Disagreement: The Historians' Conversation

The scholarly literature on the Japanese surrender is extensive, and the historians this article's brief names represent its principal lines of argument. Richard Frank's Downfall, published in 1999, is the most detailed reconstruction of the American decision making in the war's final months, built on American archival sources, and it argues that the atomic bombings and the Soviet entry together, rather than either alone, produced the surrender. William Manchester's American Caesar, the classic MacArthur biography, treats the Missouri ceremony as the culmination of MacArthur's Pacific career and emphasizes the commander's stagecraft and political intelligence. D. Clayton James's multi volume The Years of MacArthur provides the most thorough documentary account of MacArthur's role in the surrender and occupation, drawing on the general's papers and the occupation records. Donald Keene's work on Japanese wartime and postwar experience, written from deep engagement with Japanese sources, recovers the Japanese side of the surrender decision, the cabinet deliberations, the Emperor's intervention, and the reception of defeat.

The named disagreement among these historians centers on the familiar questions of the war's end: the relative weight of the atomic bombings and the Soviet entry in producing the surrender decision, the sincerity and significance of MacArthur's reconciliation rhetoric, and the wisdom of the Emperor's preservation. Frank's archival reconstruction gives substantial weight to both the bombs and the Soviet intervention, resisting single cause explanations. Manchester's biographical frame emphasizes MacArthur's personal role in shaping the surrender's political outcome. Keene's Japanese perspective complicates any account that treats the surrender as a purely American achievement, recovering the agency of the Japanese statesmen who chose defeat over national suicide. The scholarly consensus, to the extent that one exists, holds that the surrender was overdetermined, produced by multiple converging pressures, and that the Instrument's fourth paragraph, the Emperor's preservation, was the necessary condition that allowed those pressures to resolve into a decision rather than a catastrophe.

## House Thesis: The Coalition's Final Demonstration

The Japanese Instrument of Surrender is, among the documents this series has close read, the purest expression of the committee architecture thesis, because it is the moment at which the Allied coalition's institutional capacity is displayed in its most formal and most final form. Consider what the ceremony required: the coordination of nine sovereign governments in a single legal act, the agreement of the United States, Britain, China, and the Soviet Union on the text of the surrender, the subordination of inter service and inter Allied rivalries to a common protocol, the choreography of a ceremony that satisfied American, British, Chinese, Soviet, and Commonwealth sensibilities simultaneously, and the construction of an occupation framework that would govern seventy million people through their own government. No command architecture coalition of the war produced anything comparable. The Axis had no joint surrender instrument, no shared ceremony, no coordinated legal framework for defeat, because the Axis had never built the institutional machinery for sustained multinational coordination in the first place.

The contrast is structural, not merely circumstantial. The Tripartite Pact was a diplomatic alignment, not an institution; it created no combined staff, no joint planning machinery, no shared legal framework, and when defeat came, Germany and Japan surrendered separately, to different captors, under different terms, with no coordination between them. The Allied coalition, by contrast, had spent the war building exactly the institutions that the Missouri ceremony displayed: the combined chiefs of staff, the summit conferences, the shared doctrines of unconditional surrender, the integrated commands. The Instrument is the capstone of that institutional edifice, the document in which the coalition's capacity for coordinated action is rendered in its most concentrated form. The nine national signatures are not decoration. They are the evidence.

## What This Close Read Illuminates

A close read of the Japanese Instrument of Surrender illuminates, first, the document's legal architecture: five paragraphs that incorporate Potsdam by reference, surrender the armed forces wherever situated, order the ceasefire and preserve the assets, settle the Emperor's status, and bind Japan's successors to compliance. It illuminates, second, the ceremony's political choreography: the choice of the Missouri, the order of signatures, the rhetoric of reconciliation, the flyover's double meaning, and the material details, the flag, the pens, the tables, through which the war's end was made visible and memorable. It illuminates, third, the human particulars that the official record tends to flatten: Shigemitsu's limp and top hat, Umezu's reluctant signature, Cosgrave's misplaced name and the Japanese correction, the four hundred aircraft overhead. And it illuminates, fourth, the document's afterlife: the occupation framework it authorized, the indirect governance through the Japanese state, the preservation of the Emperor, and the seven year transformation that followed.

The close read also clarifies what the Instrument does not do, and the silences are as instructive as the text. The document says nothing about war guilt, nothing about reparations, nothing about the atomic bombings, nothing about the Soviet Union's late entry into the war, nothing about the colonial empires whose restoration the Allied signatures implied. Those silences were deliberate: the Instrument was a surrender document, not a peace treaty, and its drafters understood that loading it with political settlements would have risked the signature itself. The peace treaty would come six years later, at San Francisco, in a different political world. The Instrument's achievement was narrower and, in its way, more difficult: to end the largest war in history with two pages, twelve signatures, and twenty three minutes, and to do it in a way that made the peace that followed possible.

## Drafting the Instrument: The Lawyers Behind the Two Pages

The Instrument's two pages were drafted in Washington and Manila in the last two weeks of August 1945, by a small group of military lawyers and State Department officials working under extraordinary time pressure. The drafting problem they faced was unusual: they had to produce a document that would be legally airtight, politically acceptable to four major Allied governments plus the Commonwealth signatories, and simple enough to be signed without negotiation on the deck of a battleship. The Potsdam Declaration supplied the substantive terms, which meant the drafters did not need to write a peace treaty; they needed to write a receipt. The key decisions were structural: incorporation of Potsdam by reference rather than restatement, the tripartite formula binding Emperor, government, and Imperial General Headquarters, the "wherever situated" geographic clause, and the fourth paragraph's carefully ambiguous treatment of the Emperor's authority. Each of these choices was debated in cables between Washington, Manila, and the capitals of the other signatory powers, and the final text reflects a consensus that was remarkable given the speed.

The drafting history matters because it explains the document's most distinctive feature: its refusal to say more than necessary. The American drafters, led by officials who had watched the German surrender negotiations produce confusion at Reims and a second ceremony at Berlin, were determined to avoid any language that could be read as an opening for Japanese counter proposals. The Japanese government was shown the text in advance through diplomatic channels and given to understand that it was not negotiable; the delegation that boarded the Missouri had been instructed to sign, not to discuss. This take-it-or-leave-it character is sometimes criticized as high handed, but it reflected a hard-won lesson of the Pacific war's final month: every day of negotiation in August had been a day in which hardliners in Tokyo might reverse the surrender decision, and the Allies had no interest in giving them time. The two pages are short because brevity was a strategy.

## The Destroyer That Brought the Delegation

The Japanese delegation reached the Missouri aboard a Japanese destroyer, and the choice of vessel was itself a small piece of the ceremony's choreography. The destroyer, flying a white flag, carried the eleven man party from the Tokyo shore to the anchored fleet, passing through waters crowded with American warships. The white flag was required by the surrender protocols: the delegation traveled under a flag of truce, and the destroyer's captain was under orders to follow an exact route at an exact time. The journey took the Japanese party past the massed Allied fleet, an armada that stretched to the horizon, and the visual impact of that passage was not lost on the delegates. Shigemitsu, who had seen the Japanese navy at its height, was now seeing the force that had destroyed it, arrayed in review.

The destroyer's voyage also solved a security problem that had preoccupied MacArthur's staff. Hardline elements in the Japanese military had attempted a coup on the night of August 14 to 15, the Kyujo Incident, in an effort to prevent the surrender broadcast, and the possibility of further resistance could not be entirely dismissed. The delegation's movements were therefore controlled down to the minute, with American naval units escorting the Japanese destroyer and with contingency plans in place for any disruption. That the voyage passed without incident was itself significant: it demonstrated that the Japanese military chain of command, the same chain that Umezu's signature would bind, was functioning and obedient. The destroyer flying the white flag was the first visible proof that the surrender decision held.

## The Rehearsal: How the Ceremony Was Planned

The Missouri ceremony was rehearsed, in the sense that every detail was planned on paper and walked through by MacArthur's staff, though there was no full dress rehearsal with stand-ins. The planning documents, preserved in MacArthur's headquarters records, show the staff working through the sequence with military thoroughness: the timing of the delegation's arrival, the placement of the tables, the order of signatures, the text of MacArthur's remarks, the positioning of the cameras, the timing of the flyover, and the arrangements for the press. The staff even planned for contingencies that did not occur: what to do if a Japanese delegate refused to sign, how to handle a disruption, where the signed copies would be secured. The twenty three minutes on deck were the product of two weeks of staff work, and the smoothness of the ceremony was the smoothness of a well-planned military operation.

The planning reveals MacArthur's conception of the ceremony as an instrument of occupation policy rather than a mere celebration. Every choice was weighed for its effect on the Japanese audience as well as the Allied one: the decision to hold the ceremony aboard ship rather than ashore, the decision to have the Japanese sign first, the decision to include MacArthur's reconciliation language, the decision to stage the flyover. The staff papers show a commander thinking simultaneously as a general and as a proconsul, managing the transition from war to occupation in a single morning's work. The ceremony's perfection was not vanity, though MacArthur was not immune to vanity. It was governance, conducted through symbolism because the symbols would reach audiences that orders could not.

## The Press on Deck: Correspondents as Witnesses

Several hundred correspondents were present at the ceremony, representing the press of the Allied nations, and their role was integral to the event's design. The Missouri's quarterdeck was arranged to give the cameras clear sightlines to the signing tables, and the timing of the ceremony, mid-morning in Tokyo Bay, was chosen in part to accommodate radio transmission schedules to the United States. The correspondents were not merely observers; they were the mechanism by which the ceremony would reach the global audience for which it was staged. MacArthur's staff had learned, through four years of Pacific war reporting, that the press was an instrument of policy, and the surrender ceremony was planned as a media event from the start.

The press coverage that followed fixed the ceremony's meaning in public memory with remarkable speed. The photographs of Shigemitsu's top hat, Umezu's impassive face, MacArthur's five pens, and the flag from December 7 1941 appeared in newspapers around the world within days, and the newsreel footage played in cinemas for weeks. The correspondents' dispatches emphasized the ceremony's dignity and MacArthur's statesmanship, a framing that the staff had encouraged through background briefings. There were dissenting notes: some reporters found the reconciliation rhetoric jarring after four years of brutal war, and some questioned whether the Emperor's preservation was a wise bargain. But the dominant narrative, the war ended with dignity and the peace would be built on law, held, and it shaped the public's understanding of the surrender for a generation.

## The Emperor's Absence: The Man Who Was Not There

The most important person in the Instrument's fourth paragraph was not present at the ceremony. The Emperor, whose authority the document preserved and subordinated, remained in Tokyo, and his absence was a deliberate decision by both sides. For the Japanese government, the Emperor's presence at a surrender ceremony would have been an unbearable humiliation, a visible subordination of the sacred to the profane that might have shattered the very legitimacy the Allies intended to use. For MacArthur, the Emperor's absence was equally desirable: the occupation would work through the imperial institution, and that institution's dignity had to be preserved for the work ahead. The delegation signed "by command of and in behalf of" the Emperor, and the Emperor's command was conveyed through the government's instructions, not through his physical presence.

The absence is worth dwelling on because it captures the central paradox of the Instrument's political settlement. The document's most consequential clause concerns a man who never appears, and the ceremony's most important political fact, the preservation of the imperial institution, is enacted entirely through proxies and paper. This was not an oversight but the essence of the arrangement: the Emperor's authority was to be real enough to legitimize the occupation and distant enough to be managed. The Japanese government's August strategy had been to surrender the war while preserving the kokutai, and the Missouri ceremony honored that strategy by keeping the Emperor off the deck. The empty space where he might have stood is the Instrument's silent center.

## The Sword Question: Umezu's Blade and Surrender Etiquette

General Umezu wore his sword to the ceremony, and the detail has attracted more commentary than its military significance might warrant. In the European tradition of surrender, the defeated commander's sword is offered to the victor as the symbolic act of submission; at the Missouri, no such offering occurred. Umezu kept his sword throughout the ceremony, and MacArthur made no move to demand it. The decision was deliberate and was understood by both sides. To have required the public surrender of the sword would have been a gratuitous humiliation of the Japanese military at the precise moment when the Allies needed that military's obedience for the demobilization ahead. The Instrument bound the Imperial General Headquarters through Umezu's signature, and the signature was deemed sufficient; the sword was allowed to remain a personal accoutrement rather than becoming a prop in a theater of degradation.

The sword's retention also reflected MacArthur's broader philosophy of the occupation, which would consistently prefer dignity to humiliation as the more effective instrument of control. The contrast with the German surrenders is instructive: at Berlin Karlshorst, the German delegation was treated with cold formality and marched through the protocols of defeat; at Tokyo Bay, the Japanese delegation was treated with punctilious courtesy. Both approaches achieved their immediate purpose, but MacArthur's was designed for the longer game. The occupation would require the cooperation of the Japanese bureaucracy, the military's administrative apparatus, and the imperial institution, and the ceremony's courtesies were the first installment on that cooperation. Umezu's sword, remaining at his side, was a small but legible signal that the victors intended to rule through the vanquished rather than over their ruins.

## The Local Surrenders: Implementing "Wherever Situated"

The Instrument's "wherever situated" clause was implemented through a series of local surrender ceremonies across the Pacific and Asia in September and October 1945, each conducted under MacArthur's authority as Supreme Commander. At Singapore on September 12, the Japanese Southern Army surrendered to Lord Mountbatten's Southeast Asia Command. At Saigon, at Rabaul, at Hong Kong, at Seoul, at Tientsin, Japanese commanders signed local instruments that repeated the Missouri formula and placed their forces under Allied direction. In China, the surrender of more than a million Japanese troops was complicated by the resumed Chinese civil war, and the ceremonies there were entangled with the Nationalist-Communist struggle from the start. In Manchuria, the Soviet Union, which had entered the war in August, received the surrender of the Kwantung Army and shipped hundreds of thousands of Japanese prisoners to Siberia.

The local surrenders demonstrate the practical wisdom of the Instrument's geographic clause. Had the Missouri document covered only the home islands, each theater would have required a separate negotiation, with all the opportunities for delay and evasion that the Japanese hardliners might have exploited. Instead, every local ceremony was an implementation of the September 2 act, and every Japanese commander who signed a local instrument was executing orders that flowed from Umezu's signature on the Missouri. The process was not perfectly smooth: isolated garrisons on remote Pacific islands held out for weeks, months, and in a few legendary cases years, unaware or disbelieving that the war had ended. But the legal framework held, and the demobilization of more than seven million Japanese military personnel, the largest organized disarmament in history to that point, was accomplished within months.

## The Document's Afterlife: From Deck to Archive

After the ceremony, the two copies of the Instrument began their separate archival journeys. The Allied copy, with MacArthur's five-pen signature and the nine national signatures in their hierarchical order, was returned to the United States and is held in the National Archives. The Japanese copy, bearing Shigemitsu's and Umezu's signatures and the hand-drawn correction of the Cosgrave line, was retained by the Japanese government and is preserved in Tokyo. Both copies have been exhibited, reproduced in facsimile, and studied by historians, and the Japanese copy's visible correction remains its most examined feature. The document that ended the war became, within years, a museum object, and its display history is itself a chapter in the memory of the Pacific war.

The afterlife also includes the Instrument's legal career in the occupation years. The document was cited as the authority for MacArthur's directives, for the war crimes trials, for the demobilization orders, and for the constitutional revision process. When the San Francisco Peace Treaty was signed in 1951, formally ending the state of war, it did not supersede the Instrument so much as build upon it: the treaty restored Japan's sovereignty, but the six years of occupation that made the treaty possible had been conducted under the Instrument's authority. The two pages signed in twenty three minutes thus governed the most transformative period in modern Japanese history, a fact that the document's brevity tends to obscure and that its archival afterlife helps to recover.

## V-J Day and Memory: How September 2 Was Remembered

September 2 was proclaimed as V-J Day in the United States, the counterpart to V-E Day in May, and the date entered the American calendar as the formal end of the Second World War. The commemoration took various forms: the Missouri herself was eventually preserved as a memorial, berthed at Pearl Harbor within sight of the Arizona Memorial, so that the beginning and the end of America's Pacific war face each other across the water. The ceremony's imagery, the flag, the pens, the signatures, became the standard visual shorthand for the war's conclusion, reproduced in textbooks, documentaries, and public memorials. In Japan, the date is remembered differently: August 15, the day of the Emperor's broadcast, is the day of national remembrance, while September 2 belongs to the diplomatic record.

The divergence of memory is itself historically significant, because it reflects the two different endings the war had. For the Allies, the war ended with a legal act performed before the world, and September 2 is the date of that act. For Japan, the war ended with the Emperor's voice on the radio, and August 15 is the date of the national experience. Neither date is wrong, and the Instrument's own text does not adjudicate between them; it simply records the formal conclusion. The memory of September 2 has also been shaped by the ceremony's success as media: because the Missouri morning was so thoroughly photographed and filmed, it dominates the visual record of the war's end, and the images have a way of crowding out the messier, less photogenic realities of the August decision and the local surrenders. A close read of the Instrument is, among other things, an exercise in looking past the famous images to the document beneath them.

## The Instruments of Ratification: How Tokyo Accepted the Terms

The signatures on the Missouri were the final step in a ratification process that had consumed the Japanese government for most of August 1945. The sequence began with the Potsdam Declaration's delivery on July 26, continued through the atomic bombings of Hiroshima on August 6 and Nagasaki on August 9 and the Soviet Union's entry into the war on August 9, and reached its crisis in the imperial conferences of August 9 to 10 and August 14, at which the Emperor personally broke the cabinet's deadlock in favor of acceptance. The Japanese reply of August 10 accepted Potsdam with a single reservation concerning the Emperor's prerogatives; the Allied reply of August 11, drafted by Secretary of State James Byrnes, answered that reservation with the formula that became the Instrument's fourth paragraph. The final Japanese acceptance was communicated on August 14 through the Swiss and Swedish diplomatic channels, and the Emperor's radio broadcast on August 15 announced the decision to the nation. Each of these steps was a decision point at which the surrender might have failed, and the Instrument's text is best read as the legal crystallization of that month-long political struggle.

The ratification history explains why the Instrument takes the form it does. The August 10 reservation and the August 11 reply had already settled the Emperor question in principle; the Instrument merely gave that settlement legal form. The "wherever situated" clause reflected the Allies' fear, sharpened by the Kyujo Incident of August 14 to 15, that elements of the Japanese military might reject the Emperor's decision and continue fighting; the document was drafted to leave no commander any legal ground for continued resistance. And the requirement that the signatories act for the Emperor, the government, and the Imperial General Headquarters reflected the Allies' understanding, hard won through the August cables, that only a surrender binding all three centers of Japanese authority would hold. The two pages are the tip of a month of diplomacy, and the close reader who knows the August history can see every clause as the answer to a specific August problem.

## The Fleet Review: Naval Power as Ceremony Backdrop

The Missouri did not sit alone in Tokyo Bay on September 2. Around her was anchored the largest fleet ever assembled in Japanese waters: American battleships, carriers, cruisers, destroyers, and submarines, joined by British, Australian, New Zealand, Canadian, Dutch, and French warships, more than two hundred and fifty vessels in all. The fleet was the ceremony's silent second text, a demonstration of the naval power that had made the surrender necessary and that would enforce its terms. The Japanese delegation's destroyer passed through this armada on the way to the Missouri, and the delegates could see from the water what the Instrument's words would confirm on paper: the war at sea had been lost beyond recovery, and the force that had won it was present in overwhelming strength.

The fleet's presence was the product of deliberate planning by Admiral Nimitz's staff, who understood that the surrender ceremony was also a strategic signal to the region. The Pacific war had been, above all, a naval war, and the assembly of the fleet in Tokyo Bay was the naval war's final statement. The carriers whose aircraft would perform the flyover, the battleships whose guns had bombarded the Japanese home islands, the submarines that had strangled Japan's shipping, all were visible to the Japanese delegates and to the cameras. The Instrument's legal language about "all Japanese armed forces and all armed forces under Japanese control wherever situated" gained its full force from this visible context: the surrender was not an abstraction but a recognition of a military reality that filled the bay to the horizon. The document said what the fleet showed.

## The Surrender's First Hours: From Deck to Tokyo Radio

When the ceremony ended at approximately 9:30 AM, the Instrument's third paragraph, the cessation of hostilities, was already in effect in principle, but the practical work of ending the war took hours and days to accomplish. MacArthur's headquarters immediately began transmitting the surrender orders through the Japanese government's channels, and the Japanese military's communication network, still largely intact, relayed the ceasefire to commands across the empire. In Tokyo, the government issued the implementing orders that the Instrument's fifth paragraph required, directing ministries and military headquarters to comply with the Supreme Commander's instructions. The first test of the document's authority came within hours: isolated commands that had not received the orders, or that doubted their authenticity, had to be brought into compliance, and the Japanese chain of command, acting under the authority Umezu's signature had bound, performed the task.

The first hours also brought the occupation's first movements. Advance parties of American troops began landing at Japanese airfields within days, and the formal occupation of Japan commenced with the arrival of the first American forces at Atsugi on August 28, before the ceremony, and accelerated after September 2. The Instrument's framework, government through the existing Japanese state under the Supreme Commander's direction, was tested immediately and held: the Japanese bureaucracy, which had administered the war, turned its machinery to administering the surrender. The speed of the transition surprised even the Allies. A nation that had fought with extraordinary tenacity for four years demobilized with extraordinary discipline in a matter of weeks, and the Instrument's careful binding of every center of authority was vindicated by the result. The document worked because the men who signed it commanded obedience, and the obedience was rendered.

## The Pens' Afterlife: Relics of the Signing

MacArthur's five pens did not return to his desk. Each was given to an individual or institution chosen to mark the moment's significance: one to General Wainwright, who had surrendered the Philippines in 1942 and had now lived to see the war's end; one to General Percival, who had surrendered Singapore; one to West Point, MacArthur's alma mater; and the others to institutions and individuals associated with the victory. The distribution was characteristic of MacArthur's sense of history: the pens were not souvenirs but symbols, and their dispersal spread the ceremony's meaning across the Allied world. Wainwright's pen carried a particular poignancy, closing the circle that had begun with his humiliation at Corregidor three years earlier.

The relic-making extended beyond the pens. The table on which the Instrument was signed, the flags, the chairs, the blotter, all were preserved and eventually displayed, and the Missouri herself became the ceremony's largest relic. The transformation of working objects into sacred ones is a familiar process in the aftermath of great events, but it deserves the close reader's attention here because it began on the day itself: MacArthur was already thinking about the pens' destination while he was still signing. The Instrument was a legal document, but it was also, from the moment the ink dried, a historical artifact, and the men on the deck understood that they were handling the future's property.

## Why the Japanese Instrument of Surrender Still Matters

The Japanese Instrument of Surrender still matters because it is the legal foundation of everything that followed in the Pacific: the occupation, the constitutional transformation, the alliance system, and the postwar order in East Asia. Every subsequent American Japanese agreement, from the 1951 security treaty to the present, descends in a direct line from the authority that the Instrument conferred on the Supreme Commander and through him on the occupation. The document also matters as a study in how wars end, and specifically in how coalitions end wars: through procedure, through precision, through the careful management of symbols, and through the willingness to preserve enough of the defeated state to make the peace administrable. The German surrenders showed what happens when the defeated state disappears; the Japanese Instrument showed the alternative, and the alternative worked.

The document matters, finally, as an artifact of a particular kind of power: the power to end a war not with vengeance but with a plan. MacArthur's reconciliation rhetoric, the preservation of the Emperor, the decision to govern through the Japanese government, the nine nation signing framework, all of these were choices, and different choices were available. A Carthaginian peace was imaginable in September 1945; many Americans demanded it. The Instrument chose otherwise, and the choice shaped the next eighty years of Pacific history. A two page document, signed in twenty three minutes on a battleship in Tokyo Bay, is not the whole explanation of what followed. But it is where the explanation begins.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many paragraphs does the Instrument contain and what does each one do?

The Instrument contains five paragraphs, each with a distinct function. The first accepts the Potsdam Declaration's provisions by reference and defines the Allied Powers. The second proclaims the unconditional surrender of all Japanese armed forces wherever situated. The third orders the immediate cessation of hostilities and the preservation of military assets. The fourth subordinates the Emperor's and the government's authority to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers. The fifth binds the Emperor, the government, and their successors to carry out the Instrument in good faith. The architecture is deliberately spare: incorporation, surrender, ceasefire, political settlement, and future obligation, in that order, with no paragraph doing another's work.

### What legal technique lets the opening paragraph incorporate the Potsdam terms?

The opening paragraph uses incorporation by reference, a standard legal technique in which a document adopts the provisions of another document without restating them. By accepting "the provisions set forth in the declaration issued by the heads of the Governments of the United States, China, and Great Britain on 26 July 1945, at Potsdam," the Instrument imports the entire thirteen article Potsdam framework, its threats, its terms, and its postwar promises, as the substantive content of the surrender. The technique served two purposes: it kept the Instrument to two pages, and it prevented any dispute at the signing table about what the terms actually were, since the terms were the already published Potsdam terms, accepted as a finished object rather than negotiated line by line.

### Why does the document surrender forces "wherever situated" instead of naming theaters?

The phrase "wherever situated" was a deliberate choice to make the surrender global in a single legal act. In September 1945, Japanese forces remained under arms across Manchuria, Korea, China, Southeast Asia, the Netherlands East Indies, the scattered Pacific islands, and the home islands. Naming each theater would have invited arguments about whether omitted or misdescribed areas were covered, and it would have required separate surrender negotiations in every region. The blanket geographic clause bound every Japanese commander, in every theater, to the same act of submission, and it authorized MacArthur to direct the subsequent local surrenders, at Singapore, Saigon, Rabaul, and elsewhere, as implementations of the Instrument rather than as independent agreements.

### What did the preservation clause require of Japanese commanders?

The third paragraph required Japanese forces to "preserve and save from damage all ships, aircraft, and military and civil property," a clause aimed at preventing the scuttling and sabotage that defeated forces have historically used to deny their conquerors the fruits of victory. The drafters had the German experience of May 1945 in mind, when the Allies struggled with the deliberate destruction of naval vessels and industrial assets. By writing preservation directly into the surrender act, the Instrument made any post surrender destruction a violation of the surrender terms rather than a legitimate act of war. The clause also served the occupation's practical needs: intact ships, aircraft, factories, and records were assets the Allies intended to inventory, seize, or supervise, and the paragraph ensured they would survive to be administered.

### How did the fourth paragraph keep the Emperor while subordinating him?

The fourth paragraph states that "the authority of the Emperor and the Japanese Government to rule the state shall be subject to the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers," a formulation that preserves the imperial institution while making it legally subordinate to Allied authority. The Emperor is not abolished, which satisfied the Japanese government's minimum demand, the preservation of the kokutai, and the Emperor is not guaranteed, which preserved the American unconditional surrender doctrine. The wording descends from the Byrnes Note of August 11 1945, which had answered Japan's conditional acceptance of Potsdam with language keeping the Emperor in place while reserving Allied control. The paragraph's artful ambiguity was its function: both sides could sign it, and the occupation that followed was built on the space the ambiguity created.

### What does the "successors" clause in the final paragraph accomplish?

The fifth paragraph's undertaking is made "for the Emperor, the Japanese Government and their successors," and the successors clause is the Instrument's mechanism of permanence. It binds not only the men who signed on September 2 1945 but any future Japanese government, closing the legal argument that a change of cabinet, a change of ministry, or even a change of constitutional order could release Japan from the surrender's obligations. The clause looks forward to the occupation years and the compliance disputes they would bring, and it gives the Supreme Commander open-ended authority to take "whatever action may be required" to enforce the terms. In a document of five short paragraphs, the successors clause is the one that extends the Instrument's force beyond the lifetimes of its signatories.

### Who were Shigemitsu and Umezu and what authorities did they bind?

Mamoru Shigemitsu was the foreign minister, a career diplomat who had served as ambassador to the Soviet Union and Britain and had participated in the August cabinet deliberations that produced the surrender decision; he signed for the Emperor and the Japanese Government, binding the civilian and political authority of the state. Yoshijiro Umezu was the Army's chief of staff, a hardliner who had opposed surrender until the Emperor's intervention; he signed for the Imperial General Headquarters, binding the military chain of command. The dual signatures reflected Japan's parallel structure of authority, in which civilian and military lines ran separately to the throne, and the Instrument's drafters understood that a surrender binding only one line could be repudiated by the other.

### Why did the Japanese sign before any Allied representative?

The surrendering party signs first as a matter of surrender protocol: the defeated acknowledge submission before the victors record their acceptance. Shigemitsu and Umezu placed the first two signatures on the Instrument, binding the Emperor, the government, and the Imperial General Headquarters, and only then did MacArthur sign for the Allied Powers, followed by the national representatives. The sequence is the ceremony's constitutional diagram in miniature. It also had a practical function: the Allied signatures attested to the receipt of a surrender already tendered, which meant that no Allied representative could be said to have negotiated the terms at the table. The document was presented as a finished object, and the signing order reinforced that the morning's business was signature, not discussion.

### What determined the order of the Allied national signatures?

The Allied national signatures followed MacArthur's collective signature in an order reflecting each power's assessed weight in the Pacific war: the United States, China, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, Australia, Canada, France, the Netherlands, and New Zealand. The order was not alphabetical, which would have been the neutral diplomatic convention, but hierarchical, and it was read as such by everyone present. The arrangement solved a genuine protocol problem: at a legal ceremony, unlike a summit conference, precedence cannot be finessed through the fiction of perfect equality, because a legal act requires a definite order. The solution, MacArthur first as the embodiment of the Allied Powers collectively, then the nations in descending order of Pacific consequence, was accepted without objection and was followed by the subsequent local surrender ceremonies.

### Which nations signed and what did the roster signify?

Nine nations signed: Japan's two delegates, then MacArthur for the Allied Powers collectively, then representatives of the United States, China, Britain, the Soviet Union, Australia, Canada, France, the Netherlands, and New Zealand. The roster encodes two different logics of coalition membership. China and the Soviet Union signed by the logic of contribution, China for its eight year war against Japan and the Soviet Union for its decisive August intervention. France and the Netherlands signed by the logic of status, as colonial powers whose empires the settlement would formally restore. The Commonwealth nations, Australia, Canada, and New Zealand, signed by both logics, having contributed forces throughout the Pacific campaigns. The nine nation framework foreshadowed the Allied Council for Japan and the Far Eastern Commission, the institutions that would give the coalition's breadth a continuing form.

### What did MacArthur's opening remarks declare about the war's causes?

MacArthur opened by stating that the war's issues, "involving divergent ideals and ideologies, have been determined on the battlefields of the world and hence are not for our discussion or debate." The sentence performed a deliberate act of rhetorical closure: the war's causes would not be litigated at the ceremony, not because they were unimportant but because they had been settled by force and the ceremony's business was the future. He then committed both victors and vanquished to "that higher dignity which alone befits the sacred purposes we are about to serve," framing the occupation as a shared undertaking rather than a punishment. The remarks were the first articulation of the reconciliation philosophy that would govern the next six years, and their deliberate avoidance of vengeance set the tone for an occupation conducted through law and administration rather than retribution.

### How did MacArthur's closing prayer shape the ceremony's tone?

MacArthur closed the proceedings with the words, "Let us pray that peace be now restored to the world and that God will preserve it always. These proceedings are closed." The prayer's religious register was a deliberate choice by a commander who understood the ceremony as a moral event as well as a legal one. By invoking divine preservation of the peace, MacArthur lifted the surrender out of the purely military frame and gave it a sacral quality that the newsreels carried around the world. The prayer also completed the arc of his remarks: the opening had closed debate about the past, the body of the ceremony had performed the legal transfer, and the closing committed the future to a higher purpose. The combination of religious-moral framing with the overwhelming military display of the flyover that followed gave the ceremony its characteristic doubleness, reconciliation in words, power in the sky.

### What was the Cosgrave signing-line error and how was it fixed?

Canadian representative Lawrence Moore Cosgrave signed his name on the line below the one reserved for Canada, beneath the French signature, on the Japanese copy of the Instrument, whose closely spaced signature lines invited the mistake. The error was discovered after the ceremony, and Japanese officials corrected the document by drawing a line from Cosgrave's misplaced signature to the correct Canadian designation, annotating the correction by hand. The corrected copy, with its visible amendment, is preserved in the Japanese archives. The incident reveals the seriousness with which both sides treated the document's formal correctness: a surrender instrument with a misplaced signature is a defective legal act, and the defeated party's officials took care to repair the victors' error. The correction remains the ceremony's most human footnote.

### Why did MacArthur select a battleship as the venue?

MacArthur chose the USS Missouri for reasons that combined symbolism, practicality, and courtesy. As the newest and most powerful battleship in the fleet, the Missouri announced that the surrender would take place under the guns of American naval power. Her name honored President Truman's home state, a costless courtesy to the commander in chief. Her size accommodated the delegations, the press, and the hundreds of witnesses, while her quarterdeck provided a natural stage that was intimate enough for the cameras and grand enough for history. A shore ceremony in ruined Tokyo was rejected because it would have staged the Japanese delegation's humiliation in a devastated city, a spectacle MacArthur wanted to avoid with the occupation about to begin. The battleship, sovereign American territory that happened to float, allowed the ceremony to display power and restraint at once.

### What did the December 7 1941 flag behind the tables signify?

Behind the signing tables flew the American flag that had flown over the Capitol on December 7 1941, brought to the Missouri at MacArthur's direction. The flag's presence was a silent statement of historical symmetry: the ensign that had been flying when the Japanese attack began the Pacific war would fly when the war formally ended. For the American audience, it closed the narrative arc from Pearl Harbor to Tokyo Bay, from the first day of the war to the last. For the Japanese delegation, it was a quiet reminder of the war's origins and of the power that had been roused. The flag was one of several material details, the mess tables, the green baize, the five pens, through which the ceremony wrote its second text, the text of memory and meaning that ran alongside the legal text of the Instrument.

### How did the Japanese surrender document differ from the German instruments?

The German surrenders of May 1945 were military-technical documents, dense with provisions about ceasefires, disarmament, and command authority, negotiated with a collapsing regime whose government had disintegrated and whose leader was dead. The Japanese Instrument, by contrast, was a two page political-legal document presented as a finished text to a functioning government, incorporating the Potsdam Declaration by reference and settling the single decisive political question, the Emperor's status, in its fourth paragraph. The German ceremonies were hasty, the Reims signing nearly furtive; the Japanese ceremony was choreographed over two weeks for maximum legibility. And where the German surrenders left Germany's political future entirely to improvisation by occupation authorities, the Japanese Instrument's preservation of the Emperor and the government made possible the indirect occupation that followed.

### What occupation structure did the Instrument's wording authorize?

The Instrument's third and fifth paragraphs, with their references to Japanese government agencies acting at the Supreme Commander's direction and to whatever action he might require, authorized an indirect occupation: MacArthur would govern through the existing Japanese state rather than replacing it with direct military government. The arrangement was possible because the fourth paragraph had preserved the Emperor and the government as functioning authorities, and it was desirable because it economized on occupation manpower while retaining the administrative capacity needed to govern seventy million people. The indirect structure shaped everything that followed, from the emergency food shipments of the winter of 1945 to the constitutional revision of 1946 to 1947, and it distinguished the Japanese occupation fundamentally from the direct Allied administration of Germany.

### Which dates compete as the war's ending and why does September 2 hold?

Three dates have legitimate claims. August 14 1945 marks the political decision, when the Japanese cabinet, following the Emperor's intervention, communicated acceptance of the Potsdam terms through diplomatic channels. August 15 marks the public ending, when the Emperor's broadcast announced the acceptance to the Japanese people. September 2 marks the formal, legal, internationally witnessed conclusion, when the Instrument was signed aboard the Missouri. This article holds September 2 as the formal conclusion because it is the date that made the ending legible beyond Japan: the August decision was understood only by governments, the August broadcast was heard chiefly by the Japanese, but the September ceremony was photographed, filmed, and reported worldwide. Wars end in layers, and the Instrument supplied the final layer, the one that could be archived, cited, and shared.

### How did the Instrument treat the Soviet Union's standing?

The opening paragraph defines "the Allied Powers" as the United States, China, and Great Britain, "and subsequently adhered to by the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics," folding Moscow into the legal framework through the phrase "subsequently adhered to." The Soviet Union had not signed the Potsdam Declaration in July because it was not yet at war with Japan, but by August 8 it had declared war, and its representative, Kuzma Derevyanko, signed the Instrument in fourth place among the national signatories. The paragraph's quiet clause thus resolved a potentially awkward question of standing without discussion, and it reflected the reality that Soviet entry into the Pacific war, however late, had been one of the final pressures on the Japanese cabinet. The Soviet signature also foreshadowed the complications of the early Cold War, when Moscow's interpretation of its Pacific rights would diverge sharply from Washington's.

### Where can the Instrument's text and the ceremony footage be studied?

The Instrument's text is reproduced in the standard documentary collections of the Second World War's ending, including the State Department's Foreign Relations of the United States volumes for 1945 and the published papers of the occupation, and facsimiles of both the Allied and Japanese copies, the latter showing the Cosgrave correction, are held in national archives. The ceremony was filmed by military cameramen, and the newsreel footage, showing the delegations' arrival, the signings, MacArthur's remarks, and the aircraft flyover, is preserved in film archives and widely reproduced. MacArthur's Reports of General MacArthur, the official history of the occupation's early phase, contains the staff's account of the ceremony's planning. Together, the documentary and visual records make the Missouri ceremony one of the most thoroughly documented twenty three minutes in recorded history.
