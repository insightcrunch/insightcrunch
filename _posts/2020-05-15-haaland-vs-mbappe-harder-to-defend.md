Ask whether Haaland or Mbappe is harder to defend and almost every answer you get will be an answer to a different question, which is who is the better footballer. That question has its own evidence and its own audience, and this article is not about it. The question here is narrower, colder, and far more useful to anyone who has to prepare a team: if you had to build and deliver a defensive plan against one of these two forwards, using the players actually available to you, which plan would be harder to execute and which would be more likely to break? That version has an answer. It simply does not have the same answer for every side, and the reason it does not is the most interesting thing about the comparison.

The reason the argument never settles online is that both camps are usually right about different teams. A coach with two quick centre-backs and a disciplined offside line will tell you the depth runner is the more manageable problem, because the tool that neutralises him is the tool that coach already owns. A coach with a slow, experienced back line that defends brilliantly inside its own box will tell you the opposite, because the reception in behind is the thing his side cannot survive. Both are describing their own squad, not the forwards. Once you separate the threat from the defending team, the disagreement collapses into something a coach can actually use.

This article resolves the question on four dimensions that a defending coach can act on, produces a comparison table you can take into a meeting, and then names a verdict for three different types of side. It leans on the plans rather than restating them, so where the counter-plan detail belongs elsewhere it is linked rather than repeated. The full sequenced plan against the depth runner lives in the [counter blueprint for stopping Erling Haaland](/2020/01/15/how-to-stop-erling-haaland/), the space management plan against the acceleration forward lives in the [blueprint for stopping Kylian Mbappe](/2020/02/15/how-to-stop-kylian-mbappe/), and the taxonomy that both sit inside, the one that sorts forwards by threat type rather than by reputation, is set out in the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/).

## What "harder to defend" actually means

Defensive difficulty is not a synonym for quality, and treating it as one is what wrecks the discussion before it starts. A forward can be the more complete player and the easier planning problem at the same time, because planning difficulty is a function of how many of your defensive resources a threat consumes and how many of those resources you happen to have. A side that concedes very little against one profile and very much against another has not discovered which forward is better. It has discovered what it is built to survive.

So the question is defined here as follows. Harder to defend means that the counter-plan against him requires more of your defensive resources, tolerates fewer errors before it fails, and degrades faster when a single component is missing. Those three tests are all measurable in the sense that matters to a coach: you can look at your own squad on a Tuesday and know whether you have the thing the plan requires. That reframing is the whole contribution of this article, and everything below is built on it.

Three consequences follow immediately. The first is that the verdict must be conditional. Any answer that does not name the defending team is an answer to a question nobody asked. The second is that output is irrelevant here. Goals scored, chances converted, and honours accumulated are measures of a forward's effectiveness, not measures of the cost of defending him, and a forward can be extremely effective against opponents who never had a coherent plan in the first place. The third is that a single fixture proves nothing in either direction, because a single fixture is a sample of one against one plan executed by one set of players on one pitch.

### What is the difference between the better forward and the harder forward to defend?

The better forward produces more, across more situations, over a longer period. The harder forward to defend consumes more of the specific defensive resources a given team lacks. These are separate measurements, and a side can face the better player and still find the other one the more frightening preparation problem.

That distinction sounds academic until you sit in a video meeting. A coach preparing for a fixture does not need to know which forward would win a popularity contest or a ranking exercise. He needs to know how many players he has to commit, where he has to commit them, what he is giving up elsewhere by committing them, and which of his own players is going to be exposed if one link in the plan fails. Those are the questions this comparison answers.

## The two threats, stated precisely

Before anything can be compared, both threats have to be described at the level of the action rather than the reputation. Two forwards can score the same number of goals from completely different mechanisms, and the mechanism is the only part a defence can plan against.

Erling Haaland is a left-footed Norwegian centre-forward, tall and quick for his frame, whose scoring is heavily concentrated inside the penalty area and close to goal, with a career arc that runs through Bryne, Molde, Red Bull Salzburg, Borussia Dortmund and Manchester City. The threat has three component actions. He runs the shoulder of the last defender with timing keyed to the passer's head-up moment rather than to his own position. He attacks the near post from cutbacks and low crosses, arriving into a space rather than beating a marker. He finishes first time, most often with the left foot and sometimes with the head, which shortens the goalkeeper's decision window to almost nothing. His touch counts are low relative to a complete forward, which is a design feature of how his sides use him rather than a defect, and understanding that is the difference between a plan that works and a plan built on a misreading. The full anatomy of the limitations, including the ones that are claimed but not real, belongs to the [breakdown of Haaland's weaknesses](/2020/02/01/erling-haaland-weaknesses-explained/) and is linked rather than reopened here.

Kylian Mbappe is a right-footed French forward who operates from the left and through the middle, developed at Monaco before Paris Saint-Germain and Real Madrid, and a World Cup winner with France. His defining quality is acceleration and top speed while carrying the ball, and it expresses itself most dangerously when he receives facing forward with grass ahead of him, either running the last line or attacking the inside channel from a wide starting position. He finishes with either foot, with the right dominant when he cuts inside from the left. The threat is a function of three variables rather than three actions: the distance between him and the last line at the moment of reception, whether that reception is facing forward, and whether a second defender can arrive before he reaches full speed. The limitations and the arguments about them belong to the [breakdown of Mbappe's weaknesses](/2020/03/01/kylian-mbappe-weaknesses-explained/).

Set side by side, the two descriptions already reveal the shape of the comparison. One threat is resolved inside the penalty area and is preceded by a run that begins outside it. The other is resolved anywhere between the halfway line and the six-yard box and is preceded by a reception that can happen almost anywhere. One is a box occupier who runs to get there. The other is a runner who arrives at the box already moving at a speed the defence cannot match. Those are not variations on a theme. They stress entirely different parts of a defensive structure, which is why the honest answer to the comparison depends on which part of your structure is weakest.

## Why the usual framings fail

Three framings dominate the discussion and all three are analytically empty, so it is worth clearing them out before building anything on top.

The first is the framing of the question as a proxy for a ranking. Once the discussion becomes about who is greater, every observation gets recruited into a case, and the moment a claim is recruited into a case it stops being evidence. A depth runner is described as one-dimensional by people arguing for the other side, and an acceleration forward is described as a transition specialist by the same mechanism in reverse. Both descriptions are compressions of a real tactical property into an insult, and neither helps a defender who has to stand in a line on Saturday and decide whether to step or drop.

The second is the framing by output. Goals and assists measure what a forward produced against the defending that actually happened, which is a mixture of his quality, his teammates' quality, the opponent's plan, and the opponent's execution. Output cannot distinguish between a forward who beat a well-executed plan and a forward who was never planned against properly. Defensive cost can, because defensive cost asks what the plan has to contain, not what the scoreboard said afterwards.

The third is the framing by direct meetings. Head-to-head fixtures are the smallest possible sample, they are contaminated by everything from the state of the tie to the tactical instructions of two managers with objectives of their own, and they are the least generalisable evidence available. A forward who was quiet in a specific fixture may have been quiet because his own side needed him to occupy two defenders while somebody else scored, which is the threat working exactly as designed.

## The four dimensions of defensive cost

The comparison runs on four questions. Each one is chosen because a coach can answer it about his own side before he answers it about the forward, and because each one maps onto a resource that is either present in a squad or absent from it. The two forwards separate cleanly on three of the four. The fourth is where the honest disagreement lives, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

### Where does the decisive action happen relative to the defensive line?

The depth runner's decisive action happens behind or on the last line, then resolves inside the box. The acceleration forward's decisive action happens in front of the line, at the reception, and the resolution can occur anywhere from the halfway line to the goalmouth. One threat is a line problem. The other is a runway problem.

This dimension decides more about your shape than any other, because it determines whether the plan is written for the defensive line or for the space in front of it. Against a depth runner, the moment that matters is the instant the passer is allowed to lift his head, and the whole plan is an argument about how to prevent that instant from coinciding with a gap between the last defender and the goalkeeper. Everything else follows: the line height, the willingness to step, the offside discipline, the goalkeeper's starting position, and the centre-back's decision about whether to track the run or hold the line and trust the flag.

Against an acceleration forward, the moment that matters is the reception. If he receives facing his own goal, the threat has already been reduced by more than half regardless of how fast he is, because acceleration needs a direction and turning costs him the yard that acceleration was supposed to buy. If he receives facing forward with fifteen metres of grass in front of him, no individual defensive quality in football reliably solves the situation. The plan is therefore a plan about receptions, not a plan about the line.

The practical consequence is that the two threats point your preparation at different players. The depth runner points it at the centre-backs and the goalkeeper. The acceleration forward points it at the full-back, the nearest midfielder, and the rest defence behind the ball when your own side is in possession. A coach who has spent a week rehearsing an offside line has not rehearsed anything relevant to the second problem, and a coach who has spent a week rehearsing pressing angles and cover distances has not rehearsed the first.

The dimension also explains a widely misread pattern. Sides that defend deep against a depth runner often feel safer and then concede more, because dropping deep removes the space behind and simultaneously hands him the territory in which his box work is strongest, multiplying the cutback threat. Sides that defend deep against an acceleration forward often reduce his most dangerous version and then concede a different way, because the deep block invites the wide reception and the cutback geometry his teams are built to exploit. The same defensive decision produces opposite failure modes against the two threats, which is the clearest possible evidence that they are not variations on one problem.

### How much time exists between the trigger and the shot?

Against the depth runner, the interval between the trigger and the shot is short and fixed: the pass is played, the run has already started, and the finish is first time. Against the acceleration forward, the interval is longer but the defence is losing ground throughout it, so the extra time is not usable time. One compresses the decision. The other extends the pursuit.

That distinction matters because defensive plans are recovery plans as much as prevention plans. A structure that is beaten once but recovers is a functional structure. A structure that is beaten once and cannot recover is a fragile one, and the difference between them is almost entirely a question of how much time the threat allows between the first error and the shot.

The depth runner allows almost none. The pass, the run and the finish are effectively a single action, and the goalkeeper's decision window is short enough that his starting position has to be decided before the sequence begins rather than during it. The consequence is that the plan has to be preventive with very little tolerance for error. There is no second defender arriving in time, no recovery run that gets there, and no goalkeeper technique that reliably compensates. Either the pass is prevented, the timing is disrupted, or the situation is lost. That is a brittle plan by construction, and it is why the passer-first principle exists: the first defender in a plan against a depth runner is the one nearest the ball, not the one nearest the striker.

The acceleration forward allows more time in the literal sense, since a carry from a wide reception to a shooting position takes several seconds. But every one of those seconds is spent by defenders running backwards or diagonally at a speed disadvantage, which means the extra time produces very little extra defensive value unless a second defender was already positioned to arrive. This is the single most misunderstood part of defending against pace. The situation is not lost because the first defender is slower. It is lost because the second defender was not already in a position from which arriving was possible. The plan against him is therefore not brittle in the same way. It has more recovery states, but each recovery state has to be built into the shape before the ball is lost, which requires an entirely different kind of discipline: positional discipline while your own team is attacking.

That last point is worth dwelling on, because it is where most sides quietly lose these fixtures. Rest defence is a possession behaviour. It is decided by where your full-backs are standing while you are trying to score, by whether your holding midfielder is on the correct side of the ball, and by whether your centre-backs have held a sensible distance between them while the attack is in progress. A side that cannot organise its rest defence has already lost the plan against an acceleration forward before the ball changes hands, and no amount of defensive coaching in the defensive phase repairs it.

### How many defenders does the threat occupy?

The depth runner occupies two defenders continuously through gravity rather than contact: the near centre-back who cannot leave him and the far centre-back who cannot leave the near-post space. The acceleration forward occupies one defender continuously and a second one conditionally, but the second is the more expensive because he has to come from midfield.

Occupation cost is the dimension that most often decides which threat actually hurts a specific side, and it is the least discussed. A threat that pins two defenders permanently has already changed your shape everywhere else, because those two players cannot participate in anything: they cannot step into midfield, they cannot press the passer, and they cannot join the counter. A threat that pins one defender and then periodically demands a second one is cheaper in the aggregate but more disruptive at the moment of demand, because the second defender is subtracted from the part of the pitch where your side was trying to build something.

The depth runner's occupation is almost entirely static. He does not need the ball to cost you two players, and this is the property most often mistaken for a limitation by people counting his touches. A forward who costs a defence two players while touching the ball rarely is not being marginalised. He is producing an overload for his teammates in every other zone, and any comparison that reads a low touch count as evidence of a smaller threat has made an error of direction.

The acceleration forward's occupation is dynamic. From a wide starting position he holds a full-back, and the full-back's obligation is not merely to mark but to maintain a specific distance and a specific body orientation, which is a much heavier tax than marking. Then, when the ball comes near, the plan demands a second defender to turn a one against one into a two against one, and that defender is usually a central midfielder who was the reason your side had control of the middle of the pitch. So the cost is not one player. The cost is the middle of the pitch, intermittently, at moments you do not choose.

The verdict on this dimension depends on what your side is trying to do with the ball. If you are a low block with no intention of building, losing a midfielder to a wide double-up costs you very little, because that midfielder was not going to be used constructively. If you are trying to control possession, the same double-up is enormously expensive. And the reverse holds for the static occupation of two centre-backs: a possession side that wanted its centre-backs stepping into midfield is heavily taxed by a permanent box occupier, while a deep block that never intended to step barely notices.

### How much does each threat degrade when the space is removed?

This is where the honest disagreement lives. Removing space in behind degrades the depth runner substantially and immediately, but it does not remove him, because his second action operates inside a compressed box where space was never available. Removing space in front degrades the acceleration forward substantially, but it converts him into a different problem rather than a smaller one.

The clean version of the argument says the depth runner is space-dependent and the acceleration forward is space-dependent, so both collapse against a compact block, and therefore the whole comparison is a question of which one collapses further. That version is too clean, and it is where most confident answers go wrong.

Take the depth runner first. Compress the pitch and you remove the run behind, which is the highest-value version of his threat. What remains is a tall, quick forward occupying the space between two centre-backs inside a crowded penalty area, attacking the near post on cutbacks and finishing first time from close range. That residual threat is smaller than the original, but it is precisely the threat a compressed block is worst equipped to handle, because a compressed block concedes wide areas and therefore concedes crosses and cutbacks, which are the delivery types his second action feeds on. So compression trades a low-frequency, high-quality threat for a high-frequency, medium-quality one. Whether that trade is good depends entirely on whether your box defending is any good.

Now the acceleration forward. Compress the pitch and you remove the runway, which is the condition his threat is a function of. What remains is a forward receiving in tight areas with defenders close to him, and in that situation his effectiveness drops more sharply than the depth runner's does, because his primary quality has nowhere to express itself. But the compression also changes the fixture: a side that sits deep against his team invites sustained possession, and sustained possession produces a large number of low-quality attacks in which one defensive lapse restores the runway instantly. The threat has not been removed. It has been converted from a continuous threat into an intermittent one that arrives without warning, and intermittent threats are harder to stay concentrated against for ninety minutes.

So the honest reading of dimension four is this. Compression degrades the acceleration forward more than the depth runner in absolute terms. Compression degrades the acceleration forward less reliably than the depth runner over the course of a full fixture, because his restoration condition is a single error rather than a structural change. Anyone who tells you this dimension has a clean answer has picked a side.

## The resource-cost verdict

The claim this article advances is called the resource-cost verdict, and it can be stated in one sentence. The harder forward to defend is not the better forward but the one who consumes more of the defensive resources your particular team happens to lack.

The value of the claim is that it converts an unanswerable argument into an answerable one. "Who is harder to defend" has no truth value as stated, because it contains no defending team, and a question about defensive difficulty with no defender in it is not a question. "Who is harder for a side with a compact medium block, two centre-backs without recovery pace, and a disciplined holding midfielder" has a truth value, and it can be tested against evidence anyone can gather by watching.

The claim also disciplines the analysis in a useful way. It rules out output as evidence, because output measures the forward and not the cost. It rules out honours and recognition, because those measure the forward and his teams. It rules out direct meetings, because a fixture measures two managers as much as two players. What survives as evidence is the mechanism, the resource each mechanism taxes, and the honest audit of what a specific squad has. Everything in the table above is derived from those three, and nothing in it depends on a number a reader would have to take on trust.

Two objections are worth answering directly, because both are reasonable.

The first says the claim is a dodge, a way of avoiding a verdict by declaring the question relative. It is the opposite of a dodge. The verdict rows in the table are specific, they are falsifiable in the sense that a coach can check them against his own experience, and they commit to an answer in each case. A verdict that names its conditions is stronger than a verdict that hides them, not weaker. What the claim refuses is the single universal answer, and it refuses it because the single universal answer is the part that is false.

The second says that at the very top level the conditions converge, since every elite side defends with a high line and a compact block, so the relative framing dissolves and one answer emerges. That is closer to true than the first objection, and it deserves a real response. Elite sides do converge on line height and compactness, but they do not converge on the two resources that decide this comparison, which are centre-back recovery pace and rest-defence discipline. Those vary enormously between sides that look identical on a formation graphic. The convergence is in the picture, not in the personnel, and the personnel is where the cost is paid.

## The verdict for an organized side with pace

For a side with a coherent defensive structure and genuine recovery pace at centre-back, the acceleration forward is the harder problem, and the deciding factor is that your pace is spent in the wrong phase.

Recovery pace against a depth runner is preventive. It lets you hold a higher line, which shortens the space you are defending, which makes the pass into that space harder to execute and easier to read. The quality is used before the crisis. Recovery pace against an acceleration forward is remedial. It is used after the reception, in a chase, against an opponent who is running in the direction he chose with a start he chose, which is the least valuable use of speed available to a defender. So a side rich in pace gets full value from the quality against one threat and a heavily discounted value against the other.

The plan against the depth runner for this profile is the most comfortable defensive assignment either forward offers. Hold a medium line rather than a deep one, put the first pressure on the passer rather than the striker, and accept the wide reception as the concession. The line height is affordable precisely because your centre-backs can survive being beaten once, and the pressure on the passer is what removes the head-up moment the run depends on. The plan still requires discipline, particularly in the box on cutbacks, but it does not require a quality you do not have.

The plan against the acceleration forward for this profile is harder, and the difficulty is located somewhere counterintuitive: it is in your attacking phase. The reception that hurts you happens after a turnover, and the geometry of the turnover was determined by where your players were standing while you were trying to score. A side with fast defenders often compounds this, because confidence in recovery pace encourages full-backs to push higher and centre-backs to hold a wider split, which is exactly the arrangement that produces a facing-forward reception with grass in front of it.

The specific instruction that changes this fixture is unglamorous. The near-side centre-back holds his position relative to the ball rather than relative to the forward, the far-side full-back tucks in early rather than reacting to the switch, and the holding midfielder positions himself on the ball side of the pitch during your own attacks so that he is a second defender rather than a spectator. None of this is exciting and none of it shows up in a highlight. It is also the difference between a side that concedes twice on the counter and a side that concedes nothing.

### Should a fast defence defend Mbappe with a high line or a low one?

Neither extreme. A very high line hands him the runway his threat is a function of, and a very low line concedes the wide reception and cutback geometry his teams are built around. The workable answer for a fast defence is a medium line with early inside-lane screening and a full-back who delays rather than commits.

The reason the medium line works for this profile and not for others is that the medium line requires the block to move together over ten to fifteen metres repeatedly, which is a fitness and communication demand as much as a tactical one. A side with pace can afford to sit slightly higher within the medium band, which shortens the distance between the block and the press and reduces the number of clean receptions available in the first place. The pace is being used to compress the pitch rather than to chase, which is the only way pace ever pays properly against an acceleration threat.

## The verdict for an organized side without pace

For a well-drilled side whose centre-backs read the play superbly and cannot run, the depth runner is the harder problem, and the deciding factor is that the plan against him requires the single resource this profile lacks.

The reason is structural rather than a matter of individual duels. The plan that contains a depth runner without recovery pace has to remove the pass rather than survive it, which means the defensive burden migrates forward into midfield and into the pressing structure. That is achievable, and good sides achieve it regularly, but it is a plan with a narrow tolerance. The screening midfielder has to deny the vertical lane every time, not most of the time. The forward pressing the first passer has to arrive with the correct body angle to force the ball sideways rather than merely running at him. The line has to hold rather than drift backwards under pressure, which is the hardest instruction in football to sustain, because the instinct of a defender who knows he is slow is to buy insurance by dropping, and dropping is what creates the very reception zone the plan was built to deny.

The residual threat is the second reason. Even when the pass in behind is denied, the depth runner remains inside the box, and a side that has dropped to protect itself has by construction conceded the wide areas that feed cutbacks and low crosses. So this profile ends up defending the version of the threat that a compact block is worst equipped to handle, while having spent all of its structural discipline preventing the version it could see coming.

The acceleration forward is more manageable for this profile than most coaches expect, and the reason is that organisation is precisely the resource his threat taxes. Compactness removes the runway. Early screening of the inside lane removes the facing-forward reception in the most dangerous zone. A full-back who has been taught to delay on the outside shoulder without committing, with a midfielder positioned to make the second arrival possible, turns a large proportion of his receptions into carries toward the touchline, which is the harmless direction. None of those tools requires speed. They require rehearsal, communication, and the discipline to not dive in, and a well-organised slow defence usually has all three.

The counterintuitive practical advice for this profile is to spend the training week on the fixture against the depth runner rather than the one against the carrier, which is the opposite of what most staffs do because the carrier looks more frightening. The carrier's plan is a plan this side already half owns. The runner's plan is the one that has to be built.

### Can a slow defence realistically contain a depth-running striker?

Yes, but only by moving the defensive burden forward. The pass has to be denied at the source rather than survived at the line, which means the screening midfielder and the pressing forward carry the plan. The back line's job becomes holding its position under pressure rather than winning a race it cannot win.

That reframing changes who gets coached during the week. In most sides the preparation for a fast striker is delivered to the centre-backs, which is close to useless when the centre-backs are not the ones who can affect the outcome. The players who decide this fixture are the number six and whichever forward is nearest the ball when the opponent's centre-back looks up. Coaching them, and then rehearsing the moment when they both fail so that the line knows what to do next, is the entire plan.

## The verdict for a disorganized side at any level

For a side that cannot reliably hold a shape, whether that is a struggling professional team or a Sunday league side with one training session a week, the acceleration forward is the harder problem by a clear margin, and the deciding factor is that disorganisation is his restoration condition.

This is the verdict that matters most, because it applies to more teams than the other two combined and because it is the one almost never given. Analysis written for elite conditions assumes the block exists. Below a certain level the block does not exist, and the whole calculation changes.

A depth runner needs a specific pass, delivered at a specific moment, into a specific space, by a passer who has been allowed to look up. Every one of those conditions is a link in a chain, and a disorganised defence still breaks the chain frequently by accident, because the disorganisation is symmetrical: the line is untidy, but so is the timing that would punish it, and a mis-timed run is an offside. The threat is real and it is dangerous, but it arrives in identifiable phases and it can be survived for stretches without anything the defence would recognise as competence.

An acceleration forward needs one thing: a facing-forward reception with space in front. A disorganised defence produces that condition several times per half without being pressured into it, through a full-back caught upfield, a midfielder on the wrong side of the ball, a centre-back stepping without cover, or simply a clearance given away in a bad area. His restoration condition is a single error, and a disorganised side generates single errors continuously. So the threat that requires a chain is intermittently survivable and the threat that requires one broken link is not.

The practical instruction for this profile is to simplify aggressively. Drop the line, protect the inside channel with a midfielder who is instructed to do nothing else, tell the full-back to show the carrier toward the touchline and never to dive in, and accept giving the ball away rather than losing it in dangerous areas. That plan concedes territory and possession happily, and it is the correct plan, because the alternative is not a better plan but the same plan executed with more ambition and more holes. The [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/) sets out the general version of this simplification for each threat type, and it is the right starting point for a coach who has to prepare for a forward this article does not cover.

## What each threat does to each of your players

A comparison that stops at the team level is not yet usable, because plans are delivered to individuals. Below is the position-by-position audit of what each threat costs, written from inside the duel.

The near-side centre-back faces the heaviest asymmetry. Against the depth runner his problem is a decision made before the ball moves: hold the line and trust the offside, or track the shoulder and concede the line. The mechanism that makes this hard is that the run is keyed to the passer, not to the defender, so the defender is always reacting to a cue he cannot see while marking a man he can. The technical answer is to take his primary reference from the ball and his secondary reference from the runner, which is the reverse of the instinct. Against the acceleration forward his problem is different and arrives later: whether to step out to the reception or hold the central space. Stepping without cover is the single most common way this fixture is lost, and the correct default is to hold unless a midfielder has already arrived to screen behind.

The full-back's asymmetry runs the other way. Against the depth runner his job is largely about the cutback: recovering to the near-post space, or covering it when the centre-back is dragged out. Against the acceleration forward he is the plan. His starting distance, his body orientation, and his refusal to commit are the difference between a carry toward the touchline and a carry into the box. The technical detail worth rehearsing is the outside shoulder: showing the carrier toward the line rather than inside, at a distance close enough to prevent the free carry and far enough to survive the first touch past him. That distance is not a constant. It shortens as the carrier's speed drops and lengthens as it rises, which is why the duel cannot be reduced to a rule and has to be practised.

The holding midfielder is the most under-coached player in both fixtures. Against the depth runner his job is to deny the vertical lane and to press the passer's supply rather than the passer himself, which removes the head-up moment before it exists. Against the acceleration forward his job is positional discipline during his own side's possession, so that when the ball is lost he is already on the correct side to make the second arrival possible. Neither job is visible. Both decide the outcome.

The goalkeeper's job splits cleanly. Against the depth runner the decisive choice is his starting position, made before the sequence begins, because the first-time finish leaves no time to adjust. Against the acceleration forward the decisive choice is whether to leave the line during the carry, and the answer is usually no, since a carrier at speed beats an advancing goalkeeper more often than he beats a goalkeeper who has set himself and narrowed the near angle. In both cases the goalkeeper's contribution is made earlier than the crowd thinks.

The wide forward on the defensive side is the player nobody prepares. Against the acceleration forward, a wide forward who fails to track a full-back's overlap creates the exact two-against-one that turns the duel into an unwinnable one, and no defensive coaching downstream repairs it. Against the depth runner his contribution is the first press on the supply, and a lazy pressing angle from the wide forward is what allows a centre-back to look up in comfort. The largest available improvement in both fixtures, for most sides at most levels, sits with the players furthest from their own goal.

## The aerial and near-post dimension, treated fairly

The comparison is usually run on the ground and then a sentence about heading is added at the end, which understates how much the aerial question changes a defensive plan.

The depth runner's aerial threat is not principally about winning contested headers, which is how it is usually discussed. It is about arriving into a space at the near post from a low cross or a cutback, where the header or the first-time finish is uncontested because nobody was assigned to the space. That is a spacing problem, not a duel problem, and the players who solve it are the far centre-back and the recovering full-back rather than whoever is nominally marking. A side that responds by putting its strongest header of the ball tight to him has answered the wrong question and has usually made the situation worse, because a tight marker gets dragged and the space he vacates is the space the delivery is aimed at.

The acceleration forward's aerial contribution is smaller and differently shaped. His arrivals into the box more often come from carries and from runs across the face of a defender rather than into a vacated area, and the finishing that follows is usually with the feet. The defensive consequence is that the box assignment against him is closer to a conventional marking job with a cover behind, while the box assignment against the depth runner is a zone-and-space job with the marking almost secondary.

The set-piece addendum runs the same way and is worth stating plainly, because it is where a comparison of open-play threats can quietly mislead a coach into an incomplete plan. A tall forward who attacks a space is a dead-ball problem that requires an assignment, a blocker-aware first contact, and a rehearsed second-phase arrangement. A forward whose threat is acceleration is a dead-ball problem of a different kind: he is the one who stays high while your side attacks, which is a counter-attack problem disguised as a set-piece decision, and the arithmetic of how many players you keep back is a decision you make before the corner is taken rather than after it is cleared.

Neither of these dimensions changes the top-line verdict, but both change the shape of the week. Against the depth runner, more of your box work is spacing and assignment rehearsal. Against the acceleration forward, more of it is rest-defence arithmetic and the discipline of leaving players back when your side is chasing the fixture.

## Chance quality and the difficulty of the finishes you are conceding

There is a second-order question hiding underneath the comparison, and it explains a lot of the disagreement between people who watch closely. The two threats do not merely produce different numbers of chances. They produce chances of different kinds, and the kind determines what your defensive plan is even trying to achieve.

The depth runner's chances tend to be simple in execution and difficult to prevent. A first-time finish from close range after a run in behind is not a technically demanding action for an elite forward, and treating it as one leads to the mistake of hoping he misses. The defensive plan therefore has to be preventive, because the conversion assumption has to be pessimistic. This is why the plan against him has such narrow error tolerance and why the passer-first principle carries so much weight: once the sequence has begun, there is no realistic defensive resource left to deploy.

The acceleration forward's chances tend to be harder in execution and more preventable in structure. A shot taken at the end of a carry, from a narrowing angle, with a recovering defender in the frame, is a genuinely difficult finish, which is precisely why he has to be elite to convert them at the rate he does. The defensive implication is that the plan against him can afford to be probabilistic rather than preventive. Forcing the carry toward the touchline, delaying it by half a second, and making the second defender's arrival possible do not eliminate the chance; they degrade it. Against this threat, degrading chances is a legitimate objective. Against the depth runner, it is not, because there is very little to degrade between the pass and the finish.

That difference has a direct consequence for how a coach should judge his own team's performance afterwards. Against the runner, review the fixture on whether the pass was available, not on whether the finish went in. Against the carrier, review it on the quality of the chances conceded, not on their number. Reviewing either fixture on the scoreline tells you almost nothing, and reviewing both by the same standard guarantees you learn the wrong lesson from one of them.

## The formation question: back four or back three

Coaches ask which defensive arrangement suits each fixture, and the honest answer is that the arrangement matters less than what it lets you do with the middle of the pitch, but there is a real asymmetry worth naming.

Against the depth runner, a back three has a genuine structural advantage that is often misattributed. The advantage is not the extra body against one forward, which is close to irrelevant, since the second centre-back was already occupied by the near-post space. The advantage is that a back three lets the wide centre-backs step out to press a receiving forward without leaving a two-against-one behind, which supports the real plan, and it gives the middle centre-back a permanent occupation of the space the runner attacks. Against that, a back three often produces higher wing-backs, which is exactly the arrangement that produces the space in behind on the transition. So the shape helps the plan and hurts the phase in which the plan is most likely to be tested, and the net effect depends on whether your wing-backs are disciplined.

Against the acceleration forward, a back four with a genuinely committed holding midfielder is usually the more robust arrangement, because the fixture is decided by the availability of a second defender in the inside channel rather than by the number of centre-backs. A back three can work, and works well when the wide centre-back is comfortable defending in space and the wing-back ahead of him understands that his defensive obligation begins during his own team's attack. It fails badly when the wing-back is an attacker in a defensive shirt, because a wing-back caught high is the cleanest possible route to a facing-forward reception with grass ahead.

The general rule the two cases share is that the formation is a container, not a plan. A side that changes shape without changing the instruction has changed nothing, and a side that keeps its shape and changes the instruction has usually done everything necessary.

## The comparison at youth and amateur level

Almost all analysis of these two threats is written for elite conditions, and almost all of the coaches reading it work somewhere else. The verdict changes below the top level in a way that is both clear and rarely stated.

At amateur and youth level, the depth-running threat is substantially less dangerous than its elite version, and the reason is supply. The threat depends on a pass that is difficult to execute: weighted, timed to a run that has already begun, and played by a passer with his head up under some degree of pressure. Below a certain standard of passing, that ball is played late, played short, or played into the goalkeeper's hands a large proportion of the time. The run is still a problem, and the offside line is still stressed, but the chain is broken frequently by the attacking side's own limitations rather than by the defence.

The acceleration threat does not degrade in the same way, because it does not depend on a difficult pass. It depends on a facing-forward reception with space ahead, and at amateur level that condition is manufactured constantly by turnovers, poor clearances, full-backs caught upfield, and midfielders on the wrong side of the ball. The quickest player on a Sunday league pitch, receiving in space, is close to unanswerable by individual defending, and the only real solutions are collective and unglamorous.

The practical instruction for the amateur coach follows directly. If you have one session a week and a fast forward is coming, spend it on two things and nothing else: the full-back's outside-shoulder delay, which is a technique that can be taught in twenty minutes and rehearsed in a small area, and the rule that one midfielder stays central and goal-side whenever your side commits players forward. Neither requires fitness, talent, or numbers. Both remove the majority of the condition the threat depends on.

If a tall runner is coming, spend the session on the pressing angle of the nearest forward and on the line holding its position under pressure, and accept that the defensive line will be beaten sometimes and that the goalkeeper's starting position matters more than usual. Do not respond by dropping the line to the edge of your own box, which is the standard amateur reflex and which converts a manageable problem into a permanent siege in the area where you are least equipped to defend.

### How much does amateur passing quality change the depth-running threat?

A great deal. The run is unchanged, but the delivery it depends on is one of the harder passes in football, and below a certain standard it arrives late, short, or straight to the goalkeeper often enough that the chain breaks by itself. The carrier's condition needs no such pass.

## How to identify which threat you are facing in twenty minutes

The comparison is only useful if a coach or analyst can classify a forward quickly, since most sides do not face these two specifically and do face somebody who behaves like one of them.

Watch the reception first and ignore the goals entirely. If the forward's dangerous receptions happen behind or on the last line, with his first action being a finish rather than a carry, you are facing the runner profile, and the plan should be built around the supply and the head-up moment. If the dangerous receptions happen in front of the line with his first action being a carry, you are facing the carrier profile, and the plan should be built around reception geometry and the second defender.

Then check occupation. Count how many defenders remain committed to him during a passage in which he does not touch the ball. Two continuously is the box occupier's signature and is the reason his touch counts mislead people. One, with a second joining only when the ball approaches, is the carrier's signature.

Then check what happens after the block compresses. Watch a period in which the opponent is defending deep and organised. If the forward keeps producing near-post arrivals and first-time attempts from cutbacks, the residual threat is a box threat and your box work is the priority. If he disappears for long stretches and then reappears the moment a turnover occurs in a bad area, the residual threat is a restoration threat and your rest defence is the priority.

Those three checks take one viewing and they produce a plan direction rather than a description. They also generalise, which is the point: the taxonomy in the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/) sorts every forward you are likely to face into a small number of threat types, and the classification is the first decision in any counter-plan worth delivering.

## Which defender profile suits each fixture

Selection is where the comparison becomes a team-sheet decision, and the two threats reward almost opposite profiles, which is why a squad built to handle one is rarely built to handle the other.

The depth runner rewards a centre-back who defends space rather than a man: comfortable turning and running, willing to hold a line under pressure without dropping, and disciplined enough to take his primary cue from the ball rather than from the forward. The quality that matters most is recovery over the first fifteen metres from a standing or backpedalling start, which is a different athletic property from top speed and is far more coachable in terms of the starting position that reduces the distance required. The quality that matters least is upper-body strength, which is why the traditional response of selecting the most physical available centre-back so often produces a worse outcome than selecting a slightly weaker defender who reads the passer.

The acceleration forward rewards a full-back who can delay without committing and a centre-back who is comfortable covering the inside channel diagonally at speed. The full-back's key attribute is not pace either, which surprises people, though pace helps. It is the ability to retreat at an angle while staying square enough to react to either direction, which is a technical and postural skill, and the discipline to accept being beaten on the outside as a good outcome. A quick full-back who dives in loses this duel more often than a slower one who delays properly, and that single fact is why the reflex to select for speed against a fast forward is such a poor plan on its own.

For a coach with a squad rather than a wish list, the useful version of this is a substitution and rotation question. If your best defensive unit against one threat is different from your best unit against the other, name both units in advance and rehearse both, rather than discovering the mismatch on the day. And be honest about the case where the profiles collide: a defender who is excellent in one fixture and exposed in the other is not a bad defender, and treating him as one is how sides lose useful players for the wrong reason.

## In-match adjustments and what to change at half time

Both plans have predictable failure signals, and the value of naming them in advance is that the adjustment becomes a decision made calmly during the week rather than a reaction at speed.

Against the depth runner, the signal that the plan is failing is not a goal. It is the frequency with which the opponent's centre-backs or deep midfielders are getting their heads up unpressured. If that is happening repeatedly, the line height is irrelevant, because the ball is going to arrive eventually and the only variable left is how much space it arrives into. The adjustment is to move the pressing responsibility rather than the line: change who presses the first passer, change the angle he presses from, or add a body to the supply lane, and leave the back line where it is. The instinctive adjustment, which is to drop the line, treats the symptom and worsens the underlying problem for the reasons the fourth dimension explains.

The second signal is a run pattern that keeps arriving between the centre-backs rather than around them. That is a spacing issue between the two of them rather than a pace issue, and the adjustment is a distance instruction, not a substitution.

Against the acceleration forward, the failure signal is the location of your turnovers, not the number of them. Losing the ball repeatedly in the opponent's half with your full-backs high is the signal, and it is visible long before it produces a chance. The adjustment is to your own attacking arrangement: hold one full-back, position the holding midfielder on the ball side, and instruct the forwards to close the immediate counter rather than to press the whole line. That is an in-possession adjustment made to solve an out-of-possession problem, which is why staffs so often miss it.

The second signal is the full-back being beaten on the inside rather than the outside. Beaten on the outside is the plan working. Beaten on the inside means the body orientation has drifted or the screening midfielder has stopped arriving, and the fix is a reminder rather than a change.

Both plans also have an endgame version worth deciding in advance. If you are protecting a lead, the depth runner's fixture becomes more dangerous, because a side chasing the fixture will push players forward and the space behind grows regardless of your intentions. If you are chasing, the acceleration forward's fixture becomes more dangerous for the mirror reason, and the discipline of leaving two players back while committing everybody else is the single decision that decides how the last twenty minutes go.

### What is the biggest in-match mistake teams make against either forward?

Dropping the defensive line in response to the first scare. Against the depth runner it hands him the box territory his second action feeds on. Against the acceleration forward it invites the sustained possession from which a single error restores his runway. In both fixtures the correct first adjustment is upstream of the back line.

## What the comparison looks like from the attacking side

A defensive comparison is incomplete without the mirror, because the mirror explains why each threat is built the way it is and therefore why the defensive plans have to concede what they concede.

A side built around a depth runner is built to create one specific moment, and everything else in the structure exists to manufacture it: the width that stretches a back line, the deep pass-first midfielder who can execute the delivery, the wide players whose cutbacks feed the near-post arrival. The consequence for a defence is that the threat is heavily signposted and heavily rehearsed on both sides. You know what is coming, and so does he, and the fixture becomes a contest about whether your denial is better rehearsed than his supply.

A side built around an acceleration forward is built to preserve a condition rather than to manufacture a moment. It wants turnovers in usable areas, it wants him receiving with his body already open, and it is willing to concede possession to get there. The consequence for a defence is that the threat is far less signposted, and the fixture becomes a contest about your own discipline in a phase you are not thinking about, which is your attacking one.

That mirror is the deepest reason the verdicts land where they do. One threat asks whether your defensive plan is well built. The other asks whether your whole team is well organised, including when it has the ball. Very few sides fail the first test and pass the second, and a good number of sides pass the first and fail the second, which is why the acceleration threat ends up harder for more teams overall even though the depth runner is harder for a specific and identifiable profile.

## What each plan surrenders, stated plainly

Every counter-plan is a trade, and a plan whose cost has never been said out loud gets abandoned the first time the cost appears on the pitch. Both of these plans are expensive, and the expense is paid in different currencies.

The plan against the depth runner surrenders possession and wide territory. Pressure on the supply means committing players forward at moments when your side would otherwise be compact, which leaves the flanks open and hands the opponent an easy sideways outlet. A crowd watching this sees a team that cannot get the ball, and a coach watching it sees a team removing the only pass that actually hurts them. The second surrender is more uncomfortable: the plan accepts that the striker will occupy two of your defenders permanently, and it declines to solve that, because solving it would require a third defender and would concede the middle of the pitch. Accepting a permanent local overload against you, in order to avoid a worse one elsewhere, is a decision that has to be made in the meeting rather than in the seventieth minute.

The plan against the acceleration forward surrenders the ball in front of the block and a large volume of sterile opponent possession. It also surrenders ambition in your own attacking phase, because the rest-defence arrangement that protects you costs a full-back and a midfielder who would otherwise be joining the attack. That is the harder surrender to hold, because it costs you goals at the other end and the cost is invisible while it is being paid. Sides abandon it when they go behind, which is precisely when abandoning it is most dangerous.

There is a third surrender common to both, and it is the one coaches concede last. Both plans require accepting that the forward will have moments, and that a moment is not evidence that the plan has failed. A depth runner who gets in behind once in ninety minutes against a plan built to allow that once has not beaten the plan. A carrier who produces two dangerous carries from twenty receptions has been contained by the definition that matters. Judging either plan by whether the forward was invisible guarantees you abandon a working plan halfway through, which is how most sides actually lose these fixtures: not to the forward, but to their own reaction to him.

Naming all three surrenders in advance converts them from evidence of failure into confirmations that the plan is running. That is a communication task rather than a tactical one, and it is worth as much as any instruction in this article.

## The arguments people actually have, answered

Six positions circulate constantly whenever this comparison comes up. Each is stated here in its strongest form and then answered with mechanism, because a comparison article that ignores the arguments its readers arrived with has not engaged with the topic.

### "One of them is a system product and the other is not"

The strong version of this position is not the insult it usually becomes. It says that one forward's threat requires a specific supply structure to exist, while the other's threat can be generated from a broken situation with almost no structural support, and that a threat which requires a structure is easier to defend because you can attack the structure instead of the forward.

The mechanism check confirms half of it and reverses the other half. Both threats require structure. The depth runner requires a passer who can execute a specific pass at a specific moment, plus teammates whose positioning creates the gap he runs into. The acceleration forward requires a turnover in a usable area, plus teammates whose positioning ensures the reception is facing forward rather than facing his own goal. Neither generates the condition alone. What differs is which structure is easier for a defence to attack, and there the answer is genuinely asymmetric: it is easier to deny a specific pass from a specific player than it is to deny every turnover in a full fixture. So the position has a real insight buried inside it, and it points in the direction of the depth runner being the more attackable threat at the level of supply, which is the same conclusion the third dimension reaches from a different route.

Where the position fails is in the inference that a structurally supported threat is a lesser player. That inference is unrelated to defensive difficulty and belongs to the ranking argument this article does not run.

### "Transition threat matters more than box threat"

This position says that modern defending is mostly about transitions, that box defending has become a solved problem for well-drilled sides, and that therefore the transition threat is inherently harder to plan against.

It is right about the first clause and wrong about the second. Transitions are where a disproportionate share of high-quality chances originate, and a threat that specialises in them is attacking the phase in which defensive structures are least organised by definition. But box defending is not solved. It is the phase in which the most sides are weakest relative to how much they believe they are competent at it, and the reason is that box defending is a marking-and-spacing problem that requires five or six players to be simultaneously correct, which is far harder to rehearse than a pressing trigger. A side that has never lost a training session to defending cutbacks will find that its box defending is exactly as good as it has been coached to be.

The verdict: transition threat is harder for the average professional side, box threat is harder for the average amateur side, and both statements are consistent with everything else here.

### "A low block handles one but not the other"

This is the sharpest of the six because it is nearly a technical claim rather than an opinion. The position holds that dropping deep removes the runway, so a low block solves the acceleration forward, while a low block feeds the box occupier and therefore fails against the depth runner.

The first half is broadly correct with an important caveat. The low block does remove the runway and does reduce the acceleration forward's most dangerous version, but it converts him rather than removing him, and the converted version arrives unpredictably off a single error. A low block that concedes sixty minutes of possession will produce several such errors, and the block's protection is therefore a function of concentration rather than geometry.

The second half is correct without caveat and is the least understood point in the whole comparison. A low block against a depth runner removes the pass in behind and simultaneously hands him the exact territory where his second action is strongest, while conceding the wide areas that generate the deliveries that action feeds on. That trade is bad for most sides, and the plan set out in the [counter blueprint against Haaland](/2020/01/15/how-to-stop-erling-haaland/) argues for a controlled medium line with aggressive ball pressure for exactly this reason, while naming the squad conditions under which the deep line genuinely is the better choice.

### "Pace beats power at the top level"

The position says that at elite level everybody is strong, so a physical advantage is neutralised, while a speed advantage is not, and therefore the acceleration threat is the more durable one.

The framing is wrong in a specific way. Neither threat is a power threat or a speed threat. The depth runner's advantage is not strength but timing, and the timing is what a defence has to defeat. Treating him as a power forward produces the standard failed plan, which is to put a strong centre-back tight to him and contest physically, when the situation is decided several seconds earlier and thirty metres away. Likewise the acceleration forward's advantage is not raw speed but the fact that he chooses the moment and the direction, which is why an equally fast defender still loses the duel often enough to make a pace matchup a poor plan on its own.

Once both threats are restated correctly, the position dissolves. The durable question is not power against pace. It is timing against runway, and a defence answers those two with different resources.

### "Head-to-head results settle it"

They do not, and this deserves a plain answer rather than a diplomatic one. A direct fixture between two sides is a sample of one, played under conditions chosen by two managers with objectives that may have nothing to do with either forward. A forward can be invisible in a fixture because his side needed him to hold two defenders while somebody else exploited the space, which is the threat functioning exactly as designed. A forward can score in a fixture against a plan so poor that the goal carries no information about how hard he is to defend. Neither outcome tells you what the plan costs.

The evidence that does carry signal is repeatable: what the threat requires to exist, which resource denies it, and how many sides possess that resource. That evidence is available to anyone who watches with the right questions, and it does not depend on results at all.

### "The question is meaningless"

The final position is the most respectable of the six. It says that defensive difficulty is so dependent on context that no comparison is possible and the whole exercise is entertainment.

The premise is correct and the conclusion does not follow. Context dependence does not make a question meaningless. It makes the question incomplete, and incomplete questions are completed rather than abandoned. Add the defending team and the question becomes answerable, testable, and useful. The three verdict rows of the table are the completed versions, and a coach can check any of them against his own experience within one fixture. What is genuinely meaningless is the uncompleted version, and the people who hold this position are usually reacting to that, correctly, and then throwing away more than they need to.

## Turning the comparison into preparation

The comparison earns its keep in the training week, and the way it does so is by telling you which week to plan.

The first practical use is diagnostic. Before either fixture, audit your own side against the four dimensions rather than against the forward. Ask whether your centre-backs can survive being beaten once. Ask whether your block can compress without dropping. Ask whether your rest defence exists as a rehearsed arrangement or as a hope. Ask which of your players is going to be exposed if the first line of the plan fails. Those four answers determine which of the two fixtures is your harder fixture, and the determination will be stable across the season rather than changing with the opponent.

The second use is prioritisation. Almost every staff spends its preparation on the more famous threat rather than the more dangerous one for its own side, which is how a well-organised slow team ends up rehearsing pressing traps against a carrier when it should have spent three sessions denying a single vertical pass. Prioritisation by resource cost fixes that, and it is free.

The third use is honesty about concessions. Both plans concede something, and a plan whose concession has not been named out loud will be abandoned by the players the first time the concession appears. Against the depth runner you concede possession and wide territory. Against the acceleration forward you concede the ball in front of the block and a large amount of sterile opponent possession. Say both out loud in the meeting, so that when the crowd groans at a sideways pass conceded on purpose, the players know it was the plan rather than a failure. A staff that wants to keep those plans, the audits behind them, and the session notes that follow in one organised place can [build your opposition plan free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/football-match-planner.html) and keep the dossier alongside the clips it came from.

### How do you decide which of the two fixtures to prepare for first?

Prepare first for the threat that attacks the resource you lack, not the one with the larger reputation. If your centre-backs cannot recover, the depth runner's fixture needs the extra session. If your shape dissolves during your own attacks, the acceleration forward's fixture does. The audit takes twenty minutes and reorders the whole week.

## The closing verdict

Neither forward is universally harder to defend, and any article that tells you otherwise has quietly picked a defending team on your behalf without saying which one.

What is true universally is the split. One of these threats is defeated by cutting supply, which is a task that lives in midfield and in the pressing structure and is largely decided before the ball ever reaches the last third. The other is defeated by refusing to commit, which is a task that lives in the back line and in rest defence and is largely decided by discipline in your own attacking phase. Very few sides at any level are genuinely good at both, because the two disciplines are trained differently, coached to different players, and rewarded differently by the crowd. So the harder forward, for you, is whichever one attacks the discipline your side has not built.

For a side with pace and structure, the acceleration forward is the harder fixture, because your best resource is spent chasing rather than preventing. For an organised side without pace, the depth runner is the harder fixture, because the plan against him requires the one thing you cannot buy on a Thursday. For a side that cannot hold its shape, the acceleration forward is harder by a clear margin, because his restoration condition is a single error and you supply those continuously. Those three verdicts are the answer, and the deciding factor is named in each.

The transferable skill, and the reason this comparison is worth the length, is the conversion itself. An argument with no defending team in it cannot be settled. Put the defending team in, define difficulty as resource cost, and the same argument becomes a planning exercise with a verdict at the end of it. That method works on any pair of famous opponents, and it is set out in general form in the guide to [building a match plan against a superstar](/2032/02/15/match-plan-against-a-superstar/), which is where a reader should go next if the pair they actually face on Saturday is not this one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is Haaland or Mbappe harder to defend?

There is no single answer, and the reason is that defensive difficulty is a property of the pairing rather than of the forward. For an organised side with genuine recovery pace at centre-back, Mbappe is the harder fixture, because your best defensive resource is spent chasing rather than preventing. For an organised side without pace, Haaland is harder, because the plan against a depth runner requires holding a line you cannot hold. For a side that cannot keep its shape at all, Mbappe is harder by a clear margin, because his threat is restored by a single positional error and disorganisation supplies those continuously. Name your own profile first and the question answers itself.

### Q: Who is more dangerous in transition, Haaland or Mbappe?

Mbappe, and the gap is not close, because transition is the phase his threat is a function of. A turnover produces the exact condition he needs, which is a reception facing forward with space ahead and a defence that has not yet reorganised. Haaland is dangerous in transition too, but his version requires a teammate to execute a specific pass into a specific space at a specific moment, which is a chain with more links to break. The practical consequence for a defence is that the transition plan against Mbappe belongs in the attacking phase, in the form of rest defence and the positioning of a holding midfielder, while the transition plan against Haaland belongs with whoever is nearest the ball at the moment of the turnover.

### Q: Is Haaland or Mbappe harder for a low block to handle?

Haaland, which surprises coaches who assume a deep line solves every fast forward. A low block removes the runway that Mbappe's threat depends on and converts him into an intermittent problem restored by errors. Against Haaland the same block removes the run in behind and simultaneously concedes the territory where his second action is strongest, because a compressed block gives up wide areas and therefore gives up the cutbacks and low crosses that feed his near-post arrivals. The trade is a rare high-quality chance exchanged for frequent medium-quality ones, and whether that trade is good depends entirely on how well your side defends inside its own box, which most sides overrate.

### Q: Do you need a different defensive shape for Haaland than for Mbappe?

You need a different instruction more than a different shape, but the shapes do diverge. Against Haaland the workable arrangement is a controlled medium line with aggressive pressure on the passer, because the plan is to remove the head-up moment rather than to survive the pass. Against Mbappe the workable arrangement is a compact block with the inside channel screened early and a full-back instructed to delay rather than commit. A side that changes formation without changing those instructions has changed nothing, and a side that keeps its formation and changes the instructions has usually done everything that was required.

### Q: Which defender profile suits Haaland and which suits Mbappe?

Against Haaland the fit is a centre-back who defends space rather than a man: comfortable turning and running, able to hold a line under pressure, and disciplined enough to take his cue from the ball rather than from the striker. Physical strength matters far less than coaches assume, and selecting the most physical available defender frequently makes the situation worse by encouraging a tight mark that gets dragged. Against Mbappe the fit is a full-back who can retreat at an angle while staying square, who accepts being beaten on the outside as a good outcome, and who never dives in, supported by a centre-back comfortable covering the inside channel diagonally at speed.

### Q: Who scores the more difficult chances, Haaland or Mbappe?

Mbappe, in the technical sense, because a shot taken at the end of a carry from a narrowing angle with a recovering defender in the frame is a harder action than a first-time finish from close range. That is not a criticism of Haaland; it is a description of two different chance profiles, and the easier finish is the one that is harder to prevent. The defensive implication is the useful part. Against Mbappe you can degrade a chance and count that as a success, because the difficulty of the finish is doing defensive work for you. Against Haaland degradation is barely available between the pass and the shot, so the plan has to be preventive.

### Q: Does Haaland or Mbappe punish a high line more?

Both punish it, through different mechanisms, and the one that punishes yours more depends on where your line is beaten. Haaland punishes a high line whose reference is wrong: if defenders watch the runner instead of the passer, the timing beats them regardless of how quick they are. Mbappe punishes a high line whose distances are wrong: if the block is high but stretched, he receives facing forward with runway, and no recovery pace reliably closes that. A high line held with correct references and short distances is defensible against both. A high line held out of habit is punished by both, which is why the line height itself is rarely the real issue.

### Q: Is Mbappe harder to mark one on one than Haaland?

Yes, but the framing hides the more useful point, which is that neither fixture should be approached as a marking job. Marking Mbappe one against one is a losing proposition for almost any defender, because he selects the moment and the direction, and the duel is decided by whether a second defender can arrive rather than by the first defender's quality. Marking Haaland tightly is actively counterproductive, since a tight marker is easier to drag and the space he vacates is where the delivery is aimed. The workable versions are delay plus cover for one and space ownership plus supply denial for the other.

### Q: Is Haaland or Mbappe more dependent on the system around him?

Both depend on structure, and the honest difference lies in which structure is easier to attack. Haaland's threat requires a passer capable of a specific delivery and teammates whose positioning creates the gap, which is a chain a defence can attack at the source. Mbappe's threat requires a turnover in a usable area and teammates who ensure the reception is facing forward, which is a condition a defence must deny continuously rather than at a single point. Framed as a defensive question rather than as a ranking, that asymmetry means the supply against Haaland is the more attackable of the two, which is a tactical observation and not a judgment on either player.

### Q: Which of Haaland or Mbappe is tougher for an amateur defence?

Mbappe, and the reason is supply quality rather than anything about the forwards. Haaland's threat depends on a difficult pass, weighted and timed to a run already in motion, and below a certain standard that ball is played late, short, or straight to the goalkeeper a large share of the time. Mbappe's threat depends only on a facing-forward reception with space in front, and amateur football manufactures that condition several times per half through poor clearances, full-backs caught upfield, and midfielders on the wrong side of the ball. The fix is collective and unglamorous: one midfielder stays central, and the full-back delays instead of diving in.

### Q: Would you rather face Haaland or Mbappe in a cup tie?

Take the fixture that attacks the resource you have rather than the one you lack, and in a single tie that calculation shifts slightly toward facing Haaland if your side can defend its box in numbers, because his threat requires a chain of conditions that a well-rehearsed plan can break repeatedly across ninety minutes. Facing Mbappe in a single tie is harder for most sides, because a tie usually includes a period when you must chase, and chasing produces exactly the turnovers and stretched distances his threat feeds on. The endgame arithmetic, meaning how many players you leave back while pushing, decides more of that tie than anything decided beforehand.

### Q: Who is stronger in the air, Haaland or Mbappe?

Haaland is the greater aerial threat, and his height and leap make that unsurprising, but the defensive point is that his aerial danger is mostly not about winning contested headers. It is about arriving unmarked into a space, usually at the near post, from a cutback or a low cross, where the header or first-time finish is uncontested because the space was unassigned. That makes it a spacing problem rather than a duel problem, solved by the far centre-back and the recovering full-back rather than by whoever is nominally marking him. Mbappe's box arrivals more often come from carries and runs across a defender, finished with the feet.

### Q: Does Haaland or Mbappe punish a defensive mistake faster?

Haaland, measured from the mistake to the shot. Once the pass is released into the space behind, the run is already in motion and the finish is first time, so the interval between the error and the attempt is close to the minimum available in football and no recovery resource arrives in time. Mbappe's punishment takes longer in seconds but the extra time is not usable, because the defence spends it running backwards at a speed disadvantage. The practical distinction is that one error against Haaland is usually terminal, while one error against Mbappe is survivable if a second defender was already positioned to arrive, which is a rest-defence decision made earlier.

### Q: Can the same defender cope with both Haaland and Mbappe?

Rarely as an individual, and the question is better asked about the unit. The qualities that suit one fixture are close to the opposite of the qualities that suit the other, so a squad that handles both usually does it with different personnel or different instructions rather than with one defender who covers everything. The useful response for a coach is to name both defensive units in advance and rehearse both, rather than discovering the mismatch on the day. A defender who is excellent in one of these fixtures and exposed in the other is not a poor defender, and treating him as one is how sides discard useful players for the wrong reason.

### Q: Would you plan against Haaland or Mbappe first?

Plan first for the threat that attacks the resource you lack, which is almost never the threat with the larger reputation in your dressing room. Audit your own side against four questions: can your centre-backs survive being beaten once, can your block compress without dropping, does your rest defence exist as a rehearsed arrangement rather than a hope, and who is exposed when the first line of the plan fails. If the answers point at recovery pace, prepare the Haaland fixture first. If they point at shape during your own attacks, prepare the Mbappe fixture first. The audit takes twenty minutes and reorders the entire week.

### Q: Is Haaland or Mbappe the harder matchup for a back three?

Mbappe, in most versions of a back three, and the cause is usually the wing-backs rather than the defenders. A back three helps against Haaland because a wide centre-back can step out without leaving a two against one behind, and the middle defender can permanently occupy the space the run attacks. The same shape tends to push wing-backs high, which is the cleanest route to the facing-forward reception with grass ahead that Mbappe's threat requires. A back three defends him well only when the wing-back treats his defensive obligation as something that begins during his own team's attack rather than after the ball is lost.
