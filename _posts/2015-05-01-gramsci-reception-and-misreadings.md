Antonio Gramsci died in a guarded Roman clinic in April 1937, leaving behind thirty-three prison notebooks that almost nobody had read. Within half a century his name stood behind one of the most used and least agreed concepts in the human sciences, and readers on five continents were citing him for propositions he would have found strange, in disciplines he never entered, on the strength of editions he never saw. The puzzle of his afterlife is not simply that he was interpreted in many ways. Every major thinker is interpreted in many ways. The puzzle is that the interpretations contradict one another on elementary points: on whether his central concept names consent or coercion, on whether he trusted parliaments or despised them, on whether he was an economist who strayed into culture or a cultural theorist who abandoned economics, on whether he planned a conquest of the institutions or warned that no such conquest could be planned in the abstract. Contradictions of that size, among readers who are neither careless nor dishonest, usually have a material cause. In this case the cause can be dated, shelved and catalogued. Different readers met different books.

The word that travels furthest illustrates the problem. Hegemony appears in a media studies textbook as a name for the manipulation of audiences by owners of newspapers and channels. It appears in an international relations journal as a name for the preponderance of a single great power over an order of states. It appears in a work of political theory as a name for leadership built on negotiated consent, secured by real concessions and always open to withdrawal. It appears in a conspiracy narrative as a name for a secret plan to rot a civilisation from inside its schools. Four usages, one word, and at most one of them close to the sense the notebooks give it. A reader who moves between those literatures without a map will conclude either that Gramsci was incoherent or that his interpreters are frauds. Neither conclusion is needed. Each usage descends from the notebooks by a traceable route, through a specific edition, a specific translation, a specific discipline's needs and a specific decade's politics, and the divergences accumulated along the routes the way copying errors accumulate in a manuscript tradition. This article maps the routes. Its claim, named here because the whole argument hangs on it, is the transmission test: the Gramsci a reader has met is predicted by the route through which they met him, and asking which edition, which discipline and which decade an account descends from resolves most of the apparent contradictions between accounts of what he said.

Seven portraits organise the map. There is the national-popular Gramsci constructed by the Italian Communist Party's postwar edition, the party prisoner presented as the theorist of an Italian road. There is the Eurocommunist Gramsci of the nineteen seventies, whose war of position was read as a warrant for a parliamentary strategy. There is the cultural studies Gramsci built in Britain, the analyst of popular common sense and political realignment through whom most English-speaking students meet the concept of hegemony. There is the post-Marxist Gramsci of the mid nineteen eighties, detached from any necessary anchoring in class. There is the subaltern studies Gramsci of South Asian historiography, the theorist of history written from below. There is the international relations Gramsci, whose borrowed vocabulary named the architecture of world order and then slid toward naming mere dominance. And there is the philological Gramsci, restored since the critical edition by scholars who read the notebooks in their original sequence and ask what the fragments, dated and layered, actually contain. Each portrait had reasons. Each was drawn from a partial corpus under specific pressures, and each will be treated here as an interpretation with a history rather than as a crime against the text. The corrections come later, and they are reserved for claims the notebooks demonstrably contradict.

## A manuscript nobody could read whole

Every reception history begins with the state of the evidence, and the state of this evidence explains more of the sequel than any interpreter's ideology. The Prison Notebooks were not a book. They were a working archive accumulated between 1929 and 1935 in cells at Turi and in guarded clinics, written in exercise books supplied under prison rules, in a compressed hand, under a censorship that read what it allowed to exist. Gramsci wrote without his library, from memory and from the journals the censor passed, and he wrote in a deliberately oblique vocabulary at the points where a direct word would have stopped the pen: the philosophy of praxis for Marxism, the founder for its originator, a Russian revolutionary under his adopted shorthand, the popular manual for the era's authorised textbook of the doctrine. He revised as he went, copying passages from earlier notebooks into later ones and reworking them there, so that hundreds of fragments exist in two or three states, like drafts pinned at different stages of a long argument he never assembled. When the writing stopped, stopped by the collapse of his body rather than by completion, there was no order of reading, no table of contents, no final arrangement. There were notebooks, numbered by other hands, and a mind's workshop preserved entire.

The physical survival of the workshop was itself an accident managed by devoted people. His sister-in-law Tatiana Schucht, who visited, corresponded and understood what the pages were, safeguarded them as they left the prison system; the economist Piero Sraffa, his friend from the Turin years, opened the account at the Milan bank through which the notebooks passed to safety and eventually to Moscow, where the party's archive held them through the war. The point of recalling the chain is not pathos. It is that the text of Gramsci reached print at all through the custody of a political organisation, at a moment when that organisation had urgent uses for a founding martyr and a national theorist, and that the first editions were therefore also acts of custody. What the Italian public received between 1947 and 1951 was a selection arranged by other minds into shapes the author had never given it. The readers of the nineteen fifties did not meet the notebooks. They met an edition, and they met it in good faith, unaware that the meeting had been staged.

A second consequence followed from the fragment form, and it operates still. A finished treatise disciplines its interpreters: it states proportions, answers objections, ranks its own claims. Fragments invite completion, and every generation of readers has completed them in the direction of its own questions. The interpreter who finds a notebook passage on schools will make an educational theorist; the one who finds the notes on American industry will make an economist of Fordism; the strategist will find the military metaphors and the historian the Risorgimento. None of these is inventing material. The material for at least seven distinct Gramscis genuinely sits in the corpus, which is precisely why adjudicating between portraits by quotation alone settles so little, and why the prior question of transmission, of what had actually reached a given reader and in what arrangement, does so much work. The thinker's own biography, told in the opening article of this cluster, explains where the concepts were formed; the present article explains why readers who all cite the same name so often describe different men.

## The first Gramsci and the party that published him

The construction began with an act of mourning and restitution. In 1947 the publisher Einaudi issued the prison letters, and the volume won a national literary prize and fixed the public image of the author before a line of the theory could be assessed: the suffering prisoner, writing to his family with irony, tenderness and an unbroken intellectual appetite, became Italy's exhibit of what fascism had done to its finest minds. Then, between 1948 and 1951, came the notebooks themselves, in six thematic volumes prepared under the direction of the Communist Party's leadership, with the editorial labour performed chiefly by Felice Platone. The volumes sorted the fragments by subject: historical materialism and the philosophy of Benedetto Croce first, then the intellectuals, then the Risorgimento, then the notes on Machiavelli and the modern state, then literature and national life, then a miscellany of past and present. Each volume read like a treatise by a systematic author. The notebooks' order, their repetitions, their layers of drafting vanished behind a clean topical architecture, and the Gramsci who emerged was coherent, civic, national in horizon, in dialogue with the great idealist Croce as an equal heir of Italian culture, and pointing toward a democratic road to socialism that his party, under Palmiro Togliatti, was in those very years proclaiming as its own.

Nothing in that construction required bad faith, and the record does not support an accusation of it. The editors worked with the standards of their time, in a country where the party had to argue, against a hostile state and a vigilant church, that communists were the legitimate heirs of the national culture rather than agents of a foreign power. A Gramsci presented as the continuator of the Risorgimento's unfinished business and as Croce's Marxist counterpart served that argument because it was also, in large part, true: the notebooks really do sustain a profound engagement with Italian history and with Croce, and the national-popular really is one of their organising ideas. What the edition did was narrower and more consequential than falsification. It selected, and it arranged, and arrangement is an interpretation that does not announce itself as one. Fragments written in 1930 and reworked in 1934 stood side by side as simultaneous statements. The sharper polemics against the party orthodoxies of his own camp, the notes in which the young council communist of Turin still speaks, the pages of linguistic and literary analysis that fit no political brief, all receded. And one document was simply absent: the long letter of October 1926 in which the newly arrested leader had warned the Soviet party against its internal methods and pleaded for unity, a letter Togliatti had received, judged undeliverable and kept from the record, which reached print only decades later and complicated, on arrival, the portrait of a founder in effortless harmony with the movement he led.

### Why did the first readers meet Gramsci in thematic volumes?

Thematic volumes were the practical choice of editors who held a disorderly archive and a public waiting for a thinker. Sorting fragments by subject produced readable books from unnumbered notebooks, matched the publishing habits of the time, and let a party introduce its dead leader as the systematic theorist of themes it needed discussed: the nation, intellectuals, the state.

The distinction matters for everything that follows. The party edition was a construction, but it was a construction from real materials by men who had known the author, and its Gramsci was a possible Gramsci, one emphasis among several the corpus supports. Where later correction is justified is not against the editors' honesty but against the downstream illusion, shared by friend and foe alike, that the thematic volumes simply were the notebooks, so that whoever quoted the volumes quoted the man. The illusion held for a generation because no alternative text existed against which to check it. A scholar in 1960 who wished to contest the party's Gramsci had to contest him with the party's own pages. That dependency, more than any doctrine, is what the phrase transmission names, and it is why the history of this thinker's reputation is inseparable from the history of his editions, a dependence the companion guide to the notebooks sets out in full in [the reader's guide to the Prison Notebooks](/2015/02/01/prison-notebooks-readers-guide/).

It also explains the tone of the early literature, which later readers often misjudge. The first commentaries were written inside a party culture that treated the notebooks as a legacy to be applied, and they argue about Gramsci the way councils argue about a founder's intentions, with strategy always in the room. When the Italian debates of the nineteen fifties asked whether he had theorised a distinct Italian road, or whether his concept of the intellectual blessed the party's alliance policy toward Catholic and lay culture, they were not distorting an otherwise pure scholarship. They were doing the only thing the transmission route made available: developing a political inheritance whose textual form had already been chosen to be developable in those directions. The academic Gramsci, the one footnoted in universities, grew out of that party Gramsci as out of a chrysalis, and carried its emphases, above all the national-popular framing and the Croce dialogue, into seminar rooms that imagined themselves far from the party's concerns.

## What the arrangement did to the arguments

An edition that rearranges is not a neutral container, and three specific deformations flowed from the thematic form. The first was the erasure of development. Gramsci's thinking moved: the early prison notes breathe the air of the defeated council movement and of a sterner Leninism, while the later notebooks, drafted after 1932, complicate the account of the state, deepen the analysis of consent and absorb hard lessons about the resilience of the order that had defeated him. Presented by subject, those movements disappear, and passages from different strata acquire a false simultaneity, so that a reader could build a doctrine by juxtaposing fragments the author himself had revised away from. The second deformation was the erasure of the censor. The oblique vocabulary reads, in a smooth thematic text, as a stylistic preference, and generations of readers took the philosophy of praxis for a coinage about emphasis rather than what it also was, a password at a checkpoint. The third deformation was generic: set out as continuous essays with editorial titles, the fragments looked like conclusions, and a workshop of hypotheses hardened into a system with the author's name on it, a system he had explicitly declined, in a note on method, to pretend he possessed.

### Did the party edition hide a different Gramsci?

It withheld rather than hid. One letter stayed unpublished for decades, fragments uncongenial to the postwar line appeared late or in reduced form, and the arrangement pressed a workshop into a system. But the volumes printed most of the theory most readers still use, and the fuller text, once available, enlarged the portrait more than it overturned it.

The correction, when it came, therefore had a particular shape. It did not reveal a secret author behind the public one. It revealed strata, hesitations and second thoughts, and it revealed how much of what had been debated as Gramsci's settled doctrine was the sediment of an editorial decision taken in Turin and Rome between 1947 and 1951. Scholars who lived through both texts describe the experience as meeting a thinker twice: the first time as a monument, the second as a mind at work. The monument was not a lie. It was a selection with the selection invisible, which is the most effective kind, and the invisibility, not the selection, is what the transmission test exists to detect in any portrait, friendly or hostile, scholarly or journalistic, that a reader may meet.

## The letters and the face of the prisoner

Before the theorist there was the correspondent, and the sequence left a permanent mark on the reputation. The prison letters, published in 1947, presented a man writing to his wife, his children, his mother and above all to Tatiana, in a voice of extraordinary composure: asking after a child's schooling, describing a plant on a windowsill, requesting books, managing his own despair with jokes at its expense. Italy, emerging from fascism and civil war, took the book to its heart, and the literary prize it received certified that the communist prisoner belonged to the nation and not only to his party. That reception was genuine and honourable, and it also fixed a hierarchy in the public mind that scholarship has struggled against ever since: the letters, being readable, human and short, became the canonical Gramsci for the general reader, while the notebooks, being fragmentary and hard, became a quarry from which professionals extracted concepts. A thinker whose most-read text is his correspondence will be remembered as a witness first and an analyst second, and a good deal of the soft Gramsci of popular memory, the saint of the cell whose politics melt into his suffering, descends from that order of publication rather than from anything in the theory itself.

The martyrology had a further consequence that specialists feel in their bones. Because the face was known before the arguments, later portraits could trade on the face: the authority of the prisoner who wrote under censorship licensed interpreters to present conjectures as the prisoner's silenced truths. Claims about what Gramsci would have said, had he been free to say it, circulate in every reception stage and are unfalsifiable in proportion to their pathos. The transmission test is partly a defence against this licence. It insists that a portrait be graded by the paper trail it rests on: which letters, which fragments, in whose arrangement, reaching print in which year. A claim that cannot name its route may still be moving, but it has surrendered its claim to be scholarship, and the reader is entitled to treat it as devotion instead.

## A party theorist in a cold war

Through the nineteen fifties and sixties the notebooks were worked, in Italy, as the theoretical capital of a mass party navigating a polarised republic. The questions put to the text were the party's questions. Did the analysis of the Risorgimento as a revolution manqué, a transformation accomplished from above without the people, license the strategy of progressive democracy, the claim that the party should lead a broad national reconstruction rather than await a collapse of the state? Did the theory of intellectuals sanctify the courtship of teachers, technicians, Catholic intellectuals and the whole mediating stratum of Italian society? Did the war of position describe, avant la lettre, the long institutional labour the party was in fact performing in municipalities, unions and cooperatives? Reading the debates of those years, in the party's journals and at its study conferences, one watches a classic being manufactured in real time: the fragmentary author is asked for warrants, and the fragments, arranged topically and therefore conveniently, supply them. The 1958 conference on Gramsci studies in Rome, which gathered scholars inside and outside the party, marks the moment the custodianship became partly academic; the arguments did not become less strategic, but they acquired footnotes, and footnotes, as this article will keep insisting, are where transmission becomes visible.

Two developments inside that period matter for the later map. The first was the gradual publication of the pre-prison writings: the journalism of the war years, the Ordine Nuovo articles of the council period, the Southern Question essay of 1926, left unfinished at his arrest. These texts complicated the monument in a productive direction, restoring the young organiser whom the notebooks' civic serenity had half concealed, and giving the party's historians a revolutionary youth to set beside the prison sage. The second was the opening of a genuine scholarly quarrel about the architecture of the theory, conducted in the journals of the Italian left, over whether hegemony named a strategy for a class still seeking power or a description of how bourgeois rule already worked, and over the place of the economic in a theory accused, by admirers and enemies alike, of dissolving production into culture. That accusation, as the later sections of this article show, says more about which notebooks each side was reading than about the corpus as a whole.

## Bobbio's question and the location of civil society

The most consequential single intervention of the Italian period came from outside the party, from the liberal socialist philosopher Norberto Bobbio, in a paper presented at the Cagliari conference of 1967 and argued over for a decade afterwards. Bobbio observed that in the classical Marxist schema civil society belongs to the base, to the structure of material relations, whereas in Gramsci the concept sits among the superstructures, alongside the state, as one of the two floors on which hegemony is exercised. The observation was technical and explosive at once. If civil society is where consent is organised, then the decisive terrain of politics is institutional and cultural rather than immediately economic, and a Marxism that follows Gramsci has quietly reversed the causal architecture it professes. Bobbio pressed the point without hostility; he admired the thinker and doubted the orthodoxy of the inheritance. The replies, from Jacques Texier, from Christine Buci-Glucksmann and from a generation of Italian scholars, refined the question into the form in which students still meet it: whether the relocation of civil society is a revision of Marx, a restoration of an Hegelian strand within him, or a correction forced by the observation of how modern states actually hold together.

### Why did Bobbio's reading provoke a decade of argument?

Because a footnote about categories carried a strategic charge. If consent is organised in the superstructures and decides outcomes there, then cultural and institutional work is not decoration on an economic struggle but the struggle's main theatre, and every party, union and school had to reconsider where its real contest lay.

For the reception history, Bobbio's intervention matters as the hinge between the party Gramsci and the academic one. After it, the notebooks could be studied as a theoretical system in their own right, argued with by philosophers who owed the party nothing, and the questions changed from warrants for a line to problems in a doctrine: the boundary between state and civil society, the status of the economy, the coherence of a concept of hegemony that seemed, in different fragments, to widen or narrow its grip. It also exported the argument. The French debate of the nineteen seventies, in which Buci-Glucksmann's Gramsci and the State proposed the notebooks as the foundation for a theory of the capitalist state, and in which Althusser's school regarded the same texts with a suspicion bordering on dismissal, was fought with weapons forged in the Italian discussion, and it seeded the divergent national receptions that a later section surveys.

## The Eurocommunist Gramsci

In the nineteen seventies the portrait acquired a flag. Eurocommunism, the name given to the attempt by the Italian, Spanish and, more briefly, French communist parties to define a road to socialism compatible with pluralist democracy and independent of Moscow, needed ancestors, and it found in the notebooks the most distinguished ancestor available. The war of position, Gramsci's figure for the long siege of a state buttressed by the trenches of civil society, was reread as the theory of that road: advance through institutions, win hegemony in society before and rather than storming the state, accept the terrain of democracy as the terrain of transformation. Enrico Berlinguer's strategy of the historic compromise, announced after the Chilean coup of 1973 destroyed a left government that had won an election and lost the society around it, drew openly on this reading; Santiago Carrillo's Eurocommunism and the State gave it systematic form in 1977, citing the notebooks against the very idea of a dictatorship of the proletariat. For a few years the Eurocommunist Gramsci was the most politically weighty of all the portraits, debated by governments and intelligence services as well as by scholars, and the thinker's face appeared on the covers of news magazines as the posthumous theorist of a possible Western communism.

As a reading it had real textual footing, which is why it persuaded serious people. The notebooks do insist that in societies with a developed civil society the frontal assault fails, that hegemony must precede and undergird any durable change of power, and that the coercive seizure of a state whose social defences stand intact wins a Winter Palace and loses a country. What the Eurocommunist reading did was to convert a diagnosis about the structure of modern power into a complete strategy with an endpoint the text does not supply. The war of position in the notebooks is a war: it presupposes an antagonist whose hegemony is to be displaced by a counter-hegemony built to the point where a different order becomes possible, and the military metaphor keeps the moment of confrontation inside the concept even where the metaphor's owner judges the moment distant. Reread as electoralism, as the patient accumulation of votes and offices within an order whose legitimacy is conceded in advance, the figure lost its antagonist and became a counsel of indefinite postponement, which is why, when Eurocommunism itself collapsed with the parties that carried it, its Gramsci was buried alongside them and had to be dug out again by readers with other questions. The concept as the notebooks state it, the concessions it demands and the leadership it describes, is set out in [the complete guide to hegemony](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/), and the distance between that concept and its electoral afterlife is one of the cleanest illustrations the transmission test can offer.

### What did the Eurocommunists need Gramsci to certify?

They needed a founding authority for a road their parties were already travelling: socialism through democratic institutions, independence from the Soviet model, alliance across the old confessional divide. The notebooks supplied the vocabulary of hegemony and position, and his standing as a martyr of fascism supplied the legitimacy that living leaders could not manufacture for themselves.

A quieter consequence of the Eurocommunist season deserves recording, because it shaped the texts through which the rest of the world was then reading. The political prominence of the notebooks raised the market and the urgency for translations and for scholarship outside Italy, and it fixed, in the international mind, an association between Gramsci and a moderate, parliament-compatible Marxism that the Anglophone cultural left would shortly inherit and transform. When the British readers of the nineteen seventies took him up, they took up a thinker already framed by Rome: the theorist of consent against the theorist of the coup, the analyst of culture against the economist of crisis. The frame was not false, but it was a frame, chosen in one capital's argument and carried, largely unexamined, into other countries' very different arguments.

## The long wait for an English Gramsci

The English-speaking world met the prisoner late, partially and out of order, and the lateness is a material fact with intellectual consequences. A slim volume of the political writings, The Modern Prince and Other Writings, appeared in Louis Marks's translation in 1957 and sank almost without trace; the letters followed in Lynne Lawner's translation in 1973. The event that founded the Anglophone reception was the Selections from the Prison Notebooks of 1971, edited and translated by Quintin Hoare and Geoffrey Nowell Smith, a single large volume drawn overwhelmingly from the notes on politics, the state and intellectuals, organised by its editors into a coherent treatise and furnished with an introduction that presented its author as the supreme theorist of the superstructures. For most English readers, for two decades, this selection was Gramsci. Seminar syllabuses, the citations of sociologists and historians, the vocabulary of the New Left Review and of the cultural studies then being born, all drew on its seven hundred pages, and its emphases became the thinker's emphases in English: Machiavelli and the party, the national-popular, the intellectuals, the Risorgimento, the American notes. What the selection left in Italian was as consequential as what it carried across: most of the notes on literature and language, the folklore studies, the Dante canto analysis, the journalism, and the connective tissue of hesitation and revision that makes the notebooks a workshop rather than a doctrine.

### Why did English readers wait so long?

Because the corpus had first to be published in Italian, under a party's custody, in a form its custodians found usable, and because no English publisher would risk a fragmentary Marxist classic until the politics of the nineteen sixties created the audience. By the time the selection arrived, the Italian debate about it was twenty years old.

The delay had one effect that Anglophone scholarship rarely notices in itself. English readers encountered the notebooks after the Eurocommunist framing and alongside the French controversies, so the questions they brought were second-generation questions: not what the fragments meant in the wreckage of Italian fascism, but what the concept of hegemony could do for an analysis of British decline, American media or the crisis of social democracy. A reception that begins with applications produces a different literature from one that begins with exegesis, more inventive and less patient, and the English Gramsci literature is exactly that: brilliant deployments of a partially read author, followed, decades later, by a philological correction arriving from the same Italian scholarship the deployers had skipped. The sequence repeats, in miniature, the whole history this article recounts, which is why the transmission test treats the year and language of an encounter as evidence of the first rank.

## Translation as an invisible editor

Even where the same fragments were available, the words on the page differed, and some of the most durable misreadings are translation artefacts wearing the disguise of doctrine. The 1971 translators worked honourably and well, but every rendering they chose quietly pre-decided an argument. The pair senso comune and buon senso, which in the notebooks mark the difference between the incoherent folklore a person inherits and the critical good sense buried inside it, flatten in English toward a single phrase about common sense, and with the flattening goes the notebooks' claim that popular consciousness is contradictory, containing the material of its own correction. The distinction mattered enormously to the cultural studies readers who built on exactly that claim, and the best of them, working with Italian-reading colleagues, knew it; the textbook tradition that followed them often did not. Elsewhere the rendering of the stato integrale as the integral state carried across a claim about the fusion of coercion and consent that weaker paraphrases dissolved into a remark about big government. And the decision, standard in English, to translate the subalterni straightforwardly as the subaltern classes or groups preserved a word whose deliberate vagueness in Italian, part censor's password and part analytical wager, was itself an argument about how dominated groups are to be named before they can name themselves.

### Which writings did the first English selection leave out?

Most of the cultural notebooks: the notes on language and linguistics, on folklore and popular literature, on the serial novel and the theatre, the long reading of a canto of Dante, and the journalism of the Turin years. English readers accordingly met a political theorist, where the corpus holds a student of language who became one.

The companion volume that repaired part of the omission, the Selections from Cultural Writings of 1985 edited by David Forgacs and Geoffrey Nowell Smith, arrived fourteen years after the political selection and into a landscape already furnished. By then the cultural studies Gramsci had been built from the political volume plus essays and lectures, and the newly available materials, on language, on the national-popular in literature, on Americanism's cultural machinery, confirmed the builders in their design rather than revising it. Transmission history has a ratchet: the first text to arrive defines the questions, and later texts are read as evidence for positions the first text made thinkable. Readers who doubt the ratchet should compare the Gramsci of an English syllabus built before the cultural selections with the Gramsci of one built after, and notice that the second is not a corrected version of the first but a different distribution of the same man's attention.

## The Birmingham Gramsci

The most creative of all the receptions, and the route by which the largest number of living readers have met the name, was built at the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies at Birmingham and in the work of Stuart Hall. The centre, founded under Richard Hoggart and directed by Hall from the end of the nineteen sixties, needed a Marxism that could take popular culture seriously without either celebrating it as resistance or dismissing it as narcotic, and the notebooks, in the Hoare and Nowell Smith selection, supplied it: a theory in which consent is real, organised and contestable, in which the popular classes are neither dupes nor heroes, and in which the analyst's task is the concrete study of a conjuncture, the specific balance of forces at a moment. Hall and his colleagues applied the apparatus with a fidelity to its spirit that still rewards reading. Policing the Crisis, the centre's collective study of 1978, traced how a moral panic about mugging was assembled into consent for an authoritarian turn in British policing and law. Hall's essay of 1979 on the great moving right show named the Thatcher project an authoritarian populism and read it, in strictly Gramscian grammar, as a hegemonic project: a bid to reconstruct common sense itself, conducted through the language of nation, family and freedom, and aimed at detaching popular grievances from their old Labourist moorings. The analysis was contestable and contested; what matters for the map is that it was recognisably the notebooks' method, conjunctural, concession-counting and hostile to every shortcut that treats ideology as a syringe.

### How did Stuart Hall put hegemony to work?

As an instrument for reading a moment rather than as a label for a system. Hall tracked how a political project assembled consent across press, parliament and popular grievance, counted the concessions it paid and the enemies it named, and asked where the settlement could crack, which is the notebooks' own procedure carried into British materials.

Two features of the Birmingham reception deserve emphasis because later textbook versions dropped both. The first is that Hall's Gramsci imposed obligations: if hegemony is consent organised through institutions, then analysing it means studying institutions one by one, their histories and their internal contradictions, and the Birmingham literature at its best does exactly that archival and ethnographic labour. The second is that Hall kept the economy inside the frame. His conjunctures are moments of capitalist crisis, his Thatcherism an answer to the exhaustion of the postwar settlement's economics as much as to its culture, and the charge that the cultural studies Gramsci floats free of production fits the epigones better than the founder. What the epigones kept was the vocabulary; what they dropped was the procedure. By the time hegemony entered the media studies textbook as a standard term, it had often shrunk to a synonym for the influence of media owners over audiences, a usage that inverts the notebooks at the decisive point, since for the prisoner consent was precisely what could not be reduced to the effects of messages on minds. [Gramsci and modern political strategy](/2015/04/01/gramsci-and-modern-politics/) examines the further career of the concept in strategic argument; the present concern is only with how a method became a label.

## The textbook flattening

The flattening deserves its own section because it is the misreading with the widest circulation and the thinnest textual basis. In the flattened version, hegemony names a kind of soft domination in which the ruling ideas seep from media and schools into passive heads, securing a false consciousness that a critical education can dispel. Every element of that sentence reverses a notebook claim. Consent, in the notebooks, is active: it is organised through institutions people inhabit for reasons of their own, and it must be continuously renewed, which is why hegemonic orders are busy, anxious and expensive rather than serene. Concessions are constitutive, not decorative: a leadership that concedes nothing real, in the prisoner's analysis, rules by domination and forfeits the name of hegemony altogether. And the dominated are not empty vessels; their common sense is a stratified deposit in which a critical nucleus, the good sense that knows where its interests lie, survives inside the inherited folklore, which is why education in the notebooks is a drawing-out conducted with respect rather than a filling of voids. The scholarly correction of the flattened version was made early and well: Abercrombie, Hill and Turner's study of the dominant ideology thesis argued in 1980 that subordinate classes have rarely been shown to absorb the ideologies imputed to them, and that capitalist order rests far less on shared beliefs than the textbook story supposes. The correction is, in substance, a return to the notebooks from their summary.

### Why do textbooks turn hegemony into manipulation?

Because manipulation is teachable in a paragraph and consent is not. A syllabus needs a portable definition, and the claim that media owners bend audiences fits the space a lecture allows. The negotiated concept, with its concessions, institutions and contradictory common sense, demands the slow work the format cannot schedule.

Why did the flattened version survive its correction? Partly because it flatters its users: the analyst who sees through the manipulation stands outside the machine, exempt from the common sense he diagnoses in others, and the notebooks deny precisely that exemption, insisting that the intellectual too is formed inside the deposit he studies. Partly because the flattening serves a pedagogy of suspicion in which unmasking counts as understanding, and partly because the first English text, read quickly, can be made to support it by readers who take every remark on newspapers and schools as a remark about propaganda. The transmission test catches the case cleanly: ask of a manipulation-centred account which fragments it cites, and the answer is characteristically a handful of pages about the press and about intellectuals, read without the companion claims about concessions and activity that the same notebooks state with equal emphasis a few gatherings away.

## Anderson's antinomies

The most rigorous internal audit of the concept came from within the Marxist tradition itself, in Perry Anderson's long essay on the antinomies of Antonio Gramsci, published in New Left Review across 1976 and 1977. Anderson, who had done as much as anyone to introduce continental Marxism to English readers, turned on the notebooks an exacting philological attention and reported that the central terms would not sit still. Hegemony, across the fragments, slides between definitions: at times it names consent as opposed to coercion, at times the union of both in a single supremacy, at times a cultural leadership exercised before power and at times the whole mode of rule of a class in power. The boundary between state and civil society moves with it, so that the same institutions, schools, churches, associations, are assigned now to one side of the line and now absorbed into an enlarged state. Anderson's verdict was double. The oscillations were real, and they were the record of a thinker wrestling, in fragments he could not assemble, with a genuine problem no predecessor had solved: how consent and force combine in the durable orders of the West. The essay remains the standing caution of the reception, the demonstration that the incoherence later readers project onto the interpreters begins, in part, in the workshop itself.

For the map, Anderson's intervention performed a sorting that still governs serious work. After the antinomies, a responsible account of hegemony must declare which of the notebook senses it is using and defend the choice against the fragments that point the other way; an irresponsible one can still quote the word in its warmest sense and move on. The sorting also marks the limit of the transmission test, and honesty requires the limit to be stated here rather than in a footnote. Transmission explains why readers diverge; it does not by itself decide which reading is right, and where the corpus genuinely underdetermines a question, as Anderson showed it does at several joints, the map of routes must give way to argument about the text. The later sections return to that boundary, because the strongest objection to this entire article stands exactly there.

## The post-Marxist Gramsci

In 1985 Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe published Hegemony and Socialist Strategy, and the reception acquired its most elegant and most contested portrait. Their book began, honourably, as an archaeology: tracing how the concept of hegemony had entered Marxist thought as a patch for the failures of economic determinism, first in Russian social democracy, then in the prisoner's notebooks, it argued that Gramsci had carried the logic of articulation, the joining of elements with no necessary belonging, further than his own framework allowed him to admit. Where the notebooks still anchored the hegemonic subject in a fundamental class whose position in production unified its politics in advance, Laclau and Mouffe cut the anchor. In their reconstruction, social identities are formed in discourse itself, a people is constructed rather than found, and the chain of equivalences among democratic demands, feminist, ecological, anti-racist, working-class, need acknowledge no hierarchy and no pre-given centre. The prisoner was thus honoured as the thinker who opened the door and gently criticised for not walking through it, and the resulting theory, radical and plural democracy, carried his central word into a post-Marxist universe he would not have recognised as his own.

The scholarly row that followed was instructive in transmission terms. Defenders of the classical reading, Norman Geras most formidably, replied that a hegemony without material anchoring dissolves the very constraints that made the concept analytic: if any identity can articulate with any other, the theory predicts every outcome and explains none, and the concessions condition, the demand that leadership pay in real goods, evaporates into a rhetoric of linkage. Laclau and Mouffe's answer, that the material is itself inside discourse rather than outside it, moved the dispute onto philosophical ground where the notebooks, genuinely ambiguous at this joint, could be cited by both sides. The fuller treatment of the detachment from class belongs to [the guide to post-Marxism](/2022/03/01/post-marxism-explained/); what the map requires is the route. The post-Marxist Gramsci descends from a reading of the notebooks, in French and English selections, through the Althusserian theory of ideology that preceded it, at the moment when the Western left, its parties collapsing, went looking for a theory of politics without a guaranteed subject. The portrait is brilliant, consequential and, by the test of the concessions condition and the class anchoring that the notebooks restate in fragment after fragment, a departure that its authors, to their credit, mostly conceded.

## The subaltern route to South Asia

The most geographically distant reception began in 1982 with the first volume of Subaltern Studies, a collective project of historians working on South Asia under the editorship of Ranajit Guha. Guha's opening manifesto, a critique of the historiography of colonial India, announced the borrowing in its title's key word. The colonial archive, he argued, and the nationalist archive that answered it, shared an elitism: both wrote the history of the nation as the biography of its leaders, and the politics of the people, the autonomous domain of subaltern action, appeared in each only as noise, as spontaneity, as crime. Against both, the collective took from the notebooks the category of the subaltern and the project of the fragmentary notes on Italian history in which the prisoner had sketched a method: trace the history of subaltern groups through the scattered evidence of their initiatives, their defeats and the record kept by their rulers, knowing that such a history arrives broken or not at all. The fit was uncanny and openly acknowledged. The prisoner's own subject, a peasant south dominated by a north that despised it, mapped onto the colony with a precision no metropolitan theorist could have planned, and Gramsci became, in Indian historiography, a working instrument rather than a citation of piety.

### Why did Indian historians seize on the subaltern notes?

Because the notes offered a method for their exact predicament: archives kept by rulers, a national story told by elites, and a popular politics that surfaced as disturbance in other men's records. The southern question had trained the prisoner on colonial relations inside one country, and the category travelled with its method intact.

The Indian career of the category then outgrew its charter in ways the reception historian must record without partisanship. The collective's later volumes turned from the recovery of subaltern consciousness toward the critique of the categories of recovery themselves; Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, in a famous essay of the late nineteen eighties, asked whether the subaltern, and specifically the subaltern woman, could speak at all within archives and theories that claimed to restore her voice, and the question split the inheritance between those who kept faith with Guha's recuperative project and those who followed the turn to discourse. Meanwhile the word escaped the discipline entirely and entered the general vocabulary of the humanities as a loose synonym for the oppressed, a flattening as thorough as the media textbook's, against which the philologists protest in vain. The longer history of Marxist thought on the subcontinent, within which this borrowing sits, is traced in [the complete guide to Marxism in India](/2017/12/01/marxism-in-india-complete-guide/), and the comparison is instructive: on the subaltern route, a category coined in a cell to outwit a censor became the charter of a historiographical school and then a common noun, three existences for one word, each predictable from its route.

## The Gramsci of world order

International relations took the prisoner up at almost the same moment, by an independent path. In two essays of the early nineteen eighties, the Welsh scholar Robert Cox proposed that the discipline's theory of world order be rebuilt on historical and material foundations, and found in the notebooks the conceptual kit: hegemony redefined as a structure of world order in which material capabilities, ideas and institutions fit together into a configuration that presents itself as universal and elicits the consent of the governed states and classes; the historic bloc transposed to the scale of a world; the distinction between hegemonic and merely dominant orders. Cox's famous opening sentence in the 1981 essay, that theory is always for someone and for some purpose, could stand as the motto of the whole appropriation: the notebooks were conscripted against a discipline that treated its categories as timeless. A school formed around the borrowing, the neo-Gramscians of international political economy, Stephen Gill and James Mittelman among its builders, produced a substantial literature on globalisation, on the discipline of transnational capital and on the counter-hegemonic possibilities of social forces organised across borders. It is a serious literature, and its Gramsci is in several respects a faithful one: consent is constitutive for Cox, an order maintained by coercion alone is expressly not hegemonic in his usage, and the material base is never dissolved into ideas.

### Is the international relations hegemony the same concept?

In the school's founding texts, largely yes, transposed to world scale with consent and institutions intact. In the discipline's wider usage, no. The word there had an older career naming the preponderance of a leading state, and the borrowed term kept sliding back toward that meaning in the mouths of readers who never opened the notebooks.

## How hegemony shrank to dominance

The slide is worth anatomising because it shows a misreading being manufactured by a discipline rather than by an editor. International relations had used the vocabulary of hegemony before Cox, in the hegemonic stability literature associated with Charles Kindleberger and Robert Gilpin, where it named the preponderance of a single power able and willing to underwrite an open world economy: leadership as market share and gunboats, consent incidental. When the neo-Gramscian term entered the journals, it entered a house where the word was already furnished with that meaning, and readers who met the new literature casually, or met summaries of it, assimilated the prisoner to the chair that was waiting. Textbooks completed the work, defining international hegemony as the dominance of one state over others and appending the Italian's name as the concept's venerable source, an attribution that would have astonished him, since the condition his analysis names is precisely the one in which dominance has been transcended into leadership that others accept as their own. The correction is available in the school itself: Cox and his successors repeatedly insist that a coercive order is domination and not hegemony, and that the confusion voids their argument. But the insistence travels more slowly than the label, and the label now does duty, in thousands of pages, for the very concept its source defined by exclusion. A reader equipped with the transmission test will ask, of any use of the word in this discipline, which of the two ancestors it descends from, and will find that the question sorts the literature with almost mechanical reliability.

## Other ports of entry

Italy, Britain, India and the international relations journals do not exhaust the map, and a compressed survey of the other routes shows the same law operating under different flags. France received the notebooks through its own party's selections in the nineteen fifties and read them in the shadow of a domestic argument: Althusser's school, dominant in Parisian Marxism, treated the prisoner with respect and suspicion in about equal measure, valuing the analysis of ideology while charging his humanism and historicism with theoretical softness, so that the French Gramsci long appeared as a brilliant predecessor to be surpassed rather than a system to be inhabited; Buci-Glucksmann's Gramsci and the State of 1975 broke from that verdict and founded a French literature of the integral state, while the party's own Eurocommunist season briefly raised the stakes before the whole field subsided into the academy. Germany met the prison writings in translations sponsored in part by the student movement's publishers, and the German Gramsci consequently arrived wearing the colours of 1968, a theorist of cultural revolution read by a generation in revolt against its fathers, before settling into the custody of careful editors. Latin America produced one of the most original receptions anywhere: the Argentine group around the journal Pasado y Presente, with José Aricó as its deepest reader, translated the notebooks early and argued, in Aricó's Marx and Latin America, that the prisoner's method, his attention to the national-popular and to the peculiarities of societies formed by incomplete revolutions, fitted the continent better than the orthodoxies imported from Europe; in Brazil, Carlos Nelson Coutinho built a parallel case for a Gramscian reading of a society modernised repeatedly from above. In each country the same corpus yielded a different thinker, and in each case the difference is legible from the route: the edition translated, the party or movement that sponsored it, the domestic argument it was conscripted to settle.

## The critical edition changes the ground

In 1975 the ground under every portrait shifted, when Einaudi published the critical edition of the notebooks edited by Valentino Gerratana. The four volumes printed the notebooks in their original order, all of them, with the layers of drafting distinguished: the first versions of passages, the texts copied and reworked in later notebooks, the translations the prisoner had made as exercises, all set out with an apparatus that let a reader watch the thought being made. The effect on the scholarship was comparable to the effect of a critical edition in biblical or classical studies, and the comparison is exact: doctrines that had rested on juxtapositions dissolved when the juxtapositions were shown to be editorial; arguments from the silence of the notebooks had to be retried now that the whole archive was present; dates attached themselves to fragments, and with dates came contexts, the prisoner's reading of a specific crisis or a specific adversary's new book. The Gramsci Institute in Rome, custodian of the manuscripts, became the laboratory of a scholarship increasingly international in personnel and method, and in time the work acquired its own institutions: a national edition of the complete writings undertaken in Italy, a complete English translation of the notebooks begun under Joseph Buttigieg at Columbia University Press, its first volumes appearing from 1992, and a dedicated international society of scholars coordinating the enterprise across countries.

The philological turn that followed is the seventh portrait, and the strangest, because its aim is to abolish portraiture in favour of the workshop. Its practitioners, Gianni Francioni prominent among the founders with his reconstruction of the notebooks' internal chronology, argue that the unit of Gramscian thought is not the concept but the developing fragment, and that the thought has a rhythm, a pattern of return and revision across the years of the cell, which thematic reading destroys. On this view the prisoner's key terms are not definitions to be extracted but instruments he was still machining, and the scholar's first duty is chronological: to know, for any passage, when it was written, from what earlier draft, under the pressure of which events and which reading. The turn has produced readings of great power. It recovered the notebooks on language and translation as central rather than decorative, restoring the linguist whom the early selections had misplaced. It clarified the genesis of the philosophy of praxis as a considered position rather than a censor's password, while keeping the password in view. And in the work of scholars such as Fabio Frosini, Guido Liguori and Peter Thomas, it reopened the relation between the notebooks and the Marxist tradition with an precision the party era could not afford, finding in the fragments a sustained, critical dialogue with Lenin, with Bukharin's manual and with the orthodoxy of the Third International.

### What does the philological reader do that earlier readers could not?

Date the fragments. The critical edition lets a passage be placed in its year, its notebook and its layer of drafting, so an argument built on a fragment can be tested against what its author had read, suffered and revised by then. Earlier readers, holding a thematic arrangement, could not perform that test at all.

It would falsify the map to present the philological Gramsci as the final one, the true face at last. The philologists have their own route and their own temptations: a devotion to genesis that can shrink the thinker to his sources, a professional interest in difficulty that can mistake the workshop for the whole achievement, and editions so complete that the ordinary reader, for whom the portraits were made, is quietly disenfranchised in favour of the initiate. Nor has the critical text ended dispute; it has relocated dispute onto firmer ground, which is a different and better thing. What the turn has settled is procedural. After Gerratana, a claim about the notebooks that ignores the chronology and the layers is not wrong by definition, but it is unlicensed, in the way that a claim about a treaty that ignores its drafts is unlicensed. The transmission test is, in this sense, the philological conscience generalised to the whole reception: it asks of every portrait the questions the critical edition taught scholars to ask of every fragment.

## The conspiracy Gramsci

One portrait remains, and it differs from the others in kind. In a family of polemical narratives circulating in several countries, Gramsci figures as the mastermind of a deliberate, patient subversion: the theorist, so the story runs, who taught his followers to abandon the barricades and instead infiltrate the schools, the universities, the press and the churches, rotting a civilisation's values from within until it fell without a battle. The figure in the story is assembled from parts of the record this article has surveyed, and its construction deserves a factual description, because readers who meet the narrative deserve to know how it was built. The war of position supplies the strategy, read as infiltration rather than as the open organisation of consent. The phrase about a long march through the institutions supplies the slogan, although the phrase is not his: it belongs to the German student leader Rudi Dutschke, who coined it in the nineteen sixties in deliberate echo of a famous military retreat, and its attribution to the prisoner is an error of transmission of the purest kind. The Frankfurt School, treated in [the history of the term cultural Marxism](/2015/10/01/cultural-marxism-term-history/), is then fused with him into a single conspiracy of cultural subversion, although the Frankfurt theorists were exiles and pessimists who shared neither his party, his optimism about popular classes, nor his project, and although the notebooks themselves lay unpublished in an archive throughout the years when the alleged plan was supposedly being executed by people who could not have read them.

The narrative misrepresents its subject at the points where checking is easiest. Hegemony, as the notebooks define it, is consent, and consent cannot be manufactured in secret by a cadre, because the mechanism requires the open winning of majorities through institutions those majorities inhabit and can leave; a leadership discovered to be manipulating forfeits, on the theory's own terms, the very asset it was said to be accumulating. The prisoner's actual writings on schools defend a rigorous, traditional curriculum against progressive dilution, a position which sits oddly in the portrait of a saboteur of education. And the documentary spine of the story, the claim of a coordinated plan handed down through named institutions, dissolves under the same test this article applies everywhere: no edition, no chain of custody, no text. Readers who hold versions of this narrative are not served by ridicule, and none is offered; the narrative answers real experiences of cultural change that its holders find otherwise unexplained, and it is defeated, where it is defeated, only by the slower work of showing what the record contains. What the record contains is a prisoner whose ideas travelled because institutions he never controlled, parties, universities, publishers and disciplines, found them useful for arguments of their own, which is a stranger and more interesting story than the conspiracy, and has the additional merit of being documented.

### Where did the slogan about a long march through the institutions originate?

With Rudi Dutschke, the German student leader, who used it in the nineteen sixties to describe a strategy of working within institutions while opposing them, borrowing the image of the Chinese Red Army's Long March. Gramsci, dead three decades earlier, never wrote the phrase, and his war of position names a different and older metaphor.

## The misreadings, corrected from the record

The map has implied the corrections as it went; this section states them together, in the form in which a reader meeting them in the wild can use them. Each correction is a claim the notebooks demonstrably contradict, which distinguishes this list from the portraits, where the corpus underdetermines and judgement must be argued rather than cited.

### What do people most often get wrong about Gramsci?

The recurring errors are these: treating hegemony as manipulation rather than negotiated consent involving real concessions, treating civil society as the voluntary sector, treating the war of position as electoralism, and assuming he replaced economic analysis with cultural analysis, which his notebooks on industry contradict.

Hegemony as manipulation fails on the concessions condition. A leadership that merely implants beliefs has, in the notebooks' taxonomy, achieved at most a fragile domination, because implanted beliefs cost the implanters nothing and bind the believers to nothing; the orders the prisoner calls hegemonic are those in which the leading group has paid for its leadership in sacrifices of its own corporate interests and has organised that payment into institutions the led experience as partly theirs. Manipulation is what hegemony is defined against, and an account that equates them has discarded the concept's discriminating edge, its ability to sort orders into those that rest on agreement renewed daily and those that rest on fear renewed nightly.

Civil society as the voluntary sector fails on location. In the notebooks civil society is not the world of charities and clubs standing innocently between family and market; it is the whole trench-system of the integral state, the schools, churches, parties, unions, press and professions through which a social order teaches itself to its members, and its institutions are as saturated with power as any ministry. The flattening matters because it reverses the political moral: where the voluntary-sector reading sees a sphere to be celebrated against the state, the notebooks see a terrain to be studied as the state's own outer works, neither good nor bad in itself, and decisive because it is where consent is manufactured or lost.

The war of position as electoralism fails on the metaphor's grammar. Position warfare in the notebooks keeps its antagonist, its objective and its culminating moment; what it renounces is not confrontation but the fantasy that confrontation can be scheduled before the balance of forces in society has been shifted by years of organised presence in the trenches. An electoralism that concedes the legitimacy of the existing order and competes for office inside it is, in the fragments' own terms, a different activity, nearer to what the prisoner analysed under the name of transformism, the absorption of opposition leaders into the governing bloc, which he treated as a technique of the other side.

The organic intellectual as a working-class academic fails on the definition's axis. The notebooks classify intellectuals by function, not by origin or by the content of their sympathies: every social group creates its own organisers and elaborators, and the organic intellectual of the working class is the stratum that gives that class coherence and self-awareness in economic, social and political fields, a party organiser or a technician of production as readily as a writer, while a professor of proletarian origin who elaborates the worldview of another class performs, functionally, another class's work. The biographical reading turns a sociology into a compliment and loses the analysis, which is unsparing in both directions.

## The economist whom the cultural reading forgot

The most documentable of all the misreadings is the claim that the prisoner abandoned economics for culture, exchanging the factory for the library as Marxism's true scene. It survives because the receptions that transmitted him had uses for a theorist of culture and because the notebooks' economic writing is scattered, technical and hard to excerpt. The record, read whole, is unambiguous. An entire late notebook, the twenty-second, is given to Americanism and Fordism, a sustained analysis of high wages, prohibition, sexual regulation and the assembly line as a single civilisational wager: an attempt to manufacture a new kind of worker for a new organisation of production, with the five-dollar day read as an hegemonic payment, a concession purchasing consent to an otherwise intolerable labour discipline. The notes engage Taylorism in its engineering detail, ask what the rationalisation of production does to the nervous system and the family, and weigh the claim that American industry had discovered in the wage itself an instrument of social government. A reader who has worked through that notebook cannot repeat the abandonment story; a reception that never opened it can repeat the story for decades, and did.

Nor is the notebook an anomaly. The prisoner's economics runs from his earliest journalism on tariffs and the southern market, through the Southern Question essay's anatomy of the agrarian bloc and the function of southern intellectuals in sealing it, to the notebooks' treatments of the rate of profit's popularisers, of the crisis of 1929 as it unfolded in the newspapers the censor admitted, and of the structure of the Italian economy whose unification he analysed as a revolution accomplished without its Jacobins. His extended critique of Bukharin's popular manual, the authorised economics textbook of his movement, is simultaneously a methodological treatise: against a Marxism of iron laws he sets a philosophy of praxis in which economic structures constrain without scripting, and in which the market itself is a determinate product of political and legal arrangements rather than a state of nature. One may judge that economics heterodox or incomplete. What the transmission test forbids is the inference, drawn by receptions that met only the cultural selections, from the prisoner's interests to his omissions. The culture was never a replacement for the economy in the notebooks. It was the place where the economy's human material was formed, and the prisoner studied both because his question, how orders hold and how they might be remade, refused to let him choose.

## The letter that arrived late

No single document illustrates the transmission principle better than the letter of October 1926. In the weeks before his arrest, reading reports of the struggle inside the Soviet party between the majority around Stalin and the oppositions, Gramsci wrote to the Soviet Central Committee, in the name of the Italian party's leadership as he understood its mind, a letter of extraordinary delicacy and firmness. It affirmed solidarity, and then it warned: the methods being used against the opposition, the treatment of dissent as treason, were corroding the unity they claimed to defend, and the Italian party feared the consequences for the whole movement. The letter was entrusted to Togliatti, then the Italian representative in Moscow, who judged it undeliverable in the form written, replied to its author with a careful explanation of his refusal, and kept the text in his keeping. Gramsci was arrested within the month. The letter slept.

It was published in 1964, in the year of Togliatti's death, and its arrival detonated inside the portrait the party edition had built. The founder of the postwar cult appeared, in his own hand, as a man who had looked at the Soviet regime at the height of its consolidation and recorded, in the movement's own language, a principled fear of its methods; and the party's postwar leader appeared as the man who had decided that the founder's fear should not be delivered. Every element of the episode is documented, and every element was indigestible for the monument, which required a founder aligned with the movement's history and a successor aligned with the founder. The debate that followed, in the Italian press and in the party's journals, was ostensibly about a letter and actually about custodianship: who owned the dead man, and what did ownership permit them to withhold. For the reception history the moral is structural. A portrait can be destabilised decades after its construction by a single change in the transmitted corpus, which proves that the portrait was always a function of the corpus available, and not, as its holders imagined, a direct vision of the man. Readers who internalise that proof have understood the transmission test before it is named.

## The Southern Question as a bridge text

Some texts travel under their own power, along routes separate from their author's main corpus, and the unfinished essay on the southern question is the great Gramscian example. Written in 1926 as an intervention in his party's argument about alliances, abandoned at his arrest, and published in exile in Paris in 1930, the essay analysed the Italian south as an internal colony: a peasant mass held in place by a bloc of landowners and southern intellectuals, exploited by northern industry through the tariff and the tax system, and courted by every government as a reservoir of votes and soldiers. Long before the notebooks were available anywhere, this essay was being read, in the original and in translation, by men and women whose own countries presented the same geometry: a modernising coast or capital, a despised agrarian interior, a stratum of mediating intellectuals sealing the arrangement. It is not an accident that Latin American and Indian readers often met the essay before the notebooks, and formed their Gramsci around it. The essay made him, for two continents, primarily the theorist of the agrarian question and of the intellectual as the hinge of domination, and interpretations formed on that bridge text still colour the secondary literature of those regions, where the cultural studies Gramsci of Birmingham can seem, even now, a distinctly northern affair.

The bridge text also demonstrates a subtler law of the reception: fragments travel better than systems. A long unfinished essay on a concrete problem can be carried across languages and decades because its argument is inspectable whole; the notebooks, by contrast, travel as selections, and selections are arguments made by editors. Wherever in this history a reception is unusually faithful to its source, the source usually proves to be a text that arrived intact, and wherever a reception swings wild, a selection, a translation choice or a party's arrangement is generally found at the origin, doing silent work. The corollary for readers is practical. When an account of the prisoner seems to you luminous and complete, ask first not whether it is true but whether what it rests on ever arrived where its author was standing.

## The Gramsci of the education faculties

One faculty adopted him so thoroughly that its version deserves a row of its own in any fuller table. The notebooks contain an extended meditation on schooling: on the old classical curriculum and the new vocational schools, on Latin as a training of the mind, on the teacher as an intellectual and on the single common school he proposed, in which the children of workers would receive, without dilution, the rigorous formative education that the ruling class reserved for its own. His position surprises readers formed by progressive pedagogy. The prisoner of fascism defended effort, sequence, even a measure of the old discipline, arguing that the children of the subaltern classes were cheated precisely when reformers spared them the hard acquisitions through which ruling classes reproduce their competence. The English scholar Harold Entwistle built a well-known book on that tension in 1979, calling its subject, in his title, conservative schooling for radical politics, and the education literature of the following decades, in the work of Michael Apple, Henry Giroux and many successors, made hegemony a standard instrument for analysing curricula, textbooks and the hidden sorting of schools. The education Gramsci is in one respect the most faithful of the popular portraits, because it rests on notebooks the political selections had sidelined and takes his difficulty seriously. In another respect it repeats the family pattern: a faculty found the fragments that answered its questions, and its Gramsci, the theorist of the classroom, is as partial as the strategist's or the sociologist's, and partial in the direction of its discoverer's needs, exactly as the transmission test predicts.

## The delayed reception in the East

East of the old divide, the reception ran late and cold, for reasons the record makes intelligible. The prisoner had criticised, in the notebooks, the authorised manual through which the Soviet movement taught its economics, and his 1926 letter, had it been known, would have ranked as a graver offence; as it was, rumour and the reports of Italian comrades sufficed to mark him in the Stalin years as a figure of doubtful reliability, a national deviationist whose cult in Italy was tolerated as a local eccentricity. Selected writings in Russian appeared only in the thaw years of the late nineteen fifties, and the full notebooks were never, in the Soviet period, the living text they became in the West; the regimes that claimed his tradition had institutional reasons to prefer their Marxism in finished manuals rather than in a workshop full of second thoughts. In the people's democracies the pattern varied with the latitude of the local regime: read with interest where intellectual life had some air, as in Yugoslavia's looser dispensation, and elsewhere shelved as an Italian classic, honoured and unopened. The irony is structural and worth stating plainly. The movement in whose name he was imprisoned by its enemies kept his most searching work at arm's length for two generations, while the capitalist universities he never entered became the workshops of his resurrection. Transmission, once again, was decided less by the text's content than by the institutions that held the keys to its reproduction.

## After the party: a national classic

The strangest transformation came last. When the Italian Communist Party dissolved itself in 1991, closing the history that had begun in the split of 1921, the custodianship of its founder-thinker lapsed, and the portrait escaped into the general culture of the republic. The process had been under way for years, in school curricula that taught the letters as Italian prose, in the quiet of the non-Catholic cemetery in Rome where the urn of his ashes rests among poets, in anniversaries of his death marked by institutions that no party controls. Detached from the custody that built him, Gramsci became something none of the seven portraits had predicted: a national classic, claimable in principle by any Italian tradition, cited by politicians of opposed camps as an authority on the nation, on intellectuals, on the South, his concepts naturalised so deeply into the language of public argument that speakers use them without knowing their source. Scholars have watched the apotheosis with mixed feelings. A thinker whom everyone owns is a thinker no one has to read, and the classic on the plinth is exposed to the final misreading, veneration without study, against which the only defence is the one this whole cluster of articles attempts: to keep returning the concepts to the texts, the texts to their editions, and the editions to their history.

## The machinery that freezes a portrait

Between the editions and the individual reader stands a machinery that reception histories neglect at their cost: the syllabus, the handbook, the examination and the reference entry. A syllabus freezes a portrait by selection under scarcity; twelve weeks of a course on modern political thought can hold one Gramsci, and the one it holds, chosen for teachability, becomes for most graduates the thinker himself. A handbook chapter freezes it further, compressing a portrait into two thousand words of attributed summary, and compression always selects the most portable claims, the definition, the slogan, the contrast with a neighbour. Examination questions then certify the compression: candidates learn which Gramsci is expected, the one whose hegemony is consent, whose intellectuals are organic, whose state is integral, and they reproduce him in the hall with an accuracy that tells the examiner nothing about the notebooks and everything about the machinery. Reference works complete the circuit, their entries written from the handbooks, their authority then cited by the next generation of handbook writers. None of this is conspiracy or incompetence; it is the ordinary metabolism by which a culture digests a difficult author, and every major thinker passes through it. But the metabolism explains the tenacity of the misreadings catalogued in this article better than any interpreter's malice. A misreading installed in the machinery reproduces itself each academic year without further assistance from its inventors, and dislodging it requires not a better argument alone but a better machine: new editions in the classroom, new questions in the hall, new entries in the references. The cluster to which this article belongs is built on that understanding, and [the exam guide for the cluster](/2015/05/15/gramsci-exam-answers/) attempts, in its small jurisdiction, to install the corrected portraits where the examinations can find them.

## An industry of interpretation

A final piece of evidence for the transmission thesis is quantitative. The secondary literature on this one prisoner grew, within two generations of his death, into an industry with its own instruments: an international bibliography of Gramsci studies, begun by the American scholar John Cammett and continued by the scholarly society, cataloguing many thousands of items in dozens of languages; specialist journals and newsletters; a standing calendar of conferences on several continents; complete editions under way in more than one country. No comparable industry attends thinkers whose texts arrived whole and whose meaning was settled early. The industry exists because the corpus invites and requires mediation: someone must establish the text, date the fragments, gloss the passwords, trace the routes, and the army of mediators, each answering a real need, is itself the largest single cause of the divergence this article maps. That is not an argument against the industry, to which this series is indebted on every page. It is an argument for reading its products with the test in hand. In a field where mediation is unavoidable, the reader's skill is not finding an unmediated Gramsci, for there is none on offer. The skill is auditing the mediation, which is learnable, and which the concluding sections now formalise.

## The objection from incompleteness

Every map of this kind must face the strongest objection to its own existence, and the objection here is formidable. It runs as follows. The notebooks are unfinished notes, written in code, under censorship, by a sick man working from memory, never revised into a book and never intended for print in their surviving form. They contradict themselves, as Anderson's audit showed, at the joints where a system would need to be firmest. Under those conditions there is no determinate Gramsci behind the receptions for the receptions to be measured against. The search for the real thinker behind the seven portraits is itself the eighth fiction, and the portraits are not distortions of an original but free constructions on an inkblot, each as legitimate as the materials allow, which is to say each as legitimate as every other. On this view the transmission test is a ritual of false precision: routes can be traced, certainly, but tracing a route cannot grade a portrait, because grading requires a standard, and the standard is exactly what the fragments refuse to supply.

The objection deserves its full strength because a weak version of it is simply true, and the answer begins with concessions. The notebooks do underdetermine a great deal: the exact proportion of consent to coercion in a mature hegemony, the institutional shape of the regulated society he projected, the fate of the state in that society, the standing of the party he led in a theory he wrote after its defeat, all lie open in the fragments, and interpreters who have filled those spaces with confident systems have exceeded their evidence, as the best of them, under cross-examination, admit. A reader who finishes this article believing that the record settles every dispute among the seven portraits has misunderstood it as thoroughly as a reader who believes it settles none.

For the objection proves too much. Between a text that determines everything and a text that determines nothing lies the territory where scholarship actually lives, and the notebooks occupy it densely. They determinately support a body of claims that no honest construction can deny, and the misreadings corrected in this article are all violations of that determinate core. The concessions condition is stated, restated and applied across notebooks separated by years; it is not a mood. The location of civil society among the superstructures of the integral state is argued, with the Bobbio debate turning precisely on its textual solidity. The analysis of Fordist industry exists, in extenso, and cannot be argued out of the corpus by a reception that prefers a purely cultural author. The classification of intellectuals by function is defined with examples and edge cases. A construction that contradicts those anchors is not one legitimate portrait among others; it is an error, and the fact that the same corpus leaves the theory's upper storeys unfinished does not pardon it. The transmission test therefore has a standard after all: fidelity to the determinate core, candour about the underdetermined remainder, and a route that explains, without excusing, every departure from either. Graded by that standard the portraits sort themselves, not into saints and forgers, but into those that know their own mediations and those that mistake them for the man.

## The word had a life before him

One cause of divergence predates the notebooks entirely, and no account of the reception is complete without it. Hegemony is an old word. In Greek it named the leadership of one city over a league of others, a leadership that could rest on consent of the allies or on the fleet behind the invitation. In the Marxist tradition before the prisoner, the term had a precise career in Russian social democracy, where Plekhanov and Axelrod, and after them Lenin, used it for the claim that the working class should lead the democratic revolution beyond its own immediate interests, heading allies it did not absorb. Gramsci inherited that usage, broadened it under the pressure of the Italian defeat, and set it down in fragments that assume the reader knows the older senses. Many of his readers did not. A classicist meeting the word brings the Greek league with him; a Leninist brings the alliance strategy; a sociologist brings nothing but the context of the page; and each finds the page confirming the sense he brought, because the broadened concept genuinely touches all three. The lesson generalises across the whole lexicon. Subaltern, common sense, passive revolution, Caesarism, historic bloc: every technical term in the notebooks has a prehistory and a penumbra, and receptions diverge partly because the penumbras differ by language and discipline. Philology's insistence on dating and context is the discipline's answer to the penumbra; the transmission test is the reader's portable version of the same discipline.

## The Croce problem

No figure bends the early reception more than Benedetto Croce, and the bending was partly the edition's doing. The first thematic volume led with the confrontation with Croce, the philosopher-king of Italian culture, and thereby announced the terms on which the party wished its prisoner judged: as the heir who had absorbed the best of Italian idealism and carried it beyond itself. The framing had substance; the notebooks' engagement with Croce is relentless and intimate, a lifelong argument with the thinker Gramsci called, in effect, the secular pope of Italian intellectuals, whose historicism he raided and whose separation of politics from economics he fought. Croce himself, reviewing the prison letters with a generosity that surprised both camps, praised the man as a fellow spirit in the republic of letters while noting that as a philosopher the prisoner belonged to another church. That exchange set the key of the Italian reception for a generation: Gramsci read as a chapter in the national philosophical story, the last act of the drama that ran from Vico through the Risorgimento's historians to Croce and his Marxist antagonist. Foreign readers, meeting the notebooks without that drama in their heads, have repeatedly underestimated how much of the text is addressed to it, and Italian readers have repeatedly overestimated the same share. The two errors are symmetrical, and both are transmission effects, produced by the presence or absence of a national context that the text assumes and never explains.

## The appropriation from the right

A map that omitted the most ironic reception would fail its own standard. In the nineteen seventies and eighties, intellectuals of the French Nouvelle Droite, Alain de Benoist and the circle around the review Nouvelle École foremost among them, announced their admiration for the prisoner and their intention to practise his strategy against his heirs: a metapolitics, a long cultural preparation of the terrain, fought for opposite values with borrowed weapons. The appropriation was explicit, literate and entirely serious about the texts it used, and it drew the obvious moral from the notebooks' analysis: that political power follows cultural hegemony, that a camp which loses the schools and the vocabulary will in time lose the state, whatever its electoral arithmetic. Scholars of the reception treat the episode as a limiting case with high diagnostic value. It demonstrates that the notebooks' strategic grammar is genuinely detachable from their author's allegiances, that a reader can reproduce the analysis of consent with fidelity while inverting every conclusion it was built to serve, and that the transmission test, which grades portraits by route and by fidelity to the determinate core, must be supplemented at exactly this point by the acknowledgement that fidelity and allegiance are different variables. The episode also performs a service for the correction of the conspiracy portrait: the documented appropriation of Gramsci by his declared enemies, in the open, in print, citing chapter and verse, is what the real transmission of his ideas into hostile camps looks like, and it resembles a secret infiltration in no particular at all.

## The counter-memory inside communism

The party's portrait had a rival inside its own house from the beginning, and the rival kept a different Gramsci alive through the years of the monument. The founders of Italian communism had quarrelled before they were imprisoned, and the followers of Amadeo Bordiga, the party's first leader, displaced by the Gramsci leadership with Moscow's backing in the mid nineteen twenties, preserved a counter-memory in which the true revolutionary was the uncompromising Bordiga and the prisoner a gifted tactician whose elevation marked the party's absorption into manoeuvre. That memory, carried in small journals and exile networks, mattered little to the public portrait, but it mattered to the archive of interpretations, because it preserved readings of the Ordine Nuovo period and of the Lyons theses that the official line preferred to soften, and because it reminds the historian that custodianship was contested even among the custodians. A parallel counter-memory ran through the Trotskyist tradition, which claimed the 1926 letter's author for its own indictment of the bureaucracy once the letter surfaced, and through the councilist currents that claimed the young Gramsci of the factory occupations against the party leader he became. Each counter-memory is a portrait too, drawn from a partial corpus for a living argument, and the fact that they were preserved by the monument's enemies does not exempt them from the test applied to the monument itself. What it does is enrich the archive against which the test can be run: a reader can now set the Bordigist, the Trotskyist, the councilist and the official Gramscis side by side, date their routes, and watch four parties to a quarrel of the nineteen twenties still conducting it, by means of footnotes, a century later.

## Fragments in the digital archive

A further turn of the transmission story is technological, and its direction is double. The manuscripts have been digitised by their custodians, the critical texts circulate in searchable form, and a student anywhere can now perform in an afternoon the operations of dating and cross-reference that once required a fellowship in Rome. That is the philological turn democratised, and it weakens every portrait that depended on its audience's inability to check. The same networks, however, have industrialised the decontextualised fragment: the notebook sentence lifted into a graphic, the aphorism attributed without notebook or year, the quotation that the prisoner never wrote travelling faster than the correction. The motto of the Turin newspaper, pessimism of the intellect and optimism of the will, which he borrowed from the French writer Romain Rolland and set at the head of his journal, circulates in a dozen languages as his coinage and his creed, a benign case of a universal process by which portable phrases shed their provenance. Nothing in the process is new in kind; the party edition also detached fragments from their contexts, and the textbook did the same. What has changed is the speed and the absence of a custodian. Where transmission once had gatekeepers whose selections could be studied and blamed, it now has currents, and the reader's defence is the same skill this article has practised throughout, applied without institutional help: ask of the fragment in front of you where it was found, in what layer, in whose arrangement, and let the answer set the weight the fragment is allowed to bear.

## The transmission test

Everything argued so far can be compressed into a procedure a reader can carry away and use on any account of the prisoner, including the accounts in this series. The transmission test asks three questions of a portrait, in order. Which edition, selection or translation does it rest on, and what did that vehicle include, omit and arrange? Through which discipline, party or movement did it reach its author, and what questions was that intermediary asking of the text? In which decade was it formed, and what political weather made its emphases plausible, useful or necessary? A portrait that survives the three questions with its claims proportioned to its evidence is licensed, whatever its conclusions. A portrait that cannot answer the first question at all, that does not know it has a route, is unlicensed however confident it sounds, because its confidence has been manufactured by the invisibility of its own mediation. The test does not ask a reader to become a philologist. It asks for the same scepticism a court applies to hearsay: not rejection, but a chain of custody.

### How can two honest accounts of Gramsci contradict each other?

By standing at different points of the transmission chain. One read the party's thematic volumes in a party school, the other a selection in a sociology course; one met the analyst of Fordism, the other never saw that notebook. Both report faithfully what reached them, and the routes, not the honesty, diverge.

Three worked examples show the procedure earning its keep. Consider first the contradiction over the party. One literature presents the prisoner as the supreme theorist of the party, the modern Prince, the collective intellectual that organises a class into a historical force. Another presents him as the theorist of the councils, of workers' self-government against party tutelage, and reads the party writings as a concession to the discipline of defeat. The routes explain the split without adjudicating it into a winner: the first literature descends from the notebooks read through the party edition, in a party that needed a founder for its own centrality; the second descends from the Ordine Nuovo writings of 1919 and 1920, restored to circulation in the nineteen fifties and canonised by the councilist and operaist currents that needed a founder against the party. Both corpora exist; both emphases are documented in their author's hand at different ages; the contradiction dissolves into a chronology, and the residue, the genuine question of how the council communist became the party theorist, is a biographical problem of great interest that neither portrait, mistaking its half for the whole, can even state.

Consider next the contradiction over democracy. The Eurocommunist literature made the prisoner a democrat of the long road, almost a pluralist before the letter. A sterner literature replies with the fragments on the party's internal regime, on the dictatorship of the proletariat affirmed as a phase, on Lenin praised precisely for the Jacobin virtues, and concludes that the democratic portrait is a forgery of convenience. Apply the test. The first portrait rests on the war of position and the hegemony fragments, selected in the nineteen seventies by parties exiting the Soviet orbit; the second rests on fragments of 1930 and 1931 and on the pre-prison record, selected by critics whose polemical target was those same parties. The record, whole, shows a Leninist rethinking the conditions of his Leninism in a West whose density he had underestimated, neither converting to liberalism nor freezing at the barricades, and it shows the rethinking unfinished. The test cannot complete the thought for him. What it can do, and what neither interested portrait does, is prevent the reader from inheriting a completion disguised as a report.

The third example is the one with which this article opened, the four lives of the word hegemony. Manipulation in the media textbook, dominance in the international relations survey, consent in the political theory seminar, conspiracy in the pamphlet: the test sorts them by route in seconds. The textbook descends from cultural studies at two removes, with the concessions amputated en route. The survey descends from hegemonic stability theory wearing a borrowed name. The seminar descends, at its best, from the notebooks themselves in a modern edition. The pamphlet descends from no edition at all, which is why its Gramsci can be made to confess to plans the documents nowhere contain. Sorting is not refutation, and the seminar's portrait still owes argument at the underdetermined joints. But a reader who can sort has already escaped the condition the whole reception history exploits, the condition of meeting a portrait without knowing that it is one.

## The separate careers of the words

The test operates on portraits; it also operates, with even finer resolution, on single terms, because each of the notebooks' instruments has led a separate life with its own route and its own drift. Passive revolution, coined for the Risorgimento, the transformation accomplished from above while the people watched, escaped into general historiography and development studies, where it now names a family of modernisations without mass mobilisation, from the bureaucratic industrialisations of the nineteenth century to the postcolonial settlements analysed by Partha Chatterjee for India, where the term organises an account of a national state that achieved the forms of modernity while keeping its popular classes in tutelage. Caesarism, the notebooks' name for the catastrophic equilibrium in which a third force arbitrates between exhausted camps, serves historians of regimes the prisoner never studied. Transformism does quiet work in the analysis of party systems that absorb their oppositions. Common sense, in the flattened translation, wanders the humanities as a synonym for prejudice, carrying none of the stratification, the good sense inside the folklore, that made it an instrument. In every case the pattern is the one this article has generalised: the term travels, the term's route decides its acquired meaning, and the reader who would use the term precisely must run the test on the word before running it on the world. A small discipline follows, worth the paragraph it costs. When a Gramscian term does heavy work in an argument, cite it the way the philologists cite a fragment: with its notebook sense stated, its acquired sense acknowledged, and the distance between the two treated as a fact to be explained rather than a blemish to be hidden.

## Fidelity, fertility and the grading of portraits

If portraits are unavoidable, they can still be graded, and the grading criteria are worth stating because they convert the transmission test from a suspicion into a standard. Three grades of virtue can be distinguished. The first is fidelity: proportion between a portrait's claims and the determinate core of the corpus, with the underdetermined spaces marked as spaces. The second is candour: the portrait's awareness of its own route, its willingness to name its edition, its discipline and its decade's needs, so that a reader can discount them. The third is fertility: the portrait's power to generate new knowledge when applied, to organise archives, movements or classrooms in ways that teach their users something true about the materials at hand. The interesting result of applying the three grades across the map is that they do not coincide. The post-Marxist portrait scores low on fidelity at the anchor points and high on fertility, having organised a generation of democratic theory; the party portrait scores high on fidelity to a real stratum of the work and low on candour, having concealed its own editorial hand; Hall's portrait scores high on all three, which is why it survives its critics; the conspiracy portrait scores zero on fidelity and candour while achieving, in the economy of attention, a fertility of pure infection, spreading by the mechanisms it falsely describes. A reader armed with the grades need not choose one portrait and burn the rest. The mature relation to a thinker of this kind is a portfolio, held knowingly: the philological portrait for the text, the strongest disciplinary portraits for the work they do, the party portrait for the history it embodies, and the whole portfolio audited, at intervals, by the test.

## Among the historians

Professional historians received him as a rival before they received him as a source, and the two great Italian quarrels show the test operating inside a single discipline. The first concerned the Risorgimento. The notebooks' account of Italian unification as a passive revolution, a national transformation accomplished by diplomacy and Piedmontese arms without the agrarian revolution and the popular mobilisation that might have made a Jacobin Italy, became, through the party edition's Risorgimento volume, the orthodox reading of the national past on the left. In 1959 the liberal historian Rosario Romeo answered with Risorgimento e capitalismo, arguing from the economic record that Italian capitalism had developed with no need of the missing revolution, that the agrarian contracts the Gramscians indicted were compatible with accumulation, and that the failed revolution was a myth manufactured by measuring Italy against a French template. The quarrel ran for decades, through conferences and the cultural pages, and it changed the history it fought over: later scholarship on the unification, whatever its verdict, works with evidence about land, credit and industry that the quarrel forced into the open. Note the transmission structure inside the dispute. Romeo was answering the edition's Gramsci, the Risorgimento fragments arranged as a system; the philological work that later redated and recontextualised those fragments shifted the ground under both parties, a reminder that even a discipline's settled controversies are indexed to the state of the text at the time they formed.

The second quarrel concerned fascism, the regime of the prisoner's own cell. The reading associated with his name made fascism intelligible as the reaction of a frightened bloc, agrarian reaction and industrial capital arming the petty bourgeoisie's resentments against a working class that had frightened it in the red years, and held that reading for a generation as the Marxist orthodoxy on the subject. Renzo De Felice, whose monumental biography of Mussolini redrew the field from the nineteen sixties onward, defined his enterprise partly against it, insisting on fascism's own revolutionary self-understanding and on the consensus the regime genuinely enjoyed, a word he wielded to force the Gramscians onto ground where their founder's own central concept, consent, cut against their inherited polemic. That the sharpest challenge to the Gramscian historiography of fascism arrived armed with a Gramscian instrument is the kind of irony the transmission test teaches a reader to expect: concepts outlive the portraits that carried them, and do their best work in the hands of the portrait's enemies.

## The anthropology route and its discontents

Anthropology took the concept up in the nineteen eighties and nineties, as the discipline turned from studying beliefs as systems to studying domination as a process, and the borrowing produced both a classic refinement and a celebrated rebellion. The refinement is associated with William Roseberry, who proposed in an influential essay of 1994 that hegemony be used not for a finished ideological dominion but for the contested material and discursive framework within which domination is disputed: a common language of contention, imposed in part by the powerful, within which the subordinate also learn to phrase their claims. The rebellion is associated with James Scott, whose studies of Malaysian peasants and whose Domination and the Arts of Resistance of 1990 argued that subordinate groups are seldom taken in at all, that the deference performed in public is a transcript staged for power while a hidden transcript of mockery and refusal circulates offstage, and that theories of hegemony mistake the stage for the village. The debate between the refinement and the rebellion is, for the purposes of this article, a gift, because both parties claim the prisoner. Roseberry's Gramsci is the analyst of contradictory consciousness, for whom the framework is real precisely because it is contested; Scott's target, he allows, is the flattened textbook notion of ideological incorporation, and his hidden transcripts have been read by sympathetic critics as the good sense of the notebooks under another name. When two anthropologists fighting over a concept can each plausibly recruit its author, the explanation is not that the author is infinitely elastic. It is that the notebooks contain both the theory of organised consent and the theory of the subordinate's double consciousness, in fragments written years apart for different arguments, and that each discipline's route delivered the fragment it needed.

Feminist scholarship ran a parallel course with a thinner textual inheritance, and its honesty about the thinness is exemplary. The notebooks address the organisation of consent in factories, parties and schools far more than in households, and their author wrote relatively little about women directly. Michele Barrett's study of women's oppression, published in 1980, nonetheless took hegemony as a principal instrument for analysing how patriarchy secures agreement to arrangements that injure the agreeing parties, precisely because the concept, unlike cruder theories of ideology, could model consent that is real, interested and revocable; and a generation of feminist historians used the same instrument on girls' schooling, on women's magazines, on the common sense of respectability. The portrait that results is confessedly an application rather than an exegesis, and its best practitioners say so, which the grading criteria of this article reward: candour about the route converts a liberty into a method.

## Read under dictatorships

There is a geography to this history that the metropolitan accounts underplay. For readers living under the dictatorships of the twentieth century's second half, the prisoner was not an ancestor but a colleague, and his texts arrived by the routes that repression leaves open. The Spanish-reading world met the notebooks first in editions prepared in Buenos Aires and Mexico City by exiles of two civil wars, and those volumes crossed back into Franco's Spain in luggage and by post, to be read in a country where the author's party was illegal and his name unprintable; a generation of Spanish intellectuals formed its idea of a democratic road in argument with a book they were not supposed to possess. In Brazil the notebooks were published from the mid nineteen sixties, under a military regime, in editions prepared by scholars who included Carlos Nelson Coutinho, and the theory of passive revolution found, in a country repeatedly modernised by decree, the readership it seemed written for. In Chile the prisoner's categories were working tools in the arguments of the Allende years and then, after the coup of 1973, in the exile literature that tried to understand how a society's consent had been reorganised by terror. In South Africa the liberation movement's study circles and the universities' fierce debates over the nature of the apartheid state drew on the same vocabulary, hegemony and the national-popular doing service in arguments about a country whose divisions, like the Italy of the southern question, ran through its map. In each case the transmission conditions, clandestinity, exile publishing, the seminar as a cell of the opposition, selected for the strategic Gramsci and the analyst of consent under duress, and against the academic refinements, which arrived later, by safer routes, to find the ground already occupied.

## The operaist counter-reading

Inside Italy itself, the party's humanist portrait faced in the nineteen sixties a challenge more wounding than any from the academy, because it came from readers who claimed the factory against the monument. The workerists of the journal Quaderni Rossi and its successors, Raniero Panzieri and Mario Tronti the decisive figures, reread the notebooks with the party edition in one hand and the Fiat shop floor in the other, and announced that the custodians had inverted their author. The real Gramsci, on this reading, was the theorist of the councils and of Americanism and Fordism, the thinker for whom the point of production was the school's rival and the state's foundation, whose analysis of Ford's factories contained a whole theory of how capital reorganises society from the assembly line outward; the Crocean sage of the thematic volumes was the party's alibi for a cultural politics that had abandoned the factory gates. The operaist reading powered a decade of militancy and a distinguished literature, and then, in the manner of the receptions it despised, hardened into a portrait with its own omissions, slighting the notebooks on intellectuals, on the party and on the state that its hero had spent his maturity writing. Symmetric judgment is the test's whole ethic: the workerists corrected a real deformation with evidence, restored a real stratum of the corpus to view, and then repeated the family error of enthroning their stratum as the man. Their episode is the reception in miniature, and it is why this article insists that the test be applied to friends of the truth as rigorously as to its enemies: the route explains the operaists exactly as it explains the custodians, and neither is diminished by the explanation.

## The biographers' Gramsci

Between the editions and the portraits runs a third channel, the biography, and it has carried its own characteristic distortion with great charm. The foundational life, Giuseppe Fiori's of 1966, was written by a fellow Sardinian with access to family memory, to the island's places and to survivors who had known the schoolboy, the journalist and the deputy; it gave the reading public a person, hungry, stubborn, funny and doomed, and it taught two generations to approach the theory through the suffering that framed it. Alastair Davidson's intellectual biography of 1977 redressed the balance toward the Turin formation and the Ordine Nuovo years, and later lives, including Dante Germino's study of the political thought, refined the picture further. The biographical channel matters to the reception because it supplies the connective tissue that portraits silently use: the decision to read the notebooks as the fruit of defeat, or as a prisoner's defiance of his jailers, or as the last work of the council movement, is usually taken from a life before it is argued from a text. The test applies here with special force. A biography is a route with a narrator, and the narrator's own formation, Fiori's Sardinian loyalty, Davidson's Australian distance, the party historians' custodianship, stands between the reader and the record as surely as any editor's arrangement. The remedy is not to refuse biography, without which the fragments float in a void, but to read two lives written from different routes and to treat their disagreements as the outline of the evidence itself.

## The Turin writings and their separate fate

The pre-prison corpus led a double life throughout the reception, and its fate explains several portraits that the notebooks alone cannot. The journalism of the war years and the Ordine Nuovo writings of the council period were collected by the party's publishers in the nineteen fifties, but they circulated in a different economy from the notebooks: cheaper, more polemical, quoted in movement papers and factory bulletins rather than in monographs, and read by militants as a manual where the notebooks were read as a scripture. Within that corpus one fragment of the young Gramsci achieved a fame entirely independent of Marxist politics. As theatre critic of the Turin socialist press he had championed Luigi Pirandello when the metropolitan critics were still dismissing him, reviewing the plays with a seriousness that Pirandello scholarship continues to cite, because the young critic saw, before the profession did, that the Sicilian's theatre of fractured identity was the serious Italian drama of the age. The episode is a perfect transmission fossil. It shows a major judgement of the author's surviving in a discipline, theatre studies, that has no use for the rest of him, preserved because one guild's archive kept what another's discarded. Whole Gramscis have been built from less.

The separation of the two corpora also distorted the political portrait in a way the map should record. Readings formed on the Turin writings prize the insurrectionary of the red years; readings formed on the notebooks prize the strategist of the long siege; and since the two sets of texts reached different publics through different publishers at different prices, the quarrel between the young and the mature Gramsci was for decades conducted between audiences who had literally not read the same books. The modern editions, which print the whole record in sequence, have made that quarrel obsolete in principle. In practice the audiences persist, because syllabuses and movements, the machinery surveyed earlier, preserve the separations that print has abolished.

## The professors' favourite

A reception history that spared its own guild would fail its test, and the guild's interest in this author is neither hidden nor innocent. Of all the Marxist classics, the prisoner is the one in whose system the intellectual is a protagonist: the theory of organic and traditional intellectuals assigns to teachers, writers, clergy, organisers and experts the decisive work of manufacturing consent, and a profession that lives by manufacturing interpretations will naturally cherish the thinker who told it that interpretations rule the world. The cherishing has been fruitful beyond any reasonable expectation; the secondary literature counted in thousands of items, the conferences, the journals and the chairs are real achievements of understanding. But the guild's Gramsci bears the guild's watermark. He is disproportionately the theorist of culture, of education, of discourse, of the superstructures in which professors work, and disproportionately less the analyst of the factory council, the party cell and the wage, the terrains where professors are visitors. Even the philological turn, the guild's conscience, exhibits the bias in a refined form, preferring the questions that archives and editions can answer to the questions that only politics could. Stating the bias is not an accusation of bad faith, any more than it was for the party's editors. It is the transmission test applied at home, and its practical consequence is a rule of reading: when an academic portrait finds the prisoner agreeing with the academy's own scale of importance, check the route twice. The agreement may be earned. The history of this reception shows how often agreement has been arranged instead, by the innocent mechanism of a profession reading the fragments that describe itself.

## The anniversaries

The portraits were periodically reset by the calendar, and the resets deserve a line in the map because they function as republication events for reputations. The fortieth anniversary of the death in 1977 brought the great Italian conferences of the Eurocommunist season, at which the party's Gramsci was celebrated and contested in the same halls, and from which a shelf of proceedings carried the arguments into the universities. The fiftieth anniversary in 1987 met a party in decline and a scholarship in ascent, and its Gramsci was correspondingly more textual and less strategic. The centenary of the birth in 1991 arrived in the year of the party's dissolution, and its commemorations, scholarly and civic, performed the transfer of custody described earlier, from the movement that founded the cult to the republic that inherited the classic. Each anniversary worked the same quiet alchemy: it justified new editions, new translations, new conferences and new journalism, and each wave of new mediation restated the portrait for a public that had not witnessed the previous restatement. A reader who dates an account of the prisoner and finds an anniversary in the neighbourhood has learned something real about the account's emphases before reading a word of it, which is the transmission test in its most mechanical and most reliable form.

## The citation discipline

The philological turn left ordinary readers one gift of immediate practical value, and a section of this article would be incomplete without handing it on. Since the critical edition, scholarship cites the notebooks by a standard address: the notebook number and the paragraph number, conventionally printed with the letter Q for quaderno, so that a reference of the form Q13, followed by a section sign and a number, sends any reader to the same fragment in any serious edition or translation. The apparatus also marks the fragment's layer, distinguishing first drafts from single drafts and from the reworked second versions the prisoner copied into his special notebooks, the distinction printed in the scholarship as the difference among A, B and C texts. The consequence for the general reader is a new power of audit. A quotation offered without an address, in a polemic or a textbook, can now be asked for its papers; a quotation with an address can be visited, read in its layer and its company, and weighed against the uses being made of it. Half the misreadings catalogued in this article would have had shorter lives had their first audiences possessed that power, and the other half survive precisely where the power goes unexercised. Citing well is the transmission test practised at the scale of the sentence.

## The Spanish route and the translator as mediator

The Iberian and Latin American receptions turn on a figure the Anglophone literature rarely honours, the translator as mediator, and one name concentrates the lesson. Manuel Sacristán, the Spanish philosopher who worked in Mexican exile's publishing orbit and in Barcelona under a regime that watched him, translated and introduced the prisoner for the Spanish-speaking world, preparing the great anthology through which several generations, on both sides of the Atlantic, first met the notebooks. Sacristán was no neutral conduit. He was a rigorous and independent Marxist thinker with his own positions, and his introductions argued with the text they presented, warning against the devotional reading and insisting on the fragments' character as work in progress. The Spanish route thereby acquired, at its source, a critical inoculation that other routes had to develop for themselves, and the strength of the subsequent Spanish and Latin American scholarship, its relative freedom from both the party monument and the textbook flattening, owes a measurable debt to a mediator who declined to disappear. A complete Spanish edition founded on the critical text was in time published in Mexico, closing the route's long dependence on anthologies. The general law stands behind the case: every reception has its Sacristáns, Hoares and Nowell Smiths, its Gerratanas and Platones, and the transmission test's first question, which asks whose hands the text passed through, is at bottom a request for their names.

## The silence of the economists

The map has one eloquent blank. Professional economics, the discipline of the prisoner's own first questions, has largely declined to receive him. His writings on Fordism are read by sociologists of work and by historians; his critique of the popular manual is read by philosophers; his notes on the southern market are read by historians of Italy; but the journals and curricula of economics proper pass him by, and histories of economic thought grant him, at most, a footnote among the Marxists. The blank is itself a transmission datum. Economics professionalised around formal models in the decades when the notebooks appeared, and a fragmentary, literary, historically saturated mode of economic reasoning had no carrier in the discipline's journals, no translator into its idiom, and therefore no route; where no route exists, even a major body of work does not arrive. The contrast with sociology, which built the concept of hegemony into its foundations, measures the difference a carrier makes. It also disciplines the article's own thesis against a lazy reading of it. Transmission explains arrival; it does not manufacture merit, and the economists' silence is partly a judgement, delivered in the discipline's own currency, that the notebooks' economics, for all its penetration, is not cumulative in the way the discipline requires. Both things are true, and the test, which exists to sort routes rather than to canonise their freight, is content to record them side by side.

## The letters, completed

Half a century after the selection that made him famous, the letters acquired their complete critical edition, prepared by Antonio Santucci and published by Einaudi in the nineteen nineties, and an English edition of comparable scope, Frank Rosengarten's, appeared from Columbia University Press in the same decade. The fuller record altered the first portrait in ways the transmission test leads one to expect. The 1947 selection, shaped for a public being introduced to a martyr, had privileged the family correspondence, the letters in which the prisoner is son, husband and father, stoical and tender. The complete letters restore the political correspondent: the exchanges with party figures, the negotiations over books and money and visits, the strategic silences, and also the darker register the selection had softened, the quarrels, the suspicions of betrayal that prison planted in a mind cut off from verification, the letters in which despair is not transfigured but simply present. Biographers and historians drew on the completed archive to complicate the saint into a man, sometimes with a relish for complication that is its own distortion. The sequence closes the circle of this article's argument. The first text of Gramsci that the world received was a selection of his letters, and the portrait it founded, the witness in the cell, survived every subsequent correction by the notebooks, by the critical edition and by the philologists, until the letters themselves, completed, corrected it. In this reception, nothing is exempt from transmission, not even the evidence that founded the cult.

## Legend and record in the survival story

Even the bare physical facts arrive encrusted, and a short audit of the encrustation makes a fitting coda to the evidence. Popular accounts disagree on how many notebooks there are, the count varying between twenty-nine and thirty-three according to whether the four notebooks of translation exercises are reckoned in, a trivial discrepancy that no popular source bothers to explain and that therefore circulates as a small mystery. The notebooks are said, in schoolbooks and documentaries, to have been written in code, a phrase that swells a real but partial practice, the substitution of a handful of terms under censorship, into an image of an undeciphered cipher rescued by genius, whereas the first editors read the hand without heroic difficulty and the passwords yield to a page of context. The trial of 1928 is remembered through the prosecutor's declaration that the authorities must stop this brain from functioning for twenty years, a sentence reported in the press of the time, endlessly requoted, and impossible to verify to the standard the phrase's fame demands, so that the most famous sentence about Gramsci may be a journalist's compression of a prosecutor's rhetoric. None of these encrustations is wicked. Each is the ordinary product of a story retold by people who needed it portable. But together they show the transmission process operating at the smallest scale, on facts that fit in a sentence, and they justify the article's insistence that the reader's question is never only whether a statement is stirring, or even whether it is sourced, but how many hands, with what needs, the statement passed through on its way to the page.

## The Western Marxist frame

Some portraits are made not by editors of the author but by cartographers of the tradition, and the most influential act of cartography in this history is Perry Anderson's Considerations on Western Marxism of 1976. Anderson grouped the prisoner with Lukács, Korsch and the Frankfurt School in a tradition defined by defeat: formed after the revolutionary wave of 1917 to 1923 had broken, divorced from mass practice, migrating from economics and politics toward philosophy, aesthetics and the theory of culture, and increasingly housed in universities. The grouping was a map, and like all maps it taught its users what to see. Two generations of syllabuses inherited it, and with it a Gramsci whose essence is the turn to the superstructures, filed between History and Class Consciousness and the Dialectic of Enlightenment as a station on Marxism's road from the factory to the seminar. The philological and political scholarship has since protested, with justice, that the prisoner fits the frame worst of the figures it holds: he alone among them led a mass party, wrote as a strategist with an organisation's fate in his hands, and filled his notebooks with the economics and the military analogies the frame says the tradition abandoned. Yet the frame survives, because it explains something real about the company in which the century read him, and because a syllabus needs a shelf. The episode generalises the test once more. A thinker is received not only through editions and disciplines but through the neighbourhood in which the tradition's historians house him, and the neighbours, chosen by the cartographer, colour every reading that begins from the map.

## The generation of the long sixties

Between the party's custodianship and the academy's, there stands the brief, incandescent reception of the student generation, and its portrait, though short-lived, left deposits in all the others. In the years around 1968 the notebooks and the Turin writings circulated in cheap editions among readers who wanted from the dead a warrant for a politics that was neither social democratic nor Stalinist: the young Gramsci of the councils served the movement's anti-authoritarianism, the theorist of hegemony served its turn toward culture and everyday life, and the prisoner himself, murdered by a regime while still young, served as proof that seriousness and sacrifice were not the monopoly of the orthodoxies being rejected. The German students, whose leader supplied the long march slogan the conspiracy literature later stole, read him in the editions prepared by the Argument publishing house, the seed of the complete German edition that Wolfgang Fritz Haug would bring to completion decades later. The Italian movement read him against his own party, in the operaist key already described. The French read him least, their Marxist imagination held by other masters, which is one reason the French reception flowered late and philosophically. What the sixties contributed to the permanent record, beyond its own excesses, was a demonstration that a portrait's decade can outweigh its text: the same fragments that certified patience in the Eurocommunist reading certified impatience in the movement reading, and both certifications were performed in good faith by readers who could quote. The difference lay in what the readers needed history to promise them, and need, as this article has argued throughout, is a transmission medium.

## The dispute over the philosophy of praxis

The philological turn's most consequential internal dispute concerns three words. The philosophy of praxis, the prisoner's standard replacement for the name of his own doctrine, was read for decades as a password and nothing more: a phrase chosen to pass a censor, semantically empty, behind which the word Marxism stood unchanged. A body of newer scholarship, Fabio Frosini and Peter Thomas prominent in it, argues that the password does work no synonym could do. On their reading the phrase asserts a position: that the doctrine is a conception of the world in which thought and practice, philosophy and politics, history and the theory of history, are one activity, against both the materialism of the popular manual, which made thought a reflex of matter, and the idealism of Croce, which made practice a footnote to spirit. The dispute is not decorative. If the phrase is a password, the notebooks' philosophy is an orthodoxy under guard, and the party portrait, which needed an orthodox founder, is strengthened. If the phrase is a position, the notebooks contain a deliberate refounding of the doctrine, and every portrait must be regraded against a thinker more original and less compliant than his custodians advertised. The evidence is genuinely two-sided, the phrase plainly did pass the censor, and the notebooks plainly meditate on its meaning with a persistence that passwords do not require; the mature scholarship increasingly holds both truths together, and the dispute's real lesson for the map is that even the smallest unit of the corpus, a single term of art, can carry two receptions inside it, awaiting the reader's route to decide which one he will hear.

## The Catholic reading

The most quietly consequential of the national receptions ran through the institution the prisoner had studied as the supreme historical school of hegemony, the Catholic Church. His analysis of the Church was the opposite of anticlerical polemic: he treated it as the most successful hegemonic apparatus in European history, an organisation that had held the allegiance of the peasantry for a millennium by training its own intellectuals from the people's ranks, by conceding forms and festivals to popular life while keeping doctrine's centre intact, and by refusing, as he put it in substance, to let religion become a property of the learned. Italian Catholic intellectuals returned the compliment of being taken seriously. In the decades of dialogue between communists and Catholics, when the party of Togliatti and then of Berlinguer sought a modus vivendi with the Church's Italy and the Church's intellectuals sought terms with modernity, Gramsci was the Marxist the Catholic journals could argue with, because he alone among the tradition's founders had treated their institution as a subject of history rather than as a fraud to be unmasked. The traffic shaped both parties: the national-popular strategy absorbed the lesson that Italy could not be understood against its Catholic substance, and Catholic social thought absorbed, often without attribution, the categories of hegemony and of the organic intellectual through which it redescribed its own crisis of consent. A reception that flows between adversaries, leaving deposits in both, is the strongest evidence this history offers that the notebooks describe something real about how belief is organised; frauds do not usually equip their opponents.

## The American route

The United States, whose industry the prisoner had analysed with such penetration from his cell, was nearly the last major country to read him, and the delay is among the map's most instructive. The beachhead was scholarly and early: John Cammett's study of the origins of Italian communism, published by Stanford in 1967, treated its subject with an archival seriousness the American academy had not previously spent on him, and Cammett's lifelong bibliography of the secondary literature made him the reception's own registrar. The wider arrival waited for the nineteen seventies, when the journal Telos carried translations and dossiers to a New Left audience hungry for a Marxism untainted by the Soviet connection, and when Carl Boggs's Gramsci's Marxism of 1976 presented him to American sociology as the theorist of a democratic and cultural road. Even then the American portrait formed under a local pressure the map should name: in a country without a mass socialist party, the notebooks were received from the start as academic theory, their strategic core read as metaphor, and the American Gramsci became accordingly the most purely conceptual of the portraits, a supplier of instruments to cultural studies, education and the theory of the state, detached from any organisation that might have embarrassed the seminar by asking what the instruments were for. The gain in rigour was real. So was the loss, measurable in the surprise with which American readers, decade after decade, rediscover that the theorist of consent was also a party leader who expected his concepts to be used by people who intended to win.

## The linguist recovered

One recovery of the philological decades deserves separate record, because it changed the understood origin of the central concept. In 1979 the Sicilian linguist Franco Lo Piparo published a study arguing that the key to the notebooks lay in the author's university formation: that Gramsci, trained under the neolinguist Matteo Bartoli, had formed his idea of hegemony on the model of linguistic change his teacher had mapped, in which an innovation spreads from a centre of prestige across a territory not by command but by adoption, as speakers freely take up the forms of those they admire. On this account the most famous political concept of the century began as a generalisation from the life of words, and the notebooks' obsessive return to language, to translation, to the national question of a common Italian, is not a hobby beside the theory but the theory's native soil. The thesis was contested, as strong theses are, by scholars who found the political sources, the Russian debates and the Leninist vocabulary, sufficient to explain the concept's birth. Its effect, contested or not, was to redirect a generation of readers toward the early notebooks and the Turin lecture rooms, and to make the biography's first chapters, the scholarship boy among the dialect maps, into required reading for the theory's students. A concept's birthplace, once moved, moves the concept: the hegemony that descends from linguistics is a subtler instrument than the hegemony that descends from propaganda, and the distance between the two is exactly the distance this article has been measuring all along.

## The postcolonial route beyond India

The subaltern borrowing had a distinguished fellow traveller whose route ran through literature rather than historiography. Edward Said built the account of colonial culture in Orientalism, in 1978, partly on the prisoner's distinction between political and civil society and on his analysis of how a ruling culture secures the consent of the ruled to their own depiction; and in his later work he set at the head of his method the notebooks' injunction that critical consciousness begin by compiling an inventory of the traces history has deposited in the self. Through Said the concepts passed into the study of empire's literatures across the world, and from there into the general equipment of the humanities, where a reader may now meet the vocabulary of hegemony in a study of the nineteenth-century novel without any Italian, any Marxism or any prison in sight. The postcolonial route completes the demonstration the Indian route began. In both, the concepts did their best travelling where the traveller's own society presented the geometry the prisoner had analysed, a metropole and a subordinated interior, a culture of the rulers and a common sense of the ruled, so that the fragments arrived not as doctrine to be believed but as instruments already warm from a recognisable use. Reception, at its most successful, is recognition.

## The relic and the glass case

A final channel of transmission is neither textual nor academic. The manuscripts themselves have become relics, held by the Gramsci foundation in Rome, exhibited under glass on the great anniversaries, photographed for the newspapers as other nations photograph a founding charter; the house at Ghilarza where the boy grew up is a museum; the ashes rest in Rome's non-Catholic cemetery, in a grave that receives, still, the delegations of parties and the flowers of strangers. Relic status is a real force in a reputation, and not a benign one only. It certifies importance, and it also embalms: the thinker under glass is safe from the reader, his difficulty converted into aura, his name available for ceremonies at which nothing he wrote is at risk of being discussed. The Italian republic's embrace of its communist prisoner, the school editions of the letters, the stamps and the street names, belong to this channel, and the transmission test applies to it as to all the others: a portrait carried by veneration has a route, and the route explains its characteristic content, which is the face without the arguments. Against the glass case there is only the remedy this whole cluster of articles exists to supply, the slow return of readers to a text that resists them, in editions that show their workings, with the questions this article has formalised kept open on the desk.

## The New Times Gramsci

The British reception has a party chapter of its own, later and stranger than the Italian original. In the nineteen eighties the British communist party's monthly journal, under its editor Martin Jacques, made the notebooks the theoretical charter of a renovation: the celebrated New Times analysis of 1988 and 1989 argued that the world of Fordist mass production and mass parties was giving way to a fragmented, post-Fordist order of niche markets, plural identities and individualised consumption, and that a left formed in the old world would have to rebuild its hegemony, its common sense and its alliances, exactly as the prisoner's method prescribed. Stuart Hall was among the project's principal theorists, and the line from Birmingham to the party journal is the British reception in a single career: the same concepts, conjunctural analysis, hegemonic project, authoritarian populism, passed from the seminar into a party's attempt to survive its century. The attempt failed with the party, which dissolved in 1991 as its Italian parent did, but the analysis outlived the vessel and migrated, concepts and all, into the modernisation discourse of the Labour governments that followed, whose architects had read their Gramsci in that journal's circle and learned from him that elections are won on the terrain of common sense before they are won on policy. A reception history that confined itself to books would miss this entirely. Portraits are also carried by journals, by party schools and by the staff work of politics, and the British case, in which a dying party's magazine served as the transmission belt between a prisoner's notebooks and a governing programme, is the neatest modern proof that the test's second question, which asks through which movement a portrait travelled, is never pedantry.

## The state theorists' line

Political science received him through a debate about the state, and the line it formed is distinct enough to mark on the map. The Greek-French theorist Nicos Poulantzas, whose argument with Ralph Miliband over the capitalist state had defined the field's agenda, turned in his last work, State, Power, Socialism of 1978, to the notebooks for the concepts his structuralism had lacked: the state as a relation rather than a thing, the integral state dissolving the liberal boundary between public coercion and private consent, the analysis of authoritarian statism as a form of the modern order rather than its exception. From Poulantzas the line runs to Bob Jessop, whose strategic-relational theory of the state, the most systematic Anglophone state theory of its generation, is built openly on the notebooks' foundations, hegemony included, and through Jessop the prisoner's categories entered the working vocabulary of comparative political economy, where they are used by scholars who may never have read a page of the Italian debates that forged them. The state theorists' Gramsci is the most architectonic of the portraits: less quoted than systematised, less a voice than a set of load-bearing distinctions. Its fidelity is high at the core, its candour variable, its fertility, measured in the research programmes it still organises, exceptional. It also illustrates the last refinement of the test: a portrait may arrive so thoroughly digested into a successor theory that the route vanishes, and the reader meets the concepts as Jessop's or as Poulantzas's, the prisoner's name surviving in a footnote. Transmission does not always preserve its labels, and the historian of this reception learns to recognise the freight by its shape.

## A worked audit

The test and the grades are easily stated and, like all instruments, are learned by use. Take, then, a paragraph of the kind a student meets weekly, a textbook's summary composed for this demonstration: Gramsci, the textbook says, taught that the ruling class rules through ideology, spreading its values through the media and the schools until the workers accept their subordination as natural; his remedy was a long march through the institutions, in which intellectuals would spread counter-values until the culture had been won. Run the audit. The first sentence fails the fidelity grade at the core: rule through ideology, without the concessions, the institutions' relative autonomy and the activity of consent, is the manipulation misreading, and its route is the flattened cultural studies of the handbooks, two removes from Hall and four from the notebooks. The phrase about acceptance as natural fails the citation discipline: no notebook address exists for it, and the notebooks' account of common sense as contradictory, with good sense alive inside it, contradicts the picture of a sealed subordination. The remedy sentence compounds a slogan the author never wrote with a strategy the slogan's actual author, Dutschke, framed for another country and another decade, and presents as the prisoner's plan a conquest of culture by infiltration that his theory of consent makes incoherent. Three sentences, four transmission errors, and not one of them requiring malice: the paragraph is the machinery of the syllabus working exactly as designed. A reader who can perform that audit has no further need of this article, which is the condition every instrument should aim to bring about. The instrument, once learned, transfers to every other classic whose readers meet him through portraits.

## The East Asian route

The map extends further east than most Western accounts follow it, and the extension obeys the same law. Japanese scholarship, with its long tradition of complete editions and exact translation, built a Gramsci of philological solidity early, and the Japanese literature on the notebooks is among the world's most thorough, a fact invisible to readers who cannot follow it into its language, which is itself a transmission datum: routes have borders, and reputations stop at them unless a translator ferries them across. In China the concepts arrived in force with the cultural studies wave of the reform decades, when scholars arguing about an emerging commercial culture and a retreating party-state reached for a vocabulary of hegemony and civil society that seemed written for their questions; the Chinese Gramsci is accordingly a theorist of culture and of the social sphere, his party and his economics left, once again, at the border. Neither case adds a new portrait so much as it confirms the rule under new conditions: a corpus of this kind does not diffuse like a gas. It travels in caravans, along routes built by translators, exiles, parties and syllabuses, and at every frontier the cargo is inspected, taxed and partly confiscated by the needs of the receiving country.

## The decade of silence and the Paris bridge

Before the letters founded the cult, there was a decade in which the name survived at whisper volume, and the whisper's route prefigured everything. In Italy the death of 1937 passed almost unrecorded; a regime that had imprisoned the man had no interest in announcing that prison had finished its work, and the party that might have mourned him publicly was itself illegal, decapitated and scattered. The memory was carried instead by the exile communities, above all in Paris, where the Italian anti-fascist emigration published its journals, quarrelled over its past and preserved, in the recollections of men like Angelo Tasca, who had been present at the party's founding and broken with its course, an account of the Turin years and of the leadership struggles that no Italian reader could then consult. Exile transmission has its own physics. What survives is what the exiles argue about, and the exiles argued about responsibility for the defeat, so that the earliest stratum of the reception, laid down before any edition existed, is a stratum of blame and vindication within which the dead man figured as evidence. When the editions came after the war, they were built by the victors of that exile argument, and the strata of the quarrel are detectable in the monument's foundations to this day. The lesson completes the map's temporal range. Transmission does not begin with publication. It begins with the first retelling, and the first retellings of this life were made by survivors with scores to settle, in a foreign capital, in whispers.

Tasca's case concentrates the decade's lesson. A founder of the Turin journal, a leader of the party in its first years, expelled at the end of the nineteen twenties for resisting the ultra-left turn imposed from Moscow, he remade himself in Paris as a historian of the movement he had lost, and his studies of fascism's rise and of the party's birth, written under a pseudonym and read across the European left, were for years the only connected accounts of the world that had produced the notebooks. In them the dead leader appears already in a fixed role, the gifted second of a tragedy whose script Tasca was settling with the men who had expelled them both, in different senses, from the movement. Foreign scholars who learned their Italian communism from the exile literature absorbed that role with the facts, and traces of the absorption persist in reference works whose authors have never heard Tasca's name. The phenomenon deserves its place at the head of the reception because it is the purest available: a portrait formed from memory, grievance and genuine knowledge, transmitted without a single published line of its subject's mature work, and still detectable underneath the editions that were supposed to replace it.

## The seven Gramscis

The argument of this article can now be set out in the compact form the brief of the series requires, as a table of the seven portraits with their periods, their routes of transmission, their foregrounds and omissions, and the places where a reader is likeliest to meet them. The table is a finding aid, not a substitute for the sections that built it, and its cells are deliberately blunt: nuance lives in the prose, and the table's work is to let a reader locate, at a glance, the portrait in front of them.

## What survives the audit

Set the seven portraits side by side and a residue emerges that none of them disputes, and the residue is the closest thing this reception offers to bedrock. Every serious portrait agrees that the prisoner made consent, its manufacture and its withdrawal, the central problem of modern politics. Every one agrees that institutions, schools, churches, parties, newspapers, unions, are the machinery in which that problem is worked on, and that a politics which ignores the machinery loses to one that studies it. Every one agrees that intellectuals, broadly defined as the organisers of a social order's self-understanding, are a decisive stratum and not a decoration. And every one agrees, however it grades his answers, that the questions were posed from inside a defeat, by a man auditing his own side's failure with the censor reading over his shoulder, which is why the notebooks carry, even in their driest taxonomy, the pressure of a life. The agreements matter for the grading of the disagreements. Where the portraits divide over ground the corpus settles, the corrections of this article hold; where they divide over ground the corpus leaves open, the division is the thinker's unfinished business, and arguing it is not a misreading but the continuation of the work by other means.

The series to which this article belongs has now examined the concepts one by one: the consent that must be paid for, in the cluster's [pillar treatment of hegemony](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/); the texts in their editions, in the companion guide to the notebooks; the wars of position and manoeuvre, the intellectuals, the civil society in which the whole drama sits. This article has examined the examiners. Its conclusion is a discipline rather than a verdict: never ask only what a portrait of Gramsci says, ask how it reached you, and let the answer apportion your trust. The prisoner, who spent his last decade tracing how ideas travel through institutions and are changed by the travelling, would have recognised the discipline. It is his own method, turned upon his afterlife, and there is no better evidence that the method works than the fact that it survives the turning.

## The social historians' Gramsci

British social history, the most distinguished historical school of its generation, received the prisoner with a respect it granted few theorists, and the encounter left marks on both sides. Eric Hobsbawm, whose own formation lay in the party the notebooks' author had founded the British counterpart of, presented the English selection to the readers of the New York Review of Books in 1974 in an essay whose title, the great Gramsci, announced the verdict of a historian not given to inflation. For Hobsbawm the prisoner was the outstanding Marxist thinker of the century precisely because he had done what historians do: he had asked how specific societies, in their concrete institutions and inherited common senses, actually hold together and actually change, and the endorsement carried the concept of hegemony into staff rooms that no party school could have reached. E.P. Thompson's engagement was more combative and for that reason more revealing. Thompson, the century's great historian of English popular culture, took up the term hegemony for his studies of the eighteenth century, of the gentry's theatre of rule and the plebeians' counter-theatre within it, and then policed its use with characteristic ferocity, warning against versions in which the concept became a blanket smothering the evidence of resistance. The Thompsonian Gramsci is thus a tool under licence: hegemony admitted as a description of a field of force, never as a verdict that the force prevailed. Around these encounters grew the History Workshop movement, founded by Raphael Samuel at Ruskin College in Oxford, whose journal and conferences built a history from below for which the notebooks' notes on folklore, on popular literature and on the subaltern supplied, once the cultural writings were Englished, a charter discovered after the fact. The school's mature judgement, that the prisoner was the rare theorist whose concepts survived contact with archives, explains the historians' portrait better than any doctrine: among the seven, theirs is the Gramsci certified by use, and the certification was earned in a discipline the transmission routes reached late and by accident.

## Complete editions as national acts

The last phase of the map can be read in the publishing programmes of nations. A complete edition is never only a scholarly instrument; it is an act of canonisation, a state's or a culture's declaration that an author belongs to its permanent furniture, and the race to complete Gramsci, run independently in several countries across the closing decades of the twentieth century, is the reception's final institutional form. France, which had argued about him longest after Italy, produced the complete notebooks from Gallimard under the direction of Robert Paris, the scholar whose own career, spanning the prisoner's reception in France and in Latin America, embodied the routes this article has traced. Germany completed the prison writings in the Argument edition prepared by Wolfgang Fritz Haug and his collaborators, a work of decades that closed the circle opened by the student movement's paperbacks. Brazil, where the theory of passive revolution had found its most receptive foreign audience, received a complete edition of the notebooks at the century's turn under the editorship of Carlos Nelson Coutinho and his colleagues, the culmination of a national scholarship that had been reading the prisoner, under dictatorship and after, for forty years. Italy itself, not to be outdone by its exports, undertook the national edition of the complete writings, the state at last performing for the communist prisoner the office it performs for its certified classics. Each edition re-founded the portrait in its country, because each made the same quiet argument the original Einaudi volumes had made in 1948, that this author is to be read whole, in order, forever. The differences among them, of apparatus, of emphasis, of the introductions that frame them, are the national receptions preserved in scholarly form, and a comparative study of the editions alone, their title pages and their prefaces, would reproduce the whole argument of this article without opening a single notebook.

## The ashes of Gramsci

In 1957 the poet and filmmaker Pier Paolo Pasolini published the poem that gave the cultural reception its enduring image. The Ashes of Gramsci is a meditation conducted at the grave in Rome's non-Catholic cemetery, the poet standing before the urn of the prisoner and arguing with him across twenty years: the dead man lucid, disciplined, certain of history's direction; the living poet scandalous, divided, drawn to the very popular world the theory analysed and unable to inhabit the certainty the theory promised. The poem made the grave a station of Italian culture, a place writers visit in their work as pilgrims once visited shrines, and it fixed a tone for the prisoner's presence in the national imagination that no edition could have achieved: Gramsci as the conscience one argues with, the superego of the Italian left and of Italian letters, honoured precisely by being resisted. Cultural transmission of this kind, through poems, films and the iconography of the cell and the glasses, bypasses every gate the scholarly test polices, and it is arguably the channel through which the largest public knows the name. It also produces the softest portrait of all, the prisoner as pure moral witness, his theory melted down into his example. The map records the channel without contempt. A culture that argues with its dead at their graves is healthier than one that files them, and the argument, in Pasolini's version, is of an intellectual seriousness the devotional literature rarely reaches. But the reader of this article will note the route, and discount accordingly: what reaches us through the poem is the man as a mirror for his visitors, and the visitors, generation after generation, are the portrait's true subject.

## The schoolroom classic

In Italy the transmission acquired a classroom, and the classroom deserves its line because it manufactures the base layer on which all later Italian readings rest. The prison letters entered the anthologies from which the nation's schoolchildren learn their prose, excerpted for the purity of the style and the nobility of the sentiments: the letter about the hedgehog, the letters to his sons, the requests for news of the children's schooling, the serenity under sentence. Generations of Italians therefore meet the prisoner before they can meet the theorist, and meet him as a classic of the language and a martyr of the conscience, in the company of the patriots of the Risorgimento whose fate his own fate rhymes with. The schoolroom portrait is the party portrait's gentler heir, national rather than partisan, and it explains a fact foreign observers miss: the reverence that surrounds the name in Italy across the political spectrum is not an achievement of the movement that claimed him but of the anthology that adopted him. It also explains the characteristic Italian blind spot, the difficulty of treating the notebooks, in the land of the letters, as a technical work to be quarrelled with rather than as the relic of a saint to be venerated. Every nation that adopts a difficult author into its classrooms performs the same exchange, difficulty surrendered for belonging, and the transmission test, applied to the schoolroom, simply asks the reader to remember that the exchange was made, and at what price.

## The Italian right's Gramsci

The French appropriation had an Italian counterpart with a sharper edge, because in Italy the prisoner was national property and the contest over him was a contest over the country's own past. From the nineteen seventies, intellectuals of the Italian post-fascist right, regrouping after the long exclusion of their tradition from cultural legitimacy, studied the notebooks with the attention of men who recognised their own exclusion in the theory's terms: if hegemony decides whose ideas count as common sense, then a camp locked out of the universities, the publishing houses and the press is suffering from a hegemonic defeat, and the remedy its theorist prescribed, the patient construction of a counter-culture, was available to any camp willing to do the work. The right's Gramsci journals and conferences of those decades make disquieting and instructive reading: the analysis is often exact, the allegiance inverted, the debt acknowledged with a candour the left's own popularisers did not always match. Italian scholars of the reception treat the episode as the limiting case that proves the map's central distinction. A portrait can be faithful to the machinery of the theory and false to its purpose, and grading it requires both axes, fidelity and allegiance, kept separate. The conspiracy literature's Gramsci, by contrast, fails the first axis while pretending to the second, which is why the two phenomena, so often confused in polemic, must be kept apart: one is an enemy's honest theft of a weapon, the other is a story about a weapon that never existed.

## The American cultural studies variant

The United States built its own cultural studies on the Birmingham foundations and then rebuilt the foundations, and the rebuilding altered the portrait once more. In the hands of the American school's leading theorist, Lawrence Grossberg, and of the journals and conferences he animated, hegemony became the master concept of a discipline defining itself against both the old mass-culture pessimism and the new celebrations of popular resistance: a way to describe a culture as a field of forces in which pleasures are real, power is real, and neither cancels the other. The American variant travelled the world with the discipline's expansion, and in many countries the first Gramsci a student meets is now this one, at three removes from the notebooks: the prisoner read by Birmingham, Birmingham read by Illinois, and the whole tradition compressed into the proposition that culture is a terrain of struggle. The proposition is true to its source in the way a coastline is true to a continent, and the reader equipped with the test will know what has been lost in the generalisation: the concessions, the institutions' specificity, the party, the state, the economics, everything, in short, that made the concept an instrument of analysis rather than a banner for a discipline. The case is the gentlest in this article and in some ways the saddest, because nothing in it is error exactly. It is dilution, the fate of every strong idea that succeeds, and the notebooks themselves, in their notes on how common sense degrades a philosophy into a folklore, describe the process with an accuracy their author's afterlife keeps confirming.

## The publishers

A reception history that names editors and translators should end its survey with the institutions that employed them, because publishing houses are the transmission's load-bearing walls, and in this history they were never neutral. Einaudi, the Turin house that issued the letters and the thematic volumes and, a generation later, the critical edition, was itself a hegemonic project in the prisoner's exact sense: a private firm that set out, under its founder Giulio Einaudi, to build the culture of a democratic Italy by deciding what the nation would read, publishing Croce and the prisoner between the same covers as heirs of one national conversation. The Gramsci the world received was thus, at the origin, an Einaudi author, shaped by the house's standards of prose, design and intellectual seriousness as much as by the party's strategy, and the conjunction of the two custodians, the party that owned the politics and the house that owned the prestige, explains the monument's unusual finish. In Britain the 1971 selection came from Lawrence and Wishart, the Communist Party's publishing house, a provenance English readers largely ignored while absorbing the selection's authority, so that the Anglophone Gramsci was born, like the Italian, under a party imprint whose editorial hand went unexamined by its beneficiaries. The post-Marxist portrait arrived from Verso, the publishing arm of the New Left Review, whose list was itself an argument about what Marxism should become; the philological Gramsci's English voice came from Columbia University Press, a university house whose series and subventions framed the notebooks as a world classic rather than a movement text. Four houses, four frames, one corpus. The transmission test's first question, which asks what vehicle a portrait arrived in, is at bottom a question about such frames, and the reader who learns to read a title page, publisher, series, editor, date, as the first page of the argument has acquired the skill in its most portable form. Books do not reach us. They are delivered, and the delivery is part of the meaning.

France supplies the pattern in duplicate, and the duplication is itself diagnostic. The French party's house issued its selections of the prisoner from the late nineteen forties, framing him for a readership of militants; the complete notebooks, when France produced them, came from Gallimard, the most consecrated literary house in the country, under scholarly direction, framing him for the republic of letters. Two French Gramscis thus had two title pages before they had two readers, the militant's and the classic's, and the long French argument between a political and a philosophical reception was pre-enacted, silently, in the choice of imprint. Wherever this history is studied closely, the same doubling appears: a movement house and a prestige house dividing the corpus between them, and the general reader, who rarely notices an imprint, receiving from the division a thinker already sorted by the shelf the book was bought from.

The economics of delivery deserve their sentence too, because they explain the durability of the selections against the completeness of the editions. A selection is cheap, teachable and finishable; a complete edition is none of the three, and the market, which includes the syllabus market, rewards the portable form in every generation, so that the corrected text and the popular portrait proceed on separate tracks indefinitely, the one cited in the journals and the other installed in the classrooms. Paperback economics wrote the reception's class structure: the thematic volumes and the 1971 selection were the texts of readers who bought books, while the critical edition, priced and weighted for libraries, was the text of readers who consulted them, and the two publics have been arguing past each other, each certain it has read Gramsci, ever since. When the digital copy finally collapsed the price difference, it collapsed the excuse as well, and the persistence of the portraits since then measures how much of transmission was never about access at all, but about the frames that access arrives wearing.

## The keepers of the archive

Behind the editions stands the archive, and the archive has keepers. The original notebooks rest with the Gramsci foundation in Rome, the institution descended from the party's own institute, and for decades the manuscript pages could be consulted only there, under supervision, by scholars whose projects the custodians judged. The arrangement was defensible on grounds of conservation and indefensible in its effects, since it made the ultimate court of appeal in Gramscian disputes a room in Rome to which one party in the disputes held the key; more than one scholarly quarrel of the middle decades carries, in its footnotes, the marks of access granted or delayed. The keepers' gradual opening of the archive, through the critical edition first and through photographic and digital reproduction in time, is therefore an event in the history of the thought and not merely of its storage. Every widening of access redistributed the authority to say what the prisoner wrote, from custodians to editors, from editors to any reader with a library card, and finally to any reader with a screen; and each redistribution was followed, as this article's law predicts, by a new portrait drawn by the newly admitted. The democratic moral is cheering and the scholarly moral is sobering. The text is now as open as scholarship can make it, and the portraits multiply regardless, because the scarcity was never the whole cause. Interpretation is not a ration to be ended by supply. It is the permanent condition of a corpus that arrives unfinished, and the archive's opening changed only who may join the argument, which is to say it changed everything except the argument's necessity.

## Why this prisoner

It remains to ask why the phenomenon happened to this author and not, in anything like the same degree, to his peers, because the answer gathers the whole argument into a single explanation. Other Marxist classics were censored, exiled, fragmented and fought over; none accumulated seven portraits in a century. The difference lies in a conjunction that no other case reproduces. The corpus was unfinished in a uniquely inviting way, systematic enough to promise a doctrine and fragmentary enough to license its completion, so that each generation could find its own questions already waiting in the workshop. The custody was political at the origin, so the first portrait was built by an organisation with a nation's culture to win, and built well enough to set the terms for all its successors. The concepts were engineered, by a mind trained in linguistics, to travel: hegemony, subaltern, common sense and the rest are portable instruments, detachable from their matrix in a way that the technical vocabulary of Capital is not, and portable instruments get carried into disciplines their maker never entered. The man himself died young, in custody, undefeated in print and uncompromised by the decades that compromised so many of his contemporaries, so that the portrait trade never had to discount for a late recantation or a shameful old age. And the country that produced him made him, through its schools and its anniversaries, a classic of the national language, giving the receptions a homeland in which the name is honoured by people who will never open a notebook. Remove any one of the five conditions and the history simplifies: a finished book, a neutral first editor, a technical vocabulary, a long life, a merely sectarian fame, and the maps in this article would have had three portraits to draw instead of seven, and far less to teach. The conjunction is the explanation, and it is also the warning with which the survey properly ends. The conditions that made the prisoner the most interpreted Marxist of his century are the conditions under which interpretation is least able to settle, and the reader who has followed the routes to this point possesses the only settlement the case allows: not a final portrait, but the knowledge of how portraits are made, and the habit of asking, of each new likeness, the three questions before believing it.

This article is not exempt from its own instrument, and candour requires its route to be stated before the reader is left alone with it. It was written in English, in the philological period, for readers who arrive through study rather than through a movement, and it therefore stands closest to the seventh portrait, with the seventh portrait's temptations: a scholar's preference for the questions editions can answer, and a mapmaker's preference for routes over verdicts. Its selection from the reception is itself an arrangement, foregrounding the transmissions that changed the concepts and giving less room to those that merely repeated them. A reader formed inside one of the traditions mapped here will find its emphases to contest, and the contest is invited on the terms the article has supplied: name the route that produced the objection, and let the fidelity of the rival account be tested against the determinate core rather than against the mapmaker's taste. The series around this article shares the exposure. Its concepts have been presented in the philological manner, dated, layered and sourced, because its editors judge that manner the most licensed the present state of the texts allows; a generation formed by another route will rebuild the cluster in its own image, and the rebuilt cluster will be evidence for the same law. Transmission ends only when reading does.

## For students and exam candidates

Examiners reward the candidate who can say which Gramsci a question is about. The seven-row table in this article is built for revision: learn to attach each portrait to its period, its edition and its characteristic omissions, and you can answer both the direct questions on the receptions and the indirect ones, where a question about hegemony quietly assumes the cultural studies portrait or an international relations question assumes the dominance usage. Two habits earn marks out of proportion to their cost. Name the edition behind any account you cite, since a marker reading an answer that knows the thematic volumes from the critical edition knows it is in safe hands. And never repeat the long march slogan as the prisoner's words; identifying its real author is the kind of correction examiners collect. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

This article is designed to be taught against itself. Set the seven portraits before a seminar and let the group assign each new reading, a chapter of Hall, a page of an international relations textbook, a polemical pamphlet, to its row of the table, defending the assignment from the reading's own citations; the exercise teaches the transmission test faster than any lecture on method, because students discover within the hour that the citations give the route away. The worked audit section supplies a second exercise: distribute any short textbook summary of the prisoner and require the three questions to be answered for it in writing, with the fidelity, candour and fertility grades awarded and justified. Where the seminar needs the underlying materials rather than the map, the concept itself is set out in the cluster's pillar treatment of hegemony and the editions are anatomised in the guide to reading the Prison Notebooks, and pairing the map with either text shows students the difference between an argument and the history of its travels, which is the distinction the whole cluster exists to teach. A third exercise, for advanced groups, sets the objection from incompleteness as a formal debate, one side defending the claim that the fragments license every portrait equally, the other required to win its case from the determinate core alone, the concessions condition, the location of civil society, the Fordism notebook, without appealing to any system the prisoner never built.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For working researchers the article's usable residue is procedural. Cite the notebooks by their standard addresses, notebook and section, with the layer of the text where the layer matters, and treat any secondary account's silence about its edition as a finding in itself. The bibliography of the secondary literature, begun by Cammett and maintained by the international society, is the fastest route into a reception whose scale defeats browsing, and the comparative study of the national complete editions remains an underworked field in which the argument of this article can be tested against title pages and prefaces alone. Journalists face the sharper deadline version of the same discipline: when the prisoner is invoked in a controversy, the three questions take ninety seconds and will usually reveal that the invocation descends from the pamphlet route rather than from any edition, a fact that can be reported without taking sides in the controversy itself. The two neighbouring territories most often confused with this one are disentangled in the companion articles on [the history of the term cultural Marxism](/2015/10/01/cultural-marxism-term-history/) and on Marxism in India, the first for the conspiracy usage and the second for the subaltern route in its full setting. Historians of the reception itself will find the richest unworked seams where this article could only point: the comparative bibliography of the translations, the archives of the publishing houses, and the party schools' curricula, in which the portraits were taught to the generations who then wrote the books the rest of the world reviewed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What do people most often get wrong about Gramsci?

Four errors recur across otherwise unrelated literatures. Hegemony is misread as manipulation, although the notebooks make consent conditional on real concessions paid by the leading group. Civil society is misread as the voluntary sector, although the notebooks place it inside the integral state as the trench system where consent is manufactured. The war of position is misread as electoralism, although the metaphor keeps its antagonist and its culminating confrontation. And the author is said to have swapped economics for culture, although a whole notebook anatomises Fordist industry. Beneath all four sits a single habit: quoting a portrait as though it were the man, without asking which edition, discipline and decade delivered it. That habit, rather than any one doctrine, is what the receptions surveyed in this article keep reproducing, and it is the first thing a careful reader learns to refuse. Each of the four errors can be corrected from the notebooks themselves, in passages whose notebook and section addresses any modern edition will supply.

### Q: How did the Italian Communist Party shape his reputation?

The party held the manuscripts, and custody became authorship of the reputation. It published the prison letters first, in 1947, fixing the image of the suffering witness before the theory could be read. It then issued the notebooks between 1948 and 1951 in thematic volumes, arranged by subject under its direction, foregrounding a national-popular thinker in dialogue with Croce whose work pointed toward the party's own proclaimed Italian road. It kept one document back for decades, the letter of 1926 warning the Soviet leadership against the methods being used on its own opposition, whose eventual publication complicated the founder's harmony with his movement. And through its schools, journals and conferences it taught that constructed figure to two generations as the thinker himself. None of this required forgery; selection, arrangement, timing and silence did the work, which is why the case remains the standard lesson in how organisations manufacture ancestors.

### Q: What is the Eurocommunist reading of Gramsci?

It is the reading formed in the nineteen seventies, when the Italian, Spanish and French communist parties sought a road to socialism through pluralist democracy and independence from Moscow, and needed a founding authority for the journey. Its key move was to take the war of position, the notebooks' figure for advancing against a state buttressed by civil society, as a warrant for the long parliamentary and institutional road itself, with hegemony won in society as both means and destination. Enrico Berlinguer's historic compromise and Santiago Carrillo's systematic statement of 1977 are its canonical texts. The reading had genuine footing in the fragments, which do insist that frontal assault fails in the developed West. Its weakness was the endpoint it silently supplied: the notebooks never dissolve the war into the election, and when the parties carrying the reading collapsed, their Gramsci was buried with them until other readers excavated the metaphor whole.

### Q: How did cultural studies use Gramsci?

The Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies, under Stuart Hall, took hegemony as an instrument for analysing how political orders assemble and lose consent in a specific moment. Hall's school studied moral panics, policing, popular newspapers and the reconstruction of common sense by the Thatcher project, which he named authoritarian populism, always counting the concessions a settlement paid and the grievances it organised. The method was faithful to the notebooks in procedure even where its materials were British: conjuncture first, slogans last. Through textbooks and teacher training the Birmingham Gramsci became the channel through which the English-speaking student typically first encounters the name. A flatter descendant travelled with it, in which hegemony shrank to a tale of media owners bending passive audiences, a usage that drops the concessions, the activity of consent and the contradictory common sense, and survives because it can be taught in a paragraph where the full concept cannot.

### Q: What did Laclau and Mouffe change about hegemony?

In Hegemony and Socialist Strategy of 1985, Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe kept the prisoner's central word and cut its anchor. In the notebooks, the subject that builds hegemony is still, at bottom, a fundamental class whose position in production prefigures its capacity to lead allies. In the reconstruction, no such anchoring survives: identities are formed within discourse, demands link into chains of equivalence without hierarchy, and a people is an achievement of articulation rather than a datum of the economy. The change made the concept portable across feminist, ecological and democratic movements, at the price its critics, Norman Geras most sharply, identified at once: detached from material constraint and from the concessions condition, hegemony risks explaining every alliance and therefore none. The authors presented the operation as completing a logic the notebooks began. Whether it completes or abandons that logic remains the live dispute at the centre of the post-Marxist portrait.

### Q: How is Gramsci used in international relations?

Robert Cox introduced the notebooks to the discipline in essays of the early nineteen eighties, transposing hegemony to the scale of world order: a configuration in which material capabilities, shared ideas and institutions fit together so that the order presents itself as universal and draws consent from the states and classes living under it. The school that formed around the borrowing, the neo-Gramscians, kept the founder's discipline, insisting that an order held by coercion alone is domination and not hegemony at all. The discipline's wider usage is another matter. International relations already possessed the word in the hegemonic stability literature, where it named a leading state's preponderance, and the borrowed term slid back toward that older meaning in textbooks and summaries, until thousands of pages were attributing to the prisoner a concept, dominance, that his analysis defines by exclusion. Asking which ancestor a given usage descends from, Cox's or the stability school's, sorts most of the confusion.

### Q: Why is Gramsci named in conspiracy narratives?

Because a polemical narrative, assembled in several countries, casts him as the strategist of a patient cultural subversion: the thinker who supposedly taught his followers to seize schools, universities and media and rot a civilisation from within. The figure is assembled from detachable parts. Its strategy is borrowed from the war of position, misread as infiltration rather than as the open organisation of consent. A slogan about a long march through the institutions supplies the motto, although the phrase belongs to the German student leader Rudi Dutschke, writing decades after the prisoner's death. The Frankfurt School is then fused into the plot as fellow conspirators, although its exiled pessimists shared neither his party nor his project. The narrative misrepresents the record at every checkable point, above all in proposing a secret plan executed from notebooks that lay unpublished in an archive. The term's full history is treated in the companion article on cultural Marxism.

### Q: Did Gramsci abandon economics for culture?

No, and the claim survives only where the notebooks are known through selections. A complete late notebook is devoted to Americanism and Fordism, analysing high wages, prohibition and the assembly line as one civilisational wager, with Ford's five-dollar day read as a hegemonic payment purchasing consent to a punishing labour discipline. His economics runs from the early journalism on tariffs and the southern market, through the Southern Question essay's anatomy of the agrarian bloc, to the prison critique of Bukharin's popular manual, the authorised economics textbook of his own movement. The cultural notebooks study where the economy's human material is formed, in schools, churches, newspapers and common sense; they supplement the economic analysis rather than replacing it. Receptions that met only the cultural selections inferred an omission the corpus does not contain, which is why this particular error is the cleanest demonstration in the whole reception that a selection can manufacture a doctrine.

### Q: What is the philological turn in Gramsci studies?

It is the reorientation of scholarship that followed Valentino Gerratana's critical edition of 1975, which printed the notebooks in their original order with the layers of drafting distinguished. Scholars formed by that edition, Gianni Francioni prominent among its founders, argue that the unit of the prisoner's thought is the developing fragment rather than the finished concept, and that interpretation must begin from chronology: in which year a passage was drafted, out of which earlier version, under the pressure of which circumstances and which reading. The turn restored the language and translation notebooks to the centre of the corpus, reopened the meaning of the philosophy of praxis, and gave ordinary readers the citation discipline of notebook and section numbers by which any quotation can be audited. Its own temptation is a genetic piety that can shrink a thinker to the sum of his sources, but its procedural settlement stands: after the critical edition, a claim that ignores chronology and layers is unlicensed, however familiar it sounds.

### Q: Which version of Gramsci are you actually reading?

The one your route delivered, and the route can be recovered from evidence in front of you. Check the edition or selection behind your source and note what it includes and leaves out. Check the discipline or movement carrying the account and ask what questions it needed the prisoner to answer. Check the decade in which the account formed and the political weather that made its emphases useful. A media textbook will usually be transmitting the flattened cultural studies portrait. An international relations survey may be transmitting dominance under a borrowed name. A party history will be transmitting the national-popular construction, a post-Marxist syllabus the de-anchored concept, a research monograph, increasingly, the philological workshop. None of this settles whether the account is right; the notebooks' determinate core does that, where it speaks. But a reader who can name the version has stopped mistaking the portrait for the man, which is the precondition of judging it.

### Q: How does the transmission test grade a portrait of Gramsci?

The test asks three questions in order: which edition, selection or translation the portrait rests on; through which discipline, party or movement it travelled; and in which decade it formed. It then awards three grades. Fidelity measures proportion between the portrait's claims and the determinate core of the corpus: the concessions condition, the placing of civil society among the superstructures, the Fordist economics, the functional definition of intellectuals, with underdetermined questions marked as open. Candour measures whether the portrait knows and names its own route, so that a reader can discount the mediation. Fertility measures whether applying the portrait generates knowledge about real materials. The grades do not coincide, which is the test's value: the post-Marxist portrait is fertile and candid but loose at the anchors, the party portrait faithful to a real stratum and silent about its own editorial hand, and Hall's application scores high on all three.

### Q: What should a reader check in an edition before trusting its Gramsci?

Five things, in the order an auditor would ask for them. First, completeness: does the edition print the whole corpus or a selection, and if a selection, whose principle chose the contents. Second, arrangement: are the fragments in the order the author left them, or sorted by subject under editorial titles, a decision that manufactures doctrines out of juxtapositions. Third, layers: can the reader distinguish first drafts from reworked versions, since a claim's weight changes with its stratum. Fourth, translation: how the hinge terms are rendered, common sense above all, where two distinct Italian phrases collapsing into a single English one has built doctrines by itself. Fifth, the frame: the introduction and the publishing house, which announce, for those who read title pages, the portrait the edition intends. An edition that survives the five checks can still be argued with, but it cannot quietly argue for you.

### Q: Why did different countries receive different Gramscis?

Because a corpus of fragments does not diffuse; it is imported, and every import is inspected by the receiving country's needs. Italy received a national founder through its party and its schools. France received a philosopher through a domestic argument about the state, filtered by a party's selections and a rival school's suspicions. Britain received an analyst of consent through a party publisher's selection, at the moment its politics needed one. India received a method for history from below, because the southern question's geometry matched a colony's. Latin America received the theorist of the national-popular and of modernisation from above, carried by exiles and translators. The United States received a conceptual toolkit, there being no mass party to embarrass the seminar. In each case the edition translated, the sponsor who paid for it and the argument it was conscripted to settle are recoverable facts, and once recovered they predict the portrait with uncomfortable reliability.

### Q: How did translation choices build a misreading?

By deciding arguments before the reader reached them. The notebooks distinguish the incoherent inherited folklore, senso comune, from the critical nucleus buried within it, buon senso, and English renderings that flatten both toward a single phrase about common sense dissolve the distinction on which the theory of contradictory consciousness rests; readers of the translation then debate a vagueness the original does not contain. The 1971 English selection compounded the effect by its contents, carrying the political notebooks across in full while leaving most of the writings on language, literature and folklore in Italian for another fourteen years, so that the Anglophone world formed its portrait of a political theorist before it could meet the student of language who became one. Timing is a translation choice too. The earliest text to reach a public sets the questions, and its successors are read as evidence for positions it made thinkable, a ratchet that no subsequent correction fully reverses.

### Q: What separates the seven Gramscis from one another?

Three variables, and only three, do most of the separating work. The edition differs: thematic volumes, a political selection, a critical text, or none at all in the pamphlet case. The intermediary differs: a party, a university discipline, a historiographical school, a journal, a movement, each asking the corpus its own questions and hearing the answers it has ears for. And the decade differs, since the political weather of the postwar reconstruction, of the Eurocommunist seventies, of the cultural studies ascendancy and of the philological consolidation made different emphases plausible, useful or necessary. What the seven portraits share is equally definite: a corpus whose core claims about consent, concessions, institutions and intellectuals survive every route, and whose unfinished upper storeys guarantee that the portraits will keep being drawn. The differences among them are therefore not a scandal to be deplored but a record to be read, the history of the century's needs written across one prisoner's workshop.
