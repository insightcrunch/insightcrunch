How do you take a point, let alone a win, off a France side that has not conceded control of a single half at this World Cup 2026? That is the puzzle Graham Potter and his Sweden players carry into the Round of 32 at the New York New Jersey Stadium on June 30, a knockout tie in which the loser flies home and the winner walks on toward the last 16. France vs Sweden pairs the tournament's clearest favorite against a side that reached the knockout rounds by the thinnest available margin, and the gap in expectation could hardly be wider. The interest lies in whether that gap survives contact with a single-elimination game, where one lapse, one set piece, one moment from a striker of Alexander Isak's class can rewrite a night that looked settled on paper.

Les Bleus arrived in North America ranked at the very top of the world and left the group stage with a perfect record, three wins from three and a goal difference that flattered nobody but themselves. Sweden arrived through a playoff they were never supposed to need, then blew hot and cold across three group games before scraping into the last 32 as one of the eight best third-placed teams. This preview lays out how each side reached this point, what the head-to-head history says, the tactical questions that will decide the tie, the men most likely to settle it, and a grounded prediction built only on what is knowable before kickoff.

The knockout math is brutally simple. There is no second leg, no aggregate cushion, no third game to recover a bad night. Whoever prevails in New Jersey earns a Round of 16 date with Paraguay in Philadelphia on July 4, and whoever loses is finished. For France that pathway looks like a runway toward a third star. For Sweden it represents the sort of one-off where a well-drilled underdog, playing with freedom because almost nobody expects anything, can occasionally trip a giant. The tension between those two readings is the story of the night.

## What is at stake in France vs Sweden

Single-elimination football strips away the safety net that carried both teams here. In the group stage a defeat can be absorbed, a slow start forgiven, a rotated lineup justified by the calendar. From the Round of 32 onward none of that applies. France vs Sweden is a straight shootout for a place in the last 16, and the framing changes how both managers will approach risk, tempo, and substitutions.

For Didier Deschamps, the stakes carry a personal weight that colors everything France do this summer. This is his final tournament in charge of the national team, the last act of a fourteen-year reign that has already delivered the 2018 title and a 2022 final lost on penalties. Deschamps is one of only three men to win the World Cup as both a player and a manager, and the one honor still missing from his own story is a second title as a coach to sit beside his 1998 medal as a player. He will not extend his contract beyond this competition. Every knockout game from here is a game he cannot get back, and a side this talented will feel the expectation that comes with a send-off narrative already written in the French press.

For Sweden the stakes are framed differently, and that difference is a weapon. Potter's team was not supposed to be in the United States at all. They finished bottom of their UEFA qualifying group and reached the finals only through the playoff route, so simply being alive in the knockout bracket already exceeds the baseline anyone set for them in the autumn. That freedom matters. A team with nothing to lose defends with fewer nerves and attacks with fewer inhibitions, and Potter has spent six months building a group that believes the knockout stage is where its tournament really begins. Sweden do not need to match France for possession or territory. They need to survive, to stay level deep into the game, and to make the tie a lottery that a single Isak or Viktor Gyokeres finish can swing.

The stakes also shape the neutral's view of the tie. France against Sweden is not billed as a coin toss, and pretending otherwise would insult the reader. The honest question is not whether France are favored, because they plainly are, but by how much, and what specific route Sweden would have to travel to make the evening uncomfortable. That route exists. It is narrow, it depends on France dropping their level, and it leans heavily on two elite center-forwards and a back line that holds its shape for ninety minutes and beyond. Whether Sweden can walk it is the entire drama.

## How France reached the World Cup 2026 Round of 32

France came through Group I with the only perfect record among the pre-tournament favorites, nine points from nine and a level of control that grew more emphatic with each match. Deschamps set his side up to win the group outright, and they never looked like doing anything else.

The campaign opened on June 16 at the New York New Jersey Stadium, the same venue that stages this Round of 32 tie, with a 3-1 win over Senegal. That result carried extra history, given that Senegal famously beat France on the opening day of the 2002 World Cup, and this time Les Bleus answered the challenge of a strong African side by pulling clear in the second half after a competitive opening period. Kylian Mbappe led the line and set the tone, and France's greater quality in the final third told once the game opened up.

Six days later in Philadelphia, France beat Iraq 3-0 in a match remembered as much for a weather delay as for the football, and Mbappe reached a personal milestone by winning his 100th international cap in the same fixture. Iraq, back at a World Cup after a four-decade absence, competed for long spells but could not live with France's cutting edge. The win effectively sealed France's place in the knockout rounds with a group game to spare and let Deschamps look ahead to the final matchday with the top seed already within reach.

That final group game, on June 26 in Boston, delivered France's most complete attacking display of the group stage, a 4-1 win over Erling Haaland's Norway that confirmed top spot. The match had been billed as a heavyweight meeting between two of Europe's most dangerous forwards, and it was France's supporting cast that seized it. Ousmane Dembele, the reigning Ballon d'Or winner wearing the number 7 shirt, produced a first-half hat trick that turned a marquee tie into a statement. Norway still advanced as group runners-up, and Senegal joined the knockout field as one of the best third-placed teams, but the night belonged to France, who finished the group stage looking like one of the two or three sides genuinely capable of lifting the trophy. Readers who want the fuller build-up to that Norway clash can revisit our [Norway vs France preview](/2026/06/26/norway-vs-france-preview/), which set out the top-spot stakes before kickoff.

Across the three games France scored ten goals and conceded twice, and the underlying pattern was consistent. Deschamps prioritized defensive solidity and midfield control, trusting a front line stacked with match-winners to do the damage once France won the ball back. It is a template that suits knockout football, where the ability to stay compact and then punish a single error is worth more than sustained possession for its own sake. France did not have to chase any of their group games, and that comfort let Deschamps manage minutes across a deep squad rather than lean on the same eleven every time. The group route is captured in our earlier [France vs Senegal preview](/2026/06/16/france-vs-senegal-preview/) and [France vs Iraq preview](/2026/06/22/france-vs-iraq-preview/), which framed each pre-match picture as it stood.

### How dominant were France in the group stage?

Total control, if not always total ruthlessness. France won all three Group I games, beat Senegal, Iraq and Norway, and topped the group with a perfect nine points and a plus-eight goal difference. They led every match, never trailed at any stage, and rotated with the group already won, which is exactly the platform a knockout run is built on.

## How Sweden reached the World Cup 2026 Round of 32

Sweden's route to this tie reads like a survival story, and understanding it is the key to understanding why they arrive with so little pressure and so much conviction. They finished bottom of their UEFA qualifying group with two points and no wins, a campaign so poor that it cost Jon Dahl Tomasson his job in October 2025 and brought Graham Potter home to take charge. Only their Nations League ranking kept a playoff door open.

Potter walked through it. In March 2026 his reshaped Sweden beat Ukraine 3-1 in a playoff semifinal in Valencia, then edged Poland 3-2 in the final in Solna to book a place at the finals that had looked lost only months earlier. A fluent Swedish speaker who once took Ostersund from the fourth tier into European competition, Potter had an immediate steadying effect, and he arrived at his first major tournament as a head coach with a group that already trusted him.

The group stage itself was a study in volatility. Sweden opened on June 14 in Monterrey with a 5-1 win over Tunisia, an eye-catching statement of attacking intent that briefly suggested a dark horse had arrived. Six days later reality bit hard, a 1-5 defeat to the Netherlands in Houston that exposed the defensive fragility lurking beneath the goals. The campaign then came down to a final group game against Japan in Arlington on June 25, and Sweden dug out the 1-1 draw they needed to survive, sneaking through as one of the eight best third-placed teams rather than on merit within their own group. Our [Sweden vs Tunisia preview](/2026/06/14/sweden-vs-tunisia-preview/) and [Japan vs Sweden preview](/2026/06/25/japan-vs-sweden-preview/) tracked the two ends of that journey, the flying start and the nervous finish.

The pattern that emerges is a team that scores but does not stop others scoring. Across their tournament so far Sweden had found the net in every game while conceding freely, and they had not kept a clean sheet in their recent run. That is a profile that can trouble weaker or more open opponents and get punished by ruthless ones. Against France, the challenge flips entirely. Sweden will not out-attack Les Bleus over ninety minutes, so the survival plan depends on the one thing they have struggled to deliver, a disciplined defensive night that denies France the space their forwards feast on.

### Did Sweden deserve to reach the knockouts?

By the rules, yes, and that is all that matters now. Sweden qualified as one of the eight best third-placed teams, a legitimate path in the expanded thirty-two-team knockout bracket. They banked a big win over Tunisia and a decisive point against Japan. The manner was nervy, but the ticket is real, and knockout football does not ask how you arrived.

## The head-to-head history of France vs Sweden

These are old acquaintances. France and Sweden have met more than twenty times in their history, and the New Jersey tie will be around their twenty-fourth meeting. France hold a clear historical edge, leading the all-time series by a comfortable margin of roughly twelve wins to six, and they have dominated the fixture in the modern era, winning four of the last five encounters between the nations.

The most recent meeting came in the UEFA Nations League in November 2020, and it followed a script Sweden supporters will recognize with a wince. Sweden struck early through Viktor Claesson inside the opening five minutes, only for France to reassert control and win 4-2, with Olivier Giroud scoring twice, Benjamin Pavard adding another, and Kingsley Coman finishing the job late after Robin Quaison had briefly narrowed the deficit. It was a game that captured the modern dynamic between the sides, Sweden capable of landing a punch, France carrying too much firepower to be contained across a full match.

For the last Swedish win you have to go back to June 2017 and a qualifier for the 2018 World Cup, when Ola Toivonen struck a famous ninety-third-minute winner to beat France 2-1. That result stung at the time, yet both nations still reached the 2018 finals, where France went on to be crowned world champions and Sweden reached the quarter-finals before losing to England. The lesson embedded in that history is instructive for this tie. Sweden have shown they can beat France in a single game, but doing so has required a late moment of the kind that knockout football occasionally grants an underdog, rather than a sustained ninety minutes of superiority.

History, of course, does not play the match, and neither current squad is the one that met in 2020 or 2017. Mbappe was a rising star in those meetings rather than the finished article and record-breaker he is now, and Sweden's team has been rebuilt almost from scratch under Potter. Still, the broad shape of the rivalry, France as the heavy favorite who usually finds a way, Sweden as the dangerous outsider who needs a moment, maps neatly onto the pre-match picture in New Jersey.

### Have France and Sweden ever met in a World Cup knockout?

Not until now. Their previous meetings have come in qualifiers, the Nations League, and friendlies rather than at a World Cup finals, which makes this New Jersey tie a first. That novelty matters a little for Sweden, because it means there is no scarring French tournament memory to overcome, only a long league and qualifying history that France have largely controlled.

## France's attacking riches present an impossible problem

The defining feature of this France side is not a single star but a surplus of them, an attacking department so deep that Deschamps can leave a Ballon d'Or contender on the bench and lose almost nothing. That depth is the reason France are favored not just to win this tie but to go a long way in the tournament, and it is the specific problem Sweden must solve.

At the center sits Kylian Mbappe, the France captain and, at twenty-seven, already the most decorated forward of his generation. He is his country's all-time leading scorer, having overtaken Olivier Giroud, and he reached his one hundredth cap during the group stage. His four goals across the Group I games, two against Senegal and two against Iraq, carried him to sixteen World Cup goals, level with Miroslav Klose's men's tournament record and within touching distance of history. A Real Madrid player since 2024, Mbappe combines top-end speed with a finisher's calm, and in a knockout game his ability to turn a single half-chance into a goal is exactly the quality that decides tight ties. Sweden cannot man-mark him out of the game, because dropping a defender to shadow him simply opens space for the players around him.

Those players would headline most other squads. Ousmane Dembele, the reigning Ballon d'Or winner, arrived in North America at the peak of his career and underlined it with a first-half hat trick against Norway. His pace and directness from the right flank force opponents to defend deep and reactively, and he stretches a back line horizontally in a way that creates the central gaps Mbappe exploits. On the other side, or through the middle, Deschamps can call on Michael Olise, a genuine Ballon d'Or contender in his own right after a season of high performances at Bayern Munich. Olise is a different kind of threat, a technically devastating creator who manipulates the ball in tight areas and picks passes that unlock a low block, precisely the sort of player who can break down a team that sits deep and defends its box.

Behind and around that trio the options keep coming. Bradley Barcola offers electric running and a left-footed finish from the right. Desire Doue, a Champions League winner with Paris Saint-Germain, brings fearless dribbling and a knack for the big occasion. Rayan Cherki adds invention off the bench, Marcus Thuram gives France a more physical center-forward profile, and Jean-Philippe Mateta offers a different focal point again. The message for Sweden is uncomfortable. There is no single player to stop, no obvious way to reduce France's threat to a manageable size. Contain one and another steps forward, and the front line's combined ability to hurt teams in transition means the danger arrives in the seconds after France win possession, before a defense can reset.

That transition threat is the tactical heart of the matter. France are content to cede the ball and defend with a compact block, then spring forward at speed the instant they regain it. Sweden's most likely route into the game, holding possession and probing, therefore carries an inbuilt risk, because every attack they commit numbers to invites the counter that France are built to launch. The riches in Deschamps' forward line are not just about who scores. They are about how quickly and how often France can convert a defensive moment into a shooting chance, and that clock is the one Sweden must somehow slow.

## Deschamps' system and the likely France lineup

Deschamps has settled on a pragmatic 4-3-3 that can shade into a 4-2-3-1 against stronger opponents, a structure designed to keep France solid through the spine while liberating the forwards ahead of it. The base is defensive control, a back four protected by a disciplined midfield, with the creativity and the goals expected to come from the front three and the runners breaking beyond them.

In goal, Mike Maignan is the established number one, a commanding presence with the distribution to start France's transitions cleanly. The back four picks itself in broad terms even if the exact names rotate, built around the elite central pairing options of William Saliba, Ibrahima Konate and Dayot Upamecano, with Jules Kounde a reliable presence at right-back and the left-back berth shared among Lucas Digne and the Hernandez options. This is a defense stocked with players who start for Arsenal, Liverpool, Barcelona and Bayern Munich, and its collective pace lets France defend with a high line when they choose and drop into a mid-block when they prefer, a flexibility that matters against two quick Swedish center-forwards.

The midfield is where Deschamps balances the team. Aurelien Tchouameni offers the deep-lying control and defensive screening that lets the fullbacks push on, while the veteran presence of N'Golo Kante, still influential at thirty-five, gives France ball-winning intensity in the middle third. Adrien Rabiot adds legs and late runs, Manu Kone brings energy, and Warren Zaire-Emery offers youthful drive. The selection here often tells you how Deschamps reads an opponent. A more conservative midfield trio signals respect and a plan to control risk, while a more adventurous one signals intent to overwhelm. Against a Sweden side that will likely sit deep, France may prioritize ball progression and control over pure destruction.

Up front, the only genuine debate is who partners Mbappe, not whether he plays. Dembele's form makes him close to undroppable on current evidence, and the second flank becomes a selection headache of the best kind, Olise, Barcola and Doue all pressing their claims. Deschamps has the luxury of choosing horses for courses, and against a low block he may lean toward the players who best combine in tight spaces. Whatever eleven he picks, the shape and the intent stay the same, and readers should treat any predicted lineup as provisional and confirm it against the official team news, since Deschamps has rotated freely with the group won and may again manage minutes with an eye on the deeper rounds.

Expect a 4-3-3 built on Maignan in goal, an elite center-back pairing shielded by Tchouameni, and a front line led by Mbappe and Dembele. The final attacking spot is a genuine choice among Olise, Barcola and Doue, and Deschamps may rotate parts of the midfield and defense, so treat any eleven as provisional until team news lands.

## Potter's Sweden and the plan to survive

Graham Potter has rebuilt Sweden in barely more than half a year, and the side that arrives in New Jersey is recognizably his, organized, possession-comfortable when allowed, and shaped around a front line good enough to punish anyone who switches off. Potter is the only English manager at this World Cup and just the second foreign coach in Sweden's history, following George Raynor, who led the nation to the 1958 final on home soil. His squad depth is the strongest Sweden have carried in years, and he has options across the pitch.

The team is captained by Victor Lindelof, a versatile defender who added a European trophy with Aston Villa this season to a career that has taken in Benfica and Manchester United. Lindelof is the calm organizer at the back, and Sweden will need every ounce of that composure to hold a shape against France's movement. Around him Potter can call on Isak Hien and Carl Starfelt in central defense and full-backs comfortable in a back three or a back four, and the manager has shown a willingness to use a three-at-the-back structure built for a compact, counter-attacking game.

The reason Sweden are never a soft touch, and the reason this tie is not a foregone conclusion, is the striking department. Alexander Isak, a Liverpool forward, and Viktor Gyokeres, at Arsenal, give Potter one of the most dangerous center-forward pairings at the entire tournament. Isak offers silk, a graceful finisher who drifts into pockets and strikes cleanly with either foot. Gyokeres offers steel, a relentless runner whose goals dragged Sweden through the playoffs and whose willingness to attack the channels stretches defenses vertically. Together they represent the specific threat France must respect, because two forwards of that caliber only need one lapse to produce the goal that turns a knockout tie on its head.

The supporting cast has genuine quality too. Anthony Elanga provides pace and directness in wide areas, Lucas Bergvall and Yasin Ayari bring Premier League class and legs to the midfield, and Mattias Svanberg offers control. The notable absentee is Dejan Kulusevski, left out with a long-term knee issue, a blow to Sweden's creativity that Potter has had to work around. The manager's task in this tie is less about how to beat France on the ball and more about how to stay in the game long enough for his strikers to matter. That means a disciplined block, minimal risk in possession near their own goal, and the patience to absorb pressure without conceding the early goal that would force them to chase.

### Which Sweden player is most likely to trouble France?

Alexander Isak. The Liverpool striker has the movement to find gaps between France's center-backs and the finishing to punish the half-chance a low block is designed to create. Viktor Gyokeres runs him close, but Isak's ability to score from almost nothing is exactly the profile that gives underdogs a puncher's chance in a knockout tie.

## The tactical battle that will decide the tie

Strip the fixture to its essentials and it becomes a contest between France's transition game and Sweden's ability to deny space. France want the game stretched and fast, with room to run behind. Sweden want it compressed and slow, with bodies between the ball and their goal. Whoever imposes their preferred rhythm will most likely control the night.

France's plan barely needs disguising. Deschamps will let Sweden have the ball in areas that do not hurt, invite them forward, and then attack the space behind Sweden's committed players the moment possession turns over. With Mbappe, Dembele and whoever completes the front line, France carry the fastest and most clinical transition threat in the tournament, and the more Sweden push for the game, the more that threat is fed. The counter-intuitive risk for Potter is that possession itself can be a trap against this opponent. Every Swedish attack that breaks down thirty yards from the France goal is an invitation for Les Bleus to sprint at a stretched back line.

Sweden's counter to that is structure and patience. A back three or a deep back four, two banks that stay compact, and full-backs or wing-backs who resist the urge to fly forward. If Sweden can keep the game between the boxes, force France to break them down through a packed central area rather than in open space, and avoid conceding the early goal that would blow the plan apart, they give themselves a platform. From that platform, the strategy is to reach the final quarter still level and then trust Isak and Gyokeres to produce a moment on the counter or from a set piece. Dead balls are a real avenue here, because they are the one phase where Sweden's physicality and France's occasional lapses in concentration could meet.

The set-piece dimension deserves emphasis. In open play France's talent advantage is enormous, but set plays flatten hierarchies, and Sweden have the aerial presence to make corners and free-kicks a genuine weapon. If the tie is tight into the closing stages, a Swedish set piece is one of the more plausible routes to a goal that the underdog can engineer rather than merely hope for. France, for their part, will know this and will need the concentration to defend their box with the same discipline they show in their pressing.

There is also the matter of game state and temperament. France have not had to chase a game all tournament, and it is fair to wonder how they would respond if Sweden did manage to lead. A side accustomed to control can find frustration creeping in when an opponent refuses to open up. Deschamps' management, the calm to keep probing rather than force it, and the quality of France's bench to change a stubborn game, would then become decisive. On the other side, Potter's challenge is to keep his players believing in a low-event game plan even as France pile up possession and territory, because the temptation to step out and chase the ball is exactly what France want to provoke.

### How will France break Sweden down if the game is tight?

Through their creators and their bench. Olise's ability to unlock a low block with a single pass, Dembele's dribbling to draw defenders and create overloads, and Mbappe's movement to find a yard between center-backs are the primary keys. If those fail early, France's depth lets Deschamps introduce fresh match-winners, a luxury Sweden simply cannot match.

## The France platform: defense and midfield control

It is tempting to reduce France to their forwards, but the reason those forwards flourish is the platform beneath them. Deschamps has built a spine that lets the attackers gamble, because it rarely leaves them exposed, and against Sweden that solidity is as important as the star names further up the pitch.

Mike Maignan sets the tone from the back. A goalkeeper of the first rank, he offers not only shot-stopping but the calm distribution that begins France's transitions, rolling or driving the ball into the feet of a midfielder who can turn defense into attack in two passes. In front of him, the center-back options are elite by any measure. William Saliba has grown into one of Europe's most complete defenders at Arsenal, comfortable defending a high line and stepping into midfield to break up play. Ibrahima Konate brings raw power and recovery pace, the ideal profile for snuffing out the long balls Sweden will aim at Isak and Gyokeres. Dayot Upamecano adds aggression and front-foot defending. Whichever pairing Deschamps selects, France defend the middle of the pitch with athletes who can match Sweden's strikers for speed and strength, which is precisely the matchup Potter needs to win and will find hard to.

At full-back, Jules Kounde offers positional intelligence and the ability to tuck inside to form a back three in possession, freeing a winger higher up. On the opposite flank the options give Deschamps a choice between the attacking thrust of an overlapping full-back and the security of a more reserved one, a decision likely to hinge on how much width Sweden commit to their own attack. The key point is that France can defend their flanks without sacrificing their attacking shape, because the personnel are versatile enough to shift structures within a single game.

The midfield is the quiet engine of everything. Aurelien Tchouameni is the fulcrum, a deep-lying controller who screens the back four, wins the second balls, and recycles possession to keep France's shape intact. His presence is what allows the full-backs to advance and the forwards to stay high, because he covers the space they vacate. Around him, N'Golo Kante remains a remarkable competitor even in the twilight of his career, his reading of the game and relentless energy giving France a ball-winner who can disrupt Sweden's rare moments of rhythm. Adrien Rabiot adds height, late runs, and a goal threat from deep, while younger options like Manu Kone and Warren Zaire-Emery offer legs and drive when Deschamps wants to raise the tempo.

The collective effect is a team that can control a game without needing the ball for long stretches, then accelerate through the gears when the moment arrives. For Sweden, this is the uncomfortable truth beneath the headline about France's forwards. Even if Potter's players find a way to blunt Mbappe and company for a spell, they will still have to break through a defensive and midfield structure that concedes little and turns defense into attack faster than almost anyone. The platform is why France are favored to control the tie, and why a Swedish upset would have to be built on moments rather than sustained superiority.

## Sweden's defensive record and the vulnerability France will target

If there is a single statistic that frames Sweden's challenge, it is their inability to keep the ball out of their own net. Across their tournament and recent run, Sweden had scored in every game while conceding freely, and they had not managed a clean sheet in weeks. That profile is the crack in the plan, because the entire survival blueprint against France depends on defending for long periods without conceding, and the evidence suggests that is the very thing this Sweden side finds hardest.

The heavy defeat to the Netherlands in the group stage was the clearest warning. Against a technically strong European side willing to move the ball quickly and attack the spaces, Sweden's defensive structure came apart, and they shipped goals in a manner that will have concerned Potter deeply. France are, if anything, a more ruthless version of that same threat, quicker in transition and more clinical in front of goal. The lesson Potter must take from the Netherlands game is that Sweden cannot afford an open, end-to-end contest, because in that kind of game France's superior finishing will tell decisively.

That is why the low block is not a preference but a necessity. Sweden's only realistic path is to compress the game, deny France the space behind, and force them to break down a packed defense through patience rather than pace. The problem is that doing so for ninety minutes and potentially beyond, against attackers of this caliber, requires a level of concentration and organization Sweden have not consistently shown. One lapse of focus, one full-back caught too high, one central gap opened by a stepped-out midfielder, and France's forwards will punish it. The margin for error is close to zero.

Potter's counter-argument is that his team defends better as a unit when it accepts the underdog role and sits deep with intent, rather than being drawn into a game it cannot win on the ball. There is truth in that. A team that commits fully to defending, that packs bodies into the box and refuses to be lured out, is a harder nut to crack than one caught between pressing and dropping. Sweden also have Lindelof, an organizer with the experience to hold a line together under pressure, and a goalkeeper who will need to have the game of his life. If Sweden are to stay in this, it will be because their defensive discipline finally matches the occasion, and because they resist the temptation to chase a game that punishes ambition.

For France, the vulnerability is an invitation. Deschamps' players will know that Sweden have leaked goals, that their defense can be stretched and split, and that sustained pressure tends to produce the error France need. The plan will be to keep the ball moving, shift Sweden from side to side, and wait for the gap that a tiring, retreating defense inevitably offers. France do not need to force it early. Their quality gives them the patience to build, and the depth to introduce fresh attackers when Sweden's legs begin to go. It is a matchup of France's ruthlessness against Sweden's fragility, and on the evidence of the group stage, that is a matchup France should win.

## The set-piece battle and the underdog's best weapon

In open play the gap between these teams is wide, but set pieces are the great equalizer of knockout football, and they represent Sweden's most plausible route to engineering a goal rather than merely hoping for one. Dead-ball situations flatten the talent hierarchy, because they turn on height, timing, delivery, and concentration rather than on the fluid brilliance that separates France from most opponents.
