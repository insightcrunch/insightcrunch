Marxist economics is a body of academic economics, practised in universities, published in refereed journals, and argued over at conferences, whose subject is not the allocation of scarce resources but capitalism itself: how that system produces and distributes its wealth, where its profits come from, why it expands and then contracts, and how the people inside it might one day replace it with something else. It is the oldest heterodox tradition in the discipline, the one against which several of the discipline's own categories were first sharpened, and it is also, by the standards of the profession's centre, a marginal field, taught in a minority of departments and largely absent from the journals that confer prestige. Both facts belong in the opening paragraph, because an honest guide has to hold them together. This is a real research literature, with named schools, settled findings in places, and live disputes in others, and it is a literature that the mainstream of economics has mostly ceased to read.

The single most useful thing a newcomer can learn is the thing almost no summary teaches. Marxist economics is not one position. It is a family of at least eight schools that share an ancestor, a vocabulary, and a set of questions, while disagreeing with one another, sometimes bitterly, about the foundations: about whether the magnitudes of Marx's value theory exist independently of the prices that are supposed to express them, about whether the rate of profit has a built-in tendency to fall, about whether the labour theory of value is the keystone of the whole structure or a scaffolding that can be taken down once the building stands. This guide follows the programme not doctrine rule from its first page to its last. Whenever a sentence would have to claim that Marxist economists believe some proposition, the sentence is false of a substantial number of them, and the honest move is to say which schools hold the view, which reject it, and what each side thinks is at stake.

## Marxist economics as a research programme

Begin with the definition the tradition would offer of itself. Marxist economics is the research programme that analyses capitalism as a historically specific mode of production organised around the extraction and accumulation of surplus value, studying how the system reproduces itself, how its product is divided among the classes that produce and appropriate it, and why it tends toward crisis. Every term in that sentence does work, and the sections below unpack them one at a time. A mode of production is a durable arrangement of who owns the means of production, who works, and who takes the surplus the work creates. Surplus value is the tradition's name for the portion of the working day whose product goes to the owners rather than to the workers. Reproduction is the process by which the whole arrangement, its factories and its workforce and its class relations, is rebuilt period after period so that production can continue. Crisis is not an accident that befalls the system from outside but a breakdown generated by the system's own normal operation.

### Why call it a programme rather than a doctrine?

A doctrine is a set of conclusions to be defended; a programme is a set of questions, methods, and founding commitments within which conclusions are contested. The schools surveyed below accept the same ancestor and overlapping questions while reaching incompatible answers about value, profit rates, and crisis, which is the behaviour of a programme, not the recitation of a doctrine.

The vocabulary comes from the philosophy of science, where a research programme is identified by a hard core of commitments its practitioners will not surrender without leaving the programme, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary theories they will revise, replace, or abandon under pressure. Apply that template here and the tradition's behaviour becomes intelligible. The hard core is thin: capitalism is a specific historical system rather than the natural condition of humankind; profit originates in production rather than in exchange; the division of society into classes is a structural fact about positions in production, not a ranking of incomes; and the system's motion is governed by tendencies that work themselves out through counteracting influences rather than by iron laws with dates attached. Everything else, the status of value magnitudes, the correct theory of crisis, the role of money, the reading of Marx's texts themselves, belongs to the belt, and the belt is where the schools fight. A reader who grasps this two-level structure can predict, for any new dispute inside the tradition, which kind of dispute it is: a belt dispute, however heated, leaves the participants inside one programme, while a core dispute marks a departure, a conversion, or the founding of something else.

This also explains a feature of the literature that baffles outsiders, its combination of scholastic textual argument with technical economics. Because the founding texts are genuinely ambiguous at several of the contested points, and because the founder left his system unfinished, arguments about what Marx meant are simultaneously arguments about what is true, and the tradition has never been able to separate exegesis from research as cleanly as older sciences separate commentary from experiment. The schools are therefore also reading communities, each with a characteristic way of handling the canonical pages, and several of the entries in the eight schools table below are distinguished as much by their hermeneutics as by their economics. That is a weakness by the standards of a mature science, and this guide will say so plainly when it reaches the strongest objection; it is also a source of the tradition's unusual longevity, since each generation can reopen the foundations without leaving the field.

### What do the eight schools actually share?

They share an ancestor, a canonical text, and a problem set: the origin of profit, the reproduction of the system, the source of its crises, and the distribution of its product among classes. They also share a refusal, the refusal to treat capitalism as the natural form of economic life rather than as one historical arrangement among others.

Below the shared problem set sits a shared analytical grammar. All eight schools distinguish between the production of wealth and its appropriation, between what a society makes and who ends up owning it, and all eight treat that distinction as the master key to the system's behaviour. All eight periodise: they divide capitalism's history into phases with different dynamics, rather than projecting one model economy across all centuries. All eight treat distribution as a matter of conflict between groups with opposing interests at the point of production, not as a technical outcome of marginal products settling quietly into place. And all eight, whatever their other differences, read the economy as a totality whose parts constrain one another, so that a theory of wages that ignores profits, or a theory of money that ignores production, is defective by construction. These are substantial agreements. They are what makes the disagreements legible as disagreements within one enterprise rather than as eight separate enterprises sharing a surname, and they are exactly what a student must separate from the disputes when first approaching the field: the shared ground explains why the schools argue with each other at all, and the disputes explain why they cannot simply merge.

## The names the field goes by

A reader searching for this literature will meet it under several labels, and the labels are not perfectly interchangeable. Marxist economics is the plainest name and the one this guide uses: it announces descent from Marx and membership in the tradition that claims him. Marxian economics is the cooler term favoured by scholars who work with Marx's categories while keeping a distance from the movements that carried his name; in the journals the distinction is often a matter of temperament rather than of content, though some writers use it to signal that their allegiance is to the arguments and not to any party's version of them. Marxist political economy stresses the older name of the discipline itself, political economy, the tradition of Smith and Ricardo within which Marx consciously worked, and it is the label under which much of the field's teaching is actually timetabled. Radical political economy is the broader American umbrella under which Marxist work has often sheltered institutionally, alongside feminist, institutionalist, and other heterodox currents that share its journals and conferences without sharing its foundations. Heterodox Marxist economics places the field inside the wider archipelago of non-mainstream approaches, useful for orientation, though the schools inside the tradition rarely describe themselves that way. None of these labels maps neatly onto the eight schools, and none should be mistaken for a school in itself; they are flags of convenience flown over a literature whose real divisions run along the lines the table draws.

## An object of study the mainstream does not pose

The deepest difference between this tradition and the economics of the textbooks is not political. It is a difference about what there is to study. The mainstream defines its subject by a universal problem: human wants are unlimited, means are scarce, and economics is the science of allocating those means among competing ends. That definition, given its canonical form by Lionel Robbins in the nineteen thirties, deliberately detaches economics from any particular social arrangement, so that a Crusoe, a household, a firm, and a planned economy all fall under the same science. The Marxist tradition refuses the detachment. Its object is not economising in general but one historically specific way of organising production, the way in which the means of production are privately owned, labour is bought and sold as a commodity, and production is undertaken for profit through sale. Where the mainstream sees the market as the general form of rational allocation, this tradition sees a specific institution with a birthday, a geography, and, built into its own account, a possible end.

### How does Marxist economics differ from mainstream economics?

Mainly in its object. Mainstream economics studies the allocation of scarce resources among competing uses. Marxist economics studies capitalism as a specific historical system, asking where profit originates, how the system reproduces itself, and why it generates recurrent crises and distributional conflict.

Everything else follows from that choice of object. A science of allocation can be indifferent to where the resources came from and to whom they initially belong, since its questions begin after the endowments are given. A science of capitalism cannot be indifferent, because its first questions are precisely about the endowments: how a class came to own the means of production, how another class came to have nothing to sell but its capacity to work, and how that separation is renewed every day by the ordinary operation of the labour market. Distribution, which the mainstream treats as an outcome to be read off from technology and preferences, is here an input as much as an output, written into the structure before any market opens. Crisis, which allocation economics tends to model as a disturbance arriving from outside the system, a shock, a policy error, a failure of expectations, is here treated as a product of the system's own success conditions, arising from profitability, accumulation, and the relations between departments of production. And the mainstream itself must be described fairly in a guide of this kind: it is a serious research programme with its own hard core, its own formidable technical achievements in the theory of choice, of games, of allocation under uncertainty, and its own internal dissents. The Marxist claim is not that mainstream economics is mere apologetics, a charge some of its own polemics have made and which this guide does not repeat. The claim is narrower and stronger: that there are questions about capitalism as a system, its origin of profit, its reproduction, its crises, which the allocation framework does not pose, and that a tradition exists which poses them.

### What does it mean to study an economy historically?

It means treating the institutions other approaches take as given, private property in the means of production, wage labour, the market itself, as products of a specific past that can be dated, compared with earlier and later arrangements, and expected to change, rather than as the permanent furniture of human social life.

The historical method has concrete consequences for how arguments are built. Categories are required to earn their generality: the concept of capital, for instance, is not applied to any tool or stock of goods in any century, but reserved for wealth deployed in the specific circuit of buying in order to sell dearer, so that a plough in a feudal village and a plough in a capitalist enterprise are different economic objects despite being the same physical object. Evidence is correspondingly historical: the tradition's characteristic works move between theoretical chapters and long empirical narratives about enclosures, factories, colonial trade, and the legal construction of the labour market, because the theory claims that the system's categories were themselves made in that history. Comparison across modes of production does the work that comparison across equilibria does elsewhere: slavery, feudalism, and capitalism are treated as different logics of surplus extraction, and the differences among the logics are the data against which claims about capitalism's specificity are tested. A reader who understands this method will understand why the tradition's books are so long, and why its practitioners regard a model without a history as a photograph of a river that claims the river does not flow.

## The five distinguishing commitments

Programmes are recognised by what their practitioners will not give up, and five commitments mark this one off from every neighbouring approach. They are stated here in the form the whole tradition, with local variations, would accept; the schools begin to diverge only when the commitments are developed into theories, which is why the table in a later section can use them as its fixed background. A reader who masters these five points possesses the grammar of the field, and can follow an argument in any of its schools even when the conclusions surprise.

The first commitment is that value is a social relation. The second is that profit originates in production, in the unpaid portion of the working day, rather than in exchange, in risk, or in the mere passage of time. The third is that capitalism is a historically specific system governed by tendencies, not the universal form of economic life. The fourth is that the proper analytical frame is reproduction and dynamics, the system's motion through time, rather than equilibrium, its imaginary rest. The fifth is that money is constitutive of the system's workings, not a veil drawn over real magnitudes that would behave identically without it. Each commitment contradicts a corresponding default of the mainstream, which is why the two traditions so often argue past one another: they are not giving different answers to the same questions so much as refusing each other's questions.

### Why does the tradition treat value as a social relation?

Because on this account value is not a property objects have by nature, like weight, nor a feeling consumers have about them, like desire. It is the form taken by the social relation among producers when their labour is coordinated through exchange, which is why the theory begins with the commodity and its form rather than with individual choice.

Develop the point and its polemical edge appears. If value were a natural property of things, the distribution of wealth would be a chapter of physics, and if it were only a registration of subjective desire, it would be a chapter of psychology; in neither case could it support a theory of class. By treating value as the social form that labour takes when producers work independently and meet only through the market, the tradition makes the economy's most basic magnitude into a relation among people, appearing, in Marx's famous image, as a relation among things. The celebrated discussion of commodity fetishism is the working out of this commitment: the social character of private labours shows itself to the labourers only in the exchange ratios of their products, so the relations of producers assume, in their eyes, the fantastic form of relations between objects. Schools differ sharply over how much weight this form analysis can bear, the value-form current building nearly everything on it, the orthodox current subordinating it to magnitudes, but none treats value as a stuff contained in goods the way calories are contained in food. That shared refusal is one of the quietest and most complete agreements in the field.

### Where does profit come from on this account?

From production, not from exchange. The worker sells the capacity to labour for a wage that covers the cost of reproducing that capacity, and then works longer than the time that wage represents, so the working day divides into a paid portion and an unpaid portion whose product the employer appropriates.

The machinery behind this answer deserves a paragraph, because it is the engine of the entire tradition and the point where its critics press hardest. The commodity the worker sells is labour power, the capacity to work, and like every commodity on the theory's own terms it has a value set by what its production costs, which for a capacity housed in a living person means the cost of the food, housing, clothing, training, and rest that keep the worker and the worker's family going. The use of this commodity, however, is labour itself, and labour is the activity that creates value. The working day therefore splits into necessary labour, the hours in which the worker produces the equivalent of the wage, and surplus labour, the hours in which the worker produces value for which no equivalent is paid. Marx names the measure of this division the rate of surplus value, surplus value divided by variable capital, the capital laid out on wages. The whole transaction is compatible with perfectly fair exchange, with every commodity bought and sold at its value, and that is the argument's sting: exploitation here is not cheating, not underpayment, not monopoly pricing, but a property of the wage relation itself when it works exactly as designed. Readers who want the full construction, its textual basis, and its critics should follow it into the companion account of [surplus value and its measure](/2010/11/01/surplus-value-complete-guide/), since the present guide's business is the field that grew from the claim rather than the claim's own proof.

The third commitment, historical specificity, has already been met in the discussion of the object of study, but its companion idea belongs here: the system's laws are tendencies. A tendency, in this vocabulary, is a pressure generated by the structure that asserts itself through counteracting influences, so that its presence is compatible with long stretches in which the observed magnitude moves the other way. The falling tendency of the rate of profit, the concentration of capital into fewer hands, the formation of a reserve army of unemployed labour: each is advanced as a tendency whose operation can be checked, delayed, or masked by countertendencies the theory itself is required to list. Critics regard this as an evasion device, a way of keeping a prediction while surrendering its falsifiability, and the charge is answered, or not, school by school. Defenders reply that every science of complex systems speaks this way, that medicine and meteorology are full of tendencies disciplined by stated counteracting causes, and that the demand for point predictions confuses economics with ballistics. The dispute is genuine and unresolved, and it returns in this guide's account of the field's marginality, since the profession's refereeing standards were built around a stricter idea of what a law should look like.

### Why does the tradition begin with reproduction rather than equilibrium?

Because its central question is how a system organised around private production and wage labour manages to continue at all, period after period, replacing its inputs, its workforce, and its ruling arrangements. Reproduction makes the continuation of capitalism itself the puzzle, where equilibrium analysis takes the system's existence as the background and studies adjustments within it.

The reproduction frame has a technical heart, the schemes Marx sketched in the second volume of *Capital*, where the economy is divided into a department producing means of production and a department producing means of consumption, and the question is posed in physical and value terms at once: what proportions must hold between the departments for each to sell its output and buy its inputs, so that the whole machine starts the next period intact or enlarged? Those schemes, the subject of a later article in this cluster, are the seed from which whole schools grew: the disproportionality theory of crisis reads them as a proof of how easily the proportions fail; the reproduction of class relations, the daily and generational renewal of a workforce that must sell its labour power, becomes in other hands a theory of education, family, and the state. Even where the schemes themselves are not used, their question persists as the tradition's reflex: not what clears this market, but what has to be true for this system to wake up tomorrow as the same system. Equilibrium reasoning is not absent from the field, several schools use it heavily, but it is demoted to a tool, where the mainstream enthrones it as the picture of what an economy is.

### Why is money more than a veil in this framework?

Because production for exchange means that social validation arrives only when a commodity is sold, so money is the form in which private labour discovers whether it counted as social labour at all. A theory in which money merely lubricates barter cannot represent unsold output, hoarding, or the specifically monetary form a crisis takes.

Classical monetary theory in the mainstream tradition long treated money as a neutral convenience layered over a real economy of goods and preferences, so that the price level could double without anything real changing. The Marxist tradition starts from the opposite end. Because producers work privately and independently, their labour is not directly social; it becomes social only when the product is exchanged, and the universal form in which that validation is registered is money. Money is therefore not an afterthought but the necessary form of appearance of value in a society of private producers, and its peculiar powers, to be hoarded, to interrupt the circuit of sales and purchases, to make sale and purchase fall apart in time and place, are the seeds of crisis in their most abstract form. A crisis, on this account, is never merely a shortage of things; it is characteristically a scramble for money, a moment when everyone discovers simultaneously that claims denominated in money cannot all be honoured. Schools divide over the ancestry of this doctrine, the monetary readings of value building their whole reconstruction on it, but the commitment itself, money as constitutive, is part of the hard core, and it is one reason the tradition's practitioners found the financial crises of the modern era less surprising in form, whatever they missed in timing, than colleagues for whom finance was a veil over fundamentals.

## The inheritance: what Marx left, and what he left open

Every school in this field is, among other things, a strategy for completing an unfinished building. Marx published one volume of *Capital* in his lifetime, the first, in 1867, and spent the remaining sixteen years of his life drafting, redrafting, and researching the continuation while his health failed. After his death Friedrich Engels assembled the second volume from manuscripts of very different dates and states of finish, and then the third, the volume that contains the theory of prices of production, the falling tendency of the profit rate, and the treatment of interest and rent, from notebooks that were never prepared for the press. A fourth body of material, the notebooks on the history of economic thought, appeared in the twentieth century as *Theories of Surplus Value*, and the great draft of 1857 and 1858, the *Grundrisse*, reached print only on the eve of the Second World War and English readers only in 1973, transforming the field's sense of its founder three generations after his death. The textual basis of the tradition has therefore never stood still: each recovery of manuscripts has redistributed authority among the schools, usually to the embarrassment of whichever orthodoxy had declared the questions closed.

### What did Marx leave unfinished?

Most of the system. The published first volume of Capital analyses production; the circulation volumes were assembled by Engels from drafts after Marx's death, the transformation of values into prices of production was left as a sketch, and the projected books on the state, foreign trade, and the world market were never written at all.

Consider what this incompleteness means practically. The theory of the state, which every later school needed, exists only as asides; the theory of the world market, under which the national cases would have been subsumed, is a plan in a letter; the account of wage labour's global forms stops where the nineteenth century's evidence thinned. Even within the published architecture, joints were left pinned rather than welded: the transformation of values into prices of production is announced, illustrated, and left with its consistency unproven, a gap the next section takes up as the tradition's longest technical quarrel. The result is that fidelity itself is underdetermined. Two scholars can both refuse to abandon a single sentence of Marx and yet build incompatible economics, because the sentences do not add up to a finished system and the choice of which to treat as load-bearing is itself a theoretical act. This is the deep cause of the programme's school structure, deeper than politics or temperament: the inheritance is rich, canonical, and incomplete, and each school is an answer to the question of how to go on. Readers meeting the field for the first time should begin where the tradition begins, with the first volume itself, and the series' [capital volume one complete guide](/2010/06/01/capital-volume-one-complete-guide/) walks through that book chapter by chapter.

## The core categories in prose

The field's vocabulary is an obstacle course for newcomers, and since this guide states its quantities as sentences rather than symbols, it owes the reader the same service for its categories. Constant capital is the portion of capital laid out on means of production, buildings, machinery, raw materials, whose value reappears in the product without expanding, transferred piecemeal as the instruments wear out or wholesale as the materials are consumed. Variable capital is the portion laid out on labour power, and it is called variable because it alone expands in production, the workforce returning more value than its own maintenance costs. The distinction is functional, not physical: the same machine is constant capital in any hands, while the wage bill's variability is the whole secret of the system's surplus. Absolute surplus value is the surplus gained by lengthening the working day; relative surplus value is the surplus gained by shortening the necessary portion of it, cheapening the worker's means of subsistence through rising productivity in the industries that produce them, the mechanism that ties exploitation to technical progress in the theory's engine room. Turnover names the cycle of capital through money, production, and commodities back to money, and its speed matters because a capital that completes the circuit twice in a year extracts its surplus twice on the same advance. Fictitious capital, Marx's most prophetic category, names the tradeable claims on future surplus, shares, bonds, and their modern descendants, whose prices capitalise an expected revenue and can therefore swell to magnitudes with no counterpart in the production that must eventually validate them. Primitive accumulation is the prehistory written in blood and fire in the first volume's final chapters, the expropriation of the direct producers, enclosures, clearances, colonial plunder, by which the two preconditions of the system, owners of money and workers with nothing to sell but themselves, were first assembled. None of these categories requires algebra to state, and all of them carry the theory's signature: each names a social relation in the dress of a thing, and each is defined by its place in the circuit as a whole rather than by the properties of the object it labels.

## Value, price, and the dispute that would not close

The foundation on which the whole programme rests is the labour theory of value, the claim that the exchange ratios among commodities are governed, beneath the noise of supply and demand, by the quantities of socially necessary labour their production requires. That theory has its own article in this series, and the present guide deliberately does not rebuild it: the foundation is set out in [labour theory of value explained](/2010/07/01/labour-theory-of-value-explained/), with its textual basis, its Ricardian ancestry, and the standard objections. What belongs here is the use the field makes of the foundation, and the crack that appeared in it, because the crack organised a century of work.

The crack is the transformation problem. In the first volume of *Capital*, commodities are analysed as exchanging in proportion to the values labour creates. In the third volume, Marx himself observes that competition among capitals equalises profit rates across industries with very different mixes of machinery and labour, so that actual prices of production must deviate systematically from values, high where machinery dominates, low where labour dominates. Marx sketched a procedure for deriving the second set of magnitudes from the first. Within a generation of the third volume's publication, Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk had declared the two volumes contradictory, and Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz had argued, with algebra, that Marx's sketch could not simultaneously preserve the two aggregate identities Marx claimed for it, total price equal to total value and total profit equal to total surplus value. Every subsequent school has had to take a position at this joint: repair the procedure, reinterpret what it was doing, or abandon the claim that prices need values beneath them at all.

### Why has the transformation problem mattered so much?

Because it is the joint where the theory's two levels meet. If the values of the first volume cannot be connected consistently to the prices of production of the third, then either the foundation or the superstructure must be rebuilt, and a century of the tradition's best technical work has been spent at exactly that joint.

The positions taken at the joint define the first three rows of the schools table. The orthodox current holds that Marx's procedure, correctly executed with the texts read whole, does preserve what it must, and that the critics mishandled the construction. The monetary and temporal reconstructions hold that the inconsistency is an artefact of a false assumption shared by Marx's critics and many of his defenders, the assumption that inputs and outputs must be valued simultaneously, and that following the circuit through time, with values expressed in money, dissolves the contradiction. The Sraffian current holds that the detour through values is simply unnecessary, since prices and distribution can be computed from the physical conditions of production directly, and draws the conclusion its founder's most rigorous follower, Ian Steedman, drew in print: that value theory, as a theory of price, is redundant. The dispute is mapped, with each solution's ledger of gains and costs, in the cluster's dedicated treatment, [transformation problem explained](/2016/04/15/transformation-problem-explained/), and a reader who wants the technical heart of the field should go there next.

One magnitude dominates the argument because so much hangs on it. State it in the prose form this guide uses throughout: the rate of profit is surplus value divided by the sum of constant and variable capital, the surplus the workforce creates measured against the total capital, in plant and materials and wages, that the employer must advance to set that workforce in motion. The formula matters because two of the tradition's largest claims are claims about it, that it governs the pace of accumulation, since profit is both the system's motive and its fuel, and that it has a tendency to fall as production mechanises, the claim examined with the crisis theories below. When schools disagree about whether the rate of profit is even well defined, whether it should be computed in values or in prices, in money or in labour time, they are disagreeing about the instrument with which the system's central motion would have to be measured, and the empirical quarrels of the last half century, in which opposed camps computing from the same national accounts announce opposed trends, are unintelligible without that background.

### How do prices enter a theory built on values?

As the necessary form of appearance of value under competition. Capitals of equal size demand equal returns, competition redistributes the surplus among them, and market prices form around prices of production rather than around values directly, so the theory claims to explain the level prices gravitate toward rather than to read values off a price tag.

The defenders of the construction add an empirical wager. If values are the gravitational centres of prices, then across an economy the correlation between commodities' labour contents and their market prices should be strong, even though any single price deviates. A line of empirical work, using input and output tables for several countries, reports exactly such correlations, high enough, its authors argue, that the value theory passes the kind of test its critics demanded. The critics reply that the correlation is nearly guaranteed by the accounting structure, since labour costs dominate most prices whatever theory is true, and that a test everything passes is a test of nothing. The quarrel is characteristic of the field at its best and its most frustrating: a precise question, real evidence, and a disagreement about what the evidence is evidence of, conducted for decades in the journals named in the institutional map below.

## The eight schools table: one inheritance, eight readings

The eight schools table gathers the field's main currents into a single view. Read it with the programme not doctrine rule in mind: the rows are not factions competing for a trademark, they are eight different answers to the question of how to continue an unfinished research programme, and their disagreements are the field's real content. Two cautions before the rows. First, the count of eight is a working map, not a census: merge the two reconstructions in the second row and the count falls, count the autonomist current, given its own discussion below the table, as a full school of economics and it rises, and the honest statement is that the tradition contains at least eight rival schools. Second, the figures named are the schools' founders and canonical representatives, named so the reader can find the literature, and their presence in a cell is identification, not endorsement.

### What does the orthodox school defend that the others surrender?

The reality of Marx's value magnitudes themselves. Where other schools redefine, reinterpret, or discard the categories of the first volume, the orthodox current insists that values, surplus value, and the tendency of the profit rate to fall are features of the capitalist economy that the texts describe correctly and that research should measure rather than revise away.

The orthodox or fundamentalist current is the tradition's memory and its conscience. Its scholars, Ernest Mandel above all in the postwar decades, with Paul Mattick arguing the same fidelity from a council communist distance, held that the apparent inconsistencies in Marx dissolve when the texts are read in their full dialectical movement rather than as a failed exercise in simultaneous algebra. On this view the transformation is not a calculation Marx botched but a real process the theory tracks, the redistribution of a fixed mass of surplus value among capitals by competition, and the two aggregate equalities are not bookkeeping identities to be imposed but claims about that process. The current's strength is its command of the whole corpus and of economic history: Mandel's great surveys of late capitalism attempted to write the twentieth century with the categories unmodified, periodising by long waves of expansion and stagnation driven by the profit rate's motion. Its weakness is the mirror of its strength. A method that can always answer a technical objection by ascending to a more complete reading of the texts risks immunising itself against the one verdict it claims to respect, the verdict of the evidence, and its critics inside the tradition have pressed exactly there: a consistency that only the faithful can perceive is functionally indistinguishable from the inconsistency it denies. The school's empirical wing, computing profit rates from national accounts over long runs, is its answer to that charge, and the ferocity of the disputes over those computations shows how much both sides think is at stake.

### What do the monetary and temporal reconstructions agree on?

On the location of the error. Both hold that the famous inconsistency in the transformation arises from valuing inputs and outputs simultaneously, at a single moment, and both repair it by following the circuit through time, with the monetary reading adding that value added in production is expressed in money from the start rather than converted afterwards.

The New Interpretation, associated with Duncan Foley and with Gérard Duménil's parallel work in France, begins from money. Value added, on this reading, is not a sum of labour hours waiting to be priced; it is the money sum the economy actually adds, and the value of labour power is the wage share of that sum, so the two aggregate equalities hold by construction and exploitation appears as the difference between the labour performed and the labour the wage commands. The temporal single-system interpretation, developed by Alan Freeman, Andrew Kliman, and their collaborators, attacks the same joint from the side of time: inputs enter production at the prices paid for them in the previous period, outputs emerge with values determined in this one, and the alleged contradiction arises only when a model forces both to be valued at once, an assumption the critics imported and Marx never made. Read temporally, the falling tendency of the profit rate also survives the famous counterargument, due to Nobuo Okishio, that cost-cutting innovation chosen by capitalists must raise the general profit rate; that theorem, the reconstructions note, depends on the simultaneous valuation they reject. The family's critics, including able ones inside the tradition, reply that consistency achieved by redefining the terms is consistency at a discount, and that a value theory whose magnitudes are read off from money sums has quietly surrendered the claim that labour time regulates anything. The quarrel is the field's most technical and, for its participants, its most consequential, since it decides whether the founder's construction stands as built or only as rebuilt.

### How did Sraffa change the terms of the debate?

By demonstrating that relative prices and either the wage or the profit rate can be calculated from the technical conditions of production and the surplus, with no detour through labour values. The result did not refute the value theory from outside; it argued, from within the classical tradition Marx inherited, that the detour was unnecessary.

Piero Sraffa's *Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities*, published in 1960 after three decades of gestation, is a short book with long consequences. Working in the classical line of Smith and Ricardo that Marx also inherited, Sraffa showed that given the methods of production in use and the physical surplus they yield, prices and one distributive variable, wage or profit rate, determine each other, and that the two distributive variables trade off along a frontier the system's technology fixes. For Marxist economics the shock was double. The construction vindicated the classical surplus approach, the vision of distribution as a division of a produced surplus between classes, against the marginalist alternative, and Sraffa's followers, Pierangelo Garegnani prominent among them, developed it explicitly as the rigorous core of a Marxian economics. But the same construction made labour values look like a redundant middle step, since the prices could be reached without them, and Ian Steedman pressed that conclusion in 1977 with a rigour the tradition could not dismiss: if the physical data give the prices, the values are a fifth wheel. The ensuing quarrel, between those who accepted the Sraffian core and rebuilt exploitation as a property of the surplus division, and those who insisted that value theory answers questions about social form that no price system can pose, is the point at which the field's centre of gravity visibly shifted from defending Marx's text to choosing which of Marx's questions to keep. The cluster returns to this current in its own article; here it is enough to register that no school after Sraffa could proceed as if the redundancy challenge had not been issued.

### What does the monopoly capital school say giant firms struggle to do?

To absorb the surplus they generate. Baran and Sweezy argued that monopoly raises the share of output accruing as surplus while narrowing the outlets for investing it profitably, so the system's characteristic problem becomes stagnation and the wasteful channels, military spending, sales effort, and finance, through which the surplus is soaked up instead.

Paul Baran's *The Political Economy of Growth* in 1957 and the joint *Monopoly Capital* with Paul Sweezy in 1966 rebuilt the tradition's crisis theory around the giant corporation. Where competitive capitalism, on the older account, was driven by a profit rate squeezed from above, monopoly capitalism generates a surplus that tends to rise, since the great firms administer prices and widen margins, while the system's capacity to absorb that surplus through investment and consumption lags. The gap is filled, in their famous inventory, by the sales effort, by military expenditure, by the growth of finance and speculation, channels that are functional for absorption precisely because they are wasteful. The school built an institution as well as a theory: the journal *Monthly Review*, founded in 1949 and edited by Sweezy and Leo Huberman, then carried by Harry Magdoff, gave the current a monthly platform independent of any party or university, and the tradition of independent Marxist publishing in America runs through it. The internal criticisms are equally well established. The economic surplus of the school's key concept is defined more loosely than Marx's surplus value, sliding between potential and actual, realised and utilised, and the theory's connection to the value foundation is attenuated to the point where sympathetic critics ask what remains distinctly Marxist in it beyond the vocabulary. The school's defenders answer that the loosening was the price of a theory that could look at the actually existing American economy, its Pentagon budgets and advertising industries, and say something true about it that the value categories, unmodified, could not reach.

### Why did analytical Marxists adopt the mainstream's own tools?

Because they held that the tradition's conclusions should earn their authority the hard way. If exploitation, class, and historical change could be modelled with optimisation, game theory, and equilibrium reasoning, and still emerge standing, they would no longer rest on a protected method; what fell under that scrutiny, several famous doctrines among them, was allowed to fall.

The September Group, named for the month of its annual meetings and nicknamed, by its own members, the No-Bullshit Marxists, gathered from the late nineteen seventies a set of philosophers and social scientists, G. A. Cohen, Jon Elster, John Roemer, Erik Olin Wright among them, around a single methodological wager: that Marxism's claims should be stated with the clarity and tested with the instruments of analytical philosophy and mainstream social science, and that whatever could not survive the translation deserved to be dropped. The results cut in every direction. Roemer rebuilt exploitation as a property of asset distributions, showing that exploitation-like transfers arise under a general equilibrium with unequal ownership and no labour market mystique, a reconstruction admirers call the tradition's rescue and detractors call its dissolution into the theory of distributive justice. Cohen defended the classical theory of history, the claim that productive forces drive development, with a philosopher's precision, and then subjected its functional explanations to a scrutiny from which they emerged qualified. Wright turned class from a slogan into a survey instrument, mapping contradictory class locations in the actual occupational structure. The school's own verdict on itself is the most instructive part: within a generation, several of its leading figures announced that the distinctively Marxist parts of the inheritance, the labour theory of value, the dialectical method, the laws of motion, had not survived the reconstruction, while the moral core, the indictment of exploitation and the demand for equality, had. Whether that outcome counts as the tradition's continuation by other means or as its honourable liquidation is a question the eight schools table leaves, correctly, to the reader.

### What does a regime of accumulation need in order to last?

A mode of regulation that fits it: wage relations, forms of competition, monetary arrangements, and state policies that together make decentralised decisions reproduce the regime instead of undermining it. The school's histories are searches for such fits, Fordism above all, and for the misfits that announce a regime's exhaustion and the crisis through which a successor is assembled.

The regulation school arose in France in the nineteen seventies, in Michel Aglietta's study of American capitalism and the work of Robert Boyer, Alain Lipietz, and their collaborators, several of them formed inside the French planning and research apparatus rather than in party schools. Its founding dissatisfaction was with both poles of the existing tradition: against orthodoxy, it denied that capitalism has one logic whose laws operate identically in every period; against pure institutional description, it insisted that periods have logics, and that theory's business is to find them. A regime of accumulation names a durable pattern in the organisation of production and the distribution of income, Fordism being the canonical case, mass production wedded to mass consumption through rising productivity-linked wages. A mode of regulation names the ensemble of norms, laws, and compromises that stabilises the regime, the wage relation, the form of competition among firms, the monetary and credit system, the shape of the state, the mode of insertion into the world economy. Crisis, on this account, is the exhaustion of a fit: the regime's own success erodes the compromises that regulated it, and the system enters a structural crisis whose resolution is political and institutional before it is economic. The school's Marxism is real but attenuated, a matter of the reproduction frame and the periodising instinct rather than of value magnitudes, and its critics press on the gap: regimes are identified by the stability they are then invoked to explain, and the theory's central terms multiply, regime, mode, model, each defined by the others, until the explanation circles. Its practitioners answer with the school's genuine comparative achievements, national histories of France, America, and Japan that neither orthodoxy nor mainstream growth theory could have written, and the argument continues there, on evidence, which is where the school always said it belonged.

### How does the social structure of accumulation approach explain long expansions?

By pointing to the institutional settlement that makes accumulation predictable for a time: labour relations, financial arrangements, state policy, and the terms of international engagement, built out of a preceding crisis and defended by the classes that benefit from them. Expansion lasts while the settlement holds, and stagnation is the sign that it has decayed faster than a successor forms.

Across the Atlantic, an American cousin of the regulation school grew from the work of David Gordon and was developed with Samuel Bowles, Herbert Gintis, Thomas Weisskopf, and others. A social structure of accumulation is the set of institutions, labour relations and the organisation of work, the financial system, the state's economic posture, the international order, within which capitalists can form stable expectations and invest. Structures are built in the aftermath of crises, consolidate during long expansions, and then decay, as their own success breeds the conflicts and rigidities that undermine them, so that history falls into long swings of construction, expansion, decay, and crisis. The approach is the most empirically ambitious in the field: its practitioners estimate profit rates, decompose their movements into the contributions of capacity utilisation, labour strength, and the terms of trade, and submit the theory of long swings to econometric test, a discipline several rival schools admire and none has matched. Its difference from the regulation school is partly temperamental, American and quantitative where the French work is conceptual, and partly substantive, since class power enters its equations as a measurable variable, the strength of labour indexed by unemployment, union density, and the social wage. The standard criticism is the one the table records: the approach can seem to explain stability by pointing at whatever was stable, and its mechanism for the construction of new structures, the moment when decay turns into rebuilding, remains more narrated than theorised. Even so, of all the schools it is the one a mainstream economic historian can argue with on shared evidence, and its long-wave histories of the American century are read well outside the tradition.

### Why do value-form theorists begin with the form of the commodity?

Because they read the opening of Capital as an argument about social form before it is an argument about quantity. The puzzle is why the product of labour takes the form of a value at all, and why labour's social character appears as a property of things; magnitudes, on this reading, are the second question, not the first.

Value-form theory is the most philosophical of the currents and, in the judgement of its practitioners, the most faithful to the text's actual order of argument. Its ancestor is Isaak Rubin, whose essays on Marx's theory of value, written in the Soviet Union in the nineteen twenties, insisted against the naturalistic reading that value is not embodied labour as a physical content but the social form labour assumes under generalised commodity production; Rubin perished in the purges and his book waited decades for its readership. The current revived in West Germany with Hans-Georg Backhaus and Helmut Reichelt, who reconstructed the dialectic of the value form from Marx's drafts and charged the whole Marxist tradition, Engels included, with having flattened the opening chapters into a crude labour-embodiment doctrine, and it continues in monetary theories of value that treat the form analysis as the theory's living core. On this reading the transformation problem is miscast from the start, because value was never a set of labour magnitudes awaiting conversion into prices; it is the social form whose necessary appearance is money, and price is value's mode of existence, not its translation. The criticism from the magnitude schools is predictable and forceful: an economics of forms that cannot say how much, cannot test, and cannot compute, has preserved the philosophy by evacuating the economics, and the great questions of crisis and distribution go unanswered while the exegesis deepens. The current's rejoinder, that the magnitude schools have spent a century computing magnitudes whose meaning they never established, states the field's deepest divide in one sentence, and the reader now has both sides of it.

### What does the autonomist current reverse in the usual story?

The direction of causation between capital and labour. In the usual telling, capital acts and workers react; the autonomist reading makes working-class struggle the independent variable, with mechanisation, reorganisation, and political restructuring understood as capital's replies to refusals it could not otherwise contain. Development becomes a sequence of moves in a conflict neither side fully controls.

The autonomist current, born in the Italian factories of the nineteen sixties around Raniero Panzieri's use of the machinery question and Mario Tronti's celebrated inversion, holds that the working class is not capital's product but its antagonist and its motor: capital develops, mechanises, reorganises, and restructures in flight from a workforce whose resistance sets the terms. Antonio Negri carried the reading into a theory of the socialised worker and, with Michael Hardt, into a global idiom that carried it far beyond economics. Its place in the eight schools table is the contested one flagged at the table's head. Its practitioners' suspicion of institutions, parties, and mediation makes it as much a political temperament as an economics, its writings are short on the measurement and testing the empirical schools prize, and its category of refusal does more rhetorical than analytical work in weaker hands. Yet its central reversal has leaked into the mainstream of the field's instincts: even schools that reject the label now routinely treat technical change as a response to labour's resistance rather than as an exogenous rain of inventions, and the history of the assembly line, of containerisation, of the relocation of production across the globe, is difficult to write without the autonomist question, whose initiative is being answered here. A map that omitted the current would misdescribe the tradition's imaginative centre of gravity, which is why this guide treats it as the frequently counted ninth school and gives it a full hearing outside the table's eight rows.

## The value controversy: the decade the foundations were fought in the open

Between the early nineteen seventies and the early nineteen eighties, the field conducted its foundational quarrel in public, in a sequence of books and journal exchanges that outsiders could follow move by move, and the episode deserves a section of its own because it is the best-documented case of the programme's schools behaving as the programme not doctrine rule describes. The opening position was consolidation: the postwar defence of the labour theory of value, associated with Ronald Meek's studies and with the first generation of Sraffa's readers, held that the transformation problem was a puzzle with solutions and that Bortkiewicz's algebra had shown where Marx's sketch needed completion rather than replacement. The challenge came in two waves. The first was constructive: Morishima's mathematical reconstruction proved the fundamental Marxian theorem, that positive profit is possible only where surplus value is positive, and at the same stroke showed that the theorem could be proved in a von Neumann framework in which labour values functioned as accounting magnitudes, a gift the orthodox received with deep ambivalence. The second wave was destructive in intent: Steedman's *Marx after Sraffa* of 1977 argued, with a completeness earlier critics had not attempted, that every proposition of interest in the value theory could be derived from the physical data alone, and that the value magnitudes were therefore redundant, an intermediate calculation that could be struck out without loss.

The replies sorted the field into the camps the table now records. The fundamentalist defence, mounted in the *Bulletin of the Conference of Socialist Economists* and in the symposium published as *The Value Controversy* in 1981, held that Steedman had refought the battle on simultaneist ground the texts do not occupy. The monetary and temporal reconstructions took their modern form in direct response, Duménil's and Foley's new interpretation and the temporal single-system reading both being, among other things, answers to Steedman dated to the controversy's hottest years. The Sraffian camp consolidated its own identity in the same exchange, accepting the label neo-Ricardian from its critics and returning it with interest. And the value-form current deepened its exegetical case that the whole controversy had misconceived its object, since a theory of social form cannot be redundant in the way a calculation can. What no participant could do, by the controversy's end, was pretend the question had not been asked. The field that emerged was smaller in its orthodox wing, larger in its reconstructions, and permanently marked by the knowledge that its foundation had survived its strongest internal audit in amended rather than original form, which is either the biography of a research programme maturing or of a doctrine dissolving, and the reader now knows enough to see why the schools still argue about which.

## Money, crisis, and the uses of instability

Crisis is where the tradition believes its object shows its nature, and the theory of crisis is accordingly the part of the inheritance every school must rebuild for itself. The dynamics begin from the reproduction frame already described. Accumulation is driven by competition: each capital must cheapen its commodities or die, cheapening requires machinery, machinery displaces living labour, and living labour is the source of surplus value, so the system's engine runs on a fuel its own operation depletes. Name the composition term once, in prose: the organic composition of capital, the ratio of constant capital to variable capital, rises as plant and materials grow faster than the wage bill, and on the orthodox reading the same mass of surplus, spread across a swelling total of advanced capital, presses the rate of profit downward against the countertendencies, cheapening of machinery, intensified exploitation, foreign trade, that Marx himself catalogued. That construction, the falling tendency, is the most famous crisis theory in the field and the most contested, with the reconstructions defending its logic against Okishio's theorem, the Sraffians treating profitability as a distributive outcome, and the empirical schools quarrelling over the national accounts.

Around that centre the other families arrange themselves. Disproportionality theories, descended from the reproduction schemes, locate crisis in the failure of the proportions among departments, investment goods against consumption goods, so that a boom built on credit and expectation collapses when the imbalance can no longer be papered over. Underconsumption and realisation theories, the monopoly capital school's home ground, locate it in the demand side: wages restrained by the very relation that produces the surplus cannot buy back the product, and the surplus must find outlets in waste, luxury, arms, or foreign vent. Profit squeeze theories reverse the polarity and locate crisis in labour's strength, a long boom tightening labour markets until wages and militancy compress margins. Financial theories, strengthened by the memory of Hyman Minsky's parallel work outside the tradition, locate it in the balance sheets, where stability breeds the leverage that makes the system fragile. The field's dynamics, its business cycle research, its long waves, and its accounts of the great crises of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries all draw on these families in different mixtures, and the full map belongs to the cluster's [marxist crisis theory guide](/2013/10/01/marxist-crisis-theory-guide/), which this guide treats as its companion on everything dynamic. What the pillar article insists on is only the shared premise the families hold in common against the mainstream default: crisis is endogenous, a product of the system's normal functioning rather than a meteor that strikes it, and any theory that can only represent breakdown as an external shock has defined away the phenomenon this tradition exists to explain.

## Money and credit: the system's nervous system

The fifth commitment, money as constitutive, has a technical development that the pillar should register, since the theory of credit is where the founder's economics touches the modern financial system most closely. In the third volume's treatment, the credit system arises from production's own needs, bills of exchange circulating among capitals before banks gather the function up, and interest-bearing capital becomes the most fetishised form of all, money apparently breeding money without passing through production at all. Two of the construction's claims remain distinctive. The rate of interest, on this account, has no natural level: profit divides into interest and profit of enterprise by the relative strength of lenders and borrowers, a division governed by custom, competition, and the cycle rather than by any productivity of capital itself, so that the price of money is the most purely conventional magnitude in the system. And credit is the great accelerator in both directions, enabling production to expand beyond the limits its realised profits would set, centralising capitals through the joint stock form, and thereby building, in the boom, the pyramid of claims whose simultaneous calling-in is the crisis's financial form. Marx's journalism on the crises of 1847 and 1857, written for the *New-York Daily Tribune* as the events unfolded, shows the construction at work as analysis rather than as system, and the manuscript's unfinished state, the credit chapters among the least revised in the third volume, left the schools their usual inheritance: the French and Italian circuit theorists rebuilt monetary macroeconomics on the bank's creation of money for production, the value-form current reads money as the theory's necessary centre, the empirical schools measure the credit cycle's place in the longer waves, and the whole province stands as the clearest case in which the founder's sketches have proved more fertile than most finished systems.

## The crisis theories in their several families

The compact account of crisis given earlier in this guide now needs its full map, because crisis theory is the field's largest single literature and the territory where the schools' differences bite hardest on evidence. Four families were named: profitability, disproportionality, realisation, and the squeeze on profits from labour's side, with the financial theories standing across them. Each family has a canonical text, a characteristic evidence base, and a standing quarrel with the family next to it, and the quarrels are more instructive than the theories taken singly, since each family's strongest argument is usually the demonstration of its neighbour's blind spot.

The profitability family's canonical modern statement is Grossmann's of 1929, an attempt to derive breakdown arithmetically: accumulation raises the composition of capital faster than the mass of surplus value can grow, until the surplus no longer suffices to valorise the accumulated stock at the required rate while feeding the capitalist class, and the system seizes. Grossmann himself insisted he had described a tendency whose countertendencies fill the rest of the book, but the arithmetic spine gave the family its form: crisis as the falling rate of profit made visible. Its evidence base is the long series of profit rate estimates discussed earlier, and its standing quarrel is with the Okishio theorem and with the rival families' charge that it cannot explain timing, why 1929 or 1974 rather than any other year, a charge its defenders return by noting that no family can, and that a theory of the ground is not refuted by the unpredictability of the earthquake.

The disproportionality family descends from the reproduction schemes and from an unlikely ancestor, Mikhail Tugan-Baranovsky, a Russian legal Marxist whose study of English crises at the turn of the century argued that capitalist production creates its own market through the demand departments exert on one another, so that crisis arises not from insufficient consumption but from the anarchic disproportion among branches, investment racing ahead in one line while its complement lags. The argument, turned against the underconsumptionists of its day, founded a lineage: Hilferding's account of the organised capitalism of cartels as a partial and unstable suppression of the anarchy, the Soviet debates of the nineteen twenties in which the schemes were worked as planning instruments, and the modern formal treatments in which the schemes become multisectoral growth models. Its evidence base is the anatomy of particular booms, the composition of investment in the upswing, and its quarrel with the profitability family is over whether the disproportion is the cause or merely the form in which a profitability crisis announces itself.

Realisation theories, the third family, descend from Luxemburg's question, who buys the surplus product when accumulation expands, and reach their mature form in the monopoly capital school's absorption problem: the surplus tends to rise while its profitable outlets lag, and the system's history becomes the history of the outlets improvised for it. The family's modern evidence is the demand side of the long postwar boom and its ending, the wage stagnation of the neoliberal decades alongside rising profitability, an anomaly the family claims as its own prediction, and the debt-fuelled consumption that postponed the reckoning in the Anglo-American economies before 2008. Its quarrel is with the fourth family, to which the same decades tell the opposite story.

The profit squeeze family reads the crisis of the nineteen seventies as the profitability family's mirror image: not capital's surplus failing but labour's success in appropriating too much of it. Andrew Glyn and Bob Sutcliffe's study of British capitalism in 1972 gave the account its classic form, full employment and militant unions raising wages and the social wage until margins were compressed and investment faltered, and the American accumulation school's measurements of labour strength supplied the econometrics. The family's quarrel with the realisation school, whether the seventies crisis was too little profit or the eighties settlement too little wage, is the field's most purely empirical dispute, conducted with shared data and opposed decompositions, and it remains the model of how the programme's schools can disagree without ceasing to be one programme. Across all four stands the financial line of work, from Hilferding's finance capital through the tradition's reading of Minsky to the analyses of the 2008 collapse, holding that whatever the underlying malady, the crisis arrives as a financial event, because the credit system is where the system's claims on the future are concentrated and where their mutual inconsistency is discovered. The cluster's crisis guide carries the full dossier; the pillar registers the map and the lesson, that in this field a crisis theory is a school in miniature, a choice about which variable is the system's Achilles heel, tested against the same handful of great breakdowns every generation re-examines.

## Distribution: the conflict the programme is about

Strip the schools of their technical vocabularies and one claim remains standing in all of them: the division of the social product between wages and profits is not a technical datum but a contested outcome, settled by the relative strength of classes and by the institutions that organise their conflict. This is the point on which the tradition differs most practically from the mainstream account it opposes. In the marginal productivity picture, each factor is paid according to its contribution, distribution is a branch of price theory, and conflict over shares is a friction, a departure from the model's smooth working. In the surplus picture that every Marxist school shares with the classical economists, the direction of explanation reverses: a surplus is produced first, as a physical and social fact, and its division between the classes is a prize over which the classes contend, with technology, law, custom, and bargaining power all entering as weapons rather than as arbiters standing outside the fight.

The wage, in this frame, is not the price of a contribution but the outcome of a contested reproduction: what counts as the necessary standard of life for a worker is historical, varying with what societies have taught their members to expect, and the struggle over its level is permanent, conducted through unions, legislation, inflation, unemployment, and the quiet disciplines of the labour market. Profits likewise are not a reward for a service, waiting or risk-bearing, in the tradition's telling, but the form the surplus takes when the means of production are privately owned, and the rate at which they accumulate is governed by the outcome of the distributional contest as much as by technology. Different schools formalise the contest differently: the analytical current as a problem in asset ownership and bargaining, the social structure of accumulation school as a measurable index of labour's strength entering the profit rate's decomposition, the classical core as the frontier along which wages and profits trade off given the methods of production. What none of them does is dissolve the contest into technology. The empirical traditions meet here too, since the long movements of the wage share and the profit share in the advanced economies, their fall and recovery and fall again across the twentieth century, constitute the single most worked body of data in the field, the terrain on which its claims about phases of capitalism stand or fall.

The distributional core explains the tradition's otherwise puzzling persistence in adjacent subjects. Its practitioners keep appearing in debates about inequality, about the falling labour share in the twenty-first century's national accounts, about the economics of care and of the household, about racial and gender divisions within the working class, because their founding grammar treats every such division as a real structure of appropriation rather than as a deviation from a harmonious norm. Feminist economists working in or near the tradition rebuilt the account of reproduction to include the unpaid labour of the household, without which the wage's adequacy cannot even be stated, and the debates between that reconstruction and the orthodox categories were among the most productive of the late twentieth century. A programme is known by the questions it cannot leave alone; the division of the product, and the power that division expresses, is the question this one cannot leave alone, and a reader who holds onto that thread can find the way through schools whose technical surfaces seem to share nothing at all.

## The theory of wages: the value of labour power and its discontents

The programme's wage theory is contained in its definition of labour power's value and in the two complications the founder himself attached to it. The definition is cost-of-reproduction: the wage tends toward the value of the goods and services required to maintain the worker and raise the next generation of workers, the training costs of skilled labour included as a premium on the same basis. The first complication is the historical and moral element, Marx's own phrase for the fact that the necessary standard is not physiological: it is set by the habits and expectations a society's workers have been formed by and have fought for, so that the wage in a rich country contains, as necessities, goods that were luxuries to its grandparents, and the floor of the labour market is a sedimented history of class struggle rather than a calorie count. The second complication is that the tendency operates through the reserve army's pressure and the cycle's phases, so that observed wages at any date reflect the balance of forces in a particular labour market at least as much as the value they gravitate around, a looseness the mainstream caricature of the theory routinely omits in order to refute the remainder. The famous charge that the theory predicts immiseration in the simple sense, wages ground down to subsistence, is answered from the text: relative immiseration, a falling share, is compatible with rising real wages, and most of the schools have long read the tendency in the relative form, though the argument about which form the founder intended has filled its share of journals.

The modern literature works the seam between the definition and the labour market's institutions. The minimum wage is read not as a distortion of a competitive price but as an intervention in the balance of forces, whose employment effects the tradition expects to be small or ambiguous for its own reasons, monopsony in the labour market being the normal rather than the exceptional case in this grammar; when the empirical literature of the nineteen nineties, associated with Card and Krueger, found small effects where the textbook model predicted clear losses, the field's journals noted with restraint that their framework had never required the textbook's answer. Efficiency wages, the mainstream's discovery that firms may profit from paying above the clearing wage, are treated in the same spirit, as the rediscovery inside a different vocabulary of the fact that the wage is an instrument of labour discipline and effort extraction, a proposition the labour process literature had built its research programme on decades earlier. The pattern is the one this guide has recorded repeatedly: the tradition's grammar generates positions that the neighbouring discipline reaches late and by other roads, and the field's complaint is not that it is refuted but that it is reinvented without acknowledgement, a complaint the marginality section has already priced into the field's standing.

## Inflation as unresolved conflict

One application of the distributional core became, for a decade, the field's most influential export to general economics: the conflict theory of inflation. In the form given it by Robert Rowthorn in 1977, inflation is the monetary expression of an unresolved struggle over distribution: workers press money wages toward a target share, firms press prices toward a target margin, the claims sum to more than the product can satisfy, and the price level rises as the mechanism by which the inconsistent claims are temporarily reconciled, with the state's monetary stance deciding whether the reconciliation proceeds by inflation, by unemployment, or by an incomes policy that brings the conflict into the open. The theory travelled because the stagflation of the nineteen seventies was embarrassing the mainstream's own accounts, and for some years the conflict model stood in the textbooks as the radical alternative that events had made respectable. Its later fortunes track the field's: as the eighties settlements broke the labour movements' capacity to press claims, inflation fell for reasons the theory itself predicts, the weakening of one party to the conflict, and the theory was filed as a period piece, though its logic reappears wherever economists reach for the language of incompatible claims to explain a price surge. The episode is registered here because it demonstrates the programme's capacity, at its best, to state a mechanism so cleanly that the neighbouring discipline must adopt it while declining to cite the adoption, a form of influence the citation counts on which the field's marginality is usually measured are structurally unable to see.

## Classes as an analytical category

The programme's most famous category is also its most misunderstood, because class in this vocabulary is not a rank in a ladder of income or prestige. A class is defined by a position in the relations of production: by whether its members own means of production, whether they must sell their capacity to work in order to live, and whether they appropriate the surplus that others produce or produce the surplus that others appropriate. Income correlates with class because position governs income over the long run, but the definition runs through ownership and function, not through the pay packet, which is why a highly paid executive who works for a salary and owns nothing but expectations occupies, in the strict construction, a different analytical place from a small proprietor with half the income and a workshop of his own.

The two great classes of the textbook summary, bourgeoisie and proletariat, are the poles of the construction, and the tradition has spent as much effort on the territory between the poles as on the poles themselves. The old middle class of independent producers, farmers, shopkeepers, artisans, has been declining for two centuries along the trajectory the theory predicts, while a new middle territory has grown in its place: managers who direct other people's capital without owning it, professionals whose credentials function as a kind of property, supervisors, technicians, and the vast salariat of the modern corporation and state. Erik Olin Wright's mapping of contradictory class locations, positions that share the marks of both great classes at once, was the analytical school's systematic answer, and the older European debates, Nicos Poulantzas arguing for a structural demarcation that swept most salaried employees into a new petty bourgeoisie, his critics resisting the sweep, remain the map of the alternatives. The argument matters beyond taxonomy because the programme's political and crisis theories all presuppose a class structure, and a structure misdrawn yields expectations falsified by every election and every strike wave.

Two discipline rules follow from the structural definition and are enforced, at their best, by all eight schools. Class is a relation, not a thing: a class exists in and against the other classes, and its interests are defined by the relation, not by the opinions or the culture of its members at any moment, so that the tradition distinguishes rigorously between a class's position and its consciousness, treating the passage from one to the other as a historical problem rather than an automatic reflex. And class is not the only structure of appropriation the analysis must carry: the tradition's long arguments with feminist and anti-racist scholarship, over whether the household and the racial order are inside the theory of class or alongside it, have been among its most productive frictions, producing the rebuilt accounts of social reproduction described in the distribution section. A reader who keeps the structural definition in view will find the schools' disagreements about class legible: they are disagreements about where the lines of ownership and appropriation actually run in economies of managers, credentials, platforms, and states, conducted by people who agree that the lines, wherever they run, are the economy's load-bearing walls.

## Labour, machinery, and the direction of technical change

One of the tradition's most distinctive claims is that technical change under capitalism has a direction, set not by the neutral progress of knowledge but by the requirements of control and profitability inside production. Marx's chapter on machinery in the first volume of *Capital* argued that the machine enters the factory as a weapon as much as a tool: it breaks the skilled worker's monopoly of a craft, cheapens labour by substituting cheaper hands and then hands in general, lengthens or intensifies the working day, and transfers the knowledge of the process from the workforce to the apparatus and its owners. The claim lay half dormant until Harry Braverman's *Labor and Monopoly Capital* in 1974 rebuilt it as the deskilling thesis, an account of the twentieth-century labour process, clerical work included, as the progressive separation of conception from execution, planning gathered into management, execution simplified until the worker is interchangeable. Braverman's book became one of the tradition's very few bestsellers inside the academy, founded a labour process literature that ran for decades, and drew the full range of the field's responses: case studies confirming the pattern in industry after industry, counter-studies finding reskilling and negotiated control, and the autonomist inversion, in which the same machinery appears as capital's flight from a workforce whose power at the point of production had made the old methods ungovernable.

The argument's modern form concerns the composition of capital and the reserve army, the pool of workers outside employment whose existence disciplines the wages of those inside it. Mechanisation that expels labour from one branch while accumulation absorbs it in another keeps the reserve army refilled from the system's own dynamics rather than from population growth, and the schools divide over the balance in particular epochs: the postwar decades, when absorption outran expulsion across the advanced economies, are the regulation and accumulation schools' home evidence, while the era of manufacturing collapse in the old industrial regions is read by others as the reserve army's return in geographical form. Automation and computation extend the same argument to cognitive labour, and the tradition's categories, conception against execution, the cheapening of a capacity by its mechanisation, the struggle over the working day reappearing as a struggle over availability and attention, are applied to the new terrain with the confidence of a programme that believes it has seen this film before. The critics' standing objection, that the theory selects its cases and that productivity's history shows as much upskilling as down, is part of the record and is argued, study by study, in the labour process journals; the pillar article's point is the narrower one, that the question itself, who gains control when a technique changes, and whose knowledge the machine confiscates, is a question this programme taught the social sciences to ask.

## Population, the reserve army, and migration

The programme's population theory is a standing refutation of the idea that its categories are Malthusian in modern dress. Marx's law of population, stated against Malthus in the first volume, holds that every mode of production has its own population law: capitalism produces, by its accumulation itself, a relative surplus population, workers rendered redundant relative to capital's needs at the very moment the system most loudly celebrates abundance. The surplus population takes characteristic forms the text distinguishes with care: the floating, workers attracted and repelled by the modern industries' cycle; the latent, the agricultural population perpetually on the point of flowing into the towns; the stagnant, the irregularly employed layer whose conditions sink below the working class's average; and beneath them the pauperised sediment the text calls the hospital of the active labour army. The reserve army is functional in the theory's strict sense: its competition disciplines wages, its availability permits sudden expansions, and its misery is not a policy failure but a condition of the wage relation's flexibility, a claim whose evidence base, the behaviour of wages and employment across the cycle, the empirical schools have worked ever since.

Migration is the reserve army's geography. The tradition's founder reversed himself on the question in full view of his readers, writing to Engels in 1870 that he had come to see the antagonism between English and Irish workers, artificially sustained, as the secret of the English working class's impotence and the material basis of the whole arrangement, since it divided the proletariat into two hostile camps and set the cheaper to police the dearer. The letters founded the field's approach: migration flows are read as capital's recruitment across borders, the divisions they open inside the working class as products of the labour market's organisation rather than as natural antipathies, and the remedies as lying in the organisation that abolishes the differential rather than in the exclusion that ratifies it. The modern literature works the same grammar on the global labour market, the export of jobs and the import of workers as two valves of one system, the remittance economies of the sending countries, the care chains by which the rich world's reproduction is staffed from the poor world's households, connecting the migration analysis to the social reproduction front described above. The founder's public reversal remains the province's methodological emblem: a category corrected by evidence, in letters anyone may read, inside a tradition whose enemies describe it as incapable of correction.

## Competition as the schools conceive it

The word competition names opposite things in the two programmes this guide has contrasted, and the difference organises much of their mutual incomprehension. In the textbook construction, competition is a state, perfect competition being the limiting condition in which no seller has power, prices are given to all, and the system's tendencies can be studied as departures from the limit. In the classical construction this tradition inherits, competition is a process and a war: capitals invade one another's spheres, cheapen, innovate, ruin, and are ruined, and the laws of the system, the equalisation of profit rates, the pressure to accumulate, the falling tendency itself, are what competition enforces on the unwilling, not what it presupposes. Marx's own formulation has competition executing the inner laws of capital as an external coercive force over each individual capitalist, which is why the tradition reads the monopolist's rise not as competition's failure but as its product, concentration being the process's own result, and why the monopoly capital school's starting point is historical rather than paradoxical. The modern reconstruction associated with Anwar Shaikh gives the process its technical form as real competition: firms set prices strategically around costs, profit rates gravitate turbulently toward equality across industries through the flow of investment, never resting at it, and the regulating magnitudes are the profit rates on new investment at the best-practice techniques, a formulation that reconnects the classical gravitation image to modern industrial data. The analytical school, true to its method, rebuilt competition with game theory and found the classical instincts partly translatable and partly not; the Sraffian construction, true to its, treats competition as the assumption that a uniform profit rate rules, the process's outcome taken as the analysis's premise. What unites the field against the textbook is the refusal to define competition by its own abolition: for this programme, a theory in which the central process reaches perfection only when nothing is left of it has mistaken the photograph for the race.## The firm, the corporation, and financialisation

The programme's theory of the firm begins where the mainstream's ends. Where the theory of the firm in the textbooks is a production function with a maximising owner, this tradition inherits from *Capital* the figure of the capitalist as the personification of capital, a character mask whose private intentions are irrelevant beside the compulsion competition imposes, accumulate or be accumulated. The joint stock company already struck Marx as the abolition of private property within the confines of the capitalist mode itself, and the twentieth century's corporation, with ownership dispersed among shareholders and control gathered in salaried management, set the tradition a problem its founders glimpsed and its schools worked in earnest. Baran and Sweezy's giant corporation, administered in price and driven to absorb its surplus, has been described; the analytical school reconstructed the manager's incentives with the tools of agency theory and found the separation of ownership and control less consequential than the sociology of the nineteen thirties had supposed, since capital markets discipline managers into the old compulsion by other means. The labour process literature took the firm as the site of the control struggle described earlier. Each treatment is the programme's signature move applied to an institution: ask not what the firm optimises but what relation it embodies, whose surplus it gathers, and what compulsions its form imposes on the people inside it regardless of their wishes.

Financialisation is the name the field has settled on for the transformation of the last four decades, in which profit accrues increasingly through financial channels rather than through production and trade, in which nonfinancial firms hold portfolios and run treasury operations rivalling their factories, households are drawn into credit and asset markets as a condition of ordinary life, and the financial sector's share of total profits swells beyond any earlier peacetime level. The tradition was not surprised by the category: Hilferding had theorised finance capital in 1910, the first volume's fictitious capital supplied the concept of claims capitalised on expected revenue, and Monthly Review's later work, on the financial explosion as monopoly capital's answer to stagnation, connected the new literature to the table's fourth row. Costas Lapavitsas and the school around the SOAS research network gave the phenomenon its systematic Marxist treatment, arguing that financialisation rests on the direct expropriation of household income through interest and fees as much as on corporate restructuring, a claim that extends the theory of exploitation beyond the factory gate and has been contested accordingly. The 2008 crisis was the field's grim examination: its crisis theories, financial among them, had described the mechanisms in advance in general form, while no school had dated the event, the standing pattern of the programme's predictive record, structural foresight without the clock, which the marginality account above has already entered in the ledger's other column.

## War, arms, and the permanent arms economy

The tradition's economics of war begins from an observation its monopoly capital school made central: military expenditure is the one form of state spending that capital, as a class, reliably tolerates at scale, since it competes with no private producer, employs without flooding the market with consumables, and absorbs product without raising the workforce's standard of life in ways that might feed back into wages. The permanent arms economy thesis, developed by the British economist Michael Kidron in the nineteen sixties and set out in his *Western Capitalism Since the War* of 1968, gave the observation its strongest form: the postwar boom's stability rested substantially on the arms budgets of the Cold War, which drained off surplus that would otherwise have pressed on the profit rate through overaccumulation, an international Keynesianism of waste that explained the golden age's length and its peculiar geography, the fastest growth in the defeated and disarmed economies, Germany and Japan, whose capital was not taxed by the drain. The thesis had the great virtue of falsifiability among friends: when arms spending's share fell and stagnation arrived on schedule in the seventies, its partisans claimed the prediction, while its critics inside the field objected that the drain argument inverted at high employment and that the thesis mistook one stabiliser for the system's secret. Kidron himself, in the tradition's honourable manner, published his own reassessment and abandoned the thesis's strong form in print. The province persists in the field's analysis of the military budget as macroeconomic policy by other means, of war's demand shocks and reconstruction booms, and of the arms sector as the state's most direct fusion with the monopoly corporation, and its history is registered here as another case of the pattern: a mechanism stated sharply enough to be wrong, argued over with evidence, and retained in weakened form by the school that fathered it.

## The state in the programme's economics

One region of the inheritance was left so fragmentary by the founder that every school has had to improvise in it: the economics of the state. Marx's projected book on the state was never written, and the tradition's fiscal sociology was built later, from the experience of the welfare state and its crises. James O'Connor's *The Fiscal Crisis of the State*, published in 1973, gave the improvisation its canonical form: the capitalist state must simultaneously subsidise accumulation, through infrastructure, training, and the socialisation of capital's costs, and purchase legitimation, through welfare and social provision, while its revenues lag both mandates, so that fiscal crisis is the form the system's contradictions take inside the budget. Around that construction the field built a literature on taxation as class relation, on public debt as a claim on future surplus, on the military budget as the monopoly capital school's great absorption channel, and on the state's role in reproducing labour power through health, housing, and education. The regulation and accumulation schools absorbed the state into their institutional ensembles, the analytical school subjected its Marxist theory to the usual reconstruction and found parts of it wanting, and the whole region remains the clearest case of the pattern this guide has stressed throughout: a genuine and unavoidable question, the economic theory of the capitalist state, on which the inheritance supplies a problem and a grammar but no finished doctrine, and on which the schools consequently disagree in the open. For a pillar article the registration is enough; the point for the newcomer is that the absence of a settled Marxist theory of the state is not an oversight the field has failed to notice but one of its acknowledged open construction sites.

## Land, housing, and the economics of the city

The rent theory's modern career runs through the city. Engels's pamphlet on the housing question, written in 1872 against Proudhonist remedies, made the founding argument: the housing shortage of the industrial city is not a malfunction to be repaired by building societies or worker home ownership schemes but a necessary product of the same relations that produce the factory, and it will be solved, if at all, by the transformation that solves those. The argument lay in the movement's canon until the urban explosions of the nineteen sixties made the city a theoretical object again, when the tradition's geographers and sociologists rebuilt rent theory for the metropolitan scale. David Harvey's work, from *Social Justice and the City* in 1973 to *The Limits to Capital* in 1982, gave the reconstruction its system: capital moves between a primary circuit in production and a secondary circuit in the built environment, switching into property, infrastructure, and the urban fabric when overaccumulation threatens the first, so that the city becomes the sink and the monument of the surplus its factories cannot absorb, and its periodic property crises are the switching mechanism's failures. Around that construction the field built its accounts of gentrification as a rent gap closing, of the landlord's return as a central figure of the financialised economy, of the global property boom as fictitious capital with an address. The thread from the third volume's rent chapters to the modern literature on housing financialisation is one of the clearest lines of descent in the whole tradition, and it is the one a lay reader is most likely to have met without knowing its source, since the conclusion that housing under this system is produced as an asset first and as shelter second is now common property far beyond the field's journals.

## Schooling and the correspondence principle

The programme's application to education produced one of its most debated books and its clearest statement of the reproduction idea outside economics proper. Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis, whose names appear in the table as founders of the accumulation school, published *Schooling in Capitalist America* in 1976, arguing that the educational system corresponds to the relations of production it serves: the school's social relations, hierarchy, external rewards, fragmentation of tasks, the grading of compliance, mirror the factory's and the office's, so that schooling reproduces not only skills but the dispositions appropriate to each level of the occupational order, and that the system's famous promise, that education equalises by rewarding talent, is contradicted by the evidence that family background predicts attainment through channels no meritocracy story can comfortably explain. The correspondence principle was attacked from every side, by liberals defending the schools' reforming potential, by mainstream economists whose human capital theory the book had directly engaged, and by Marxists who found the correspondence too functional, too seamless, a reproduction without the contradictions and the resistance that the reproduction frame elsewhere insists on. Its authors revised under fire, conceding the functionalism and rebuilding the argument with the contestation restored, and the revised position, schools as sites where the terms of reproduction are fought over rather than merely administered, became the field's working settlement. The book's importance for this pillar is generic rather than local: it carried the programme's master question into the institution charged with forming labour power itself, demonstrated that the reproduction frame could generate a decade of empirical research in a neighbouring discipline, and stands, with the audience commodity argument, as evidence that the tradition's marginality inside economics coexists with a reach, into sociology, education, geography, and media studies, that its citation counts in economics journals entirely fail to record.

## Social reproduction: the field's feminist reconstruction

The most consequential expansion of the programme in the late twentieth century came from the feminist economists who charged it with an omission at its centre. The theory of reproduction, they observed, accounted for the renewal of capital and of labour power while treating the daily and generational work that produces labour power itself, cooking, cleaning, bearing and raising children, caring for the sick and the old, as a natural background rather than as labour. The domestic labour debate of the nineteen seventies, conducted in the field's journals between the wages for housework current, which demanded the wage as the strategic lever that would expose the work's economic character, and its critics, who held that housework produced use values outside the value relation and that the demand misconceived both, forced every school to state its position on where production ends and reproduction begins. Lise Vogel's *Marxism and the Oppression of Women* of 1983 supplied the reconstruction that has lasted: a unitary theory in which the oppression of women under capitalism is grounded in capital's need for the renewal of labour power and in the kin-based household form that privatises that renewal's costs, so that the struggle over reproduction is internal to the theory of accumulation rather than an addition to it. Social reproduction theory, the contemporary name for this line of work, extends the programme's master question, how does this system reproduce itself, to the institutions the founder's generation left unexamined, and it has migrated from the tradition's margins to one of its most active fronts, with its own journals' special issues and its own internal schools, behaving, as the programme not doctrine rule predicts, exactly like the rest of the field.

## Race and the tradition's limits

A second great friction of the same decades concerned race. The tradition's classical categories treat the worker as an abstraction, labour power in general, and its critics from within the Black radical tradition argued that the abstraction concealed a history: capitalism did not merely employ racial division, it was built through it, in the slave trade, the plantation complex, and the colonial labour regimes that supplied its first accumulations and its cheapest inputs. Cedric Robinson's *Black Marxism* of 1983 gave the argument its canonical form, contending that racial ordering preceded capitalism in European civilisation and was absorbed into it, so that the resulting system is properly called racial capitalism, and that Marxism, for all its power, had universalised a European experience of class formation that the wider world's history does not repeat. The tradition's responses divided as usual: an orthodox defence holding that the categories, consistently applied, already contain the analysis of slavery and of labour market segmentation, and Robinson's own evidence shows it; a reconstructionist literature that took the charge as a research programme, writing the histories of slave and colonial labour with the theory's tools extended past the founder's horizon; and an empirical literature on segmented labour markets and racial wage hierarchies that supplied the magnitudes. The pillar article registers the debate at the level the field itself has reached: racial capitalism is now a term of art inside the tradition as well as a charge against it, the founder's own writings on Ireland, India, and American slavery are read with a new closeness by all parties, and the argument continues in the form the programme handles best, as a dispute about whether a category needs amendment or only application.

## The agrarian question and ground rent

The oldest unfinished business in the programme concerns the countryside. The third volume of *Capital* contains the theory of ground rent in its two classical forms: differential rent, arising because capitals applied to better land or better positions yield surplus profit that competition converts into rent for the landowner, and absolute rent, the tribute landownership as a monopoly can extract from the system as a whole. The theory makes agriculture a department of the general theory rather than an exception to it, and its test is the countryside's transformation: Marx expected capital to dissolve the peasantry as it dissolved the artisan, separating the direct producers from the land and converting agriculture into a branch of industry ruled by the same laws of concentration. Karl Kautsky's *The Agrarian Question* of 1899, the codification era's most durable work of applied economics, reported the process under way and its complications honestly: the small farm was indeed doomed by the economics, yet it persisted, surviving by self-exploitation, by working longer for less than any wage labourer would accept, a finding that founded a century of argument about whether peasant persistence refutes the concentration thesis or merely redefines its timetable.

The argument is not antiquarian. Most of the world's population still lived from the land when the tradition's classical texts were written, and the great development questions of the twentieth century, collectivisation's catastrophe in the Soviet Union, the land reforms of East Asia, the persistence of smallholding across the global South, the rise of agribusiness and contract farming, were fought through with these categories by practitioners who often had no other economics to hand. The schools' agrarian literature divides along predictable lines, orthodoxy stressing differentiation, the peasantry polarising into rural capital and rural labour as the theory predicts, revisionists stressing the Chayanovian logic of the family farm, its demographic cycle and its indifference to profit accounting, which the Russian economist Alexander Chayanov built into a rival theory in the nineteen twenties from within the same debates. A pillar article registers the province because it displays, in the field's oldest application, the pattern of the whole: a powerful theory of a sector's transformation, a recalcitrant reality that conforms more slowly and more deviously than the theory's first form allowed, and a literature that lives by revising the belt while defending the core.

## The world economy: empire, dependence, and unequal exchange

No map of this field is complete without the territory where its categories met the world market, because for much of the twentieth century the tradition's largest audience was outside the advanced capitalist countries altogether. The classical theories of imperialism were written inside the codification era: Hilferding on finance capital's drive outward, Luxemburg arguing that accumulation requires a non-capitalist outside to absorb its product, Lenin's pamphlet of 1916 welding these into the doctrine of imperialism as capitalism's monopoly stage. Whatever their standing as theory, these works set the questions the later schools inherited: whether the world economy is one system or a system of national systems, whether its surplus flows toward the centre or is generated in the periphery and transferred, and whether development in the poor countries is blocked by the same mechanisms that enriched the rich ones.

The postwar schools divided the territory. Dependency theory, built in Latin America by Raúl Prebisch's structuralist colleagues and radicalised by André Gunder Frank, held that underdevelopment is not an original condition but a product, the obverse of the metropolis's development within one historical process. Arghiri Emmanuel's theory of unequal exchange gave the transfer mechanism a value-theoretic form: with capital mobile and labour immobile, profit rates equalise internationally while wages do not, so trade between high-wage and low-wage economies transfers value systematically toward the high-wage side, a construction the orthodox and Sraffian schools each tried to absorb or refute. Samir Amin carried the analysis into a theory of accumulation on a world scale and of the periphery's possible delinking. The world-systems work of Immanuel Wallerstein, adjacent to the tradition and quarrelling with it, widened the unit of analysis from the nation to the system as a whole. This cluster gives the territory its own article, on dependency theory and unequal exchange, and the pillar's duty is discharged by registering the schools' shared conviction and their live disagreement: shared, the conviction that capitalism was a world system from its first centuries and that a theory of one national economy is an abstraction that must justify itself; disputed, nearly everything else, from whether unequal exchange survives the Sraffian critique to whether the periphery's industrialisation refutes dependence or redefines it. The East Asian industrialisations of the late twentieth century are the evidence both sides reach for, which is the mark of a real research frontier rather than a settled doctrine.

## Uneven development: the world economy's texture

The world economy section of this guide named the transfer mechanisms the schools dispute; the texture beneath them is governed, in the tradition's most influential formulation, by the law of uneven and combined development. Trotsky opened his history of the Russian Revolution with the claim: a backward country does not repeat the advanced countries' stages but combines them, importing the most modern forms, the largest factories in Europe, the most concentrated banking, and setting them down in a society of peasant communes and autocracy, so that the combination itself, archaic and ultramodern in one social order, generates the explosive politics and the peculiar economics of the late developer. The claim was generalised by later writers into a theory of the world economy's normal state: development is uneven because capital concentrates where it already is, and combined because the laggard's path is set by the leader's existence, so that no national economy's history is intelligible as a self-contained sequence of stages. The geographer Neil Smith rebuilt the idea as a theory of spatial development, capital's see-saw between equalisation and differentiation across territories, investment draining from the districts it has built up toward the districts whose cheapness its own earlier draining created. Orthodox economists in the field have resisted the elevation of the observation into a law, and the dependency and world-systems literatures took it in opposed directions, but as a piece of the grammar it is secure: when a writer in this tradition confronts a landscape in which the most advanced and the most archaic production stand in one frame, a Gulf financial centre financed by migrant labour housed in camps, a export platform of world-class factories ringed by subsistence farming, the tradition's reflex is to treat the combination as the system's product rather than as a halfway stage on a journey the archaic side will shortly complete.

## Globalisation in the tradition's grammar

When the word globalisation entered general discourse in the nineteen nineties, the tradition received it with an irony its practitioners did not trouble to hide: the *Communist Manifesto* of 1848 contains the classic description of the world market's formation, the bourgeoisie chasing commodities over the whole surface of the globe, battering down national walls with cheap prices, and drawing even the most barbarian nations into civilisation, and a literature descended from that text was not likely to be astonished by the phenomenon its founder had announced as the system's essence. The schools' work on the era has concentrated on three questions. The first is competition: Anwar Shaikh's reconstruction argues that real competition, turbulent and price-cutting, among capitals on a world scale drives the equalisation of profit rates and the relocation of production toward low-cost sites, a process he models as gravitation rather than as the textbook's serene equilibrium, and his long work stands as the orthodox family's most ambitious modern synthesis. The second is the global reorganisation of production itself, supply chains that fragment the labour process across jurisdictions, which the labour process and accumulation literatures read through the reserve army's globalisation: capital now recruits its disciplinary pool on a planetary scale, and national labour movements face a structure their organisations were not built to answer. The third is the hierarchy of monies and states, the dollar's privilege, the discipline the world market exercises over national policies, where the regulation school's attention to modes of insertion into the international order and the monetary theories of value meet the dependency tradition's older questions in recognisably the same place. On all three the programme not doctrine rule applies with its usual force, and the reader who has learned to ask which school is speaking has learned the field's method in its contemporary form.

## Long waves and the golden age

The tradition's most ambitious periodising claim is that capitalist history moves in long waves, expansions and stagnations of roughly a quarter to a half century, above the ordinary business cycle. The claim's ancestry is Soviet: Nikolai Kondratieff, director of the Moscow Conjuncture Institute, published his statistical case for long cycles in the nineteen twenties and perished in the purges his institute could not survive, while Trotsky, from exile, criticised the mechanical version of the idea, insisting that the long phases are not cycles with an internal clock but epochs separated by the great shocks and settlements of class struggle, wars, revolutions, and the opening of new fields of accumulation. That internal criticism, that the waves are real but their motor is historical rather than automatic, became the sophisticated position inside the field. Mandel rebuilt the theory on it: long waves of expansion are ignited by extraordinary rises in the profit rate, themselves the product of historical defeats of labour, new technologies, and new markets arriving together, while the downturns are endogenous, the wave's own success exhausting its conditions, an asymmetry, endogenous decline, exogenous and historical reignition, that his critics inside the programme call an evasion and his defenders call the only form a historical theory of periodicity can honestly take.

The quarter century after 1945 is the test case every school must explain, the golden age of high growth, full employment, and rising wages across the advanced capitalist world, followed by the long downturn after 1973. The regulation school's explanation, Fordism regulated by collective bargaining and the welfare state until its productivity engine stalled, and the accumulation school's version, a social structure built from the capital and labour accord, the regulated corporation, and American hegemony, decaying as each pillar weakened, are cousins, and both are represented in the table's institutional rows. The orthodox account reads the same years through the profit rate and the extraordinary conditions of postwar reconstruction, cheapened capital stock, defeated labour movements in the war's ruins, the backlog of unexploited technology. The mainstream's explanations, centred on catch-up growth and policy errors, are not contemptible, and the field's historians generally concede that the golden age is overdetermined among the rival accounts; what the Marxist schools claim as their distinctive contribution is the insistence that the era's end was structural, written into the settlement's own terms, rather than a sequence of policy mistakes, an insistence the subsequent half century of slow growth in the old centres has made harder to dismiss than it was in 1975.

## The economics of the alternative: the calculation debate

A programme that analyses capitalism as a historical system owes an account of what could replace it, and the attempt to give that account produced one of the great debates of twentieth-century economics, in which this tradition was the defendant. In 1920 Ludwig von Mises argued that a socialist economy, having abolished private ownership of the means of production, would have no prices for capital goods, and without such prices no rational calculation of costs, so that planning in any advanced economy was impossible in principle. Friedrich Hayek sharpened the charge into an epistemological one: the knowledge an economy needs is dispersed, tacit, and generated by the price system itself, so no planning board could assemble it. The response that defined the debate came from Oskar Lange, a Polish economist formed by both Marx and the marginalist mainstream, who proposed in the nineteen thirties that a planning board could mimic the market's trial and error, setting prices parametrically and instructing managers to produce where price met marginal cost, answering Mises on his own ground. The debate's first round is usually scored to the respondents on logic and to the critics on everything after: the actually existing planned economies did industrialise, did allocate, after their fashion, for decades, and did so with chronic shortage, distorted information, and innovation confined largely to the military sector, a record the Hungarian economist János Kornai, writing from inside the system, codified as the economics of shortage and the soft budget constraint.

The tradition's own internal arguments about planning were richer than its public defence. The Soviet debates of the nineteen twenties, over primitive socialist accumulation, over the tempo of industrialisation, over whether the law of value operated under planning, were conducted by economists of real stature before the catechism closed them. The Yugoslav experiment in self-management, the Hungarian market reforms, and the Cuban debates of the nineteen sixties each generated literatures in which the categories of the programme were turned on economies claiming the programme's own name, often by its most loyal practitioners and to their cost. In the computer age the question reopened in a new key: whether modern information technology alters the calculation debate's terms, by making the input and output matrices of a whole economy computable in something like real time, is argued in a literature that ranges from the Chilean Cybersyn experiment of the early nineteen seventies, Stafford Beer's cybernetic management system for Salvador Allende's government, destroyed with that government in 1973, to the formal proposals of Paul Cockshott and Allin Cottrell for planning in labour time with modern computing. The mainstream verdict on the whole debate has itself shifted over the decades, from confidence that Mises had settled it to a more historical judgement in which the planned economies' failures are assigned jointly to information, incentives, and politics, in proportions still disputed. For this guide the registration matters more than the verdict: the calculation debate is the place where the programme's economics met its hardest external test, and every school in the table carries some position on it, from the market socialism the analytical current rebuilt with modern tools to the orthodox insistence that Lange conceded the essential point by keeping prices at all.

## The industrialisation debate: the programme plans an economy

For one decade, in one country, this programme's economics was not commentary but policy science, and the debate it produced is the strongest evidence that its categories could do frontier work when the questions were live and the disputants still free. The question was primitive accumulation under socialist colours. Russia in the nineteen twenties was a peasant country whose industry the war and the revolution had halved; the means of production were nationalised, foreign capital would not come, there were no colonies to squeeze, and yet heavy industry had somehow to be built if the state was to survive. Where was the surplus for industrialisation to come from? Evgeny Preobrazhensky gave the question its theory in *The New Economics* of 1926. A transitional economy, he argued, is governed by two regulators in struggle: the law of value, still ruling the private peasant sector, and the law of primitive socialist accumulation, by which the state sector draws resources from that private sector through its price policy, taxation, and monopoly of foreign trade, as the bourgeoisie before it had drawn them from the countryside by cruder means. The famous scissors, the widening gap between industrial and agricultural prices that Trotsky had graphed, blades opening, before the party congress of 1923, was on this reading not a malfunction but the mechanism itself, visible.

Nikolai Bukharin led the reply, and the reply was also an economics. Squeeze the peasantry beyond a point, he argued, and the alliance on which the regime rested dissolves; worse, the squeeze destroys industry's own market, since a ruined peasantry buys no manufactures, so that accumulation must proceed at what his enemies mocked as a snail's pace, enriching the peasant as industry's customer and taxing the surplus as it grew rather than confiscating it before it existed. Inside the debate, almost as an aside, a Gosplan economist named Grigory Feldman published in 1928 the model that would outlive both disputants: divide investment between the department producing capital goods and the department producing consumption goods, and the share assigned to the first governs the economy's long-run growth rate, since the machines installed in one period decide the capacity of the next in both departments. The two-department scheme of the reproduction analysis had become, in Feldman's hands, the first modern growth model, the ancestor by direct descent of the planning models with which P. C. Mahalanobis framed India's Second Five-Year Plan and a construction the mainstream's growth theory reached independently a generation later. The debate itself was settled by neither argument: the turn of 1928 and 1929 settled it by collectivisation, at a cost in famine that belongs to the history of the regime rather than to the history of economics, and both theorists were eventually shot by the state whose economy they had tried to theorise, Preobrazhensky in 1937 and Bukharin after his show trial in 1938. The field's historians return to the episode for the same reason this pillar records it at length: it is the moment when the programme's practitioners, armed with the reproduction schemes and the theory of accumulation, posed the central question of development economics a quarter century before that discipline had a name, and argued it to a standard their circumstances, in the end, would not permit them to keep.


The debate's afterlife ran through channels its participants could not have foreseen. Development economics, when it constituted itself after the Second World War, posed the identical problem in the identical grammar: how a poor economy with a vast subsistence sector generates the investable surplus for industry, and Arthur Lewis's celebrated model of 1954, of economic development with unlimited supplies of labour, restated the dual economy's logic for the new discipline, acknowledging ancestors among the classical economists where the Marxist contributions had to be read between the lines. Feldman's allocation rule travelled more openly, carried into the Indian planning debates by statisticians who had studied the Soviet literature, so that the fastest route from the reproduction schemes to the practice of a non-communist state runs through a Gosplan journal of 1928. And the historians of the field have drawn the comparison the participants were denied: the questions the debate left open, how fast a surplus can be transferred before the transfer destroys its source, whether the peasantry is industry's victim, its market, or its gravedigger, are the questions on which the great industrialisations of the following century, in Asia above all, would silently adjudicate, with outcomes that vindicated Bukharin's warning about the source and Preobrazhensky's arithmetic about the destination in roughly equal and uncomfortable measure.


## What was the Soviet economy? The programme turned on its own name

No survey of this field can omit the question that consumed more of its polemical energy across the twentieth century than any other: what kind of economy was the Soviet Union, and by extension the states built in its image? The question was unavoidable because the answer determined whether the programme's name was attached, in the eyes of the world, to a going concern or a cautionary tale, and because the categories for answering it had to come from the programme itself. The official answer, that these were socialist economies in the transition to communism, was the catechism's, and it was rejected by nearly every school in the West on evidence the whole world could inspect, the absence of workers' control over the surplus chief among it. The Trotskyist answer, codified by Trotsky himself in the nineteen thirties, held that the Soviet Union was a degenerated workers' state: the property forms of the revolution survived, nationalised and planned, while a bureaucratic caste usurped political power, so that the economy was socialist in its foundations and parasitic in its management, a construction Ernest Mandel defended with great learning for the postwar decades. Against it stood the state capitalist readings, argued from the nineteen forties by Tony Cliff and the tradition he founded, holding that the bureaucracy functioned collectively as a capitalist class, driven by military competition with the West to accumulate for accumulation's sake, so that the law of value ruled the Soviet economy through the world market's pressure rather than through its internal markets. A third family, bureaucratic collectivism and its successors, held that the system was neither capitalist nor socialist but a new form of class society for which the classical categories had to be extended. Charles Bettelheim in France conducted the argument at the highest technical level, insisting that planning and nationalisation were juridical forms under which capitalist relations could persist, against both the official doctrine and the simpler versions of the state capitalist case.

The debate's interest for this guide is what happened to it after 1991. The system's collapse settled none of the analytical quarrels, since each school could read the event through its own construction, restoration of an always-capitalist economy, the strangulation of a transitional one, the exhaustion of a bureaucratic collectivism that could not innovate. But it changed the question's function inside the field: from a live political demarcation, on which organisations split and journals defined themselves, into a historical problem, the analysis of a vanished economy with the programme's tools, where it continues to do useful work as the test bed on which claims about planning, markets, and the law of value are checked against a complete life cycle of the alternative. The episode belongs in the pillar because it exhibits the programme's most characteristic virtue and vice in one place: the refusal to accept the official description of a society whose rulers claimed the programme's own authority, and the proliferation, in the absence of the founder's unfinished book on the state, of incompatible constructions each claiming the inheritance.

## Economics under the catechism: the paradox of the planned economies

The strangest chapter in the field's institutional history is the one in which it was an official doctrine with the resources of states behind it, and the chapter's paradox is that genuine economics flourished there in inverse proportion to the doctrine's authority. Political economy, the discipline that carried the name, fossilised into the two-part textbook, capitalism condemned by its laws, socialism vindicated by its own, with the categories guarded from the evidence by the simple device of examining students on the conclusions. Mathematical economics, by contrast, having no conclusions to guard, was permitted to become excellent: Leonid Kantorovich's linear programming, invented in 1939 to solve a plywood trust's production problem and recognised with a Nobel prize four decades later, Victor Novozhilov's theory of optimal allocation, and the Soviet school of optimal planning built the technical apparatus that a rational planning would have required, inside a system whose political economy forbade the prices and the honesty about scarcity on which the apparatus depended. Wassily Leontief, formed in the Petrograd of the revolution's first debates before his emigration, carried the input and output method to Harvard and to a Nobel of his own, a lineage the reproduction schemes anticipate. The Liberman reforms of the nineteen sixties, which proposed to judge Soviet enterprises by profitability, provoked the last great internal debate of the catechism era, in which the law of value's operation under socialism was argued at a level of seriousness the surrounding textbook literature never approached, and was then closed, as the debates of the nineteen twenties had been closed, by decision rather than by refutation. The Western field watched this record with the discomfort the Soviet question section described, and drew from it the lesson its post-1989 historians repeat: the programme's economics and the catechism that claimed it were different things, and the proof is that where the tradition was free it quarrelled, and where it was established it slept, except in the mathematical annex where the conclusions could not be prescribed because the theorems would not obey.

## The taxonomy of modes and the Asiatic question

Behind the programme's historical sections stands a taxonomy that caused its custodians acute embarrassment: the sequence of modes of production. The founder's scattered remarks support several lists, and one entry, the Asiatic mode, named for societies in which the state, as organiser of irrigation and collector of the surplus in tax and rent fused, confronted village communities that reproduced themselves beneath it, proved politically indigestible. In the Soviet codification the concept was suppressed in the nineteen thirties, since a mode in which the state itself was the exploiting power sat too close to questions the catechism could not afford, and the five-stage sequence, primitive, slave, feudal, capitalist, socialist, was canonised in its place. Western scholarship recovered the suppressed debate after the war, Karl Wittfogel's hostile reconstruction of the hydraulic thesis forcing the argument into the open, and the Indian historians' controversy over whether precolonial India fitted any European category became a test of the taxonomy's universality. The episode is small in the field's economics and large in what it reveals: the taxonomy is a research instrument in the free literature, where the modes multiply and hybridise as the evidence requires, and a loyalty test in the catechism, where the list had to be short enough to memorise and safe enough to print. A reader who understands why one concept was worth suppressing understands the difference between the programme and its official shadow better than any definition can teach it, and the guide closes its survey of the field's provinces there, on the distinction the whole article has been built to enforce.

## The transition debate: the programme does economic history

The single most celebrated research episode in the tradition's modern history was an argument about the remote past: how feudalism gave way to capitalism in Europe. It began in the pages of *Science and Society* in 1950, when Maurice Dobb, whose *Studies in the Development of Capitalism* had appeared in 1946, and Paul Sweezy exchanged a series of critiques that drew in scholars from several countries and was gathered in 1976 as *The Transition from Feudalism to Capitalism*. The question was precise and theoretical: what dissolved the feudal mode of production, an internal contradiction, the class struggle over the surplus between lords and peasants within a decaying system, as Dobb held, or an external solvent, the growth of trade and towns corroding feudal relations from outside, as Sweezy argued? The stakes were the programme's theory of historical change itself, since the two answers implied opposed accounts of how any mode of production dies, by its own contradictions or by contact with a more dynamic neighbour.

The debate's second round raised its stakes again. Robert Brenner, writing in *Past and Present* in 1976, attacked both demographic explanations, which attributed the divergent paths of Eastern and Western Europe to population movements, and commercialisation explanations of Sweezy's type, and relocated the cause in the social property relations themselves: where peasants won secure property rights, as in France, development stalled in smallholding; where lords won the power to expropriate and enclose, as in England, agrarian capitalism became possible; where lords re-enserfed the peasantry, as east of the Elbe, the same demographic and commercial pressures produced the opposite of capitalism. The Brenner debate, as the ensuing decade of exchanges in the same journal came to be called, is the tradition's showpiece for a reason: it was an argument conducted with archival evidence and comparative method, about a question the theory itself had generated, in which positions were refined and in places surrendered under the pressure of the record, and it recruited historians with no allegiance to the programme into its terms. A field accused of settling questions by quotation from its founder here settled, or honourably failed to settle, a large question by the ordinary methods of historical science, and the episode is the strongest single exhibit for the defence when the strongest objection is heard.

## The first falsification debate: Bernstein and the strategy of reform

The programme's first great internal crisis was an empirical one, and it set the pattern for all the later ones. Eduard Bernstein, Engels's literary executor and no enemy from outside, published in 1899 the argument that the facts had overtaken the theory: cartels and credit were softening crises rather than sharpening them, the middle classes were not disappearing, real wages were rising, the catastrophe the movement expected was receding, and the movement should therefore become what it already was in practice, a democratic party of reform. Rosa Luxemburg's *Reform or Revolution*, written in reply within the year, is the orthodox tradition's founding document precisely because it refused the comfortable resolution: the credit and cartel mechanisms Bernstein cited were, she argued, the crisis mechanism's own instruments, sharpening the anarchy they appeared to tame, and a movement that surrendered the final goal would find the daily goals themselves undefended, since the threat of the alternative was reform's only leverage. The debate's structure, an insider's appeal to the statistics against the system's predicted trajectory, met by a defence that reinterprets the same statistics through the theory's categories, has repeated at every subsequent juncture, over the postwar boom, over the seventies crisis, over the neoliberal decades, and the field's maturity can be measured by how much of Bernstein's evidence each generation's orthodoxy has quietly absorbed while keeping his name in the index of errors.

The strategic argument had a second life in government. The Swedish wage-earner funds proposed by Rudolf Meidner in the nineteen seventies, under which a levy on profits would issue shares to collective funds that would gradually socialise ownership of the large firms without expropriation, were the most serious attempt ever made to translate the programme's analysis into a governing labour movement's economic strategy, drafted by the trade union confederation's own economists and defeated, after a decade of mobilisation against them, in diluted form. The French experiment of 1981 to 1983, a socialist government attempting reflation and nationalisation inside an open economy and retreating under the pressure of the franc and the bond markets, supplied the regulation school and the state theorists with their canonical modern case of the structural constraints on reforming governments, the state's dependence on the accumulation it seeks to discipline. Both episodes live in the field's journals as worked examples of its central practical question, what room for manoeuvre the system's reproduction requirements leave to a government elected to change them, and the schools' answers, from the accumulation school's measurements of the profit squeeze such governments provoke to the orthodox insistence that the room is illusory, constitute the tradition's nearest approach to a policy science.

## Kalecki and the argument with Keynesianism

No figure stands closer to this tradition while standing at an angle to it than Michał Kalecki, the Polish economist who arrived at the core of Keynesian theory by the Marxist road and earlier, in essays of 1933 to 1935 built on the reproduction schemes and the division of the economy into departments, and who spent the rest of his career in a dialogue with both camps that neither has ever quite claimed. Kalecki's construction took from the tradition its class anatomy, workers who spend what they earn, capitalists who earn what they spend, his own inversion of the causality of the textbooks, and produced a theory of effective demand, of the trade cycle driven by investment's dual character, and of profits determined by capitalist expenditure, that the Cambridge Keynesians recognised as kin and that Joan Robinson championed in England. His 1943 essay on the political aspects of full employment, written while he worked at the Oxford Institute of Statistics, remains the tradition's most quoted single article in the wider profession: it argued that lasting full employment would undermine the social discipline of the sack, that captains of industry would therefore press governments to engineer the slack that orthodox policy attributed to necessity, and so predicted the political business cycle and the eventual revolt against the postwar settlement a generation before both arrived. The episode matters to this guide because it marks the field's most productive border crossing: a construction whose foundations, departments, classes, reproduction, are this programme's own, addressing the short-run questions the programme had neglected since Marx treated the cycle as a periodic form rather than a theory, and achieving inside the neighbouring discipline the influence the marginality account says the programme lacks. The schools claim Kalecki variously, as a Marxist who completed the short-period theory, as a post-Keynesian who borrowed the categories, or as the proof that the two programmes share classical roots deep enough to make the border negotiable; the argument over his estate is the argument over the border itself, and it is conducted, characteristically, with complete bibliographies.

The wider argument with Keynesianism follows the same lines at lower intensity. The tradition has generally held that Keynes diagnosed the demand failure correctly and explained it wrongly, locating it in the psychology of liquidity preference rather than in the profit expectations and distributional structure that govern investment in this programme's account; that his remedies addressed the level of activity while leaving the relations of production, and therefore the causes of the failure's recurrence, untouched; and that the postwar Keynesian settlement was, in the accumulation school's phrase, one social structure among others, with a life cycle of its own. Keynesian economists have returned the compliment in kind, absorbing Kalecki, conceding the political cycle, and declining the foundations. A pillar article need not adjudicate; it registers that the border between the programmes is the most permeable in the field, and that a reader wishing to see the tradition's categories at work on the short run should start exactly there.

## The Cambridge capital controversy: the field's near-victory

The objection section of this guide entered, as part of the defence, the episode in which the neighbouring discipline's foundations were shaken from inside its own citadel, and the episode deserves its facts. In 1953 and 1954 Joan Robinson, working at Cambridge in England in the classical and Keynesian line this programme shares, opened the question of what the capital in an aggregate production function could possibly be: a sum of heterogeneous machines and structures measurable only in prices that themselves depend on the profit rate the function is meant to determine, so that the theory's central magnitude is defined in a circle. Sraffa's construction then supplied the demonstration's engine: in a system of production equations, the same technique can be profit-maximising at both a low and a high rate of profit, with a different technique ruling between them, reswitching, and a technique can become cheaper as the profit rate rises, capital reversing, both results fatal to the parable in which a higher profit rate corresponds monotonically to less capital-intensive methods. The ensuing controversy between the two Cambridges, England and Massachusetts, ran through the nineteen sixties in the leading journals, and it ended, formally, in a concession: Paul Samuelson, summarising for the English side's symposium in 1966, acknowledged that the simple parable could not be sustained in general and that scholars must respect the facts of reswitching even where the facts were unwelcome. What happened next is the sociological point on which this tradition's telling insists: the concession was made, recorded, and set aside, the textbooks continued to teach the parable with at most a footnote, no research programme was reorganised, and the generation trained after the controversy largely never heard of it. The Marxist and Sraffian participants drew the moral the marginality account predicts: that a fundamental criticism's fate in this profession is decided less by its validity, which had been conceded, than by the availability of an alternative practice for the profession to go on with, and that the absence of such an alternative, in departments, journals, and textbooks, is the material form marginality takes. Critics of the telling reply that the aggregate parable was a pedagogical simplification whose abandonment in serious work cost little, and the argument, like the capital theory itself, remains where the sixties left it, which is to say conceded and unsettled at once.

## The keeper of the classical tradition

An underreported fact about this field is that the whole discipline of economics owes some of its own patrimony to it. The classical tradition of Smith, Ricardo, and Mill, from which both the mainstream and this programme descend, was edited, interpreted, and in part rescued by scholars working in or beside the Marxist current. Sraffa's edition of Ricardo's works and correspondence, eleven volumes prepared over three decades for the Royal Economic Society, is the monument: undertaken at the suggestion of Keynes, executed by a scholar whose own short book then reopened the classical surplus approach as a living alternative, and assisted in its early stages by Dobb. Maurice Dobb's and Ronald Meek's histories and studies carried the classical lineage into the postwar classroom, and the history of economic thought, the one corner of the mainstream syllabus where this tradition's scholars were routinely assigned and read, functioned for decades as the field's tolerated enclave inside the profession, the place where its erudition was undeniable even to those who rejected its conclusions. The enclave mattered to the programme's survival: it kept posts open, trained students who migrated from the history to the theory, and preserved the classical questions, distribution, surplus, the long run, in a professional memory from which the frontier journals had deleted them. When the mainstream's own capital controversies of the nineteen sixties forced the classical questions back into view, it was this enclave's library that the profession reached for, and the episode stands as the complement to the marginality account: the exclusion was real, and so was the dependence it concealed.

## The Uno school and the Japanese reconstruction

Japan's Marxian economics, flagged in this guide's account of the moving centre of gravity, contains the tradition's most systematic attempt to rebuild the whole architecture, and a pillar article should state its design. Kōzō Uno, whose major works appeared from the nineteen thirties to the nineteen sixties, argued that Marxist economics must be practised at three distinct levels that the classical texts leave fused. The first is the pure theory of a purely capitalist society, an idealised construction in which the commodity, value, money, and capital forms are developed in their logical purity, abstracting from every historical impurity, and Uno's reworking of the value form's dialectic at this level is the Japanese school's foundation stone. The second level is stages theory, the theory of capitalism's historical stages, mercantilist, liberal, and imperialist, in which the dominant form of capital and the characteristic policies of each stage are specified. The third is empirical analysis of concrete economies, conducted with the first two levels as instruments and forbidden to contaminate them. The architecture was a deliberate answer to the Soviet catechism's fusion of theory with the current line, and to the opposite Western habit of reading *Capital* as a description of nineteenth-century England. Its English-language transmission, through Thomas Sekine's reconstructions and the work of the school's second generation, made it the most philosophically ambitious of the national traditions, and its debates with the value-form current in Europe, over whether the dialectic of the form belongs to logic or to history, are among the field's most refined. The Uno school is not in the eight schools table, since the table maps the Western schools among which the cluster's other articles move, and its absence, like the autonomist current's contested presence, is a reminder the guide has issued before: the map is a working instrument, not a census of the world's Marxian economics.

## The audience commodity: the programme enters media economics

One of the tradition's most fertile exports began as a charge of blindness. In 1977 the Canadian political economist Dallas Smythe published his argument that communications scholarship, Marxist scholarship included, suffered a blindspot: it analysed media as ideology and superstructure while ignoring the economic question of what the mass media produce. His answer, that commercial media produce audiences, packaged by demographic qualities and sold to advertisers, with the audience's viewing itself a form of labour that reproduces labour power and learns it to consume, converted the advertiser-supported media from a puzzle of the superstructure into a branch of production with a commodity, a labour process, and a purchaser. The blindspot debate that followed, in the journals of media studies and in this field's own, drew the standard distribution of responses: development by scholars who extended the construction to the ratings industry and the measured audience; correction by those who held that audiences are at most quasi-commodities since their labour is unwaged and their sale metaphorical; and, in time, extension to the digital platforms, where the audience's activity is metered continuously and the commodity form Smythe asserted metaphorically threatens to become literal. The episode is this pillar's last exhibit for the same reason it is included at all: a programme accused of having nothing to say to new industries produced, from its oldest grammar, production, commodity, labour, surplus, the founding question of an entire subfield, and the subfield has been arguing with the answer ever since, which is the programme's idea of success.

## Ecology and the metabolic rift

The tradition's newest province illustrates its method of growth by recovery. In Marx's manuscripts and in the first volume of *Capital* there is an analysis, drawn from the agricultural chemistry of Justus von Liebig, of the metabolic relation between town and country: capitalism concentrates population in cities, ships food and fibre one way and returns nothing to the soil, and so produces a rift in the metabolism between human beings and the earth, soil exhaustion alongside urban waste. The passage was quoted for a century as a curiosity. John Bellamy Foster's *Marx's Ecology* in 2000 reconstructed the full argument and its place in Marx's materialism, and the metabolic rift became the organising concept of a Marxist environmental literature that now extends from soil science to the theory of climate change, where the rift's general form, a system compelled to treat the conditions of its own reproduction as free and inexhaustible, is applied to the atmosphere's capacity to absorb carbon. Neighbouring currents argue over whether value theory helps or hinders the application, the world-ecology work associated with Jason Moore pressing the case that the cheapening of nature belongs inside the theory of accumulation rather than beside it.

The province matters to a pillar article for two reasons beyond its content. It shows the programme's characteristic mechanism of renewal, in which a neglected stratum of the founder's texts is recovered by scholarship and turns out to organise a research front the founder could not have foreseen, the same mechanism that operated with the *Grundrisse* and the theory of the value form. And it shows the programme posing, once again, a question the neighbouring disciplines had underposed: mainstream environmental economics treats ecological breakdown as an externality, a market failure at the edge of an otherwise sound allocation mechanism, where the rift literature treats it as the predictable consequence of a metabolism organised by accumulation, a difference of object exactly parallel to the one with which this guide began. Whether the ecological reconstruction becomes a ninth school or remains a cross-cutting front is a question the field will settle by its usual method, by arguing about it for thirty years.

## The method of abstraction: from the cell-form to the concrete

One methodological commitment underlies the exegetical quarrels and deserves statement in the pillar, since it explains why the tradition's arguments so often appear to begin at the strangest possible place. The method, sketched in Marx's introduction of 1857, is an ascent from the abstract to the concrete: analysis begins not with the living whole, population, the market, the nation, whose concreteness is a chaotic conception, but with the simplest determinations, value, money, the commodity as the system's cell-form, and reconstructs the whole by successively adding the determinations that the abstract forms generate and require, until the concrete is reproduced in thought as a rich totality of many determinations. The method's discipline is that each category must be introduced at the point where the argument needs it and not before, which is why the order of *Capital* is itself an argument, why its first pages concern the commodity's two factors and not the factory, and why the schools fight over the correct beginning, the value-form current treating the opening dialectic as the whole method in miniature, the Uno school formalising the ascent as the pure theory's logic, the Soviet philosopher Evald Ilyenkov rebuilding it as a general logic of the abstract and the concrete before his work was cut short. The mainstream's method proceeds, in this tradition's characterisation, by the opposite motion, from the concrete case to the model by subtraction, stripping context until a tractable mechanism remains; and the two methods produce their characteristic blindnesses symmetrically, the model that cannot find its way back to history, the ascent that cannot stop ascending to reach a testable claim. A reader who holds the method in view will understand the field's books, their lengths, their orders, their openings, and will recognise the method's failures, the reconstructions that mistake the order of exposition for the structure of the world, when they occur.

## Critique, not doctrine: the subtitle's programme

The founder's book is called *Capital: A Critique of Political Economy*, and the tradition's most reflective current has always insisted that the subtitle is a description of method rather than a decoration. Political economy, the science of Smith and Ricardo, had presented its categories, value, capital, wage labour, rent, as the natural and eternal forms of economic life as such. A critique of political economy does not primarily correct its predecessors' answers, though it does that abundantly; it interrogates their categories, showing that each is the form of appearance of a historically specific social relation, valid for this mode of production, misleading when projected onto all others, and concealing in its very obviousness the relations it expresses. The plough example from this guide's early pages is the method in miniature: the same object is not capital in the village and is capital in the enterprise, because the category belongs not to the thing but to the relation. Read this way, the labour theory of value is itself a critical category before it is a measuring device: its first claim is that wealth under capitalism necessarily appears as value, and that a society whose members relate through the exchange of their products will generate the appearances, price, profit, the wage form, that the rest of the theory then analyses. This is the reading the value-form current elevated into the whole interpretation of the work, and the reason its practitioners regard the magnitude quarrels as secondary: on the critical reading, the transformation problem's persistence results from asking a theory of forms to behave as a theory of quantities, a category mistake the founder's method exists to expose.

The critical reading's most ambitious modern statement is Moishe Postone's *Time, Labor, and Social Domination* of 1993, which reconstructed the mature Marx as the theorist not of labour's emancipation from capital but of the historically specific centrality of labour itself. On Postone's reading, traditional Marxism, his name for most of the tradition this guide has surveyed, criticised capitalism from the standpoint of labour, indicting the distribution of its products and the ownership of its means, while leaving labour's role as the social mediation untouched and thereby affirming the very structure it opposed. The mature critique, by contrast, treats value as a form of social mediation specific to capitalism, in which people relate through their products and their labour is the substance of the bond, so that the system's overcoming would mean the abolition of that mediation rather than its fairer administration, and the domination it exercises is abstract, exercised by structures and by time itself, the compulsion of socially necessary labour time, rather than only by a class of persons. The reconstruction reorganised several fields at once: it gave the value-form current a systematic philosophy, reframed the theory of antisemitism within the tradition's terms in a much argued essay on abstract domination's personalised form, and supplied the ecological and post-work literatures with their reading of the founder. It also drew the standing objections in strengthened form: that a Marx whose economics dissolves into a critique of mediation has been rescued from refutation by being rescued from content, and that the magnitude schools' boring questions, what the profit rate did in 1974 and why, survive every reconstruction of what the questions mean.

The pillar article takes no side in that quarrel, which is the deepest in the contemporary field, but it registers what the quarrel proves about the pillar's subject. A doctrine does not generate, after a hundred and fifty years, a reinterpretation of this power that rearranges the meaning of its central terms and recruits new disciplines to the argument. A research programme does, because its founding texts contain more structure than any single generation's reading exhausts, and because its hard core, the insistence that the economy's obvious categories are historical forms to be explained rather than data to be assumed, continues to make work for anyone who takes it seriously. The reader who has followed this guide from its definition to this point possesses the field as its practitioners argue over it: eight schools and their penumbra, a shared grammar and incompatible completions, a marginal institution and an undismissable set of questions. The remaining sections discharge the guide's promises in order: an account of where the field is taught and published, of why it stands where it stands, of the strongest case against it, and of where a new reader goes next.

## The centre of gravity moves: the tradition after Marx

The field the reader meets in the journals is not the field Marx founded, and the differences are best explained by following its centre of gravity as it moved, from the workers' movement to the party school, from the party school to the state academy, and from the state academy to the Western university and, at last, to a dispersed global network. Each move changed what the economics was for, and the schools in the table are unintelligible without the itinerary.

Marx wrote as a revolutionary in exile, in the reading room of the British Museum, for a movement that did not yet possess a single state, a single university chair, or a single journal of its own. *Capital* was movement literature in the strict sense: its first readers were the organisers of the International Workingmen's Association and the German party press, and its economics was continuous with agitation, which is why the book argues simultaneously against the factory inspectors' complacency and against rival socialists. After Marx's death the movement built its schools, and economics changed character in them. The Second International codified the doctrine: Karl Kautsky, the pope of Marxism, wrote the commentaries and the popularisations through which two generations of workers learned the theory, and the Erfurt programme gave the codification a party seal. The codified tradition produced real peaks, Rudolf Hilferding's *Finance Capital* in 1910, which rebuilt the theory for the age of banks and cartels and carried its author into a finance ministry, and Rosa Luxemburg's *The Accumulation of Capital* in 1913, which attacked the reproduction schemes' complacency about markets and was savaged by the orthodox for her trouble. But codification has a price, and the price was a growing identification of the economics with the defence of settled conclusions, the habit the programme not doctrine rule exists to correct.

The Russian Revolution split the field's geography for three generations. In the Soviet Union one reading became a state doctrine with institutes, chairs, and censorship at its disposal; the brilliant debates of the nineteen twenties, in which Isaak Rubin reconstructed the value form and economists argued seriously about planning and the market, were closed down by decree, and political economy in the socialist states settled into the catechism form, two systems, two textbooks, conclusions first. In the West the tradition lived by exile and by opposition. Henryk Grossmann, working at the Frankfurt Institute before its flight from Germany, published his breakdown theory of accumulation in 1929 and spent exile in Paris, London, and New York arguing that the system's limits were arithmetical. Paul Sweezy's *The Theory of Capitalist Development* in 1942 gave the English-speaking world its first systematic textbook of the whole doctrine, written from Harvard's margins. Maurice Dobb at Cambridge carried the tradition inside the British profession's citadel, writing its economic history, recruiting its students, and performing the act of midwifery, little noted at the time, of encouraging his friend Piero Sraffa's edition of Ricardo, from which the Sraffian current would later grow. The postwar settlement then froze the map: a formalising mainstream consolidated in the expanding American universities, and Marxist economics survived in redoubts, a journal here, a department there, a party school elsewhere, its practitioners often more isolated from one another than from the mainstream colleagues down the corridor.

The nineteen sixties thawed the map. The expansion of higher education created posts faster than orthodoxy could fill them, the student movements created readers by the hundred thousand, and the field acquired in a decade the institutional skeleton it still has: the Union for Radical Political Economics in the United States in 1968 and its journal, the Conference of Socialist Economists in Britain and its journal *Capital and Class* from 1977, new translations, new controversies. The nineteen seventies were the field's most technically productive decade since the nineteen twenties, precisely because its schools were forced into open combat: the value controversy, as the British debate was called, pitted fundamentalists against Sraffians in print, the Okishio theorem was stated and answered, analytical Marxism assembled at the decade's end, and the regulation school and the social structure of accumulation approach were founded within a few years of each other on opposite sides of the Atlantic. Then the ground moved again. The collapse of the European socialist states after 1989 discredited the tradition by association in the eyes of deans and editors, including among many who could not have stated a single one of its propositions; posts vanished, programmes closed, and the field contracted back to its journals and its strongest redoubts. The financial crisis of 2008 reversed the current of curiosity without reversing the institutional contraction: sales of *Capital* rose sharply in several countries, reading groups multiplied, a generation of students arrived asking the questions the field exists to answer, and found the field smaller than its audience. That mismatch, a large curious public and a small institutional base, is the tradition's present condition, and it shapes everything from its publishing economics to its tone.

One further migration resists the Atlantic framing of the story so far. Japan built the most complete national Marxian economics of the twentieth century: *Capital* was translated early and read massively, the interwar debate on Japanese capitalism, between the Kōza and Rōnō schools over whether the Meiji land settlement left the economy feudal or made it capitalist, was a genuinely national research programme conducted in Marxist categories, and Kōzō Uno rebuilt the theory into a three-level architecture, pure theory, stages theory, and empirical analysis, that his school elaborated for decades. Work of the first rank also accumulated in India, in Latin America, where the dependency debates began, and in China, where the tradition is at once official inheritance and live research problem. A guide that treated the field as an Anglo-American story with European ancestors would misdescribe its actual demography: the eight schools were named in the West, but the readership, and increasingly the work, is global.

## The institutional map: journals, associations, and redoubts

A research programme is also an infrastructure, and this one's infrastructure is unusually visible because so much of it had to be built outside the profession's ordinary channels. The journals come first, since they are the field's true departments. *Science and Society*, founded in New York in 1936, is the senior Marxist scholarly journal in English, a quarterly that has carried the tradition's philosophy, history, and economics through every political weather since the Depression. *Monthly Review*, founded in 1949 by Paul Sweezy and Leo Huberman, is a magazine rather than a refereed journal, independent of any university or party, and its role has been different: to give the monopoly capital current and the wider radical tradition a monthly voice answerable to readers rather than to referees. The *Review of Radical Political Economics*, appearing from 1968 as the journal of the Union for Radical Political Economics, is the flagship of the American academic wing, refereed, pluralist within the radical camp, and the venue where the field's empirical work most often appears. In Britain, *Capital and Class*, the journal of the Conference of Socialist Economists since 1977, carried the value controversy and the state debates and remains the British current's organ. The *Cambridge Journal of Economics*, also founded in 1977, is heterodox rather than Marxist specifically, a home the field shares with post-Keynesian and institutionalist neighbours, and much cross-fertilisation has passed through it. *Rethinking Marxism*, founded in 1988 around the Amherst milieu, gave the poststructuralist and overdeterminationist currents their journal, and *Historical Materialism*, founded in 1997, became the European-centred journal of record for the tradition's theoretical work across economics, philosophy, and history. Around these stand the sympathetic neighbours, *New Left Review* above all, and the heterodox commons of *Economy and Society* and the *Journal of Economic Issues*, so that a determined reader can follow the entire conversation in periodicals without opening a mainstream journal at all, which is precisely the fact the field's critics cite when they call it a conversation with itself.

The associations mirror the journals. The Union for Radical Political Economics organises the American presence, running sessions at the profession's annual meetings whose grudging hospitality is itself a document of the marginality discussed below. The Conference of Socialist Economists did the corresponding British work. More recent federations, associations for heterodox economics in Britain and an international initiative for political economy founded to coordinate the pluralist teaching programmes, reflect the field's strategic decision, taken after the contractions of the nineteen nineties, to defend common institutional ground with neighbouring heterodoxies rather than to guard a pure and shrinking perimeter. The independent publishing houses belong on the same map: Verso in London and the Monthly Review Press in New York have carried the tradition's books for decades, joined by the academic lists of Pluto, Brill's Historical Materialism book series, and the university presses that take its monographs, and by the great translation projects, above all the fifty-volume collected works of Marx and Engels in English, completed as the century turned.

Departments require the durable phrasing the subject demands, since faculties change faster than reputations and this guide names no current postholder and ranks nothing. A small number of university economics departments in Britain and North America have been long associated, across decades rather than hiring cycles, with the teaching of this tradition: the economics department at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, which in the nineteen seventies made the profession's most famous experiment in pluralist hiring and has taught radical political economy at doctoral level ever since; the economics department of the New School for Social Research in New York, heir to an older institutionalist and emigré inheritance; the School of Oriental and African Studies in London, where political economy is taught within development studies; and a scattering of British programmes in political economy and heterodox economics in London and the post-nineteen-nineties universities. Beyond these, the tradition is taught as a course rather than as a programme, a term on Marx inside a history of thought sequence, a heterodox option inside a pluralist degree, and its presence in any given catalogue is best described as thin, real, and unevenly distributed. The teaching question the seeded questions ask, whether Marxist economics is taught in universities at all, therefore has a two-part answer: as a doctoral research programme, in a handful of places; as a subject students encounter, in many more, usually briefly and usually through the history of economic thought, which is where the mainstream files the tradition when it files it anywhere.

## The textbooks: how the field has taught itself

A programme's textbooks are its self-portrait at a moment, and this one's sequence of portraits tells the centre of gravity story in miniature. The movement era's textbook was Kautsky's exposition of Marx's economic doctrines, a catechism in the neutral sense, conclusions arranged for memorisation by readers who would be examined by hostile employers rather than by examiners. The modern sequence begins with Sweezy's *The Theory of Capitalist Development* in 1942, the first textbook in English to present the whole system as an academic construction, Böhm-Bawerk and Bortkiewicz included, and to treat its difficulties as difficulties rather than as heresies; for thirty years it was the door through which the English-speaking world entered the field, and its lucidity set the standard the genre still acknowledges. Mandel's *Marxist Economic Theory*, published in French in 1962 and in English six years later, attempted the complementary feat at the other scale, a two-volume reconstruction of the entire doctrine with the postwar economy as its evidence, and his introductions to the Penguin edition of *Capital* in the nineteen seventies taught a generation to read the founder with the schools held in suspension. Maurice Dobb's *Political Economy and Capitalism* of 1937 and Ronald Meek's *Studies in the Labour Theory of Value* of 1956 mark the British academy's contribution, the first an argument with the mainstream conducted on its own ground, the second the postwar defence of the value theory that the value controversy would later put on trial. The genre's scholarly summit is M. C. Howard and J. E. King's *A History of Marxian Economics*, published in two volumes in 1989 and 1992, an external history of the whole tradition written to the standards of the history of economic thought, on which this guide has drawn for its judgements of proportion; and Meghnad Desai's *Marxian Economics* of 1979 showed that the theory could be written as a compact formal textbook for students of the mainstream itself. The pedagogy has a second, quieter channel: the reading group. *Capital* has been read aloud, chapter by chapter, in party schools, union halls, prisons, and university basements for a hundred and fifty years, and the tradition's reproduction as a body of readers has depended as much on that unexamined institution as on any syllabus, a fact the field's historians note whenever the question of its survival, against every institutional prediction, is raised once more.

## The empirical programme: measurement as battleground

It would be a mistake to picture this tradition as purely textual. A large share of its journal pages is empirical: estimates, decompositions, historical series, and arguments about data that are conducted with the same ferocity as the arguments about texts. The battlegrounds are predictable from the theory. The first is the rate of profit, where the schools' opposed computations from the same national accounts were described earlier: whether the capital stock is valued at historical or replacement cost, whether the financial sector's profits count as surplus or as a deduction from it, whether government is inside or outside the productive economy, each convention moves the trend, and the literature contains an unusually candid sub-literature about the conventions themselves, since in this field measurement choices are known to be theory choices wearing statistical dress. The second battleground is the correlation between values and prices in input and output data, where strong correlations are claimed by one camp as the value theory's empirical vindication and dismissed by the other as an artefact of cost structure. The third is the long-run behaviour of distribution, the wage and profit shares across the phases the institutional schools periodise, where the American and French traditions have built their most durable findings.

The fourth battleground is prediction, and it is the one the field's critics choose. The record here must be stated without cosmetics. Dated predictions of imminent breakdown recur in the tradition's movement literature from the eighteen nineties onward and were falsified by the calendar with a regularity that damaged the programme's credit inside the profession. Against that record the defenders enter two exhibits: the tradition's structural expectations, that crises would recur endogenously, that concentration would proceed, that finance would swell to dominate production, that globalisation would follow capital's search for cheaper labour, were borne out as patterns even where every date attached to them failed; and the mainstream's own predictive record in the same decades, above all its tranquillity before the crisis of 2008, is available for comparison. The honest summary, and the one this guide adopts, is that the programme has been a better theory of the system's recurring pathologies than of their timing, and that it has too often claimed the second virtue on the strength of the first. The cluster's article on testing the tradition empirically takes up the whole measurement dossier, the testability map and the classification problem, and readers who want the verdicts rather than the map should go there after the marginality account that follows.

## Mathematics, method, and the myth of the innumerate tradition

### Does the tradition reject mathematics?

No; several of its schools are among the most formal in all of economics. The Sraffian current works in systems of simultaneous equations, analytical Marxists built game theoretic models of class and exploitation, and the empirical wing runs regressions on national accounts. What the tradition rejects is the idea that formalisation settles questions of substance by itself.

The myth persists because the tradition's most visible texts are literary and because its founder wrote before the discipline's mathematical turn. The record runs the other way at almost every point where the field's frontier was set. The reproduction schemes were a working input and output system before Leontief, whose own tables descend in part from the same classical lineage; the Soviet planning debates produced Kantorovich's linear programming, whatever the regime later did to its author's context; von Neumann's growth model of the nineteen thirties, a jewel of mainstream mathematical economics, is a classical surplus model in modern dress, a fact its commentators in both camps have noted; Morishima's *Marx's Economics* in 1973 rebuilt the value theory with linear algebra and proved, inside the formalism, the fundamental theorem linking positive profit to positive surplus value, a result whose clarity converted the exploitation claim, for a generation of mathematically trained readers, from a slogan into a theorem with stated assumptions; and Okishio's countertheorem on the profit rate, the strongest single technical objection the tradition has faced, was likewise the work of a Japanese Marxist economist arguing inside the mathematics. The schools' quarrels since have been conducted substantially in the discipline's formal languages. What divides the programme from the mainstream is not the presence of mathematics but its office: in the mainstream's self-image, the model increasingly is the theory, and a phenomenon that resists the tractable formalism is set aside; in this tradition's self-image, the formalism is an instrument subordinate to a theory stated in concepts, and a result that cannot be translated back into the concepts, about classes, surplus, and reproduction, has not yet been understood. Fair critics exist on both sides of that methodological border, but the border is about the philosophy of modelling, not about whether the tradition can count.

## Why the field is marginal: two explanations, both required

Every account of this tradition must eventually explain its address: why a programme of this age, scope, and internal variety occupies so small a place in the profession that studies its object. Two families of explanation exist, and the first rule of honesty in this territory, laid down in the brief for this article and enforced throughout the cluster, is that an account offering only one of them is incomplete by construction. The sociological explanation locates the cause in the profession's own history and institutions. The intellectual explanation locates it in the programme's genuine difficulties. Both are documented; both are resisted, predictably, by the audiences they discomfort; and the schools themselves divide over the weighting, which is one more reason the programme not doctrine rule applies to the sociology of the field as much as to its economics.

The sociological record first, stated as plainly as the evidence allows. The economics profession consolidated after the Second World War around a formalised mainstream whose departments, concentrated in the United States, controlled hiring, journals, and the definition of competence for the world. A programme whose founding commitments reject the consolidation's core, its unit of analysis, its equilibrium frame, its definition of the subject itself, was not so much refuted by that settlement as defined out of it, excluded at the level of the syllabus and the shortlist before any argument could occur. Layered over the settlement in the United States was a documented political pressure. During the nineteen fifties, loyalty investigations reached into the universities, economists were summoned to answer for their associations and their teaching, appointments were blocked or rescinded, and the lesson was learned by a generation about which subjects ended careers.

### What happened to radical economists in the nineteen fifties?

Many lost their posts or their prospects. Loyalty investigations reached into American universities, economists were summoned before committees to answer for their associations and their lectures, appointments were blocked, and a generation learned which research topics ended careers. The best documented case, a refusal to testify that reached the Supreme Court, belongs to this field's own history.

The case is Paul Sweezy's. Subpoenaed by the attorney general of New Hampshire in an investigation of subversive activities, Sweezy refused to answer questions about a guest lecture he had given at the state university and about his political associations, was held in contempt, and carried the case to the Supreme Court of the United States, which in 1957 reversed his conviction in a judgement that became a landmark of academic freedom. The episode is the documented centre of a wider pattern that included dismissals at several universities in the late nineteen forties, the disciplining of economists whose wartime service had made them visible, and a quieter, larger effect that no archive can count: the graduate students who chose safer topics, the departments that chose safer candidates, the research programmes never begun. Cold War funding streams and the great foundations' patronage of particular styles of economics completed a selection environment in which the tradition's survival at all, in its redoubts and journals, is the fact requiring explanation. Nothing in this record requires the further claim, which the tradition's polemics sometimes add and this guide does not, that the exclusion was a conspiracy; institutions select for what reproduces them without needing instructions.

The intellectual record must now be given at the same strength, because the profession's indifference was not purchased entirely with politics. The transformation dispute, described above, remained unresolved for a century by the ordinary standards of proof the discipline applies, and a field whose foundation has an open joint at its centre offers its rivals a permanent and legitimate reason for scepticism. The empirical record on the rate of profit, the tradition's most measurable claim, is genuinely contested in the way described earlier, hinging on measurement conventions about which reasonable practitioners disagree, so that the claim's confirmation status is weaker than its proponents' rhetoric and stronger than its detractors'. The record of dated prediction, the movement literature's repeated announcements of final crises, embarrassed the programme before exactly the audience whose respect it needed. And the tradition's entanglement with the states that ruled in its name imposed a cost no Western practitioner could escape: the catechism economics of the socialist countries was attributed to the programme as a whole, and the spectacle of a doctrine enforced by censorship discredited the open, quarrelling research programme that had itself been among censorship's victims. Add the internal causes the field's honest historians record, periods in which party discipline substituted for argument inside its own institutions, and orthodoxies that treated the protective belt as a second hard core, and the intellectual explanation stands on its own legs. The conclusion this guide draws is the brief's conclusion: the marginality is overdetermined, in the sense the previous cluster gave that word, produced by the profession's selection and by the programme's difficulties together, and any history that credits only one cause is a brief for a side, not an account of the field.

## The strongest objection, stated at full strength

A guide earns its authority by stating the case against its subject better than the subject's enemies bother to state it, and the case against this programme is formidable. By the ordinary standards applied to any research programme in any science, the indictment runs, Marxist economics has failed. Its practitioners have had a century and a third since the third volume of *Capital* and cannot agree among themselves whether its central magnitudes, values and the surplus they ground, exist independently of the prices that were supposed to be derived from them; the table in this very guide is the confession, eight schools with eight incompatible foundations, a state of affairs that in physics or in medicine would be recognised as a field that does not know what it is talking about. Its presence in the discipline's leading journals is effectively nil, its doctoral programmes a rounding error, its predictions, where they were made precisely enough to test, falsified by the survival and transformation of the system it expected to bury. Its textual method, in which arguments are settled by appeal to the correct reading of a nineteenth-century manuscript corpus, resembles theology's more than science's. And its standard defences, that the questions it asks are ones the mainstream cannot pose, ring hollow if the asking never converges on answers: a programme is not vindicated by the importance of its questions when generation after generation leaves them exactly as open as it found them.

The answer the tradition gives, given here in its strongest form, has three parts. First, the questions are real and they are not posed elsewhere: no other programme in economics asks where profit as a whole comes from, as distinct from what determines this firm's margin; treats the reproduction of the class structure itself as an economic variable; or models crisis as a normal product of the system's success conditions rather than as a visitation. A discipline that cannot pose the origin of its own central magnitude has a gap, and the gap does not close because the programme that points at it is untidy. Second, the comparison of convergence must be made fairly: the mainstream's own foundations have been revised repeatedly at a depth its textbooks conceal, the theory of capital itself was shaken from within Cambridge in the controversies of the nineteen sixties over reswitching and capital reversing, in which the Sraffian current's criticisms were conceded by leading mainstream theorists and then, in the famous judgement of several participants, simply left out of the textbooks; disagreement about foundations, it turns out, is not a Marxist monopoly, only disagreement conducted in the open. Third, the concession, which the brief for this article requires and which honesty would require anyway: the internal disagreement about foundations in this programme is unusual in degree, it is not the healthy pluralism of a field converging from several directions, and it is a genuine weakness with genuine costs, in duplicated effort, in mutual unintelligibility, in the inability to present a cumulative front to empirical test. The programme not doctrine rule is this guide's discipline for describing the field; it is not a spell for converting the field's deepest problem into a virtue, and the reader should hold both truths: the questions are indispensable, and the failure to settle the foundations after a century is a failure.

## Where to begin: a reading path

The newcomer who has followed this guide to its final sections deserves a path rather than a bibliography. Begin with the founder, briefly and well accompanied: the first volume of *Capital*, read with the series' companion guide at hand, remains the only entry that gives the categories in their native order, and its chapters on the working day and on machinery and modern industry can be read first by the impatient without serious loss. Add next a history of the tradition itself, so that the schools acquire faces and dates; the standard surveys by historians of Marxian economics, written from outside the schools' quarrels, are the reliable maps, and this guide's centre of gravity section supplies the itinerary they document. Then choose one school and read its canonical text whole: Sweezy's *Theory of Capitalist Development* for the classical statement, *Monopoly Capital* for the American current, Sraffa's short and forbidding book with a commentary for the classical reconstruction, Cohen's essays for the analytical temper. Only after one school is possessed should the transformation dispute be approached, since the dispute is the schools' argument with one another and is unintelligible as a free-standing puzzle. The journals named in the institutional map then provide the field's continuing present tense. At every stage the programme not doctrine rule is the reader's equipment: for any claim met in the literature, ask which school asserts it, which denies it, and what evidence or text each side treats as decisive, and the literature will stay legible even where it stays unsettled.

## The field in one view

The pillar article can now be summarised in the rule it has applied throughout. Marxist economics is a research programme organised around the extraction and accumulation of surplus value under capitalism, containing at least eight schools that share an ancestor, a vocabulary, and a problem set while disagreeing about foundations, and occupying a marginal but durable place in the economics profession for reasons that are jointly sociological and intellectual, neither of which an honest account may suppress. Its questions, the origin of profit, the reproduction of the system, the sources of crisis, the division of the product, are questions the mainstream framework does not generate, and its unfinished state, textual, technical, empirical, is the price and the proof of its continuation. The articles that follow in this cluster take up its parts in turn: the transformation problem, the Sraffian challenge, the reconstructions of value, the reproduction schemes, monopoly capital, dependency, the comparison with the mainstream, the empirical tests, and the examination room. The reader who carries the eight schools table and the programme not doctrine rule into those articles carries the map this one was built to provide.

Teach the disputes, not the doctrine, and the material organises itself. Your entry point is the measurement dossier. The field's empirical claims, on the trend of profitability, on the correlation between values and prices, on the decomposition of the wage and profit shares, are gathered and graded in the cluster's [testing marxist economics](/2016/08/01/testing-marxist-economics/), which separates what the data settle from what the conventions decide. For the primary literature, the journals mapped above are the archive of record, and the collected works and the manuscript editions are the court of appeal for the exegetical disputes, which are genuine disputes about evidence and should be reported as such. Two cautions from the field's own history will serve any researcher well. The programme's internal disagreements are real and load-bearing, so a report that quotes one school as the tradition repeats the error this guide exists to correct; and the field's predictive record must be rendered whole, structural foresight and failed dates together, since either half alone is a caricature. The associations' conferences and the heterodox federations are where the field's unpublished work surfaces first, and their programmes are a reliable guide to which disputes are live. For historians, the transition debate and the Soviet economy literature offer the tradition's methods applied to complete bodies of evidence; for journalists, the schools table is the difference between a story about a doctrine and a story about a living argument.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Marxist economics?

It is a research tradition, not a catechism: a connected set of inquiries into how capitalism generates profit, renews itself through time, divides its product among classes, and periodically breaks down. Its practitioners start from Marx's analysis of the mode of production and share his guiding questions, but they organise themselves into rival schools that dispute the foundations, several holding positions the others regard as surrender. What unifies the field is the shared problem set and the conviction that capitalism is a historical arrangement with a beginning and internal laws of motion, rather than the permanent shape of economic life. Everything else, including the status of the labour theory of value, is argued about in the open.

### Q: How does Marxist economics differ from mainstream economics?

The contrast starts with the questions each side believes are worth asking. Textbook economics takes property, markets, and endowments as its starting conditions and investigates how rational agents deploy limited means among rival ends. The Marxist tradition investigates the starting conditions themselves: how a small class came to hold the productive assets, how the majority came to depend on selling their capacity to work, and what follows from that asymmetry for growth, for distribution, and for stability. Its actors are classes and institutions as much as individuals, its time is historical rather than logical, and breakdown is for it a normal outcome of the system's own functioning, not an accident imported from outside.

### Q: What are the main schools of Marxist economics?

Eight are mapped in this guide's table. The orthodox current defends Marx's value magnitudes as written. The monetary and temporal reconstructions rebuild the value theory around money and sequence. The Sraffians keep the surplus approach and drop labour values as unnecessary. The Monthly Review school organises the field around monopoly and the absorption of a rising surplus. Analytical Marxism restates the claims with the mainstream's formal tools and keeps only what survives. The French regulation school and the American social structure of accumulation approach both explain stability through institutional settlements. Value-form theory returns to the social form of wealth. The autonomist current, often counted a ninth, reverses the causal arrow between capital and labour.

### Q: Is Marxist economics taught in universities?

Yes, though in a minority of places and rarely under its own name on the door. A handful of economics departments, in Massachusetts, New York, London, and a scattering elsewhere, have carried the tradition at doctoral level for decades, and its journals imply a corresponding archipelago of individual scholars. Much more widely, students meet the material inside history of economic thought courses, political economy options, development studies, sociology, and geography, where the concepts circulate even when the programme does not. The pattern is thin but durable: contraction after the Cold War ended, no disappearance, and periodic influxes of students whenever a crisis makes the standard syllabus look incomplete.

### Q: Which journals publish Marxist economics?

The field's own periodicals carry most of it. Science and Society has published Marxist scholarship in English since the nineteen thirties. Monthly Review, an independent magazine rather than a refereed journal, anchors the American radical tradition. The Review of Radical Political Economics serves the pluralist academic wing in the United States, and Capital and Class does the same work in Britain. Rethinking Marxism hosts the poststructuralist currents, and Historical Materialism, founded in the late nineteen nineties, functions as the European journal of record across the tradition's disciplines. Heterodox neighbours such as the Cambridge Journal of Economics publish a share of it, while the mainstream's leading journals publish effectively none, which is itself one of the facts the field's sociology has to explain.

### Q: What are the core questions Marxist economics asks?

Four questions organise nearly everything the tradition writes. Where does profit come from, not in a single firm but for the capitalist class as a whole? How does a system of competing private producers manage to reproduce itself, replacing its materials, its workforce, and its class structure period after period? Why does a system capable of producing unprecedented wealth periodically destroy value, idle its plant, and expel its workers in crises that arise from its own operation? And how is the product divided between the classes, by what mechanisms and with what consequences for growth? Around these four orbit the dependent inquiries, into money, the state, the world market, and technical change, and a school can usually be identified by which of the four it treats as fundamental.

### Q: Who are the leading Marxist economists?

The honest answer names traditions rather than a ranking, since leadership in this field is school leadership. Among the dead, the indispensable names are Maurice Dobb in Britain, Paul Sweezy and Paul Baran in America, Ernest Mandel for the orthodox synthesis, Piero Sraffa as the classical reconstruction's unwilling founder, and Michał Kalecki standing at the border with Keynesianism. The postwar generations added the founders named in this guide's table: Duncan Foley and Andrew Kliman among the value reconstructions' architects, G. A. Cohen and John Roemer for the analytical current, Michel Aglietta and Robert Boyer for regulation theory, Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis for the accumulation school, and Kōzō Uno as the Japanese tradition's legislator. Any such list is a map of the schools, not a league table.

### Q: Has Marxist economics changed since Marx?

Profoundly, and along every dimension except the problem set. Its home moved from the workers' movement to the party school to the university. Its textual basis grew as the founder's manuscripts, the Grundrisse above all, reached print generations late. Its methods absorbed the mainstream's mathematics in several schools while others doubled down on textual and historical work. Its geography widened from Germany and Russia to the Americas, Japan, India, and the global South. And its centre of unresolvable argument migrated, from the falling rate of profit to the transformation of values into prices to the status of the value form itself. What has not changed is the refusal to treat capitalism as a natural order, and the insistence that profit, crisis, and distribution are one problem stated three ways.

### Q: Does Marxist economics have a theory of prices?

It has several, which is the trouble and the interest. The classical construction derives prices of production from values: competition equalises profit rates across industries, so prices settle around centres that redistribute the economy's total surplus value among capitals in proportion to their size. The Sraffian current computes prices directly from the physical conditions of production and the wage or profit rate, arguing the value detour is unnecessary. The monetary and temporal reconstructions answer that values, expressed in money and followed through time, are consistent with the price system after all. Empirically, studies using input and output tables report strong correlations between labour contents and market prices, a finding each camp reads as vindication and its opponents read as an artefact of cost accounting.

### Q: Where should you start with Marxist economics?

With the founder's first volume and a good companion, not with the summaries of the summaries. Read Capital for the categories in their own order: the commodity, money, the working day, machinery, accumulation. Pair it with a history of the tradition, so the schools you will meet later have faces and dates. Then take one school whole, Sweezy's classical textbook for the traditional statement, Monopoly Capital for the American current, a commented Sraffa for the classical reconstruction, and only then approach the transformation dispute, which is the schools arguing with one another and makes little sense as a free-standing puzzle. This guide's reading path section lays out the sequence in full, with the journals to graduate to.

### Q: How does the programme not doctrine rule grade a school inside the tradition?

By its work on the shared questions, not by its loyalty to shared conclusions. A school earns its standing inside the tradition when it takes up the common problem set, the origin of profit, reproduction, crisis, distribution, and advances it with methods whose results others can check, even when the advance consists of demolishing a position the founder held. Fidelity is graded as a scholarly virtue in handling the texts, never as a substitute for argument. By this standard the reconstructions that surrender the letter of the value theory while repairing its consistency are insiders, and the catechism that repeats the letter while forbidding the questions is outside the programme altogether, whatever name it rules under.

### Q: What should a student separate between the eight schools' shared ground and their disputes?

Separate the grammar from the verdicts. The grammar is shared: capitalism as a historical system, profit as surplus extracted in production, reproduction as the master process, distribution as class conflict, crisis as endogenous. A student who cannot state the grammar will mistake every school for a separate subject. The verdicts are disputed: whether value magnitudes underlie prices, whether profitability tends to fall, whether the labour theory is the keystone or scaffolding, whether stability is explained by institutions or by the motion of profitability itself. Examination answers fail most often by reciting one school's verdicts as the tradition's doctrine, which is why this guide insists on naming the school whenever a claim is reported.

### Q: Why did the tradition's centre of gravity move after Marx?

Because the institutions that carried it kept being destroyed or transformed. The workers' parties that first housed it bureaucratised, and one of them acquired a state and converted the doctrine into a catechism, which discredited it abroad and froze it at home. The European catastrophe scattered its practitioners across the Atlantic. The postwar expansion of the universities then offered the only large employer whose work is argument, and the tradition that moved in became academic in method and audience, quarrelling in journals rather than in congresses. The collapse of the socialist states removed its official shadow and its taboo together, and the financial crisis of 2008 sent a new student generation toward it. Each move left deposits in the theory itself, which is why the schools differ by the institutions that raised them.

### Q: How does the guide state its ratios without displaying equations?

By writing each ratio as a sentence that names the magnitudes it relates, in the order in which a reader would compute them, and by confining symbols to the schools table, where the notation belongs to the artifact rather than to the argument. The rate of profit, for instance, is described as a relation between the surplus a workforce creates and the total capital advanced to employ it, rather than exhibited as a fraction. The convention follows the cluster's notation policy: prose first, on the ground that a reader who can follow a ratio stated in words understands it, while a reader confronted with simultaneous equations has been given a test in algebra instead of an explanation in economics.

### Q: What separates a research programme from a set of conclusions?

A programme survives the surrender of conclusions; a set of conclusions does not. A programme consists of the questions it will not stop asking, the core commitments that define its object, and the methods by which claims are produced and tested, and it can therefore lose an argument, about the falling profit rate, about the transformation, about the class structure of a particular country, without ceasing to exist. A body of mere conclusions has no such resources: falsify its propositions and nothing remains but the record of its confidence. The distinction is why this guide treats the schools' mutual refutations as evidence of the programme's life rather than of its failure, and why the catechism form, which protects conclusions by forbidding questions, is its true opposite.
