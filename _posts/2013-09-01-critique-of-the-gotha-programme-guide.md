Ask a room of well read people about the Gotha Programme and what Marx proposed for the society that would follow capitalism, and the answers arrive with a confidence the texts do not support. He refused to write recipes for the cook shops of the future, someone will say, and the room nods, because the refusal is genuine and the quotation is real. He wanted equality, another voice adds, and the nodding continues, because equality sounds like the obvious name for whatever he wanted. He distinguished socialism from communism, a third voice concludes, socialism first and communism after, and by then the conversation has assembled a Marx who exists in no single document: a thinker who said almost nothing about the future, whose little he did say can be summarised as fairness, and whose stages carry names he never gave them. Against that assembled figure stands one short, irritable, magnificent set of marginal notes, the Critique of the Gotha Programme, in which Marx described deductions from the social product, a distributive principle for a lower phase, a second principle for a higher one, and an argument that equal right itself is a defective standard. The notes were written in 1875, suppressed by the party they criticised, and printed only in 1891, eight years after their author died. Anyone asking what Marx actually proposed should be sent to them first. Almost nobody is.

Three claims organise everything in this guide, and each can be checked against the text itself, which is short enough to read in an evening. The first claim concerns concreteness. Marx's most concrete statement about a post-capitalist economy is a list of sums that must be subtracted from the total product before anyone receives anything, and that list, the deductions passage, occupies the centre of this article because it occupies the centre of the argument. The second claim concerns equality. Marx's objection to the programme's talk of fair distribution was not that fairness is unimportant but that distribution is the wrong place to look: the distribution of consumption goods follows from the distribution of the conditions of production, and a standard of equal right, applied to people who are unequal in strength, in talents and in family burdens, produces unequal outcomes while calling itself equal. The third claim concerns vocabulary. Marx described a lower and a higher phase of communist society. He did not name the lower phase socialism. That naming is Lenin's work, performed in a book written in 1917, and the near universal habit of treating socialism and communism as two successive stages descends from Lenin's terminology rather than from the notes of 1875.

A reader who holds those three claims can already see why the text matters beyond its size. The deductions passage answers the question of what a post-capitalist economy does on its first morning, before philosophy begins: it replaces worn machinery, it sets aside a fund for expansion, it insures itself against accident, it pays for administration, it funds schools and health provision, and it supports those who cannot work. Only the residue is divided among individuals, and the passage therefore converts a slogan about the proceeds of labour into an arithmetic problem with a finite answer. The equal right argument answers a different question, the question of what justice can mean inside a society still marked by the order it replaced. The terminology correction answers a third question, about who wrote the tradition's vocabulary, and it disciplines every discussion in which socialism and communism are treated as Marx's own stage names. Each claim is developed at length in the sections ahead, with the disputes about them stated alongside, because the disputes are part of what a serious reader needs.

Some guidance on how to use what follows will save confusion later. Quotations from the Critique are kept short and are drawn from the standard English renderings of the text as printed in the collected works of Marx and Engels; readers comparing translations will find small verbal differences that do not touch the arguments. Dates given for composition, congresses and publication are the dates the scholarly record supports, and where scholars disagree about a detail, the disagreement is flagged rather than smoothed over. The text itself is referred to throughout as the Critique, which is the short title scholarship uses; its German title, Kritik des Gothaer Programms, appears where the publication history requires it. Nothing in the guide treats the notes as a programme to be endorsed or a prophecy to be graded. They are presented as Marx's fullest sketch of a post-capitalist order, and assessed, in the closing sections, for coherence: whether the parts fit together, where they pull against one another, and what their author left unspecified.

## The Gotha Programme and the occasion that provoked the notes

Programmes are usually the dullest documents a political movement produces, and the draft programme of 1875 was no exception until Marx annotated it. Two German socialist organisations had agreed to merge. One, the Social Democratic Workers' Party founded at Eisenach in 1869, stood broadly in the orbit of Marx and Engels, counting August Bebel and Wilhelm Liebknecht among its leaders. The other, the General German Workers' Association founded in 1863, carried the legacy of Ferdinand Lassalle, its founder, whose ideas about state aided cooperatives and an iron law of wages had shaped its outlook long after his death in a duel in 1864. Unity required a single programme, and a draft was prepared for the congress that would meet at Gotha in May 1875. Marx and Engels, exiled in London, read the draft with dismay. In their judgement it bought unity at the price of doctrine, conceding point after point to Lassallean formulations they considered false, and saying nothing about the trade unions, the international movement, or the analysis of capital that they regarded as the movement's real achievements.

Marx's response was characteristic in form and unusual in fate. He wrote marginal notes on the draft, headed them as glosses on the programme of the German workers' party, attached a covering letter addressed to Wilhelm Bracke, a party leader he trusted, and sent the package to Germany in early May 1875 with permission for it to be shown to a small circle of named leaders. The notes were therefore neither a book nor an article nor a speech. They were an internal memorandum, written for men who were about to vote the other way, in a tone that mixed exact logical analysis with open contempt for phrases Marx believed had been adopted without thought. The congress at Gotha adopted the programme much as drafted. The merged party, which took the name Socialist Workers' Party of Germany, proceeded to grow under it, and the notes disappeared into a drawer for sixteen years. Their eventual publication, against the wishes of the party leadership, is a story this guide tells in its own section, because the suppression and the release together explain why a text of such consequence entered public discussion so late and in such a charged atmosphere.

Understanding the occasion disciplines the reading in two ways. First, the notes are polemical in origin, aimed at sentences. Nearly every famous passage in the Critique begins as a comment on a clause of the draft programme, which is why the text proceeds clause by clause rather than as a treatise with chapters. A reader who forgets the format will search in vain for transitions and summaries; the connective tissue is the programme itself, which Marx assumed his first readers had in front of them. Second, the polemical origin explains the notes' strange combination of irritation and generosity. Marx is furious about formulas, about the iron law, about the free state, about the claim that all classes besides the workers form one reactionary mass. Yet inside the irritation, where a clause about distribution forces the question of what distribution could mean after capitalism, he suddenly writes with the patience of a treatise, setting out deductions, phases and the critique of equal right in prose that has outlasted every other part of the document. The occasional text contains a systematic core, and extracting that core without flattening the polemic is the task of any honest guide.

### Why did Marx write marginal notes instead of a public attack?

Because the audience he needed was internal and the damage he feared was doctrinal rather than electoral. A public attack from London would have split the unification and handed the state a divided movement. Private glosses, circulated among named leaders, could register his objections on record, instruct the Eisenach men, and leave the merger intact.

Rhetoric mattered less to Marx here than record. The covering letter to Bracke asked that the notes be shown to a specified circle, which converted a private grumble into a deposited objection: whatever the congress voted, the London view existed in writing, dated and attributable. That distinction explains the tone. A pamphlet persuades strangers and must therefore mind its manners. A memorandum among comrades can afford sarcasm, compression and the occasional savage parenthesis, because its readers already share the premises and need only the corrections. Readers who approach the Critique expecting a manifesto will find its abruptness puzzling; readers who approach it as a memorandum find the abruptness natural, even functional, since a gloss earns its place by being shorter than the confusion it clears.

Form also shaped content in a subtler way. Because each note answers a clause, the notes inherit the programme's agenda rather than Marx's own. The programme talked about distribution, so the notes talk about distribution at a length Marx would never have chosen for a work of his own design, and the most celebrated political economy in the text, the analysis of equal right, grows out of a phrase about fair shares that he considered empty. Conversely, matters the programme ignored receive silence: the notes contain no theory of organisation, no discussion of alliances beyond a scornful paragraph, and no account of how the transition they gesture toward might be effected in a country like Germany. Editors and popularisers who present the Critique as Marx's political testament invert its actual proportions. It is a commentary, and its silences are the programme's silences before they are anyone else's.

## A short text with a long suppression

Publication histories rarely change what a text says, but they regularly change what readers think it says, and the Critique is the standing example. Marx sent the manuscript to Bracke in May 1875. The leaders who read it, Liebknecht among them, chose unity over correction and let the matter rest. Marx, who had threatened in earlier correspondence to dissociate himself publicly from a programme he considered false, in the end held his peace, calculating that a public rupture would damage the movement more than a bad programme would. Engels likewise refrained. The manuscript stayed among party papers, known to a handful of insiders, while the party itself was driven underground by Bismarck's Anti-Socialist Laws from 1878 to 1890, surviving as an electoral and cultural movement with its press abroad and its organisation at home half legal at best.

When the laws lapsed in 1890, the party prepared a new programme for its congress at Erfurt, and the old quarrel reopened in altered circumstances. Engels, now the surviving authority of the partnership, judged that the Gotha draft's errors should not be repeated in the new document, and that the most effective instruction available was Marx's own sixteen year old memorandum. In January 1891 he published the notes in Die Neue Zeit, the party's theoretical journal edited by Karl Kautsky, under the title by which the text is known. The party leadership objected strongly. Reichstag deputies protested in the press, the executive sought to disown the publication, and Engels answered with cool defiance, pointing out that the text was Marx's, that its criticisms had been vindicated by the party's own development, and that concealing the movement's founding documents was a habit unworthy of it. The Erfurt Programme adopted later that year dropped the Lassallean formulas the notes had attacked, though it also avoided several of Marx's positive formulations, a compromise outcome that satisfied almost nobody completely.

Suppression left marks on reception that persist in the way the text is cited. Because the notes became public in 1891 rather than 1875, they entered debate as a posthumous revelation rather than as a contemporary intervention, and both friends and enemies treated them with the exaggerated reverence or suspicion that posthumous revelations attract. Passages that in 1875 would have read as one comrade's sharp advice were read in 1891 as the master's final doctrine on the future society, a status their author never claimed for them and their format does not support. Engels himself, in correspondence around the publication, treated the notes as settling certain questions of programme drafting, not as a charter for institution building. Later movements, hungry for canonical warrant, upgraded them further. A careful reader restores the original proportions: a private memorandum of extraordinary analytical density, published late, whose authority derives from the quality of its arguments rather than from any intention that it serve as a constitution for anything.

### Why did Engels publish the Critique when he did?

Because a new programme was being drafted at Erfurt and Engels wanted the old errors corrected by the strongest authority available, Marx himself. Publishing the suppressed notes in the party journal forced the drafting debate onto the text's ground: the Lassallean phrases could not survive quotation beside the glosses, and the leadership's fury confirmed the blow had landed.

Timing also had a defensive aspect that accounts of the episode sometimes miss. Manuscripts of suppressed works have a way of surfacing in hostile hands, edited tendentiously and framed for scandal. By printing the notes whole, with the covering letter, in the movement's own theoretical journal, Engels controlled the circumstances of their appearance and deprived opponents of the revelation as a weapon. The party's embarrassment was real but brief, and it was the embarrassment of being corrected by its own founder, which is the most survivable kind. Engels's judgement, formed over decades of managing the partnership's literary remains, was that texts outlast sensitivities. The episode is a compact lesson in his editorial philosophy, applied everywhere in his work on the later volumes of Capital: print the document, annotate honestly, and let parties argue with evidence rather than with rumour.

Length deserves a word here, because reputation inflates it. The Critique in standard editions runs to roughly twenty or thirty pages, depending on format and apparatus: a long pamphlet's worth of prose, dense but entirely readable in an evening. Its brevity is not incidental to its influence. Generations of students have been assigned it whole, an assignment no one survives with Capital, and its arguments have therefore entered the tradition at a depth its rivals in Marx's bibliography rarely match. Brevity also concentrates the mythology. A short canonical text is quoted by phrase rather than by argument, and phrases detach easily: fair distribution, bourgeois right, the ability and needs formula, the dictatorship phrase, each circulating as a token in debates whose participants have sometimes not read the surrounding paragraphs. This guide restores the paragraphs.

## The first sentence: labour, nature and the source of wealth

The draft programme opened with a sentence that sounded like a tribute to the working class: labour is the source of all wealth and all culture. Marx's first gloss denies the sentence outright. Labour, he wrote, is not the source of all wealth, because nature is just as much the source of use values, and labour itself is only the manifestation of a force of nature, human labour power. The correction can look pedantic, a philosopher quarrelling with a compliment. It is in fact the hinge on which the whole critique of distribution turns, and grasping why Marx began there unlocks the deductions passage that follows a few pages later.

The reasoning runs in stages, each simple, the conclusion severe. Wealth in the sense that matters for the sentence, material wealth, consists of use values: food, shelter, cloth, tools, the physical content of a society's consumption. Every use value is a combination of two elements, material provided by nature and labour that gives the material form. A field yields nothing without seed, soil, rain and sun, and yields nothing harvestable without work; neither factor can be struck out of the account. A programme that credits labour alone therefore misdescribes production at its first step. Worse, Marx argues, the misdescription has a political history: the formula flatters the owners of the conditions of labour, because if labour alone creates wealth, then the labourer's dependence on whoever controls land and materials becomes invisible, and the existing distribution of those conditions can pass as natural. The bourgeois, he remarks with bitter economy, have excellent grounds for crediting labour with supernatural creative power, since it follows from the dependent condition of labour that a man who owns nothing but labour power must be the slave of the men who own the material conditions of his work.

From the correction about nature flow several consequences that the rest of the notes exploit. One is analytical: because production is always production with means that wear out and materials that are consumed, any society, whatever its property relations, must replace those means before it can celebrate any surplus; the arithmetic of deduction is already implied by the physics of production. Another consequence is strategic. The programme's sentence had been offered as agitation, a dignity claim for workers. Marx replies that a workers' party should not need a false sentence for its dignity, and that slogans borrowed unexamined from the surrounding culture carry their origins with them. A third consequence concerns the status of labour itself. In the higher phase described later in the notes, labour has become life's prime want, a formulation that startles readers who know Marx as labour's tribune. The first gloss prepares that surprise: labour in the notes is never worshipped. It is one natural force among others, glorious in its products, dangerous as a fetish, and in a society of associated producers simply the activity through which needs are met and capacities expressed.

Scholarly readers have long noticed that the nature point connects the notes of 1875 with Marx's larger project. The analysis of the labour process in the first volume of Capital treats labour as a metabolism between human beings and nature, a framing in which neither pole creates the world alone. Ecological readings of Marx, developed by later scholars working from the excerpt notebooks, have seized on the Gotha correction as an early canonical statement that production presupposes a natural world with its own conditions and limits. Other readers stress the polemical target rather than the doctrine: the sentence under attack was Lassallean currency, and Marx's objection may be read as housekeeping within a factional dispute. Both readings can be true together. Polemic chooses its occasions; doctrine decides what the polemic says. What cannot survive either reading is the programme's sentence itself, and Marx's refusal to let a workers' party found its programme on a claim about wealth that a schoolchild's harvest could refute.

### What did the programme mean by the undiminished proceeds of labour?

It meant that workers, or society on their behalf, should receive the whole value labour creates, without the shares taken by profit, rent and interest. The phrase was Lassallean currency, shorthand for ending exploitation. Marx's answer was that an undiminished product is impossible anywhere, since common needs and worn machinery must be paid for before any share is calculated.

The phrase deserves its moment of sympathy before its demolition, because its appeal is the appeal of the whole critique of exploitation compressed into one demand. Under capitalism the worker plainly does not receive the full value added by labour; large shares flow to owners as profit, to landlords as rent, to lenders as interest. Undiminished proceeds names the grievance exactly and promises its abolition exactly. Marx's objection is not to the grievance but to the arithmetic. Abolish the owners and their shares, and the obligations those shares imperfectly funded do not vanish: machines still wear, raw material is still consumed, administrators still eat, children still need schools, the injured still need support. A slogan that promises the whole product to labour promises what no organisation of production can deliver, and a party that writes it into a programme has committed itself to disappointing its own supporters or to quietly redefining the promise later. Marx preferred programmes that could survive their own success.

## The iron law of wages and the Lassallean inheritance

Among the formulas Marx most despised in the draft, the iron law of wages held a special place, because it combined a false economics with a quiet politics. Lassalle had popularised the doctrine, adapting it from the population theory associated with Malthus and the wage fund reasoning of classical political economy: wages, the doctrine held, oscillate around the level of bare subsistence, since any rise above it encourages workers to multiply until competition presses wages down again, and any fall below it reduces their numbers until wages recover. The law was called iron because it admitted no exceptions and no appeal. Marx had attacked the underlying population reasoning for years, in Capital and elsewhere, arguing that every mode of production has its own historically specific laws of population, and that the reserve army of labour under capitalism is produced by accumulation itself rather than by the bedroom habits of the poor.

His objection in the notes is partly scientific and partly organisational. Scientifically, an iron law of wages leaves the wages system itself eternal: if wages are governed by a law of nature, then trade union struggle over wages is a fight against nature, and the abolition of the wage system becomes an incoherent goal, since the law would reassert itself under any arrangements that retained wages at all. The draft programme compounded the confusion by pairing the iron law with a demand for the abolition of the wages system, and Marx pounces on the contradiction: a party cannot consistently engrave a natural law of wages on its banner and promise in the next clause to abolish the system whose wage the law supposedly governs. If the law is iron, the promise is theatre; if the promise is serious, the law is decoration.

Organisationally, the point mattered because doctrines teach members what to attempt. A movement catechised in the iron law learns fatalism about the very struggles, strikes for higher wages, campaigns for shorter hours, through which it actually grows. Marx's alternative emphasis, visible across his writings on trade unions, was that the value of labour power contains a historical and moral element, varying with a society's standards and with the balance of forces, so that wage struggles are real contests with real stakes, even though their victories leave the wages system standing and therefore cannot be the movement's final aim. The Gotha notes compress that whole position into a few contemptuous paragraphs about a borrowed law. Readers who want the fuller economics are pointed by the notes themselves toward the treatment of wages and accumulation in Capital, where the reserve army mechanism does the explanatory work the iron law pretended to do.

State aided producer cooperatives, the other great Lassallean demand carried into the draft, receive a colder and shorter treatment. Marx does not deny that cooperative production can demonstrate, in miniature, that factories can run without a class of masters standing over them; he had praised the cooperative experiments of English workers in those terms in an address for the International. His objection is to the state aid formula and to its provenance: Lassalle had courted Bismarck, suggesting that universal suffrage and state funded cooperatives might be granted from above, and a programme that asks the existing state, which Marx calls by harsh names in this connection, to midwife the new society has misunderstood both the state and the society. Existing workers' cooperatives are valuable, the notes concede, only insofar as they are independent creations of workers and not protégés of government or bourgeoisie. The sentence is brief, but it carries the anti Lassallean charge of the whole document: emancipation arranged by authority is a contradiction, whether the authority wears a crown or a sash of office.

## Fair distribution and the refusal of the phrase

Few parts of the notes have been quoted against their author more energetically than the paragraphs refusing the programme's demand for fair distribution, and few repay careful reading more. The draft spoke of a fair distribution of the proceeds of labour. Marx asks, with an interrogator's patience, what fair distribution is supposed to mean. Do the bourgeois not assert that the prevailing distribution is fair, he writes, and is it not in fact the only fair distribution on the basis of the prevailing mode of production? The trap is deliberate. Fairness, in his analysis, is a standard internal to a set of production relations: each order generates the distributive outcomes that count as fair within it, and measures grievances by its own lights. To demand fair distribution without specifying a change in the relations of production is therefore to demand either nothing or everything while naming neither. The vulgar socialism that treats distribution as the essence of the matter, and production as a technical preliminary, has the relationship backwards. Distribution of the means of consumption is a consequence of the distribution of the conditions of production themselves; change who owns and controls those conditions, and the distributive pattern follows as an effect follows a cause.

The argument is easily caricatured as indifference to justice, and the caricature has a literature of its own. Philosophers have debated for decades whether Marx condemned capitalism as unjust or rejected the vocabulary of justice as ideology, and the Gotha paragraphs are prime exhibits on several sides of that debate. The narrower reading recommended here separates levels. Marx refuses fairness as a programme slogan, because slogans must survive contact with arithmetic and with opponents who also claim fairness. He does not refuse evaluative judgement about capitalism; the same notes describe the worker's compelled dependence in language no one would call neutral. The refusal is tactical and analytical at once: tactical, because fair distribution rallies people to a criterion the other side owns; analytical, because a theory of distribution detached from production explains nothing about why distributions take the shapes they do. A separate guide in this series takes up the justice controversy in full, asking whether the charge of exploitation in Marx is [a moral or a technical one](/2011/02/01/is-exploitation-moral-or-technical/), and readers who want the equal right argument's philosophical afterlife will find its battlefield mapped there.

What the notes put in place of the slogan is more interesting than the slogan. Once distribution is demoted from essence to consequence, the questions that matter become concrete. What does the total product consist of, in a society where producers own the conditions jointly? What claims on that product precede any individual's share? By what principle is the remainder divided in a society fresh from capitalism, and by what different principle might it be divided when scarcity and the division of labour have receded? Those are the questions the notes proceed to answer, and the answers constitute the positive core for which the text is famous. The doorway to that core is an accountancy exercise that popular summaries almost always skip, and to which this guide now gives the central place Marx gave it.

## The deductions passage: the arithmetic before distribution

Suppose the programme's wish granted. The means of production belong to the producers in common; no landlord, shareholder or moneylender stands between labour and its product; the whole social product is at the disposal of the associated producers. The Lassallean phrase promises that labour now receives its undiminished proceeds. Marx's response is to take the phrase literally and destroy it with bookkeeping. If the labourer is to receive the undiminished proceeds, he writes, the word undiminished must first quietly shrink, because from the total product a series of deductions has to be made before any individual distribution can begin. He then lists them, in an order that itself teaches: the economy's claims first, then society's, then the individual's. This is the deductions passage, and its claim on the reader's attention is simple to state. Marx's most concrete statement about a post-capitalist economy is not a vision of abundance or a constitutional design but a list of subtractions, which means that the tradition's celebrated refusal to write recipes for the future coexists, in its most famous programmatic text, with a specific and quotable account of how such an economy would allocate its output on the first day it operated.

Read the list slowly, in Marx's order, and consider what each item implies. The first deduction replaces the means of production that production has used up: raw materials consumed, tools worn down, machines at the end of their service. No society can distribute what it needs to stand still. The second deduction sets aside a further portion for the extension of production, the accumulation fund from which new capacity is built; a society that consumes its entire product has chosen stagnation as a policy, and Marx, who regarded the development of the productive forces as the material premise of everything else he hoped for, treats growth provision as a deduction prior to anyone's dinner. The third is a reserve or insurance fund against accidents and dislocations caused by natural forces: harvests fail, rivers flood, fires burn, and a community that has distributed everything has nothing with which to answer misfortune except emergency levies on the already distributed. These three deductions are economic in the strictest sense. They arise, as Marx notes, from an economic necessity, and their magnitude is to be determined by available means and forces, partly by a calculus of probabilities, not by any standard of fairness. Probability, not justice, sizes the insurance fund. That sentence alone separates the passage from every utopianism of the period: the new society's first problem is actuarial.

Only after the economy has been secured do the deductions serving general social purposes appear. Administration comes next, the costs of coordinating a complex production system and a complex society, which Marx expects to diminish as the new order develops but does not pretend can be wished away at the start. Then comes the fund for the common satisfaction of needs: schools, health services, the provisions a community makes for goods consumed together rather than divided. Marx expects this portion to grow, and to grow considerably, as the new society develops, a quiet indication that he imagined collective consumption expanding at the expense of individual shares as abundance increased. Last in the list, and morally first in most readers' eyes, stands the fund for those unable to work: the ill, the injured, the old, children without support, all who cannot contribute labour and must nevertheless live. Marx places this provision inside the arithmetic, as one deduction among others, rather than outside it as charity. What official poor relief had treated as an embarrassment at the margin of the economy becomes, in the passage, a line item of the economy itself.

Three features of the passage reward emphasis, because summaries routinely lose all three. The first feature is priority: deductions precede distribution, conceptually and institutionally. The individual's claim arises only over the remainder, a fund diminished by every collective purpose the society acknowledges. Whoever wishes to argue about shares must first argue about the size of the cake remaining after replacement, accumulation, insurance, administration, common needs and maintenance of non workers have taken their portions, and each of those portions is contestable on technical grounds, forecasts of wear, of growth, of risk, rather than on the ground of desert. The second feature is universality. Nothing in the list depends on capitalism or its absence; any complex economy, however owned, faces the same subtractions, which is precisely Marx's point against the slogan. The undiminished proceeds are diminished by the nature of production itself, and the difference between modes of production lies in who decides the deductions and in whose interest, not in whether they occur. The third feature is candour about conflict. Marx does not suppose the deductions set themselves. Their size is determined by available means and forces, partly by calculation, and the remainder of the text makes plain that such determinations are social decisions, taken somehow by the associated producers, though the notes never specify the machinery. The passage opens the door to politics inside the new economy rather than closing it, a point the closing sections of this guide return to when the disputes about the text are weighed.

The passage also performs a quieter service for the theory of exploitation that readers of Capital will recognise. Under capitalism, the same deductions occur, disguised. Part of the surplus value extracted from workers funds accumulation, part funds the unproductive consumption and administrative machinery of the existing order, part trickles back through poor relief and philanthropy toward those unable to work. The extraction is real, but its invisibility is also real: no worker sees a statement of deductions, because no worker is party to the account. In the cooperative commonwealth Marx sketches, the deductions are identical in function and transformed in visibility. They are made openly, from a product the producers collectively own, by rules they can in principle inspect. Exploitation, on the account the passage implies, is not the existence of deductions but their private appropriation and unaccountable disposal. That implication gives the passage its enduring analytical bite: it supplies a criterion, the openness and collective control of the social surplus, by which arrangements can be compared without slogans.

A word about proportion guards against a misreading in the opposite direction. The deductions passage is perhaps three paragraphs of the original notes, and this guide has now spent far longer on it than Marx did. The disproportion is deliberate and defensible. In a text where an author of Marx's compression chooses to enumerate, the enumeration is the argument; every item he lists is there because the slogan he is destroying required it not to be. Commentators who rush past the list to reach the two phases miss the foundation on which the phases stand, since the lower phase's distributive rule applies only to the already diminished fund, and the higher phase's abundance is defined against the possibility, absent in the lower, that deductions for growth and common needs might shrink relative to a swelling product. Arithmetic first, philosophy second: the order is Marx's before it is this guide's.

### What should a reader notice first in the deductions passage?

That the deductions are presented as necessities, not as optional policies. Replacement, accumulation, insurance, administration, common provision and support for non workers bind any economy intending to continue. The passage converts an argument about justice into one about continuation: before distribution can be fair, production must be reproducible, and reproducibility has a price no slogan can repeal.

Contemporary analogies help, provided they are used as analogies and not as identifications. A household that received its entire income as disposable cash, with nothing withheld for rent, insurance, repairs or savings, would discover within a year that the undiminished income was a fiction produced by ignoring obligations. States make the same discovery through budgets: gross product figures mean little until depreciation, debt service and reserves are acknowledged. Marx's passage applies the discovery at the level of a whole society and draws the moral for programme writers. The analogy also clarifies his polemical target. Lassallean agitation had taught workers to measure emancipation by the share of the product returning to labour. Marx teaches them to measure it by control over the whole product and its deductions, a harder standard, less flattering to orators, and the only one consistent with his analysis of how economies actually reproduce themselves.

One further implication deserves extraction before the narrative moves on, because later debates turned on it. The passage assumes a single social fund and a single accounting centre: the total product of the associated producers, from which deductions are made in common. It does not describe independent enterprises trading with one another, nor markets allocating the deductions, nor prices performing the calculation. Whether that assumption is a strength, a simplification appropriate to marginal notes, or the seed of the planning problems that later critics pressed, has been argued for a century, and the argument is taken up in this guide's dispute sections. Here it is enough to mark the assumption clearly, since the most common classroom error about the passage is to read it as a description of a market socialism of cooperatives. It is not. It is the budget of a single cooperative commonwealth, drawn in three paragraphs, and everything distinctive and difficult about the text follows from that.

## The lower phase and distribution according to labour

What remains after the deductions is the fund of consumption goods available for individual division, and the notes now ask by what rule a society just emerged from capitalism could divide it. Marx's answer describes the lower phase of communist society, and his description begins with an insistence that readers habitually skip: this society is not presented as it will be when mature, but as it emerges from capitalist society, stamped in every respect, economically, morally and intellectually, with the birthmarks of the old society from whose womb it comes. The birthmarks metaphor governs everything that follows. The lower phase is not a failed version of the higher; it is a transitional form whose defects are intelligible as inheritances, and whose distributive rule is the best rule available to a society still organised around individual labour contributions measured against one another.

The rule itself is stated with an accountant's precision. The individual producer, having made the social deductions through the fund, receives back from society, after those deductions, exactly what he gives to it: his individual quantum of labour. He has contributed a quantity of labour in one form, and he receives a quantity of consumption goods embodying the same labour in another form. The mechanism Marx sketches is a certificate. The producer receives from society a token certifying that he has furnished such and such an amount of labour, after deducting his labour for the common funds, and with that token he draws from the social stock of consumption goods as much as the same amount of labour costs. The certificate is not money, and Marx is careful, elsewhere in his writings as well as by implication here, about the difference: it does not circulate, it cannot be accumulated into capital, it certifies a completed contribution rather than commanding the labour of others, and it perishes in the act of consumption it authorises. One day's labour embodied in coal is exchanged, through the token, for one day's labour embodied in bread, cloth or schooling for a child, the schooling having already been provided under the common deductions, a detail that shows how the individual and collective funds interlock.

Marx then makes the move that defines the lower phase analytically. The principle governing this exchange, he observes, is the same principle that governs the exchange of commodities insofar as that exchange is an exchange of equal values: a given amount of labour in one form is exchanged for an equal amount of labour in another. The content and the form have changed utterly. No private owner confronts the producer; the means of production are common; nobody can appropriate another's product, because nobody has anything to give in exchange except his labour, and nothing can pass into the property of individuals except individual means of consumption. Yet the measuring rod survives the revolution. Labour time remains the standard by which unlike contributions are rendered comparable and rewarded, and with the rod come its consequences, which the next section examines under Marx's own heading of bourgeois right.

Why should labour contribution be the rule at all, a reader may ask, if its defects are so visible to its author? The notes supply the materials for an answer even where they do not pause to give one directly. Scarcity governs the lower phase: the productive forces are those inherited from capitalism, the division of labour persists, and consumption must therefore be rationed by some principle that connects claim to contribution if the society is to elicit the labour it needs. Contribution based distribution is the rationing rule that a scarce society of free producers generates from its own conditions, much as commodity exchange generated it before. Marx's treatment is notably free of lamentation about this. He does not call the lower phase unjust. He calls its right a right of inequality in its content, like every right, and proceeds to explain why a society at that stage can have no better standard, since right can never be higher than the economic structure of society and the cultural development conditioned by it. The sentence deserves to be weighed slowly. Right, for Marx, is not a tribunal above history handing down standards to economies. It is a set of rules a given economic structure can actually operate, and criticising the lower phase for failing a standard its conditions cannot support is, in his framework, a category error, the same error the programme made when it demanded fairness in the abstract.

The analysis also clarifies what the lower phase abolishes and what it does not. It abolishes exploitation in the precise sense the tradition gives the word: no class lives by appropriating the surplus labour of another, because the surplus is deducted openly into common funds rather than privately accumulated. It abolishes the anarchy of private production decisions taken behind the backs of producers, insofar as the deductions and the plan they imply are social acts. It does not abolish inequality of result, as the equal right argument shows. It does not abolish the compulsion to labour, which persists as the condition of any claim on the consumption fund. It does not abolish the state in any sense the notes specify, a silence examined later in this guide. And it does not abolish the separation between those who plan and those who execute that the inherited division of labour imposes, which is why Marx lists the persistence of that division among the conditions the higher phase must overcome. The lower phase is thus a precisely bounded achievement: common ownership and open deduction, wearing the measuring rod of the old world like a birthmark it cannot yet shed.

### How do labour certificates differ from wages?

A wage is money paid by an employer who owns the means of production, buying labour power whose use yields more value than the wage costs. A certificate certifies labour already contributed to a society the worker co owns, claims consumption goods of equal labour value, does not circulate, and cannot hire anyone or become capital.

The distinction matters because critics have sometimes described the certificate scheme as wages under new management, and defenders have sometimes described it as the abolition of all accounting. Marx's text supports neither description. Accounting persists, in labour time; employment disappears, because nobody sells labour power to an owner distinct from the associated producers themselves. Whether labour time accounting is practicable at the scale of a national economy is a genuine and difficult question, pressed hard by later critics and considered in this guide's dispute sections. What the notes settle is the intention: the certificate is a receipt in a system of direct social labour, designed so that the exchange of equivalents, which under capitalism conceals exploitation, here conceals nothing, since the deductions are public and the equivalence is the whole of the transaction.

A further feature of the scheme is easy to overlook and important for examinations and essays alike. The hours certified are hours of social labour, labour performed for the common stock; the deduction for the common funds is made before certification, so the certificate already represents labour net of its social obligations. Two producers contributing the same hours receive the same claim, regardless of the intensity or skill of their work, unless the society chooses to weight hours differently, a choice the notes do not discuss. Marx was aware that simple and complex labour raise problems of reduction, problems he had analysed in Capital in the context of value; the notes pass over the corresponding problem for certificates in silence. Honest exposition registers the silence. The scheme as written treats labour as homogeneous for purposes of claim, and much of the later criticism of the scheme fastens exactly there, asking whether an accounting that ignores skill, danger and disagreeableness can elicit the labour a complex economy needs. The notes do not answer, and this guide does not invent an answer for them.

## Equal right as a bourgeois right

The most quoted argument in the notes, and the one whose logic students most often reverse, begins from a concession. In the lower phase, Marx grants, the governing principle is equal right: each producer receives in proportion to labour contributed, the same standard applied to all, without regard to person. Then he takes the concession apart. Equal right, he argues, is in its application an unequal right for unequal labour, because the standard is equal while the individuals measured by it are not. One worker is stronger or more skilled and supplies more labour in the same time; another is weaker or slower. One is married and supports children; another is single and supports only himself. Measured by the single standard of labour contribution, the first receives more and the second less, and the parent with dependants ends, in real terms, poorer than the single worker whose certificate matches his own. Equal right, Marx concludes in the passage's famous formulation, is therefore a bourgeois right, a right that presupposes inequality, since every right consists in the application of an equal standard to unequal individuals, who can be brought under that standard only by being regarded from a single aspect, as workers and as nothing else.

The labelling is the source of endless confusion, so it must be handled precisely. Marx calls the right bourgeois not because bourgeois persons administer it and not because it is a fraud perpetrated on workers. He calls it bourgeois because its form descends from the world of commodity exchange and bourgeois law, where formally equal parties meet as owners and the law's equality famously abstracts from everything that makes them different. The defect, he insists, is inevitable in the first phase of a society emerging from capitalism: defects are inevitable in the first phase of communist society as it is when it has just emerged after prolonged birth pangs from capitalist society. The sentence is diagnostic, not denunciatory. He is not urging the lower phase to adopt a better standard tomorrow. He is explaining why the standard it has is the standard its conditions permit, and why the persistence of a bourgeois form inside a post capitalist economy is a fact to be understood rather than a betrayal to be exposed.

Two clarifications protect the argument from its commonest misreadings. First, Marx is not rejecting equality as such. His target is a specific conception: equality as the application of one measure to everyone, which, because people differ, manufactures unequal outcomes and then certifies them as fair. The higher phase formula, needs based distribution, is in a recognisable sense a more demanding equality, equality of outcome relative to need, achievable only when abundance and changed human capacities make the measuring rod unnecessary. The critique of equal right is therefore a critique of formal equality in the name of a substantive one, and readers who cite the passage against egalitarianism have the argument backwards. Second, Marx is not describing a peculiarity of socialism. His analysis of right as an equal standard applied to unequals is general jurisprudence: any rule, from taxation schedules to examination grades, treats differently situated people identically in the dimension it measures and ignores the rest. The notes give that general truth a specific historical address, showing where the lower phase's rule comes from and what would have to change for a society to outgrow the need for it.

The argument's intellectual ancestry is worth a paragraph, since scholars dispute its implications for Marx's theory of justice. In the third volume of Capital and in earlier writings, Marx had treated legal and moral standards as reflections of production relations, an approach that led some interpreters to conclude he could not consistently condemn capitalism as unjust, since by capitalism's own standards its distributions are fair. Other interpreters answer that Marx condemned capitalism on standards he considered more fundamental, such as freedom or human flourishing, which the Gotha argument leaves untouched. The notes themselves contribute a distinctive piece to the debate: they show Marx applying the internal standards method to a society he favoured, and finding its right defective by comparison with a later stage, which suggests that his evaluations were never confined to what an order's own rules certify. The separate guide on [whether exploitation is a moral or a technical charge](/2011/02/01/is-exploitation-moral-or-technical/) develops the controversy; here the point is narrower, that the equal right argument cannot be recruited, without violence to its logic, as a licence to call every distributive complaint naive. Marx's complaint about the lower phase is not that it distributes. It is that it must measure, and that measuring is a confession of scarcity.

### Why does distribution according to labour still produce inequality?

Because the standard counts only labour while lives differ along every other dimension. Strength, skill and speed vary, so equal hours yield unequal certificates. Family burdens vary, so equal certificates support unequal numbers of people. Needs vary with health and age, ignored entirely. A rule seeing a person from one aspect reproduces difference as inequality while certifying the result as equal.

Worked examples fix the point better than abstraction. Consider two machinists in the lower phase, each certified for the same hours in a week. The first is single, healthy and childless; the certificate covers his needs with a margin he can spend on books or travel. The second supports a partner whose illness keeps her from work and two children whose schooling the common fund provides but whose food and clothing come from the family's consumption claim; the identical certificate stretches across four lives and leaves no margin at all. Nothing in the transaction is irregular. No official has favoured the first machinist, no deduction has been misapplied. The inequality is produced by the rule operating correctly, which is exactly Marx's claim: the defect lies in the standard, not in its corruption. A society that finds the outcome intolerable must change the standard, and changing the standard, Marx argues, requires changing the conditions that make a labour standard necessary, the scarcity and the division of labour that the higher phase is defined by overcoming.

It is worth stating plainly what follows for the assessment of the lower phase as Marx presents it, since later sections weigh disputes about later practice. On the text's own terms, residual inequality in the lower phase is a predicted consequence of its distributive rule, acknowledged by its author in advance, and classified as a birthmark rather than a scandal. Whether that classification is a profound piece of historical patience or a dangerous excuse available to any regime that calls itself transitional is a genuine dispute, conducted by serious critics and defenders for over a century. The text alone cannot settle it, because the text specifies neither a timetable for the transition nor an institution empowered to declare it complete. What the text does settle is narrower and still valuable: whoever invokes the lower phase to explain inequality has Marx's analysis behind the explanation, and whoever invokes it must also accept Marx's framing of that inequality as a defect destined for abolition, not as a permanent settlement to be celebrated.

## The higher phase and the needs principle

Against the lower phase's measured world the notes set a second, described in the most famous sentences Marx ever wrote about the future. In a higher phase of communist society, he writes, after the enslaving subordination of the individual to the division of labour has vanished, after the antithesis between mental and physical labour has disappeared, after labour has become not only a means of life but life's prime want, and after the productive forces have increased with the all round development of the individual so that the springs of cooperative wealth flow more abundantly, only then can the narrow horizon of bourgeois right be crossed in its entirety and society inscribe on its banners the formula of distribution according to need. The sentence is a checklist disguised as a peroration. Each clause names a condition, and the formula at the end is valid, in Marx's construction, only when every condition holds. Popular quotation keeps the banner and drops the conditions, which is rather like quoting a contract's signature while omitting its clauses.

Take the conditions one at a time, since the table later in this guide summarises them and the summary should not be asked to do the work of understanding. The vanishing of enslaving subordination to the division of labour does not mean the end of specialised tasks; surgeons and violin makers will still train for years. It means the end of lifetime confinement to a single fragment of work as a social fate, the condition Marx had dramatised decades earlier in his writings on alienation, where the worker is bound to one operation and stunted by the binding. The disappearance of the antithesis between mental and physical labour likewise names a social relation rather than a technique: the separation between those who conceive and direct and those who execute, which the lower phase inherits and which no certificate scheme by itself dissolves. Labour becoming life's prime want is the most misunderstood condition, often mocked as a prediction that work will feel like play. Its analytical content is plainer: in the lower phase, labour is a means to consumption claims and is rationed by them; in the higher, the activity itself has become a primary need of developed individuals, so the incentive machinery of certificates is no longer required to elicit it. Finally, abundance: the springs of cooperative wealth flow more abundantly because the productive forces have grown together with the all round development of the individuals who operate them. Distribution according to need presupposes that needs can be met without rationing, since a needs standard under scarcity is merely a queue administered by doctors and committees instead of by certificates.

The formula itself has a history that Marx's authorship of the notes tends to erase, and accuracy requires the correction. From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs was not coined in these notes. Versions of the principle circulated among French socialists decades earlier; Louis Blanc popularised a close form of it, and the phrase has antecedents reaching back through utopian and even scriptural distributive maxims about giving according to need. Marx gave the formula its canonical placement, as the banner of the higher phase, and his placement is what the tradition remembers. Attribution, however, belongs to the movement before him, and the confusion is common enough that this series devotes a separate guide to [misattributed Marx quotations and their real sources](/2013/08/15/misattributed-marx-quotes/), where the formula's provenance is traced in detail. The point is not antiquarian. A reader who knows the formula predates the notes understands the notes differently: Marx is not inventing a slogan in a fit of generosity but assigning an existing slogan its conditions, telling a movement that had flown the banner for decades exactly what the world would have to become before the banner described anything real.

Notice what the higher phase passage does not contain, because the absences define the text's honesty and its limits at once. No date is offered, not even an order of magnitude; the transition is conditioned on transformations of labour, of persons and of productive capacity whose pace Marx leaves wholly open. No mechanism of allocation is specified for the needs principle beyond the banner itself: who assesses need, how competing needs are reconciled under any residual scarcity, and what happens where needs expand faster than the springs of wealth are questions the notes do not pose, let alone answer. No institutional description accompanies the phase at all, no councils, no plans, no markets, nothing that a constitutional draftsman could copy. The contrast with the deductions passage is striking and instructive. Where the subject is the lower phase's economy, Marx is an accountant. Where the subject is the higher phase's principle, he is a philosopher of conditions. Readers who demand of the higher phase the same concreteness will call the passage evasive; readers who attend to its conditional grammar will call it disciplined. The dispute sections below give both judgements their evidence.

### What has to change before the needs principle can govern?

Four transformations, on Marx's own list. Enslaving subordination to the division of labour must vanish, the antithesis between mental and physical labour must disappear, labour must become life's prime want rather than a means of life, and productive forces must grow with the all round development of individuals until cooperative wealth flows abundantly enough to end rationing.

The order of the conditions is worth a moment's attention, because it rebukes a lazy reading in which technology alone delivers the higher phase. Three of the four conditions concern human beings and their relations to work; only the fourth concerns output, and even it binds output to the development of individuals rather than to machinery alone. A society that multiplied goods while leaving the division of labour untouched would satisfy, on Marx's construction, none of the banner's premises. That is why the notes, for all their brevity, cannot be recruited as a promise that growth by itself communises anything. The higher phase is a civilisation change with an economic condition attached, not an economic threshold with cultural decorations.

## The two phases table

Everything argued so far can be compressed into the two phases table, the findable artifact this guide is built around. The table separates the phases by their distributive principle, by what remains of bourgeois right in each, by the position of the state as the notes present it, by the conditions each phase presupposes or must achieve, and by the place in the text where the claim is made. Compression has a cost, and the paragraphs around the table should be read as its footnotes: the lower phase row describes a society still carrying birthmarks, and the higher phase row describes a horizon defined by conditions, not a schedule.

| Phase | Distributive principle | Bourgeois right | The state | Conditions | Where in the text |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lower phase | Consumption goods divided in proportion to labour contributed, certified after the common deductions | Present and acknowledged: equal right applied to unequal individuals, a right of inequality in content | The transitional state is named in the notes as the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat, without institutional specification | Emerges directly from capitalism with its birthmarks; common ownership established; scarcity and the division of labour persist | The deductions passage and the analysis of the certificate exchange in the notes on the programme's distribution clauses |
| Higher phase | From each according to ability, to each according to needs | Crossed in its entirety: the narrow horizon of bourgeois right is left behind once measuring by labour ends | Not described: the notes assign the state no role in this phase and discuss it only for the transition | Division of labour overcome, mental and physical labour reconciled, labour become life's prime want, productive forces grown with developed individuals | The single conditional passage setting the four conditions before the banner formula |

Read down the bourgeois right column and the article's central contrast stands in one view. In the lower phase the right is present, operative and defective, and Marx says so without embarrassment. In the higher phase the right is not reformed but crossed, because the activity that required a measuring rod, the rationing of consumption by contribution, has itself ended. Read across the state column and a different lesson appears: the notes are nearly silent about the state in both phases, naming a form for the transition and describing nothing for either settled phase, a silence that later readers filled with everything from withering away doctrines to party states. The table makes the silence visible rather than filling it, which is the honest use of a summary. Read the conditions column last and note the asymmetry this guide has stressed throughout: the lower phase's conditions are a description of where it comes from, while the higher phase's conditions are a description of what must be achieved, one row retrospective and the other prospective, separated by a transformation the text gestures toward but does not narrate.

## The state in transition and the free state formula

The draft programme demanded a free state, and Marx's gloss on the demand is the notes' most purely political passage. Freedom, he argues, consists in converting the state from an organ superimposed upon society into one completely subordinate to it, and the question a programme should answer is not whether the state is called free but what transformation the state undergoes in the transition. He then poses the question the programme evaded: what transformation will the state undergo in communist society, what social functions will remain there that are analogous to present state functions? The question, he says, can only be answered scientifically, by analysis rather than by the word free repeated in thousands of combinations. Buried in the same discussion stands the sentence around which a library later accumulated: between capitalist and communist society lies the period of the revolutionary transformation of the one into the other, and corresponding to this is a political transition period in which the state can be nothing but the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat. The sentence is quoted here for its place in the argument, its meaning being the subject of a separate guide in this series on the [dictatorship of the proletariat and what Marx and Engels meant by the term](/2013/01/01/dictatorship-of-the-proletariat/), which traces its uses across both men's writings and distinguishes the phrase from the regimes later erected in its name.

Within these notes, the sentence's function is narrower than its fame suggests. It answers the free state demand by relocating the discussion: instead of an adjective, a period; instead of a compliment, a class characterisation of transitional rule. Marx offers no institutions, no franchise, no term limits, no account of how the transitional state relates to the lower phase whose economy he has just sketched, and the juxtaposition is itself information. The man who itemised insurance funds did not itemise a constitution, because the notes' purpose was to correct a programme's economics and slogans, not to draft the polity of the transition. Engels, writing to Bebel in the same weeks of 1875, made a companion point in plainer language: the words state and free people's state were, for communists, a piece of nonsense to be dropped as soon as conditions allowed, since the state as such is a machine of suppression that a free society keeps only as long as it needs and no longer. The withering away doctrine that the tradition later systematised, largely through Engels's writings and Lenin's codification, grows from these seeds rather than from any developed theory in the notes themselves.

The free state gloss also contains a warning that cuts against several of Marx's own later followers, and neutrality in exposition requires stating it. A state that stands above society, distributing benefits and calling itself free, is in the notes' analysis a state that has kept the essential feature of the old order: an organ superimposed on the producers rather than subordinated to them. Programmes that ask such a state to deliver socialism by instalments are, for Marx, asking the problem to solve itself. That is the context of his scorn for Lassalle's state aided cooperatives, and it explains why the notes, read whole, make uncomfortable reading for traditions that later governed through exactly the superimposed organ he described. The text gives no instructions for avoiding that outcome beyond the direction of the conversion it demands, from superimposition to subordination, and the distance between a direction and a safeguard is one of the honest criticisms the dispute sections record.

### What did Marx object to in the phrase free state?

Not to freedom, but to the adjective's evasiveness. Calling the state free avoids saying what it is for, whose it is, and what becomes of it during the transition. Marx replies that freedom consists in subordinating the state to society completely, and that the useful question is which functions a transitional state performs and which wither away.

A related clause of the draft, promising the removal of all social and political inequality, draws a shorter and equally characteristic correction. Marx remarks that the phrase should have spoken of the abolition of all class distinctions, since social and political inequality in general, detached from classes, is the sort of vague formula that can be endorsed by anyone and therefore commits the party to nothing testable. The pattern is the same as with fair distribution: where the programme generalises, Marx particularises; where the programme offers a sentiment, he demands the mechanism. Readers who find the notes pedantic are noticing the method. Readers who find them bracing are noticing what the method is for.

## One reactionary mass and the remaining targets

A guide that covered only the famous passages would misrepresent the notes as a treatise on distribution with political decorations. In bulk, much of the document is a clause by clause quarrel with the draft's political descriptions, and the quarrels matter because they show Marx's analytical habits at work on questions other than economics. The draft had described all classes other than the working class as forming one reactionary mass, a phrase inherited from the rhetoric of the movement's early years. Marx objects on grounds of fact and strategy together. The middle classes, artisans, small manufacturers, shopkeepers and peasants, are not a single mass with a single politics; their interests cut across the great divide, they are being ground between capital and labour by the development of industry itself, and a programme that lumps them as reactionary forfeits the analysis that might win sections of them or at least predict their behaviour. The phrase, he argues, also flatters the working class with a false simplicity: the emancipation of labour is not advanced by misdescribing everyone else.

The objection connects with a wider theme in Marx's political writing, the refusal of moralised blocs. Classes in his analysis are positions in production, and their political conduct follows from their situations with all the messiness that real situations contain. A programme sentence that sorts humanity into the workers and a reactionary remainder has replaced analysis with a cheer, and cheers, in Marx's handling, are what programmes contain instead of thought. The same section of the notes criticises the draft for its silence about the international character of the workers' movement and about the trade unions, the organisations through which the class actually learns its strength, omissions Marx treats as symptoms of a national narrowness he despised in the Lassallean tradition. Germany's workers, in the draft's telling, were to be emancipated within Germany by a German state; the notes insist, by their objections and their silences side by side, that the movement Marx recognised was international in structure because capital was international in fact.

Demands concerning education draw a characteristic combination of agreement and suspicion. The draft called for free education and for general compulsory schooling, with the state as educator. Marx supports the schooling and resists the educator: government and church, he writes, should alike be excluded from influence over the school, and a programme that defines the state as the educator of the people has confused funding with formation. The state may properly be made to pay, through taxation, for an education it does not control; the distinction between financing a service and staffing its doctrine is one the deductions passage has already prepared, since the schools fund appears there as a common deduction under the producers' own society. Similar precision governs the notes on protective legislation: factory laws and limits on child labour are welcomed where the draft proposes them, and criticised where the draft's phrasing dissolves their enforceability. The pattern across all these clauses is consistent enough to state as a rule of the text. Marx tests every sentence by asking what institution it implies, what mechanism it names, and what it would commit the party to defend when circumstances changed. Sentences that survive the test are rare in the draft, and the survivors are mostly the ones the Eisenach tradition had contributed.

## The terminology correction: phases of one society, not two systems

### Did Marx distinguish socialism from communism?

Not in those terms. He described a lower and a higher phase of communist society, distinguished by their distributive principles. Naming the lower phase socialism, as a separate stage preceding communism, comes from Lenin's State and Revolution, and the familiar two stage vocabulary descends from him rather than from Marx.

The correction deserves expansion, because it is the point on which the notes are most often misreported in reference works and journalism alike. Marx's own usage across his writings is looser than any stage theory. In the notes he speaks of communist society in two phases, and he also uses phrases such as the first phase of communist society and a higher phase of communist society, with cooperative society based on common ownership as a near synonym for the whole. The word socialism appears in his writings in varied, unsystematic senses, often as the name of doctrines and movements in the air around him rather than as a stage succeeding capitalism. Nothing in the notes assigns socialism a distributive principle, a state form or a duration. Those assignments were made later, above all by Lenin in the fifth chapter of State and Revolution, written in 1917, where the lower phase is explicitly renamed: what is usually called socialism, Lenin writes, Marx called the first or lower phase of communism. The sentence is offered by Lenin as a clarification, and as a piece of terminology it conquered the world. Twentieth century states wrote the two stage scheme into constitutions; textbooks taught it as Marx's; examination syllabuses still reproduce it. A reader of the notes themselves will search in vain for the scheme's familiar labels, and the search is the lesson: part of what is called Marxism is a Leninist filing system imposed on Marx's texts, useful or not on its merits, but not to be mistaken for the texts.

Why does the distinction matter beyond pedantry? Because the names carry the defects. When the lower phase is called socialism, its acknowledged birthmarks, inequality under equal right, labour discipline, the persistence of measuring and accounting, become the properties of a named system that can be defended as a destination in itself, and the higher phase recedes into a ceremonial horizon invoked on anniversaries. When the phases keep Marx's own grammar, both inside the single name communism, the lower remains grammatically a beginning, defined by what it has not yet overcome. Defenders and critics of the renaming divide accordingly. Those who value Lenin's terminology point out that a distinct name for the transitional order permits honest description of its non communist features and prevents premature claims that the higher phase has arrived. Those who regret it answer that the distinct name licensed exactly the permanence it claimed to prevent, letting a phase announce itself as a system and its defects as doctrines. The notes cannot arbitrate the dispute, since their author never faced the choice. They can, and do, fix the provenance, and provenance is where any honest discussion of the question has to start. The series guide to [Lenin's State and Revolution and its reading of Marx](/2012/12/15/state-and-revolution-readers-guide/) follows the book chapter by chapter for readers who want the renaming in its full argumentative setting.

A second terminology trap waits in the same neighbourhood and catches translators. The German phrase Marx uses for the lower phase's governing standard, gleiches Recht, is rendered in English as equal right; some discussions slide from right to rights, and from rights to the modern vocabulary of human rights, which belongs to a different conceptual family. Marx's Recht here is the juridical standard governing distribution, the rule of the game, not a catalogue of entitlements against the state. Keeping the distinction straight prevents a familiar confusion in which the critique of equal right is misread as hostility to legal equality in general, when its target is a distributive measure. The notes' own context settles the question: the passage sits between the certificate scheme and the needs formula, inside an argument about how consumption goods are divided, and about nothing else.

## How the text was read: Erfurt, Bernstein and the long afterlife

Reception is not an epilogue to this text; it is half of what the text has been. The notes reached the public in 1891 as a weapon in a programme fight, and every subsequent era has reissued them for its own. At Erfurt the immediate effect was cleansing: the new programme dropped the iron law, the free state and the undiminished proceeds, and adopted a two part structure, drafted principally by Kautsky and Bernstein, whose theoretical section described capitalist development and whose practical section listed democratic and labour demands. Marx's positive sketch, the phases and the deductions, entered the programme only obliquely, as background doctrine rather than as text. The party that had suppressed the notes thus ended by governing itself with a programme nearer to them, an irony Engels savoured in correspondence. He also sent the party, in the same season, his critique of the Erfurt draft itself, objecting to its silences about the republic and the concentration of powers, evidence that the correction of programmes had become, in his last years, something like a genre of its own.

The next great reading arrived with the revisionist controversy at the end of the decade. Eduard Bernstein, Engels's literary executor and a man who had absorbed English Fabian gradualism during exile, argued that the movement should abandon its revolutionary expectations and acknowledge itself a democratic reform party. In the quarrel that followed, the Gotha notes served both camps as artillery. Orthodox defenders, Kautsky prominent among them, wielded the phases and the transitional state sentence as proof that Marx had foreseen a rupture between capitalism and any socialist order, not a smooth parliamentary glide. Revisionists answered from the same pages, noting that a lower phase stamped with birthmarks, distributing by labour and administered through deductions decided collectively, sounded remarkably like a long democratic transition, and that Marx had set no timetable that gradual accumulation of reforms could violate. The text's genuine ambiguities, above all its silence about duration and institutions, made both readings available, and the controversy taught a durable lesson about programmatic texts: they are cited most confidently where they are least specific.

Lenin's reading, performed in hiding in 1917, remains the most consequential single interpretation and the source, as this guide has shown, of the terminology the world inherited. State and Revolution seizes on the notes as the mature Marx's doctrine of the state and of post capitalist distribution, quotes the deductions and the phases at length, and systematises what Marx had sketched. Lenin's Marx is a theorist of the transitional state and of accounting and control, and the lower phase under its new name of socialism becomes in his hands a complete social order with its own principle, to each according to his work, its own discipline and its own state. The reading is powerful, textually grounded in its quotations, and transformative in its additions: the naming, the systematisation and the confidence that the sketch describes a governable programme all belong to the interpreter. Later scholarship has sorted the inheritance with some care, distinguishing what the notes say from what the codification made of them, and readers are best served by holding the two layers apart, which is why this series treats [Lenin's readers' guide material](/2012/12/15/state-and-revolution-readers-guide/) and the present text as companions rather than substitutes.

Where twentieth century states adopted written constitutions for orders they called socialist, the lower phase principle entered law in recognisable form: distribution according to the quantity and quality of work performed, with the needs formula reserved as the stated principle of a future communist stage. The fact is recorded here without evaluation, as the brief for honest exposition requires, since the notes themselves neither designed those states nor described their institutions. What can be said textually is narrower. The constitutional formula preserves Marx's distributive rule for the lower phase while detaching it from the conditions Marx attached to the transition, above all the subordination of the state to society and the open, collectively decided character of the deductions. Whether that detachment represents the rule's application or its inversion is among the standing disputes weighed below. Scholars of Soviet and Chinese economic history, meanwhile, have used the deductions passage as a benchmark in a different way, comparing its funds with the actual accumulation, defence, administration and welfare shares of planned economies, and finding the comparison illuminating precisely because the passage is a budget: it can be set beside real budgets, and the divergences measured.

Academic reception followed its own rhythm. For political theorists, the notes became the central exhibit in the Marx and justice debate already mentioned, with Allen Wood's reading of Marx as a critic of juridical standards on one side and Norman Geras's patient demonstration, in a famous article on the controversy, that Marx did condemn capitalism as unjust on the other. For historians of economic thought, the certificate scheme attracted attention as a rare specimen of non market allocation designed by a major theorist, and it entered debates about labour money that reach back to Robert Owen's labour exchanges and John Gray's proposals, schemes Marx had criticised in his early writings on money. For textual scholars, the notes posed the editorial questions common to Marx's remains: a manuscript not prepared for publication, released by Engels with light annotation, translated and retranslated, its phrases acquiring in each language a slightly different halo. The English wording most readers know descends from translations made in the twentieth century for the collected works; the German text's compactness, its legal vocabulary and its irony survive translation unevenly, which is one reason the table and the terminology sections of this guide keep returning to the structure of the arguments rather than to any single rendering's wording.

## The strongest objection: can marginal notes bear this weight

Every strong reading of the notes must face the objection that the tradition has loaded a constitution's weight onto a memorandum's frame. Stated at full strength, the objection runs as follows. A few dozen pages of glosses, written in irritation at a unification draft, addressed to a handful of party leaders, unpublished for sixteen years and never revised by their author, are asked to serve as the definitive account of post capitalist institutions for a movement that governed half of Europe. The account they contain is, even on the most generous reading, a sketch: an arithmetic of deductions without decision procedures, a distributive rule without an administration, a transitional state without institutions, a higher phase without a mechanism. The thinness is not an accident of the format, the objection continues; it is the tradition's central failure expressing itself. Marx's theory explains capitalism in thousands of pages and its successor in a pamphlet, and movements that later improvised institutions in the sketch's name were filling a vacuum the founder left, with consequences that no amount of exegesis can transfer back onto a text that never specified them.

The objection deserves respect rather than rebuttal by slogan, and parts of it must simply be conceded. The thinness is real. The notes do not contain a theory of planning, a law of the transition, or a safeguard against the superimposed state their own free state passage warns against, and anyone who claims otherwise is reading a different document. The sixteen year suppression is also real evidence about status: the party that received the notes did not treat them as a charter, and their author, who protested many public wrongs in his life, did not force their publication. Conceding these points costs the honest exposition nothing, because the notes' value was never located in their completeness.

What the objection underrates is the density and the deliberate character of what the sketch contains. The deductions passage is more concrete than critics usually allow, because it specifies the actual claims on a social product in their actual order of priority, a specification no other major socialist text of the century attempts and every real economy must somehow perform. The two phases analysis is more rigorous than its brevity suggests, because it derives a distributive standard from social conditions instead of proclaiming one, and it predicts the standard's defects from its form. The sketch is thin where its author believed specification would be dishonest, about institutions whose shape he held must depend on circumstances he could not foresee, and dense where he believed analysis could already bind, about the arithmetic any successor economy faces on its first day. That distribution of density is itself a position, and it can be argued with on its merits. The institutional question the objection rightly presses, what machinery could operate the sketch without betraying it, is the subject of a separate guide in this series on the [critique of Marxism and democracy](/2013/03/15/marxism-and-democracy-critique/), which examines the missing safeguards and the attempts, from Luxemburg onward, to supply them.

A final consideration belongs to the history rather than to the argument. The objection assumes the tradition had a choice of founding texts about the future and picked a thin one. In fact the cupboard was nearly bare, and it was bare by the founder's design: Marx's refusal to write recipes was a methodological commitment, maintained across decades against utopian competitors who wrote nothing else. The Gotha notes are what that commitment produced when a programme forced his hand, the maximum of specification he was willing to sign. Judged as a constitution, they fail. Judged as the record of a great analyst marking the exact boundary between what theory could settle in 1875 and what it could not, they are among the most instructive documents in the tradition, and the boundary they mark has not moved as far in the intervening century and a half as either the tradition's celebrants or its prosecutors like to claim.

## Standing disputes: certificates, birthmarks and the unspecified state

Three disputes about the notes have proved durable enough to organise separately, since a reader will meet all three in any serious literature and should be able to recognise their shapes. The first concerns the labour certificate scheme and whether it could operate an economy. Critics in the tradition of the Austrian economists, and later writers on the calculation problem, argue that labour time is an inadequate accounting unit for a complex economy: it cannot register the scarcity of land and materials, the differing quality and disagreeableness of work, the time structure of production, or the demand side of consumption, and a centre distributing certificates against a physical plan would lack the signals by which errors are discovered. Defenders answer on several levels. Some note that Marx's scheme is a sketch of a principle, distribution by contribution, rather than a planning system, and that judging it as a blueprint for Gosplan misreads its genre. Some argue that labour accounting can be supplemented by physical balances and iterative planning, pointing to later proposals in the literature of participatory and cybernetic planning that take the certificate idea as an ancestor. Others concede the calculation critique for a whole economy while retaining the scheme as an account of what distribution by contribution means, a standard against which actual systems can be described. The notes themselves contribute one firm datum to the dispute and no more: Marx chose labour time as the measure in full knowledge of the reduction problems he had analysed for value, and he chose it anyway, which tells us the choice was principled rather than casual, without telling us it was workable.

The second dispute concerns the lower phase's defects and the use made of them. Marx classified residual inequality, labour discipline and the measuring state of accounts as birthmarks, inevitable in a first phase and destined to be outgrown. Critics of later regimes reply that the classification is an all purpose excuse: any defect, however entrenched, can be reclassified as a birthmark, any privilege as a transitional necessity, and a phase without a specified duration or a test of completion can be prolonged indefinitely by those who administer it. Defenders of the text respond that the abuse of a classification does not refute the classification, that Marx described a society with common ownership and open deductions rather than the orders later erected, and that the birthmark analysis at least imposes a direction of judgement: defects are to be measured by whether they are shrinking as their material conditions recede. Both sides can quote the notes. Neither can quote a timetable, because there is none, and the dispute therefore reduces, at the textual level, to a question the notes leave genuinely open, how a phase knows it is ending. Honest exposition leaves the question open and marks it as the point where interpretation passes into politics.

The third dispute concerns the state, and it is the dispute with the largest consequences. The notes name the transitional state in one sentence and describe its functions in none, beyond the general doctrine that the state must be converted from an organ superimposed on society into one subordinate to it. Anarchist critics, beginning with Bakunin's polemics against Marx in the International and sharpened by the experience of later party states, argue that the silence is the theory's fatal gap: a transitional state staffed by a movement's own apparatus will consolidate its superimposition, and no subsequent withering can be relied upon. Marxist defenders answer from the wider corpus, where the Paris Commune supplies the institutional model Marx actually admired, a working body elected and recallable, paid workmen's wages, its standing army replaced by the armed people; the separate guide in this series on the [dictatorship of the proletariat](/2013/01/01/dictatorship-of-the-proletariat/) assembles that material and the arguments around it. The textual fact for the present guide is precise: the model exists in Marx's writings on the Commune, written four years before the Gotha notes, and the notes neither repeat nor contradict it. Whether a reader joins the two texts into one doctrine or treats the notes' silence as significant in itself divides interpreters along lines that map closely onto the anarchist and Marxist traditions respectively, and the division is recorded here as a standing feature of the literature rather than settled by editorial preference.

A fourth, quieter dispute deserves registration for researchers. The notes' treatment of distribution as secondary to production has been read by some economists as a refusal to think about incentives, and by others as the founding statement of an institutional economics in which distributive outcomes are symptoms. The incentive reading has force: the lower phase relies on contribution based claims to elicit labour, which is an incentive system, and the higher phase relies on labour having become a prime want, which is an anthropology. Between the two phases lies an unexamined transformation of motivation that the text asserts rather than explains, and psychologists and institutional designers who press on that joint are pressing on something real. Defenders note that Marx regarded motivations as historically formed throughout his work, so that a change in the experience of labour across generations is, within his framework, a hypothesis of the same kind as the change in needs he also predicts. The dispute is genuinely undecided in the literature, and it marks the boundary where the notes' economics ends and their philosophical anthropology begins.

## Reading the Critique: editions, placement and method

Placement is the first practical question a new reader faces, and the answer is unusually favourable: the Critique can be read early, profitably, and whole. Unlike the major works, it presupposes no long apprenticeship. A reader who knows, in outline, what capitalism is in Marx's analysis, who owns the means of production, who sells labour power, what surplus value names, can follow every argument in the notes, and the notes will repay the reader by clarifying the outline itself. The series guide to a [Marx reading order](/2013/06/15/marx-reading-order-guide/) places the text among the short political writings that give a newcomer the tradition's political economy in miniature, alongside the programme critiques and the Commune writings, before the ascent into Capital. That placement is endorsed here with one addition: the Gotha notes should be read twice, once for the polemic and once for the sketch, because the two readings foreground different texts, the first a party document thick with the quarrels of 1875, the second a small treatise on post capitalist arithmetic that happens to be wearing a party document's clothes.

Editions and translations call for a paragraph of practical caution. The notes survive as a manuscript that Engels prepared for the press in 1891, and the text in the Marx Engels Collected Works, the standard English edition in fifty volumes, is the version most Anglophone readers meet, also reproduced in popular selections of Marx's political writings. The German text is printed in the Werke and in the historical critical edition, whose apparatus records the manuscript's state, including passages where Engels's editorial hand can be compared with the original. Readers citing famous phrases should know that small choices in rendering Recht, Abzug and Verkehr shape arguments downstream: deductions, for instance, translates Abzuge, subtractions or withholdings, and the English word's fiscal flavour is a translator's deposit rather than Marx's metaphor. None of this makes the text unstable. Its arguments survive every competent translation, because they are arguments about structures, deductions, standards, phases, and not about verbal nuance. It does mean that a dissertation should not be built on a single English adjective without checking the German, a discipline the researcher persona section below repeats for a different audience.

Method, finally, is worth stating as this guide's own commitment, since the text invites two opposite abuses. One abuse is proof texting, the extraction of single sentences, the transitional state sentence above all, as warrants for positions the surrounding argument does not support. The other abuse is dismissal by format, the treatment of the notes as occasional journalism unworthy of the analysis their author gave Capital. The method used here steers between them: every famous sentence has been read inside its paragraph, every paragraph inside its clause of the programme, and the whole inside the occasion of 1875 and the reception that began in 1891. Readers who keep notes on a text of this density will find that a structured notebook, with the deductions, the phases and the disputes kept in separate sections that can be revised as understanding changes, serves better than marginalia alone; the study notes companion at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is designed for exactly that kind of layered note keeping across a long series of connected texts, and it suits a document whose arguments reward being rearranged as they are mastered.

### Where should a new reader place the Critique among Marx's writings?

Among the short political texts, after a first acquaintance with the wage labour analysis and alongside the writings on the Paris Commune. It is the most economical route into Marx's account of a post capitalist order, the clearest statement of his distributive thinking, and a text whose brevity lets a reader hold the whole argument in view.

A closing word on the assessment this guide promised at the outset, coherence. The parts of the sketch fit together with notable tightness. The deductions passage supplies the lower phase's fund; the lower phase's rule explains the persistence of bourgeois right; the critique of bourgeois right defines what the higher phase must overcome; the conditions of the higher phase describe the overcoming; the free state gloss disciplines the political form of the transition that connects the phases; and the terminology, once Lenin's layer is separated, keeps the whole inside one society's biography rather than two systems' rivalry. The incoherences are equally definite: a transitional state named and not described, a motivation transformation asserted and not explained, a timetable absent by design, and a certificate measure whose reduction problems the author had the tools to state and did not. A sketch can be coherent and incomplete at once, and the judgement this guide reaches is that the Critique is exactly that, the most coherent short account Marx gave of the society he expected, and a standing demonstration of how much even the most determined analyst left to a future he refused to script. Its afterlife confirms the double character. Movements quarried it for certainty and found a budget, a standard and a set of conditions; scholars quarry it still and find the same three things, better lit by each generation's questions. A text that can be neither used up nor finished with has the only kind of immortality that documents earn, and these irritable marginal notes, written to stop a party making a mistake in a single spring, have earned it in the plainest way available: by being more exact than the slogans they were written against, and by remaining exact after the slogans, the party and the century that contained them have all changed beyond recognition.

## The covering letter and the men who read it

Documents acquire part of their meaning from their addressees, and the Critique's addressees explain its peculiar mixture of candour and restraint. The covering letter went to Wilhelm Bracke, a Braunschweig publisher and party organiser whom Marx trusted as one of the Eisenach leaders least infected by Lassallean habits. Marx asked Bracke to show the notes, after reading them himself, to a named circle: Geib, Auer, Bebel and Liebknecht are listed in the letter, a roll call of the merged party's coming leadership. The instruction tells us how Marx imagined the document working. It was to circulate inside the leadership as a corrective brief before the congress, arming the Eisenach men with arguments against clauses they had already conceded in drafting. It failed in that purpose, since the congress adopted the draft with only minor changes, and the failure is itself a datum about the balance inside the unification: the Eisenachers valued the merger more than the corrections, and Liebknecht, who had negotiated the terms, was not inclined to reopen them at the prompting of a letter from London.

The letter also contains Marx's insurance policy, and its phrasing matters for the suppression story told earlier. He indicates that after the congress he and Engels will issue a short statement dissociating themselves from the programme, should it pass unamended, a threat they ultimately chose not to execute. Historians have read the unexecuted threat in opposite ways. One reading sees principle compromised by tactics, the London critics swallowing a programme they believed false because the movement's unity seemed worth the price. Another reading sees a mature judgement about proportion: the programme was a set of sentences, the party was a growing organisation of real workers, and sentences could be fought over later, inside a living movement, whereas a schism performed for sentences would have been remembered as the act that broke German socialism at its birth. The notes' sixteen year sleep is the residue of that judgement. Anyone who treats the suppression as simple cowardice by the recipients must explain why the authors acquiesced in it; anyone who treats it as simple wisdom must explain Engels's fury, sixteen years later, at a leadership still reluctant to print. Both elements belong in the record.

### What does the covering letter to Bracke add to the notes?

It adds the intended audience, the permitted circulation and the unexecuted threat of dissociation. Read with the letter, the notes are a memorandum meant to correct a draft before a vote. Read without it, they look like a treatise that missed its publisher. The letter is evidence about genre, and genre governs how much system a reader should demand.

Bracke himself deserves a sentence in any full account, because the choice of recipient was a judgement about character. He was a businessman inside a workers' party, a type Marx usually suspected, yet his publishing house had carried the movement's literature through difficult years and his loyalty to the Eisenach tradition was proven. That Marx routed his most sensitive internal document through Bracke tells us the Londoners still believed the party contained men who could be trusted with an unwelcome truth in writing. The subsequent history, in which the document slept undisturbed through the years of illegality, suggests the trust was honoured in the narrow sense: nobody leaked the notes to the police press or sold them to scandal. The party protected the manuscript it declined to obey, a very human combination that the reception history should record without sneering.

## A worked example: the deductions applied to a single product

Abstract lists persuade slowly; arithmetic persuades at once, so consider the deductions passage applied to a stylised product, using round proportions invented purely for illustration and labelled as such. Imagine the associated producers of some economy generate, in a year, a total social product whose labour value can be represented as one hundred units. The programme's slogan promises the producers the undiminished hundred. Marx's passage begins by withholding. Suppose replacement of consumed materials and worn equipment requires fourteen units, a figure any real economy can estimate from its depreciation accounts. Suppose the producers vote an accumulation fund of ten units to extend capacity, since their population and ambitions are growing. Suppose actuarial prudence assigns four units to the reserve against harvest failure, fire and flood. Of the hundred, seventy two remain, and nobody has yet eaten.

Administration, the coordination of production and distribution itself, salaries for planners, accountants, transport schedulers and inspectors, takes perhaps three units, a share Marx expected to shrink as society simplified its affairs. The common satisfaction of needs takes a far larger share, as he expected it to grow: schools, universities, hospitals, sanitation, parks and the care of public spaces might claim eighteen units in a society determined to socialise its comforts. Provision for those unable to work, pensions, disability support, the maintenance of orphans, takes nine. The fund of individual consumption now stands at forty two units, to be divided by certificates among the producers according to labour contributed. The slogan promised a hundred; arithmetic delivered forty two, and the difference is not exploitation, since every withheld unit returns to the producers as equipment, security, schooling, health or support in old age. The difference is the cost of there being a society at all, itemised. Nothing in the example depends on the particular numbers, which a different society would set differently at every line, by argument, by forecast and by vote. Everything depends on the order of operations: the subtractions are made first, in common, before the language of individual shares is allowed to begin.

The example also exposes where politics re enters a passage that can look purely technical. Each proportion is a decision wearing an estimate's clothes. How much accumulation a generation owes its successors, how much insurance prudence demands, how generously the unable to work should be maintained relative to the workers who maintain them, how far common consumption should expand at the expense of private choice: the passage names the funds but not their sizes, and the sizes are the substance of economic politics in any order. Marx's framework does not abolish that politics. It relocates it, from the hidden boardrooms and markets where deductions are made without the producers' knowledge, to whatever forum the associated producers use to set their own budget. Critics who call the passage apolitical have confused the absence of parties with the absence of choices. Defenders who call it democratic by itself have confused an open budget with the institutions that would debate it, institutions the text, once again, does not supply. The example's forty two units hang between those judgements, which is where the text leaves most of its interesting questions.

One more feature of the arithmetic deserves extraction, because it reverses a standard assumption about Marx's priorities. In the example, the funds for common needs and for non workers together outweigh administration many times over, and Marx's text anticipates the imbalance growing: the common fund, he writes, will increase considerably as the new society develops, while the costs of administration will diminish. The trajectory matters more than the levels. A society following the passage's logic over time shifts its surplus steadily from private consumption claims toward collective provision, not by confiscation but by the budget's own evolution as wealth grows and coordination simplifies. Whether that trajectory is a benign expansion of shared goods or a ratchet toward a society in which individual choice is crowded out by collective funds is a value dispute the text frames with unusual clarity and does not pretend to settle. It is recorded here because essays on the passage that omit the trajectory omit the only dynamic prediction the passage actually makes.

## The Critique beside Capital: two registers of one theory

Readers who arrive at the notes from the first volume of Capital sometimes experience a small shock of register, and the shock is instructive. Capital analyses an existing order with the patience of an anatomist, building categories cell by cell: commodity, value, labour power, surplus value, accumulation. The Gotha notes sketch a non existing order with the impatience of a reviewer, spending their analysis on standards and proportions rather than on mechanisms. The two registers are sometimes treated as evidence of two Marxes, the scientist and the pamphleteer, and whole interpretive schools have been built on preferring one. The notes themselves resist the split. Their central arguments are applications of Capital's categories to a hypothetical balance sheet. The deductions passage is the reproduction schema of the second volume translated into a budget: the replacement fund is constant capital consumed and restored, the accumulation fund is surplus directed to expanded reproduction, and the whole exercise presupposes the distinction between necessary and surplus labour that the first volume established. The equal right argument applies the analysis of commodity exchange, the exchange of equivalents between formally equal owners, to the certificate scheme, and finds the old form surviving inside the new content. The notes are Capital's theory turned, briefly and deliberately, toward the future it usually refuses to describe.

The comparison also clarifies the notes' most famous silence, the absence of any account of how the lower phase economy coordinates itself. Capital's author knew the complexity of a division of labour orchestrated without a centre in a way few contemporaries matched; his silence about coordination in the sketch cannot be ignorance. Interpreters divide over its meaning. One tradition, strong among the sketch's critics, holds that Marx simply assumed socialisation would dissolve the coordination problem, since production by associated producers for known needs seemed to him transparent in a way market production is not. Another tradition answers that the notes describe distribution, not production planning, and that Marx's remarks elsewhere, on bookkeeping as more necessary in collective production than in capitalist, and on the Commune as a political form, supply fragments of a coordination view that the genre of marginal notes had no occasion to assemble. The dispute is registered in the research literature under several headings and cannot be closed from the notes alone. What the comparison with Capital does establish is the standard of judgement: whatever else the sketch is, it is not the work of a man unaware that economies are complicated, and criticisms that proceed as though it were waste their force on a target that does not exist.

### How does the Critique change a reading of Capital?

It reveals the future tense that Capital's analysis keeps under discipline. The categories of value, surplus labour and reproduction reappear in the notes as instruments of a budget for a society without capitalists, showing that Marx regarded his analysis of the old order as also a description of the new order's starting accounts.

There is a further, subtler effect on the reading of Capital's own distributive passages. The first volume's account of the working day, of piece wages and of the reserve army is often read as a moral indictment resting on the worker's claim to the full product of labour, the very claim the Gotha notes dismantle. Readers formed by the notes read those chapters differently: the indictment rests not on a distributive slogan but on the analysis of a relation, the purchase of labour power and the extraction of unpaid labour within an exchange of equivalents, and its force survives the discovery that no society can pay undiminished proceeds. The notes therefore protect Capital from its own popularisers. They also explain a puzzle of reception, why Marx spent political capital in 1875 defending distinctions that looked scholastic to his own party. He had spent a decade building an analysis in which distribution follows production and slogans must answer to arithmetic. A programme that reversed both doctrines in its first clauses threatened, in his judgement, to dissolve the theory's gains into the rhetoric the theory had been built to replace.

## Sentences that are misread in isolation

Short canonical texts suffer a characteristic injury: their sentences are quoted loose from the arguments that discipline them. A service this guide can perform, before turning to its persona layers and questions, is to restore four much quoted sentences to their contexts, briefly, so that readers meeting them in the wild can test the use being made of them. The first is the refusal of recipes for the cook shops of the future, a phrase from the postface to the second German edition of Capital rather than from the notes themselves, but habitually deployed against the notes' authority. In context the phrase rebukes comrades who wanted a state socialism of detailed prescription from the theorist, and it coexists, in the same author's practice, with the Gotha sketch: Marx refused menus, not arithmetic. The second sentence is the transitional state sentence, handled earlier in this guide; in isolation it has served as a charter for regimes, while in context it answers a question about the word free and says nothing about the institutions of anybody's government. The third is the banner formula of the higher phase, whose four conditions this guide has laboured; quoted without them, it becomes a promise, while in the text it is a conclusion licensed by premises that include the transformation of labour itself. The fourth is the description of equal right as a bourgeois right, misread as a sneer at equality, when its paragraph explains that the defect lies in any equal standard applied to unequal persons and predicts the defect's abolition when measuring ends. Four sentences, four restorations; the pattern of injury is the same each time, and learning to spot it is most of what reading this text well consists in.

A related discipline concerns the phrases the notes attack, since Marx's targets are also quoted loose, as if his scorn settled their meaning. The iron law of wages, in Lassalle's own usage, was an agitational device for proving that trade unionism and cooperatives within capitalism were futile and that only political action for state aided production could help; Marx's objection, examined earlier, was to the device's economics and its fatalism, not to the observation that wages under capitalism press toward a socially defined minimum, an observation his own theory restates in historical terms. The undiminished proceeds, likewise, named a real grievance before it named an impossible arithmetic. Restoring targets is not charity toward Lassalle. It is accuracy about what the argument was, and it protects Marx's victories from the suspicion that they were won against straw.

## What the notes assume about needs

The higher phase distributes according to needs, and everything depends on what needs are allowed to be. Marx's writings treat needs as historical products rather than as a fixed biological schedule. The value of labour power in Capital already contains a historical and moral element: what a worker needs is partly what a society has taught its members to expect, and the standard rises and falls with civilisation and struggle. The Grundrisse extends the thought in the opposite direction, describing capital itself as a great producer of new needs, multiplying desires as it multiplies goods, so that the population arriving at any post capitalist threshold brings an inherited, expanding schedule of wants rather than a monk's list. The Gotha notes inherit both thoughts. The needs of the higher phase are the needs of developed individuals, all round in Marx's phrase, and the abundance that satisfies them is correspondingly demanding: not a static sufficiency but a flow of cooperative wealth growing together with the persons whose needs it meets.

That dynamism protects the formula from one criticism and exposes it to another. It protects it from the sneer that needs based distribution imagines human beings as simple stomachs whose requirements a bureau could tabulate once and for all. On Marx's account nobody could tabulate them, because they develop with the society that satisfies them, and the formula is a principle of open ended provision rather than a ration schedule. The exposure is equally plain. If needs expand with development, the finishing line of abundance recedes as it is approached, and the higher phase risks becoming a horizon in the strict sense, a line that organises travel without ever being reached. Marx's text does not address the risk, and interpreters divide over whether he would have regarded permanent approach as failure or as the human condition under a better name. The notes' defenders point out that the conditions are stated comparatively, springs flowing more abundantly, subordination vanished, rather than as absolute thresholds, which suggests a gradient of phases rather than a gate. The critics answer that a gradient without a gate cannot support the sharp juridical contrast the text draws, bourgeois right crossed in its entirety, and the tension between the gradient in the conditions and the gate in the conclusion is one of the genuine unresolved joints in the argument, worth more to a student than any summary that files it smooth.

A second assumption about needs hides inside the lower phase and is easier to miss. Distribution by labour contribution presupposes that the common deductions have already handled the needs that cannot wait for certificates: childhood, sickness, age and disability are provided from the funds before the certificate economy starts, which means the lower phase is, in modern vocabulary, a hybrid order, needs based in its collective provision and contribution based in its individual consumption. The hybridity is rarely remarked in polemics, where the lower phase is pictured as a stern meritocracy of hours. Marx's arithmetic is gentler and more interesting: the meritocratic rule governs only the residue, and the size of the residue shrinks, by the text's own trajectory, as the common funds grow. A society in the lower phase that chose to expand its schools, health services and pensions would thereby shrink the domain in which bourgeois right operates, without formally crossing into the higher phase at all. Whether that internal dynamic is a designed feature or an implication Marx did not pursue cannot be told from the notes. It can be told from the arithmetic, which is why this guide insisted on the arithmetic first.

## The household inside the equal right argument

One passage of the equal right analysis repays attention for what it assumes, because generations of feminist scholarship have pressed on it with justice. Marx's unequal individuals are a worker who is married and has children and a worker who is not; the dependants appear as burdens on a certificate earned by a labourer whose own labour alone counts as contribution. The household's work, the cooking, cleaning, bearing and raising through which labour power itself is daily and generationally produced, does not appear in the accounting, because the certificates certify labour furnished to society's common stock, and the labour of the household was not, in the arrangements Marx inherited or in the sketch he drew, furnished to any common stock. Critics in the domestic labour debate of the twentieth century argued that the omission is not a slip but a structural feature of labour based distribution: any scheme that counts only socially certified labour will render the labour that reproduces labour invisible, and will distribute its products to the certified while the uncertified depend on transfers within families, reproducing inside the new order a dependence the old order was condemned for imposing.

Defenders of the text have answers of varying strength, and the dispute is best reported rather than resolved. The strong answer notes that the common deductions transform the household's position materially: schooling, health provision and support for those unable to work are socialised in the sketch, and children are maintained partly from the common fund rather than wholly from a parent's certificate, so that the machinist example given earlier in this guide overstates the family's exposure if the common funds are read at full size. The weaker answer appeals to Marx's wider writings, where the dissolution of the old family form is predicted as part of the transition, which tells us he expected the household itself to change without telling us what claim its labour would then hold. The honest summary is that the notes distribute to individuals as workers and handle everyone else through funds, and that this architecture, progressive against the poor law world it replaced, embeds an assumption about whose activity counts as contribution that later movements had to contest from inside the tradition. The contest is part of the text's afterlife and belongs in any complete guide.

## Other sketches: the notes among Marx's futures

The Gotha sketch is the fullest of Marx's accounts of a successor society, but it is not the only one, and placing it among its siblings sharpens what is distinctive in it. The manuscripts of 1857 and 1858, known as the Grundrisse, contain the celebrated fragment on machines, in which automation shrinks necessary labour until wealth is measured by disposable time rather than by labour, a vision far more technologically exuberant than anything in the notes of 1875. The difference of register is real and has generated a literature: the younger Marx of the notebooks speculates about the tendencies of machinery with a philosopher's freedom, while the older Marx of the programme notes counts deductions with an accountant's severity. Continuity readers argue that the two registers are one theory at different temperatures, disposable time being the higher phase's abundance described from the side of production. Discontinuity readers reply that the later Marx had learned caution from two decades of political defeat and economic study, and that the notes' severity is the speculation grown up. Either way, the Gotha text is where the tradition's sober account lives, and examination questions that ask what Marx proposed are answered from its pages rather than from the notebooks' brilliance.

The Civil War in France, Marx's address on the Paris Commune of 1871, supplies the political sibling. Where the notes give distribution without institutions, the Commune writings give institutions without distribution: elected and recallable officials, the working body rather than the parliamentary one, the standing army abolished, public functions stripped of privilege. The tradition has usually read the two texts as one doctrine in two volumes, the Commune supplying the state form whose absence in the notes this guide has repeatedly marked. The composite reading is natural and probably right about Marx's own assumptions, since he wrote both within four years and praised the Commune's measures as the political form of the working class's emancipation. It remains a composite, an act of interpretation joining documents of different genres written for different emergencies, and the researcher sections of the literature are right to keep the join visible. A reader who knows exactly which claims come from which text owns a map of the doctrine that no single document provides, and can follow later controversies, in which one party quotes the Commune's institutions and another the notes' economics, with the sources sorted.

Behind both stands the earliest sibling, the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right from 1843 and the manuscripts of 1844, where the young Marx imagined the overcoming of alienation in the language of human essence and species being. The Gotha notes never speak that language. Their persons are producers with certificates, their problems are funds and standards, and their horizon is a banner sentence inherited from French socialism. Scholars who stress the continuity of Marx's project point to the substance beneath the vocabulary: the all round development of the individual in the higher phase conditions is the species being of 1844 translated into political economy. Scholars who stress rupture answer that translation is transformation, and that a thinker who had truly kept the early vision would not have written the deductions passage with such relish. The present guide takes no side in that quarrel, which belongs to the biography of an idea rather than to the exposition of one text, but it equips the reader to follow it: the notes are the mature statement, and their maturity consists precisely in submitting the early vision to arithmetic, standards and conditions, a submission the early writings never attempted and the later tradition has never stopped arguing about.

## The shape of the notes: four movements

First time readers are often disoriented by the text's architecture, which follows the draft programme's clauses rather than any plan of its author's. A map of the four movements steadies the reading. The opening movement, from which this guide drew its account of labour and nature, the undiminished proceeds, the deductions and the two phases, comments on the programme's first clause, the economic foundation paragraph in which the Lassallean formulas were concentrated. It is by far the longest and most theoretical movement, and it contains nearly everything the world remembers from the text. The second movement turns to the programme's political demands, the free state, the franchise and the character of legislation, and contains the transitional state sentence in its argumentative home among questions about what a state is and whose it is. The third movement handles the specific social demands, protective laws, the working day, restrictions on child and female labour, and reads as practical commentary from a veteran of factory legislation campaigns, approving much and tightening more. The fourth movement addresses education, the state as educator problem among others, and closes with remarks on the international character of the movement and the draft's parochial silence about it.

The map explains two features of the notes that puzzles about their reputation otherwise leave mysterious. It explains why the economics outweighs the politics so heavily in a text by a political revolutionary: the programme's first clause was its most theoretical, and the glossator's duty followed the clause. And it explains why the text feels at once systematic and fragmentary, because the system belongs to the comments on distribution, where Marx allowed himself to develop a connected argument, while the fragmentariness belongs to the later movements, where clauses were thinner and his patience thinner still. Editors who rearrange the notes thematically, as some selections do, dissolve this architecture and with it the evidence about what provoked what. The standard editions print the notes in the programme's order, as they should, and readers are advised to keep that order in view even when, as in this guide, exposition regroups the material by theme. The regrouping is a convenience of commentary. The original order is a fact of the text.

Proportion carries one more lesson. The first movement's dominance means that the tradition's picture of post capitalist society is drawn overwhelmingly from comments on a single clause about the proceeds of labour, a clause its author considered confused in every term. Had the draft programme led with the state, the notes might have left a political theory where they left an economics. Contingency of this order is uncomfortable for doctrines that prefer their founding texts necessary, but it is the ordinary condition of documents written against deadlines, and the Gotha notes are exemplary rather than exceptional in bearing its marks. What is exceptional is how much connected thought the contingency happened to extract: a clause about fair shares provoked the deductions, the phases and the critique of right, which is rather as if a heckle had provoked a treatise. The treatise exists because the heckled author had been thinking about distribution for thirty years and the clause gave him the occasion to write the thinking down.

## What a programme is for

Behind the clause by clause quarrel stands a disagreement about genre that the notes make explicit only in passing, yet it organises Marx's judgements throughout. A programme, in the Lassallean practice the draft inherited, was an agitational banner: a set of sentences chosen to rally, to flatter the movement's self image and to negotiate the price of unity between factions. Sentences earned their place by their sound and their acceptability. A programme in Marx's counter conception is a public standard of doctrine, a document by which a party can be held to account when it is tempted, in office or in opposition, to trade its analysis for applause. Sentences earn their place by being true, by surviving the arithmetic and the definitions, because a party will be governed in hard times by whatever its programme taught it in easy ones. Hence the ferocity about formulas that struck even sympathetic readers as disproportionate. Marx was not editing prose. He was refusing, on the record, to let the German party teach its members that labour creates all wealth, that wages obey an iron law, that the state can be free while standing over society, because members taught falsehoods in the programme would reproduce them in the movement's practice for a generation.

The genre dispute survived its occasion and remains the quiet subject of many later programme fights inside the tradition, including the Erfurt revision and the controversies around Bernstein, both touched earlier in this guide. Its relevance for a modern reader is not confined to socialist parties. Any organisation's founding or platform document faces the same fork between banner and standard, and the notes are a masterclass in auditing the banner kind of sentence: ask what the sentence implies about the world, ask what institution it commits you to, ask what it would sound like read back to you by an opponent in ten years. Marx's glosses apply those three questions with a consistency that can feel relentless across thirty pages, and the relenting never comes, because the genre he is defending permits none. Readers who finish the notes slightly exhausted have understood something true about them. They were written by a man who believed that movements are ruined less often by their enemies than by their own sentences, and who treated a draft programme accordingly, as the most dangerous document a party produces.

## The years of illegality and the sleeping manuscript

Between the congress of 1875 and the publication of 1891 lies the strangest period in German socialism's history, and the manuscript slept through all of it. The party unified at Gotha grew rapidly, alarming the imperial government, and after two attempts on the life of the emperor in 1878, neither of them the work of socialists, Bismarck carried the Anti-Socialist Laws through the Reichstag. The laws banned the party's organisations, meetings and publications inside Germany while leaving its candidates eligible for election, a half suppression that produced a half legal movement: deputies spoke in parliament under immunity, newspapers were printed abroad and smuggled home, and the party's real education happened in the dense associational life of clubs, choirs and lending libraries that the laws could not reach. It was in those years that the Gotha Programme itself acquired a retrospective glow, as the founding charter of a persecuted unity, which made Marx's glosses on it more unwelcome than ever. To print the founder's contempt for the charter while the charter's party was being prosecuted would have been, the leadership judged, a gift to the prosecution.

The judgement had a human texture that the publication controversy of 1891 partly obscured. Liebknecht, who had received the notes in 1875 and buried them, spent those years in and out of prison and exile, building the movement whose unity the Gotha draft had purchased. His refusal of Marx's corrections was not the timidity of a functionary but the loyalty of a founder to his own settlement, and it hardened with sacrifice. When Engels forced the notes into print, the quarrel between the two old men, conducted through intermediaries and the press, carried the bitterness of a sixteen year deferral coming due. Bebel, the other giant of the party, steered between them, and the party survived its embarrassment, as Engels had calculated it would. The episode left a permanent deposit in the tradition's habits: a suspicion of suppressed documents and a corresponding hunger for them, so that later generations of Marxists learned to ask of every canon what had been left in the drawer, and the asking itself became a school of reading. The Gotha notes were the first great lesson in that school, and their own text, a memorandum that outargued its suppression, remains the best introduction to why the school exists.

## Irony, contempt and the notes as literature

No honest guide should pretend the notes are temperate. Their prose is salted with contempt: for phrases repeated without thought, for borrowed laws, for the coquetries of leaders negotiating unity at doctrine's expense. The covering letter warns its recipient that the glosses are sharp, and they are, in the manner of a man annotating in the small hours a document he believes will mislead thousands. Yet the irony has a discipline that distinguishes it from mere abuse. Marx mocks sentences, not persons, with the partial exception of Lassalle's posthumous influence, and the mockery is always in service of a distinction: between wealth and value, between a law of nature and a law of a mode of production, between the state as it stands over society and the state subordinated to it. Read the sarcasm as punctuation and the argument stands unchanged; read the argument away and the sarcasm is unintelligible. That dependence is the test of polemical writing that deserves to survive, and the notes pass it.

The literary quality matters for a practical reason: it is why the text is read by people who agree with none of it. Political theorists of other schools assign the Gotha notes because the equal right argument is a permanent contribution to the theory of distributive standards, stated in prose a student remembers. Historians assign them because no other document shows a great movement's founding compromise being audited by its own theorist in real time. Writers of programmes in any tradition read them as a cautionary exhibit. A text suppressed by its subjects, ignored by its author in print, and published by an executor against a party's will has had to live by its quality alone, unassisted by any institution's promotion for its first sixteen years and assisted thereafter by institutions whose promotion it would have embarrassed. That it is now among the most assigned short texts in political theory is a verdict on the prose as much as on the doctrine, and guides that treat it as doctrine only miss half its mechanism of survival.

## Gross and net: the passage and the habits of accounting

One way to feel the deductions passage's modernity is to notice how thoroughly its central distinction has been absorbed into ordinary economic bookkeeping, where it now passes unnoticed. National accountants distinguish gross product from net product by deducting the consumption of fixed capital, the depreciation of the stock of buildings and machines used up in production. A country that celebrated its gross figure without the deduction would be congratulating itself on output part of which merely replaced what production destroyed, and every statistical office on earth refuses the congratulation. Marx's first deduction is the same refusal, generalised. He extends the accountant's honesty beyond the capital stock to the whole list of a society's prior claims, insurance, administration, common services, maintenance of non workers, and then asks what is genuinely available for individual division. The passage can thus be read as an essay on the difference between gross and net at the scale of a civilisation, written before the statistical apparatus existed that would make the difference routine.

The extension is where the argument lives, because the later deductions are not all of the same kind, and Marx knew it. Depreciation is a physical fact wearing an accounting dress; its size is given, within margins, by the stock and its wear. The accumulation fund is a choice about the future's claim on the present. The reserve is a judgement about risk. Common provision is a decision about which needs a society meets jointly. Maintenance of those unable to work is a commitment about membership, about who counts as one of us for purposes of support. By arranging these unlike items in a single list of deductions prior to distribution, Marx makes a theoretical claim that is easy to state and hard to refute: a society's distributive argument can only begin after it has answered, explicitly or by default, a series of prior questions that are partly technical, partly prudential and partly moral, and a politics that argues about shares while leaving the prior questions to default is arguing about the shadow of its economy. Programme writers of every persuasion since 1875 have had to live inside that insight, usually without knowing its address.

There is also a critical edge in the passage for the tradition that claims it, and neutrality requires the edge to be shown. The deductions are to be determined, Marx writes, by available means and forces, partly by a calculus of probabilities. The sentence grants the future society's administrators a wide technical discretion, and it specifies no procedure by which the producers check the calculus or contest the determination. A budget set by experts in the producers' name, with the producers' role confined to having authorised the system once, would satisfy the letter of the passage while violating the spirit of its companion doctrine, the subordination of every superimposed organ to society. Later critics of actually existing planning pressed exactly this seam, arguing that the deductions became, in practice, the commanding heights from which a new superimposition was built, with accumulation rates set beyond the reach of those whose consumption was deducted. Defenders reply that the seam is an argument for building the missing procedures, not evidence that the passage intended their absence. Both readings are live in the literature, and both begin from the same three paragraphs, which is the mark of a text that framed a problem more durably than it solved it.

## The schoolbook afterlife: how the notes were taught

Publication made the notes available; popularisation made them doctrine, and the populariser in chief was Karl Kautsky, editor of the journal that printed them and the German party's leading theorist for a generation. Kautsky's commentaries on the Erfurt Programme carried the Gotha arguments into the party's educational machinery, where they were simplified, as schoolbook doctrine always is, into teachable contrasts: labour and nature, gross and net, distribution by work and distribution by need, the lower stage and the higher stage. The simplification was a service and a fate. It carried the arguments to millions of readers who would never open the collected works, and it planed away the qualifications this guide has laboured to restore, the conditional grammar of the higher phase, the hybrid needs provision inside the lower, the silence about institutions. Much of what the twentieth century called Marx's doctrine of the future society was Kautsky's Gotha, and the distinction between the notes and their schoolbook is the first thing a researcher learns to draw.

The schoolbook also fixed the text's enemies. Generations of critics learned their Marx from summaries of the phases and the certificates, and aimed their refutations at the summaries, so that the secondary literature contains long wars in which neither side is fighting over the document itself. A reader returning to the notes after the wars is often surprised by two discoveries: how conditional the famous claims are, and how much of the argument is about the present order's concepts rather than the future's machinery. The surprise is diagnostic. It measures the distance between a memorandum written against a draft programme and the catechism later manufactured from it, and it is the strongest practical reason for the kind of close reading this guide has attempted, in which every celebrated passage is returned to its clause, its occasion and its arithmetic before it is allowed to mean anything.

## The man behind the formulas: Lassalle's shadow

No figure haunts the notes more persistently than a man eleven years dead when they were written. Ferdinand Lassalle had founded the General German Workers' Association in 1863, led it with theatrical brilliance for little more than a year, and died in 1864 from wounds received in a duel over a love affair, leaving behind a doctrine, an organisation and a legend out of all proportion to his career's length. The doctrine combined genuine working class demands, universal suffrage above all, with the iron law, the state aided cooperative scheme and a tactical openness toward Bismarck's Prussia that scandalised Marx, who had corresponded with Lassalle for years in a friendship corroded by intellectual rivalry and by what Marx regarded as plagiarism of his own ideas repackaged without their analysis. The organisation survived its founder as a church survives a prophet: his writings were its scripture, his phrases its liturgy, and the unification of 1875 required his heirs to bring the phrases with them into the merged party's programme.

Understanding the haunting explains the notes' otherwise puzzling intensity about doctrines whose author was beyond correction. Marx was not arguing with a ghost for pleasure. He was contesting custody of the German movement's mind, which the Gotha draft, in his judgement, had quietly transferred to the dead man's system at the very moment the living movement finally united. The Eisenach leaders had conceded the programme's theoretical clauses as the price of Lassallean signatures, calculating that organisation mattered more than formulas. Marx's glosses are the counter calculation, entered in writing: formulas are what organisations think with, and a party that unites on Lassalle's sentences will act on Lassalle's strategy, courting the state, neglecting the unions, preaching fatalism about wages, whatever its members intend. History's verdict on the calculation is mixed in an instructive way. The merged party did not follow Lassalle's strategy; it built unions when legality allowed, fought elections as an opposition, and produced leaders, Bebel above all, whose practice owed more to the Eisenach tradition than to the prophet. Yet its programme kept the phrases until 1891, and its theory, when Kautsky systematised it, had to be built by expelling them. Both calculations were partly right, which is why the episode resists the moralising versions told by each side's historians.

A biographical fact about the rivalry deserves record because it colours the notes' treatment of the iron law in particular. Lassalle's economics were derivative, assembled from Ricardo, Rodbertus and the classical wage doctrine, and Marx knew the assembly's sources better than its assembler had. The contempt in the glosses is therefore partly a scholar's contempt for a populariser who had taken the movement's theory from the movement's theorist and returned it simplified, with the simplifications pointing toward a politics the theorist abhorred. Later scholarship has been fairer to Lassalle than Marx was, crediting his organisational genius and his role in making German workers a political public, while confirming the derivative character of his economics. The notes are not fair to him, and were not meant to be. They are a custody dispute, and custody disputes are not where fairness lives. What they preserve, under the unfairness, is the analytical core this guide has followed: whatever Lassalle's services to the movement, his sentences about wealth, wages and the state could not survive examination, and a programme built from them would mislead the very workers it flattered.

## Two metaphors that escaped their paragraphs

Great arguments often travel by metaphor, and two of the notes' metaphors have travelled so far that their origins are forgotten. The first is the birthmark. A society emerging from capitalism bears, in Marx's image, the marks of the womb that bore it, defects that are congenital rather than chosen and that fade, if they fade, with growth rather than with denunciation. The image has structured a century of argument about transitional societies because it supplies a temporal classification for defects: a birthmark is expected at birth, shameful if celebrated, and diagnostic if it persists unchanged into maturity. Every party to the disputes recorded earlier in this guide uses the image, including those who turn it against the tradition by asking how old a society must be before its marks stop being birthmarks. The second metaphor is the horizon. Bourgeois right has a narrow horizon that the higher phase crosses in its entirety, an image that converts a legal standard into a landscape: standards limit what can be seen, and a society inside equal right literally cannot see distribution except as measurement, just as a traveller cannot see past the skyline of his own position. The image explains why Marx thought argument alone could not retire bourgeois right: horizons are not refuted, they are walked beyond, by changes in the ground. Both metaphors do philosophical work that their paragraphs could not do in propositions alone, and both repay the attention of any reader who wants to know why this memorandum, of all the century's programmes and glosses, is the one still assigned.

## Reading the programme and the notes together

The most instructive way to read the Critique, and the way its first audience in 1891 effectively read it, is with the draft programme open beside it. Modern selections sometimes print the programme's clauses as headings to the glosses, and the arrangement transforms the experience. Sentences that in Marx's prose seem to appear from nowhere reveal themselves as answers; sarcasms acquire addresses; the great theoretical flights, the deductions and the phases, are seen growing from clauses of a few lines each, like oaks from acorns planted by other hands. The exercise also reverses the usual direction of judgement. Readers who approach through the notes alone tend to convict the programme's drafters of simple foolishness. Readers who see the draft whole notice how ordinary its sentences are, how closely they resemble the platform language of reform movements in any country and century, and the recognition is chastening: the notes are an audit of the ordinary, and their standard, that every sentence must survive its own implications, is one almost no real programme anywhere meets. That is not a reason to abandon the standard. It is a reason to understand what adopting it costs, namely permanent dissatisfaction with one's own side's documents, a cost Marx paid in isolation and Engels paid in the quarrels of 1891.

The pairing also clarifies what kind of victory Engels won at Erfurt. The party did not adopt the notes; it adopted a programme from which the notes' principal targets had been removed, which is a different and more typical outcome for internal criticism. Doctrines in living movements advance by deletion as often as by proclamation: the iron law vanished from the party's documents without ever being formally renounced, the free state dissolved into democratic demands, the undiminished proceeds were replaced by the language of appropriation of the means of production. A reader tracing the phrases across the programmes of 1875, 1891 and the later revisions watches a vocabulary being retired by embarrassment, the specific embarrassment of having been annotated by the founder. Few texts in any tradition can show so direct a line from their sentences to the editing of a movement's charter, and the line is the strongest external evidence that the notes, whatever their limits as a design for a society, succeeded completely at the task their author actually set them: they made certain sentences unusable by the movement that had proposed them.

## What the sketch omits: money, markets and the world beyond Germany

A complete guide owes its reader a catalogue of the sketch's silences that goes beyond the state, because several further absences shape how the text can and cannot be used. The first is money. The lower phase's certificates are explicitly not money in function, since they do not circulate, yet the notes never explain how a society that has abolished money in distribution handles the spheres where money does more than distribute: settling accounts between regions and industries, bridging the time between production and consumption across seasons, and trading with a surrounding world that still uses it. Marx had written searchingly about money for thirty years, and his silence here is the silence of a man treating one clause of one programme, not designing a monetary constitution. Later attempts to build labour money systems discovered the difficulties empirically, and their records belong to economic history rather than to the notes.

The second silence concerns the boundary of the commonwealth. The deductions are drawn from a total social product imagined as a single fund, yet no country in 1875, and no country since, produces everything it consumes. A post capitalist Germany would import grain, cotton, metals and colonial goods and export manufactures, and every such transaction crosses the boundary between the certificate economy and the world market, where values are settled in money and labour embodied abroad cannot be certified at home. The notes, written against a programme whose horizon was national, share the horizon. Their internationalism is a matter of solidarity and organisation, asserted in the closing complaints about the draft's silence, rather than of economic design. Readers who ask the sketch how a lower phase trades will find the question unasked in its pages, and the unasked question has mattered in practice wherever attempts were made to operate contribution based distribution inside one country.

One further silence, smaller than the others and easy to overlook, concerns disagreement inside the commonwealth itself. The notes assume throughout that the associated producers can determine the deductions, choose between accumulation and consumption, and operate a certificate system, yet they never ask what happens when the producers disagree, as producers with different trades, regions, ages and appetites certainly will. No voting rule is mentioned, no minority is protected in the text, no appeal against a budget is imagined. The omission is of a piece with the missing state theory, and it points to the same explanation: Marx's polemical target was a programme that said too much of the wrong kind, and his remedy was to say only what analysis could support. Procedure, the machinery by which equals settle differences without a master, belonged in his mind to the self activity of the working class in organisations like the Commune, whose practices he had praised as discovered rather than designed. A reader may find that answer noble or evasive. The notes themselves simply proceed as if the producers speak, somehow, with a voice that can set a budget, and the somehow is left standing in the text like an unfinished wall in a house otherwise meticulously measured.

Innovation belongs on the same list, quietly. The deductions fund expansion of production, but expansion in the passage means more of the productive capacity a society already understands: more mills, more miles of rail, more output of known goods. Nothing in the sketch addresses how a contribution based economy proposes the new, the untried product, the process nobody's labour certificate anticipates, or how it tolerates the failures that experimentation guarantees. Marx, who chronicled capitalism's restless revolutionising of production with unmatched vividness, knew that dynamism was among the old order's genuine achievements. Whether the new order's budget politics, with every experiment competing in advance against schools, reserves and pensions for its share of the deductions, could match that restlessness is a question the notes neither raise nor equip a reader to answer, and economic historians who judge the sketch's realism usually begin exactly there.

A related silence is the countryside. The notes address a party of industrial workers in the most industrialising state in Europe, and their producer is implicitly the factory and workshop labourer whose contribution can be timed and certified. Agriculture, with its seasons, its weather, its family farms and its resistance to the stopwatch, fits the certificate scheme awkwardly, and the peasantry appears in the notes mainly inside the rejected phrase about one reactionary mass. Marx wrote elsewhere about the peasantry with more patience and more sociology, but within these pages the rural world is a deduction from the argument rather than a subject of it. The omission mattered historically, since every later attempt to realise anything like the lower phase confronted an agrarian majority or a large peasant sector whose relation to labour certification had to be improvised. Cataloguing the silence is not an accusation. It is a boundary marker, showing where the sketch ends and where any application of it begins to be somebody else's work, for which the text can be cited as inspiration but not as instruction.

## For students and exam candidates

Examiners return to this text because it compresses testable doctrine into a few pages, and the marks follow three structures. The first is the deductions sequence: replacement, accumulation, reserve, administration, common needs, support for those unable to work, in that order, before any individual share exists. An answer that begins distribution with the workers' claims has already lost the argument Marx is making. The second structure is the two phases contrast, lower phase distribution by labour contribution under certificates, higher phase distribution by need under four stated conditions, with the terminology correction attached: the stage names socialism and communism as successive orders belong to Lenin's later usage, not to the notes. The third structure is the equal right argument, which must be stated as a mechanism, an equal standard applied to unequal individuals, and not as a slogan about fairness. Quote sparingly and precisely, prefer the short formulations examiners recognise, and always attach a condition or a deduction to any use of the needs formula. Answers that present the higher phase as a promise fail; answers that present it as a conditional construction earn their marks.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The notes teach best when students meet the programme first. Distribute the draft's opening clauses without Marx's glosses and let a seminar defend them for ten minutes; the phrases sound generous, and the defence writes itself. Then introduce the corrections one at a time, nature beside labour, the deductions beside the undiminished proceeds, the measuring rod inside the certificate, and let the room feel how an argument can be dismantled by arithmetic rather than by counter rhetoric. Two exercises reliably produce discussion. Ask students to set the deduction proportions for a society of their choosing and defend the accumulation share against the claims of the unable to work, which teaches that the passage relocates politics instead of abolishing it. Ask them to find a distributive rule for the lower phase that escapes the equal right critique, which teaches why Marx thought the defect structural rather than corrigible. Close by separating the layers of the text's afterlife, Marx's phases, Lenin's names, the schoolbook's catechism, since students who can separate the layers can read the secondary literature without being conscripted by it.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the text is a primary source with an unusually well documented chain of custody: a manuscript of 1875, a covering letter naming its permitted readers, a suppression maintained by its recipients and acquiesced in by its authors, a publication in 1891 engineered by Engels in a party journal, and a reception that can be traced through programmes, controversies and constitutions. Cite it from a scholarly edition, check famous phrases against the German where an argument turns on wording, and distinguish in every reference between what the notes contain and what Lenin's codification added, above all the naming of the lower phase. For historians, the document's value lies in the collision it records between doctrine and unification, a collision whose papers survive on both sides, in the London correspondence and in the party's conduct. For journalists, the standing service of the text is corrective. When a report attributes to Marx a distinction between socialism and communism as stages, a promise of equality of outcome, or a blueprint of a planned economy, the notes are the quickest check available: twenty or thirty pages in which the phases carry no such names, equality of standard is criticised as defective, and the only blueprint on offer is a list of deductions. The correction is verifiable in an evening, which is more than can be said for most corrections in this field.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Critique of the Gotha Programme about?

It is Marx's clause by clause commentary on the draft programme prepared for the 1875 congress at Gotha, where two German socialist parties merged. Marx attacked the draft's Lassallean formulas, its claim that labour is the source of all wealth, its iron law of wages, its demand for the undiminished proceeds of labour and its free state, and in correcting the distribution clauses he produced his fullest account of a post capitalist economy: the deductions from the social product, a lower phase distributing by labour, a higher phase distributing by need, and the argument that equal right remains a bourgeois right.

### Q: Why was the Critique of the Gotha Programme not published for years?

Marx wrote the notes as an internal memorandum in 1875, sent to Wilhelm Bracke for circulation among a small circle of party leaders. Those leaders had negotiated the unification the draft served, and they declined to reopen its terms; Marx and Engels, unwilling to split the movement publicly over a programme, let the matter rest. The manuscript slept among party papers through the years of the Anti-Socialist Laws. Engels published it in 1891 in Die Neue Zeit, against the leadership's objections, because a new programme was being drafted at Erfurt and he wanted the old errors corrected by Marx's own text.

### Q: What does Marx say a post-capitalist economy would look like?

He describes a cooperative commonwealth in which the producers own the means of production jointly and the total social product is first diminished by common deductions: replacement of used up means of production, a fund for expanding production, a reserve against accidents, administration, provision for schools and health, and support for those unable to work. The remainder is divided among individuals. In the lower phase the division follows labour contributed, certified by non circulating tokens; in the higher phase, once scarcity and the old division of labour are overcome, it follows need. Beyond that framework the notes specify no institutions, prices or planning machinery.

### Q: What are the two phases of communist society?

The lower phase emerges directly from capitalism and bears its birthmarks: the means of production are held in common, exploitation in the sense of private appropriation of surplus labour has ended, but consumption goods are divided in proportion to labour contributed, measured by certificates, so inequality persists because an equal standard is applied to unequal people. The higher phase becomes possible only when the enslaving division of labour has vanished, mental and physical labour are no longer opposed, work has become a primary want of life, and productive wealth flows abundantly; distribution then follows need rather than contribution.

### Q: Did Marx distinguish socialism from communism?

Not as two successive stages with separate names. In these notes both phases are phases of communist society, a lower and a higher, distinguished by their distributive principles. The practice of calling the lower phase socialism and reserving communism for the higher stage was established by Lenin, who stated the renaming openly in State and Revolution in 1917 and presented it as a clarification of Marx. Textbooks, constitutions and examination syllabuses inherited the two stage vocabulary from that source. Readers who check the notes themselves find the phases but not the names attached to them in later usage.

### Q: What does equal right is a bourgeois right mean?

It means that the lower phase's distributive standard, payment in proportion to labour contributed, has the form of bourgeois law even though its content is post capitalist. A right, for Marx, applies one equal measure to people who are unequal in strength, skill, health and family burdens, and therefore yields unequal results while certifying them as equal treatment. He calls the defect inevitable in a society fresh from capitalism and no cause for scandal: the standard can only be outgrown when abundance and the transformation of labour make measuring contribution unnecessary, at which point distribution by need replaces it.

### Q: Why did Marx object to labour is the source of all wealth?

Because the sentence is false as a description of production and misleading as politics. Material wealth consists of use values, and every use value combines labour with material supplied by nature; nature is therefore equally a source of wealth, and labour itself is the exercise of a natural force. Crediting labour alone also conceals the real relation of dependence: a labourer who owns nothing but labour power must work with means owned by others, and a formula that hides that dependence flatters the owners of land and materials. Marx wanted a workers' programme founded on claims that survive examination.

### Q: What is the iron law of wages?

It was Lassalle's doctrine, adapted from classical political economy and Malthusian population theory, that wages oscillate around the level of bare subsistence by a law admitting no exception: rises in pay supposedly increase workers' numbers until competition forces wages down, and falls reduce numbers until wages recover. Marx rejected the doctrine as bad economics, since each mode of production has its own historically specific population laws, and as disabling politics, since a natural law of wages makes trade union struggle futile and contradicts the same programme's promise to abolish the wages system. A law cannot be both iron and abolishable.

### Q: Is the Gotha Critique the best source on Marx's vision?

For the political economy of a post capitalist order, yes: no other text by Marx sets out deductions, distributive principles for two phases and the critique of equal right in connected form. The qualification is genre. The notes are a sketch provoked by a party programme, concrete about arithmetic and standards, silent about institutions, and they should be read beside the writings on the Paris Commune for the political form Marx admired and beside Capital for the analysis the sketch presupposes. Used alone, the notes give the fullest short account available; used as a complete theory of a future society, they are asked to carry more than their author put in them.

### Q: How long is the Critique of the Gotha Programme?

Short. In standard editions the notes run to roughly twenty or thirty pages, depending on format and editorial apparatus: a long pamphlet rather than a book, readable carefully in a single evening. The brevity is part of the text's history and influence. Because generations of students could be assigned the whole document, its arguments entered the tradition at unusual depth, while its most famous sentences detached themselves and circulated as slogans. Readers who allow an evening for the text and a second evening for the programme clauses it answers will know it better than most of the polemics written about it suggest.

### Q: How do the deductions from the social product work in the lower phase?

Before any individual receives a share, the community deducts from the total product what continuation requires: replacing consumed means of production, expanding capacity, insuring against disruption, paying for administration, funding services consumed in common such as schools and health provision, and maintaining members unable to work. Only the residue enters individual distribution, where each producer's labour, already net of the labour owed to the common funds, is certified and exchanged for consumption goods of equal labour value. The deductions are thus not a tax on distribution but its precondition, and their sizes are social decisions governed partly by technical calculation and partly by collective choice.

### Q: What should a student separate in Marx's attack on equal right?

Three things that weaker answers merge. Separate the standard from its application: Marx grants that payment by labour contribution is the only workable standard for the lower phase, and criticises not its administrators but its form. Separate form from content: the content is new, since nobody appropriates another's labour, while the form, an equal measure for unequal persons, is inherited from commodity exchange and bourgeois law. Separate defect from scandal: the resulting inequality is presented as a birthmark to be outgrown as conditions change, not as evidence of betrayal. An essay that keeps the three separations visible can discuss the argument; one that merges them can only applaud or denounce it.

### Q: Why did Marx refuse the phrase fair distribution?

Because the phrase locates the problem in the wrong place and borrows the opponent's standard. Every mode of production generates the distribution its relations produce, and its beneficiaries call that distribution fair; demanding fairness without changing who owns and controls the conditions of production therefore demands either nothing or everything while naming neither. Marx argues that the distribution of consumption goods follows from the distribution of the conditions of production themselves, so a serious programme addresses ownership and lets distributive patterns follow. The refusal is analytical before it is rhetorical: slogans that cannot survive arithmetic, as the undiminished proceeds could not, commit a movement to promises its own success would expose.

### Q: How does the two phases table help an essay on Marx's vision?

It forces the contrasts an essay needs into a single view. Set the phases side by side under distributive principle, and the movement from measurement to need becomes the essay's spine. Set them under bourgeois right, and the argument about equal standards supplies the analysis. Set them under conditions, and the essay gains its evaluation, since the higher phase can be discussed as a conditional construction rather than praised or mocked as a prediction. The table also exposes the text's silences, above all the state, which an honest essay should register. Used this way, a summary device becomes an argument plan, and the essay writes itself from the columns.

### Q: What did Lenin take from the Gotha Critique for his own book?

State and Revolution, written in 1917, takes the notes as its central authority on the post capitalist order. Lenin quotes the deductions and the two phases at length, adopts the transitional state sentence as the core of his doctrine of the state, and draws from the lower phase an emphasis on accounting and control as the practical essence of the transition. His largest addition is terminological: he renames the lower phase socialism and presents the renaming as a clarification, thereby fixing the two stage vocabulary the later tradition inherited. He also systematises, turning a sketch written against a programme into a doctrine of stages, a transformation readers should notice even when they admire the result.
