Louis Althusser was a French philosopher who spent the nineteen sixties and seventies arguing that Karl Marx should be read as the founder of a science, not as a philosopher of human nature whose early writings on alienation held the key to everything he wrote afterwards. He taught for over three decades at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, the small institution in the rue d'Ulm that trains much of the French intellectual elite, and from a seminar room there he rebuilt the vocabulary in which Marxism was discussed across several continents. He is best known for four things: the claim that Marx's work contains an epistemological break between an ideological youth and a scientific maturity, the concept of overdetermination, the idea of structural causality, and an account of ideology as a set of material practices, housed in institutions, that produce the very subjects who live inside them. Each of those ideas is treated at length in its own article in this series; this profile exists to hold the man, the books, and the arguments in one place, and to say how they fit together.

A profile of this philosopher has to solve a problem that profiles of most thinkers do not present in the same form. The documentary record of the life contains, alongside the teaching and the books, a crime: in November of his sixty-second year he killed his wife and was judged incapable of standing trial for what he had done. Coverage of Althusser tends to fall into two failures, one of omission and one of appetite. The first kind of account gives the concepts and passes over the killing in silence, as though a reference work could protect its subject by editing his biography. The second leads with the killing and lets it colour every page of the philosophy, as though an act could serve as an argument against a set of propositions. This profile refuses both. It states the facts of the life, including that one, plainly and once, in their chronological place. It states the arguments at the length they deserve and judges them by the standards arguments are judged by. It draws no line of causation in either direction between the two orders of fact, because no evidence supports such a line, and it asks the reader to hold the same discipline.

## The shape of Althusser's contribution

Althusser described his own work as an intervention rather than a system, and the description is accurate about the form. He wrote no single large treatise that sets out a philosophy from first principles to conclusions. What he produced instead was a series of essays, seminar volumes, polemics, and corrections, each one aimed at a specific obstacle in the way Marxism was being read at the moment he wrote it. The essays of the early nineteen sixties were aimed at the humanist reading of Marx that had become dominant after the publication of the early manuscripts. The seminar on Capital was aimed at economists and philosophers who read Marx's book as a work of classical political economy with the signs reversed. The essay on ideology was aimed at every account that treated ideas as a mist floating above the real institutions of a society. To follow the contribution, the reader does better to follow the obstacles in order than to look for a doctrine, because the doctrine, such as it is, is the residue left when the obstacles have been cleared.

### What did Althusser contribute to Marxist theory?

He argued that Marx's mature work broke decisively with the humanism of the early writings, supplied the concept of overdetermination for contradictions that never act alone, replaced the expressive whole with structural causality, and recast ideology as a material practice, working through institutions and rituals, that forms individuals into subjects.

That answer compresses four contributions, and the compression hides the feature of the work that this profile takes as its organising fact. Every one of the principal concepts came from outside Marxism. The break was taken from the philosophy of science. Overdetermination was taken from psychoanalysis. Structural causality was taken from Spinoza. The theory of ideology was assembled from a remark of Pascal, from the structural linguistics that treated language as a system without a speaking subject at its centre, and from Freud as filtered through Lacan's account of how a subject is formed. Althusser never disguised the borrowings; he announced them, named his creditors, and built the framework in full view of where the parts had come from. The profile calls this the borrowed apparatus, and it treats the borrowing as the key both to what the framework can do and to the most serious charge brought against it, which is that a Marxism assembled from so many foreign parts has stopped being a Marxism at all. Both halves of that judgement are developed in what follows: the power first, in the sections on each concept, and the charge at full strength in the sections on the critics and on the objection that the whole construction cannot explain change.

One question of method belongs at the front, because readers reasonably ask how a single article can contain a philosophy and a killing without letting either interpret the other. The answer is a separation of accounts. The theory is reported from the texts: from what the essays claim, from the arguments they give, from the revisions their author published, and from the criticisms of identifiable opponents who read them and replied. The record of the life is reported from the documented facts: dates, posts held, publications, and the judicial outcome recorded after the death of Hélène Rytmann. Neither account is allowed to supply evidence for the other. Where the philosophy is weak, it is shown to be weak on philosophical grounds, in the words of critics who made the case. Where the life contains a fact that reflects badly on the man, the fact stands in the record and is not converted into a verdict on the concepts. Readers who finish the profile will know what he argued, what happened, and why the two kinds of knowledge must be kept from doing each other's work. The tradition within which the intervention was made, and the company of Western Marxism around him, are mapped as a whole in the series' [western Marxism complete guide](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/), to which this profile is the companion for its most difficult figure.

## A life in outline: Algeria, Lyon, and the camp

Louis Althusser was born in October 1918 in Birmendreïs, a suburb of Algiers, where his father worked as a bank employee. The family was of Alsatian origin, settled in Algeria, and observant in the Catholic faith that structured the household's calendar and its moral expectations. He spent his childhood between Algiers and Marseille, in the respectable, anxious world of the French colonial lower middle class, a world of examinations, savings, and respectability that he would later describe with an anthropologist's distance. The decisive early setting was the lycée in Lyon, where the family had moved and where a philosophy teacher named Jean Guitton took an interest in the gifted pupil. Guitton, a Catholic philosopher of considerable reputation, gave the young Althusser his first serious formation: a training in the classical texts, in the discipline of exposition, and in the idea that philosophy was a vocation rather than a subject. The debt was acknowledged for the rest of Althusser's life, across every difference that later opened between the teacher's faith and the pupil's communism.

In 1939 he passed the entrance examination for the École Normale Supérieure, the summit of the French educational system, and the achievement was immediately suspended by the war. He was mobilised, captured in the collapse of 1940, and spent five years in a prisoner-of-war camp at Schleswig-Holstein in northern Germany. The camp years are the biographical fact he returned to most often in his own retrospective accounts, and they can be reported without ornament. He worked, he read what could be obtained, he took part in the camp's internal intellectual life, and he experienced, inside the wire, a form of collective existence stripped of property and rank that he later contrasted with the competitive individualism of the world outside. What the experience made of him is a matter for his memoir rather than for assertion here; what is documented is that he emerged in 1945 with his place at the school still open, his faith intact but under strain, and a determination to take up the postponed life at full intensity.

The postponed life began in the Paris of the Liberation, an intellectual capital working at fever pitch. Althusser entered the École Normale Supérieure in 1945, wrote his diploma dissertation on the concept of content in Hegel under the direction of Gaston Bachelard, and passed the agrégation in philosophy in 1948, the competitive examination that licenses a teacher of philosophy in France. In the same year he joined the French Communist Party. The two decisions of 1948, the post and the party card, fixed the institutional frame of everything that followed. He was appointed to a teaching position at the school itself, as agrégé-répétiteur, the tutor known in the school's slang as the caïman, and as secretary of the school he acquired administrative weight beyond his years. He would hold that position, with interruptions for illness, for over three decades. The philosopher who wrote about apparatuses spent his entire career inside two of them: the party and the school. That is a fact of the record, and this profile records it without treating it as an explanation of the theory, since many men held the same two memberships and wrote nothing resembling his work.

The religious formation did not vanish at the party's door. Through the late nineteen forties and early fifties Althusser moved in Catholic intellectual circles, wrote on the young Marx for Catholic audiences, and took part in the period's intense argument about whether Marxism and Christianity could recognise anything in each other. The argument matters to the profile for a documentary reason: the humanist reading of Marx, against which his mature work is one long polemic, was being built in those years partly by Catholic scholars and theologians who found in the early manuscripts a Marx they could answer, and partly by communist intellectuals who found in them a Marx they could use against the frozen dialectical materialism of the textbooks. Althusser knew that reading from the inside, had practised a version of it, and broke with it in full knowledge of its attractions. His later insistence that the early Marx was a Feuerbachian, writing inside a problematic of essence and alienation that the mature Marx abandoned, was written by a man who had once found that Marx persuasive. The biography registers the fact because it explains the precision of the polemic: he was correcting a position he had held, and he knew exactly where its joints were.

A second fact of the record belongs in this outline because it runs through the whole adult life and because later sections will not return to it. From his twenties onward Althusser suffered from severe recurrent depression. He was hospitalised repeatedly, treated with the methods available in each decade, and absent from his teaching for long stretches in consequence. Colleagues and students organised the seminar's continuity around the absences as a matter of routine. The profile states this as it states the camp and the party card: as a documented condition of the life, relevant to the chronology of the work, since books appeared in bursts separated by silences that the illness partly explains at the level of the calendar. It is not used anywhere in what follows as a key to the philosophy, and it is not used, when the record reaches its gravest entry, as either an explanation or an excuse. Those refusals are part of the same discipline announced at the start.

By the end of the nineteen fifties the elements of the intervention were assembled. The party, after the secret speech of 1956 and the invasion of Hungary, was losing its intellectual monopoly inside French Marxism, and the space for a new reading of Marx was opening. The young Marx industry was at full production. Structural linguistics had given the Parisian human sciences a new model of rigour, Freudian psychoanalysis was being rethought by Lacan, and the philosophy of science practised by Bachelard and his student Georges Canguilhem had shown how a discipline could police its own concepts. Althusser, installed at the centre of the institution that trained the trainers, began to publish the essays that made his name. They appeared first in the party's theoretical journal, a venue that gave them an official weight his critics would later hold against him, and they were collected in 1965 in the volume translated as For Marx. The same year saw the publication of the collective seminar volume on Capital. With those two books, the borrowed apparatus was on the table.

## The borrowed apparatus

The namable claim of this profile can now be stated in its full form. Althusser's Marxism was built almost entirely from components manufactured outside Marxism, and he said so. The concept of a break between an ideological and a scientific discourse came from Gaston Bachelard, whose history of the sciences described knowledge advancing by ruptures, by the deliberate abandonment of the questions a discipline had previously asked itself. The concept of a problematic, the hidden system that decides which questions a thinker can pose, came from the same French philosophy of science, refined by his own student Jacques Martin. Overdetermination was Sigmund Freud's word for the way a dream image is fixed by several chains of association at once. Structural causality was Baruch Spinoza's immanent cause, a cause that exists nowhere outside its effects. The account of ideology leaned on Blaise Pascal's observation that belief follows the body's rituals rather than preceding them, and the account of the subject leaned on Jacques Lacan's rereading of Freud. Even the method of reading, symptomatic reading, was modelled on Freud's interpretation of dreams and on Marx's own detection of a blind spot in Adam Smith. A reader who lists the sources has listed the framework.

The borrowing was not eclecticism in the lazy sense, the magpie accumulation of fashionable terms, and it is important to the fairness of this profile to say why his defenders make that distinction. Each import was assigned a specific repair job. Marxism as Althusser inherited it had, in his diagnosis, four weaknesses that its own resources had failed to fix. It had no defensible account of how Marx's own work developed, and so lurched between worshipping the whole corpus and explaining away the early texts. It had no concept of a complex social formation in which politics, law, and ideology act back on the economy that conditions them, and so oscillated between crude economism and voluntarism. It had no theory of ideology beyond the picture of false ideas imposed on passive minds, a picture that explained neither ideology's durability nor its grip on those who see through it. And it had no method of reading adequate to its founding text, so that Capital was mined for quotations by every faction. The imports map onto the weaknesses one by one. Bachelard supplied the developmental account. Freud and Mao together supplied the concept of a contradiction specified by its whole context. Spinoza supplied a causality equal to a structured whole. Pascal and Lacan supplied an ideology that works through practices and produces subjects rather than deceiving them. The apparatus was borrowed the way an engineer borrows components: because they performed functions the home workshop could not.

The charge follows from the same inventory, and it was pressed from the beginning by critics who shared his politics as well as by those who did not. A framework whose developmental concept is Bachelard's, whose causality is Spinoza's, whose subject is Lacan's, and whose method is Freud's may be powerful, the charge runs, but the power is not Marx's, and calling the result Marxism is an act of branding rather than of description. The charge gained force from the direction of the borrowings. None of the sources is a work of political economy. None comes from the labour movement or from the Marxist tradition's own debates about history and strategy. The framework's centre of gravity sits in the philosophy seminar, and its characteristic operations, policing concepts, sorting texts into science and ideology, detecting symptoms, are the operations of a certain kind of academic philosophy. When the self-criticism of the nineteen seventies conceded that the work had defined the frontier between science and ideology in purely theoretical terms, many readers took the concession as an admission that the charge had been right all along: the borrowed apparatus had produced a theoreticism, a Marxism of the lecture hall, answerable to the standards of French epistemology rather than to the movement it claimed to serve. Althusser's own later formula, that philosophy is in the last instance class struggle in the field of theory, can be read as his attempt to answer the charge by relocating the borrowed machinery inside the struggle it was meant to serve. Whether the relocation succeeds is one of the questions the critics' sections below keep open.

There is a further subtlety that any honest account of the apparatus has to register, because it complicates both the defence and the charge. The borrowings changed their meaning in transit. Bachelard's break separated mature sciences from their prescientific pasts; applied to a single author's corpus, it became a tool for periodising a life, a use Bachelard never made of it. Freud's overdetermination described the formation of dream images and symptoms; applied to revolutions, it became a way of refusing every single-cause history. Spinoza's immanent cause described God's relation to the world; secularised, it became the causality of a social structure. Lacan's subject, formed in the mirror stage and in language, became the subject of ideology, hailed into place by institutions. In each case the concept arrived with its source's problems left behind and acquired new ones from its destination. Critics who call the apparatus eclectic and defenders who call it creative are often describing the same operation from different sides: the violent repurposing of concepts, which is either invention or misuse according to the standards one brings. This profile's practice is to report each concept in the form Althusser gave it, to name the source he took it from, and to let the sections on criticism record the disputes about whether the repurposing was legitimate. The table that follows is the map of that practice.

## The break: two Marxes

The intervention began as an act of periodisation. In the essays collected in For Marx, Althusser proposed that the work of Marx divides into four stretches: the early writings of a young journalist and philosopher, saturated with the vocabulary of essence, alienation, and man's self-realisation; the works of 1845, in which that vocabulary is broken; a transitional period of political writing and economic study; and the mature work, from the manuscripts of 1857 onward, in which the concepts of a new science of history, mode of production, forces and relations of production, surplus value, are forged and put to work. The hinge was 1845, the year of the Theses on Feuerbach and of The German Ideology, the manuscript Marx and Engels left, in their own phrase, to the gnawing criticism of the mice. Before the hinge, on this account, Marx wrote inside someone else's problematic: Feuerbach's philosophical anthropology, in which history is the story of an essence alienated from itself and destined to return. After the hinge, he wrote inside a problematic of his own construction, in which men appear not as the expression of an essence but as the occupants of places in a structure of production. The full comparison of the two Marxes belongs to the series article on [the young Marx against the mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/); the profile's concern is with what the claim did and why it was fought over.

### Why does the break fall where it falls?

The break is dated to the works in which Marx stops explaining history by an essence of man and starts explaining it by structures of production. The Theses on Feuerbach reject the abstract individual, and the manuscript written with Engels builds the first sketch of modes of production, so the rupture is placed where the concepts change.

The placing of the rupture was a polemical act as much as a scholarly one, and its targets explain its precision. The humanist Marx, the philosopher of alienation whose early manuscripts were read as the emotional and moral centre of the whole work, had become in the nineteen fifties a shared property: of social democrats arguing that Marxism was a humanism that Stalinism had betrayed, of Catholic scholars arguing that the young Marx could be answered by a richer account of man, of existentialists fusing Marx with a philosophy of freedom, and of communist reformers arguing, after the secret speech, that the doctrine's true face was the young humanist and that the crimes had been committed in the name of the economist. To date the break at 1845 and to classify the humanist texts as pre-Marxist was to cut every one of those arguments at the root. If the manuscripts of 1844 belong to Marx's ideological youth, then a politics founded on them is founded on the thought Marx himself abandoned, and the mature science stands clear of the uses made of the early pathos. Defenders of the humanist reading replied that the periodisation was a scholastic device, that themes of alienation persist into Capital itself, in the analysis of commodity fetishism above all, and that a break which requires its holder to discount the continuities is a break imposed on the texts rather than found in them. The dispute is recorded here in both its forms because it set the pattern for all the later ones: a precise textual claim, immediately legible as a political act, answered by a counter-claim about the texts.

Two qualifications, both from Althusser's own hand, prevent the break from hardening into the caricature that textbooks sometimes teach. The first is that a break is not an event but a prolonged labour. He spoke of Marx's long struggle to free himself from the problematic he had broken with, and he treated even the mature work as carrying residues: the Hegelian turns of phrase in Capital, the flirtation, as he called it in a famous aside, with the dialectic's own mystified form. The break inaugurates a science; it does not deliver one in finished purity, and the science remains, like every science on Bachelard's model, in permanent construction. The second qualification cuts the other way and is the more damaging one, since it was extracted by his critics and later conceded in part by Althusser himself. The rhetoric of the break divided the world of discourse into science and ideology with a confidence that the actual history of Marx's writing keeps refusing: the same pages of the Grundrisse can be quoted for the science and for the residues, and the sorting begins to look like a decision dressed as a discovery. The self-criticism examined below took up exactly this point. What survives both qualifications is the working core of the claim, which even many opponents adopted as a research discipline: that the concepts of 1844 and the concepts of 1867 are not interchangeable, that quoting across the divide as though Marx held one doctrine all his life is an error, and that any use of the early Marx to interpret the late one has to argue the continuity rather than assume it.

## For Marx, essay by essay

Because the intervention arrived as a volume of essays rather than as a treatise, the profile pauses to walk the volume, since each essay carries one part of the apparatus and the whole is otherwise easy to mistake for a mood. The opening essay, on the young Marx, performs the periodisation described above and adds the notion that would govern the school's historical work: the unevenness of a thinker's development, the way a corpus can be advanced in its politics and retarded in its concepts, or the reverse, so that no simple story of maturation fits it. The essay on contradiction and overdetermination, treated in its own section below, supplies the politics. The long central essay, on the materialist dialectic, supplies the epistemology, and it is the most Bachelardian text he ever wrote: knowledge is there described as a production, in which a theoretical practice works on raw materials, concepts and representations inherited from earlier practice and from ideology, by means of a theoretical apparatus, and produces as its result a knowledge, in a schema of three generalities that schoolmen of the seminar could recite and that critics found, in about equal measure, clarifying and scholastic. The essay on Marxism and humanism supplies the polemic, arguing that the vogue of the young Marx was an ideological phenomenon to be explained rather than a truth to be adopted. And the volume closes with pieces that show the range of the practice, including an essay on the theatre of Brecht and the Milan company of the Piccolo Teatro, in which the symptomatic method is applied to a play: the drama's displaced structure, its refusal to let the audience identify and discharge its pity, is read as a materialist aesthetic in action, a theatre that produces the spectator's critical distance instead of consuming it.

The theatre essay is worth the profile's attention beyond its charm, because it is the one place in the early work where the apparatus is turned on an artwork rather than on a doctrine, and it displays the method's genuine delicacy. The play under discussion, a chronicle of Milanese poor life, is not praised for its message, which would be the ideological reading, nor for its form in abstraction, but for the internal distance it builds between its own dramatic machinery and the audience's habitual self-recognition: the spectators are prevented from completing the play inside their own feelings and are sent out with the structure visible. Whatever a reader thinks of the verdict, the procedure is neither crude nor contemptuous of its object, and it stands in the record against the caricature of the school as a factory of denunciations. The same volume's essay on the young Marx contains the sentence, often quoted by the school's enemies and rarely in its context, that Marx's early works are to be read as one reads a night in which all cows are black when the concepts have not yet separated; the violence of the imagery was part of the period's polemical style, and its author, who had learned exposition from a Catholic philosopher of exquisite courtesy, chose the violence deliberately, a fact about the rhetoric of the intervention that assessments of its tone should register. For Marx sold, was translated, was denounced and defended, and above all was assigned: within five years it was the book through which a student in Paris, Turin, Buenos Aires, or London encountered the possibility that Marxism had a philosophy at all, as distinct from a catechism or a mood. The possibility was the intervention. Everything else in the career is commentary on it.

## The problematic and the symptomatic reading

Behind the break stood a pair of tools, one for describing how a body of thought is organised and one for reading it. The problematic is the more fundamental. Borrowed from the philosophy of science and credited by Althusser to his student Jacques Martin, the term names the structured system of concepts and questions that underlies everything a thinker writes: not the thinker's opinions, and not his worldview, but the underlying field that determines which questions can be posed, which objects can be seen, and which problems are literally unthinkable inside the system because the concepts needed to state them do not exist there. A problematic is not chosen and is rarely visible to its holder. It is detected by its effects, above all by the pattern of a text's insights and blindnesses, which arrive together, as products of the same structure. The young Marx, on this analysis, could see Feuerbach's weaknesses with perfect clarity and could not see the question of the mode of production, because his problematic, the anthropology of essence and alienation, had no place where that question could be put.

### Where did the concept of the problematic come from?

It came from the French philosophy of science, from the tradition of Bachelard, for whom a science is defined by the problems it can formulate and the obstacles it has overcome, and it reached its Marxist form through Jacques Martin, Althusser's student, whose work on the concept its teacher publicly credited in the essays of the period.

Symptomatic reading is the problematic put to work as a method, and it is the most imitated and least understood of the tools in this profile. Its model is double. Freud, interpreting a dream, treated its manifest story as the distorted product of latent thoughts, and treated the distortions themselves, the condensations, displacements, and silences, as the evidence. Marx, reading Adam Smith, noticed that Smith's text both produces and misses the concept of labour power: Smith's value theory stumbles at the value of labour, answers a question he has not posed, and the stumble, the oversight, is not carelessness but the signature of classical political economy's problematic, which could register the answer in its formulas while lacking the concept that made the answer intelligible. A symptomatic reading therefore attends to a text's lapses with the same seriousness as its assertions. It asks what the text's problematic obliged it to overlook, and it treats the pattern of sight and blindness as a single fact to be explained. Applied to Marx himself, in the seminar on Capital, the method claimed to detect the points where Marx saw what his inheritance would not let him say, and it licensed the seminar's most ambitious promise: to produce, by reading, the theory that the text practised but never stated.

### How does a symptomatic reading find what a text cannot say?

It watches for the places where a text answers a question it never asked, falters over a term it uses without defining, or falls silent at the exact point its argument requires a concept. Those lapses are read as symptoms: effects of the underlying problematic, which permitted the answer to appear while withholding the concept that would explain it.

The method's dangers were visible from the start and were named by sympathetic readers as well as enemies. A reading that treats disagreement as blindness has purchased its explanatory power at a price: the text under examination can never win. If Marx's pages confirm the interpretation, they confirm it; if they resist it, the resistance is a symptom, and it confirms it too. Critics compared the procedure to the hermeneutics of suspicion at its least falsifiable, and the comparison has some force, since the symptomatic reader stands to the text as analyst to patient, a position of epistemic privilege that the reader's own problematic conveniently escapes. Althusser's partial defence was built into the method itself: the symptomatic reading of Marx was to be conducted from the standpoint of the science Marx founded, as a physicist reads a predecessor's confused manuscript with the finished theory in hand, and the privilege claimed was the privilege of the later science over its own prehistory. The defence relocates the question rather than settling it, since the status of that science is precisely what the critics disputed. What can be said without taking sides is that the tool changed scholarly practice beyond its home: the discipline of asking what a text cannot think, and of treating its silences as structured rather than accidental, passed into literary studies, into the history of science, and into the ordinary equipment of criticism, carried there by readers many of whom had no commitment to the doctrine the tool was built to defend.

## Overdetermination and the weakest link

The essay on contradiction and overdetermination, published in the party's journal in 1962 and reprinted in For Marx, is the point where the borrowed apparatus first does political work, and it remains the most read of the early essays. Its starting point is an embarrassment in the inherited doctrine. The classical model of contradiction, taken from Hegel and inverted by Marx, pictured a society's contradictions as the expression of one simple antagonism, owners against non-owners, whose maturation would in due course transform the whole. The history of the twentieth century refused to cooperate with the picture. Revolution had succeeded in Russia, the least developed of the great capitalist powers, and failed in Germany, the most developed, with the strongest workers' movement in the world. Either the model was wrong, or the timing of history was a scandal. Althusser's answer was to borrow Freud's term for the way a dream image is determined by many chains of association converging on it, and to declare that a social contradiction is never found in the pure state the model requires. Every contradiction is overdetermined: specified, displaced, condensed by the whole uneven formation in which it sits, by the survival of older modes inside the dominant one, by the world market, by the state, by the ideological forms in which classes recognise or misrecognise themselves. The concept receives its full treatment in the series article on [overdetermination explained](/2016/01/01/overdetermination-explained/), and the profile confines itself to what the concept did to the theory of revolution.

### Why is a contradiction never found alone?

Because every contradiction exists inside a whole that assigns it force and moment. A wage dispute inside an empire, a crisis, and a collapsing state is not the same contradiction in different scenery; the relations around it enter into what it is and what it can do, which is why one antagonism can smoulder for decades and another ignite.

Russia in 1917 was the essay's worked example, inherited from Lenin's own practice. Lenin had called imperialist Russia the weakest link in the chain, and Althusser read the phrase as a theory in miniature: the revolution occurred not where the central contradiction was most mature but where contradictions of every kind, feudal survivals, a peasant war, a collapsing autocracy, a mutinous army, a world war, national grievances at the empire's edges, had accumulated and fused into a ruptural unity. Mao's writings on contradiction supplied the complementary distinctions, between principal and secondary contradictions and between the principal and secondary aspects within one contradiction, which allowed the analysis to keep a hierarchy inside the complexity rather than dissolving into a list of factors. The economy was not dismissed in this scheme. It remained determinant in the last instance, assigning to each level of the formation its degree of effectivity, its weight in the whole. But the famous sentence of the essay insists that the last instance is a horizon rather than an appointment: the lonely hour of the last instance never comes, because determination always arrives mediated by the superstructures that the economy itself has made indispensable. Economism, the doctrine that the economy acts alone and the rest is decoration, is thereby refused, and so is its mirror image, the voluntarism for which politics can do anything. What replaces both is a demand for concrete analysis, a phrase the essay revived from Lenin: there is no contradiction in general, only the specified contradiction of a conjuncture, and the science of history is the discipline of specifying it.

The concept's reception split along a line that recurs throughout this profile. To readers formed by the failures of a mechanical Marxism, overdetermination was a liberation, the licence to study the actual complexity of formations without heresy. To readers of a more austere temper, it was a solvent: a contradiction that can be displaced, condensed, and indefinitely specified can explain any outcome after the fact and predict none before it, and a theory that accommodates every result has quietly stopped being a theory. The dispute matters to the profile because both sides can quote the essay. It does contain a hierarchy, with determination in the last instance held in reserve. It also contains no procedure for weighing the instances against one another before the event, and the concrete analyses it inspired were, in the hands of followers, sometimes essays in ingenuity rather than in explanation. Althusser himself treated the concept as a beginning rather than a possession, a direction for research programmes, in kinship with Mao's instruction to analyse the particularity of each contradiction. The programmes it licensed, in the sociology of education, in the study of ideology, in the analysis of social formations in the developing world, form part of the record assessed in the closing sections of this profile.

## The conjuncture: the unit of political analysis

One term recurs through every phase of the work and binds the phases together, and the profile pauses on it because readers meet it everywhere in the literature the framework spawned. The conjuncture is the specific, unrepeatable configuration of forces at a moment: the balance of classes and fractions, the state of the state, the international pressures, the ideological weather, the accidents of personnel and timing, all of it specified at the level of the concrete. Lenin had practised the analysis of the conjuncture under the name of the current moment, with the weakest link as its most famous product; Mao had theorised the hierarchy of contradictions that a conjuncture contains. Althusser's contribution was to give the practice its epistemology. If contradictions are overdetermined and levels develop in differential times, then the object of political knowledge can never be the general law of a mode of production; it is always this configuration, whose elements are known and whose combination is not deducible from any of them. The conjuncture is where the structure becomes an event. It is also where the framework's two halves, the science and the politics, are obliged to meet, since the analysis of a conjuncture is theoretical work with practical consequences, and its errors are paid for in the currency the party dealt in. The self-criticism's charge of theoreticism can be restated in these terms: the early work had theorised the conjuncture magnificently and analysed few, and the analyses that the school produced, of May 1968 above all, arrived with the air of verdicts delivered after the battle by the side that had not fought it. The late philosophy completes the term's trajectory. In the materialism of the encounter, the conjuncture loses its structural frame and becomes the whole of reality: there is nothing behind the configuration, no instance assigning the elements their places in advance, only the configuration and its unforeseeable issue. A reader tracking one word through the career has thus tracked the career itself, from the conjuncture as the structure's moment, through the conjuncture as the theory's embarrassment, to the conjuncture as the truth the structure had been concealing. Few concepts in the Marxist tradition carry so much of their author's biography inside their definitions, and the profile marks the fact as the kind of finding the symptomatic method exists to produce.

## Structural causality and the times of the levels

If overdetermination describes how contradictions behave inside a whole, structural causality describes what kind of cause the whole is. The concept was forged in the seminar on Capital and stated in Reading Capital, and it is the most technically demanding item in the table above. Althusser distinguished three models of causality available to the tradition. The first, which he called transitive or mechanistic, is the causality of the billiard table, descending from Descartes: one body acts on another from outside, cause and effect separable, the cause exhaustible in its effect. The second, expressive causality, is the causality of Leibniz and Hegel: a whole is an essence that expresses itself in each of its parts, so that every element of a society, its law, its art, its religion, its economy, is the phenomenal form of one inner principle, and the historian's task is to read the principle off any sufficiently examined fragment. Against both, and borrowing from Spinoza, he set an immanent causality: the structure is a cause that exists only in its effects, present in them the way Spinoza's God is present in nature, not above the whole, not behind the parts, but identical with the articulated system of their relations. Marx's mature work, on this reading, thinks with the third model while often writing with the vocabulary of the second, and the task of a philosophical reading is to extract the practised concept from the borrowed words.

### What does it mean for a cause to be immanent in its effects?

It means the structure cannot be found anywhere except in the relations it organises. A mode of production is not a thing behind the factory, the market, and the law court; it is nothing apart from those instances and their connections, yet it determines how each behaves, as a grammar determines sentences it never utters itself.

The companion doctrine concerned time. Expressive causality carries an expressive theory of history with it: if every part of a society expresses one essence, then all its levels share a single present, a historical time that can be cut at any point to reveal the whole in cross-section. Althusser and Balibar denied the premise. Each level of a formation, the economic, the political, the ideological, the scientific, has its own temporality, its own rhythm of development and its own delays and advances, and these times are not reducible to one another or to a common clock. There is no contemporaneity of the whole with itself, and the writing of history becomes the construction of the concepts of these differential times rather than the narration of a single story. The doctrine of differential temporality is the least borrowed and most austere of the framework's products, and it had a bracing effect on historical practice wherever it was absorbed: periodisations that had rested on the reigns of kings or the dates of constitutions could no longer be assumed to periodise anything else, and the historian owed an argument for every alignment of a political break with an economic one. It also attracted the charge, pressed most famously by the English historian examined below, that a theory which dissolves the common time of a society dissolves history itself, leaving the analyst with timeless structures and no way to say how one formation gives way to another. That charge belongs to the strongest objection this profile must state, and it is kept for the section built to hold it.

Between the two doctrines sits a notion of knowledge that completes the early system and explains its tone of austere confidence. If the structure is invisible in the way a grammar is invisible, then knowledge of it cannot be a matter of looking harder at the facts. Althusser distinguished the real object, the society as it exists independently of thought, from the object of knowledge, the concept of that society, produced by theoretical practice working on raw materials of concepts and representations he called Generalities. Empirical immediacy, the given fact, belonged to the raw materials, not to the result. This is the source of the framework's notorious indifference to what its critics called mere empiricism, and the source, equally, of its appeal to a generation trained in the exact sciences, for whom the idea that knowledge is produced rather than gathered was the common sense of their own disciplines. It is also the point where the borrowing from Bachelard does its deepest work, since the distinction between the real and the known, and the conception of science as production, are the epistemology of the French school transplanted entire into historical materialism. Whether the transplant took is the question that the self-criticism, a decade later, would answer more equivocally than the early essays ever allowed.

## Spinoza, the secret sharer

Of all the creditors in the table of concepts and sources, one is owed more than money, and the profile singles him out because Althusser did. Spinoza is the framework's secret sharer: the philosopher of the immanent cause, from whom structural causality is taken; the critic of Scripture who, in the treatise on theology and politics, first practised something like a symptomatic reading on a sacred text, asking not what the prophets meant but what their audiences could hear; the thinker of the affects and of the multitude, whose refusal of free will and of final causes made him, in the seminar's phrase, the first theoretician of a world without a subject's guarantee. Althusser's acknowledgements grew warmer as the career proceeded. The early writings cite Spinoza as a source among others, with Marx's own debt to him, through the critique of Hegel, held in reserve. The middle work leans on the Ethics' architecture at every load-bearing point: the critique of consciousness as the sun around which an imaginary world revolves, the doctrine that men are conscious of their desires and ignorant of the causes that determine them, which the ideology essay quietly converts into a theory of the subject. The late work enthrones him: the materialism of the encounter claims Spinoza for the underground current, as the philosopher who thought substance without teleology and nature without a legislator, and the reading has been taken up by the school's heirs, Balibar above all, whose own work on Spinoza and politics is among the most distinguished products of the entire enterprise. The profile notes the devotion for the light it throws backward on the borrowed apparatus. A borrowing can be opportunistic, and several of Althusser's were; the Spinoza borrowing was a formation, absorbed over forty years, argued with, and never discharged, and it explains the framework's peculiar flavour better than any other single fact: its combination of maximal determinism with a strange serenity, its contempt for the illusions of consciousness joined to an ethics of understanding them, its God-term replaced by a structure that, like Spinoza's nature, is everywhere because it is nowhere in particular. Critics who called the system a theology were, in this one respect, more right than they knew, since the system's God had been a philosopher's God all along, with the name filed off and the immanence kept.

## Ideology, apparatuses, and the making of subjects

The most consequential single text Althusser published is not one of the books of 1965 but an essay of 1970, on ideology and ideological state apparatuses, which appeared in the party's journal and circulated for years in photocopies and translations before reaching book form in English. Because the essay is treated in full in the series article on [ideology and ideological state apparatuses](/2012/03/01/althusser-ideology-state-apparatuses/), this profile gives its argument in outline and concentrates on its place in the life of the framework: it is the point where the borrowed apparatus stops policing other people's concepts and produces a doctrine of its own, the doctrine by which Althusser is known to the far larger public that has never opened For Marx.

The essay advances in theses. Ideology, in the sense the essay fixes, is not a system of false ideas and not a lie told by rulers. It is the representation of the imaginary relationship of individuals to their real conditions of existence: imaginary not in the sense of invented from nothing, but in the sense of lived at a distance, the way a reflection is lived, so that men act inside a picture of their world that they did not draw and cannot step outside by an effort of sincerity. Ideology has a material existence. It does not float above society in a realm of ideas; it exists in apparatuses, schools, churches, families, parties, media, and in the practices and rituals those apparatuses prescribe. The essay's most quoted borrowing is Pascal's rule for the sceptic who cannot believe: kneel down, move your lips in prayer, and you will believe. The ritual comes first and the belief is its effect, not its cause. From this follows the essay's central mechanism, interpellation. Ideology recruits individuals and transforms them into subjects by hailing them, the way a voice in the street calls out and the person who turns around, recognising that the call was meant for him, becomes in that recognition a subject: free, responsible, and subjected in a single gesture, since the French and English word carries both meanings. The theory of the subject is the Lacanian inheritance made social: the subject is not the origin of ideology but its product, constituted in the act of recognising itself in the call of an authority that was there before it.

### Why is ideology described as a practice rather than a set of ideas?

Because its grip does not depend on being believed as a doctrine. Ideology lives in timetables, examinations, ceremonies, forms of address, and routines of deference; a person may privately doubt every proposition an institution teaches and still be formed, daily, by performing its practices, which is why refuting the propositions leaves the formation intact.

The essay performed several services for the framework at once, and its extraordinary diffusion is explained by their combination. It gave Marxism a theory of the school, which the tradition had treated as a neutral instrument, and thereby armed a generation of research on education and reproduction. It gave the concept of the subject a precise mechanism at the moment when the human sciences were converging on the same problem from linguistics and psychoanalysis. It explained, as the false-consciousness model never could, why ideologies survive their exposure: a practice is not refuted by an argument. And it did all this in a text short enough to be taught in a week, which no other part of the apparatus can claim. The objections arrived with the diffusion. The functionalism of the scheme, in which every apparatus works to reproduce the relations of production, left no room for the failures, resistances, and contradictions that the earlier concept of overdetermination had been built to honour; Althusser appended a note conceding that the apparatuses are not only instruments but also the site and stake of class struggle, and the concession sits in the text like a patch on a garment cut too smoothly. The account of the subject, formed by recognition, struggled to explain the subject who recognises wrongly, refuses the call, or answers a different one. And the essay's scientific confidence raised the standing question of its own status: if all ideology interpellates, from what position does the essay speak? These disputes are the living continuation of the text, and the dedicated article pursues them. For the profile, the essay marks the summit of the framework's influence and the moment its audience changed: after 1970, Althusser was no longer only a philosopher's philosopher but a theorist of institutions, read wherever institutions were studied.

## The book on reproduction

The ideology essay of 1970 was an extract, and the fact changes how it should be read. In 1969 and 1970 Althusser drafted a long manuscript on the reproduction of the relations of production, an attempt to write, at last, the systematic book the essays had always deferred: an account of how a social formation secures, day after day and generation after generation, the conditions of its own continuation, the skills and submissions of its workers, the personnel of its state, the forms of its law and its ideology. The manuscript was not published in his lifetime; the famous essay was the portion of it he detached and issued in the party journal, where it circulated as a freestanding doctrine. The whole text appeared only after his death, in the nineteen nineties, and its publication altered the scholarship, since arguments that had been prosecuted for decades against the essay's compression could now be checked against the fuller exposition they had never seen. The profile records the bibliographical fact because it is a standing warning attached to this subject: the most influential text of the framework spent twenty-five years in the world as a fragment, and some part of its reception, the confidence of its theses, the smoothness its critics indict, belongs to the fragment's form rather than to the author's settled exposition.

The parent manuscript also clarifies the essay's most disputed pairing, the distinction between the repressive state apparatus and the ideological state apparatuses. In the full text the distinction is functional and explicitly provisional: the state apparatus of army, police, courts, and administration operates predominantly by violence and secondarily by ideology, while the ideological apparatuses, churches, schools, families, parties, unions, media, and cultural institutions, operate predominantly by ideology and secondarily by repression, the school expelling, the church excluding, the family disciplining. The point of the pairing was not taxonomy but a relocation of the political: the decisive reproduction of a modern order happens in the institutions that liberal common sense classifies as private, and the school, in the epoch of mass education, is the dominant one, delivering children sorted by destination and reconciled, in the main, to the sorting. Read with the parent text, the essay's notorious functionalism softens into something more provisional and more empirical, though it does not disappear; the question of how a formation's needs come to be met by its institutions, the question of who or what does the fitting, is one the manuscript poses more honestly than it answers. The assessment matters for the profile's balance, since the critics examined below were shooting, for a quarter of a century, at the extract. Their strongest points survive the fuller text; their weaker ones were aimed at a compression the author had not chosen as his final word, and the record of the dispute should be corrected accordingly, in both directions.

## Ideology in general, and why it has no outside

Two theses of the ideology essay are quoted less often than interpellation and explain more of its author's intentions, and the profile gives them a section because summaries that omit them leave the doctrine looking like a sociology of institutions when it was meant as something stranger. The first is that ideology in general has no history. Particular ideologies have histories, rise with classes and fall with them, but ideology as such, the structure by which individuals live their relation to their conditions in imaginary form, is coextensive with social life as such, in classless societies as in classed ones, because it answers to the constitution of subjects and not to the deception of populations. He drew the parallel explicitly: as Freud held the unconscious to be eternal, not in the sense of unchanging contents but in the sense of a structure outside time, so ideology in general is an omnipresent form, and a communist society, on this doctrine, will have its ideology as surely as it will have its dreams. The thesis scandalised readers who expected Marxism to promise a world beyond ideology, and it was meant to: the promise of a transparent society, in which men relate to their conditions without imaginary mediation, was for Althusser itself an ideological promise, the humanist dream in its purest form. The second thesis follows. If ideology is the medium in which subjects are constituted, then there is no outside of ideology available to the individuals who live in it, and the familiar posture of the critic, standing clear of the illusions he exposes, is a fiction; the sciences carve out their objects by a break with the ideological field, but the scientist as a person returns to that field every evening, and the break is a labour inside knowledge, not an emigration of the soul. Together the two theses convert ideology from an accusation into a condition, and they explain the essay's odd serenity: it does not denounce its object. They also state, in advance, the deepest difficulty of the whole framework, the one the critics' sections prosecute: a doctrine that assigns every subject to ideology and reserves the break to a theoretical practice has built the hierarchy of the seminar into the structure of the world, and whether that is a discovery or a self-portrait is the question on which the assessment of the man finally turns.

## Theoretical anti-humanism, stated exactly

No phrase in the framework caused more misunderstanding per word than theoretical anti-humanism, and the misunderstanding was not entirely the readers' fault, since the phrase was coined for provocation and inherits the violence of its coinage. It arose inside the argument about the break. If the mature Marx abandoned the problematic of the young Marx, then he abandoned its central category: man, the essence that alienates itself in history and recovers itself at history's end. A science of history, Althusser argued, cannot be founded on that category, because an essence that explains everything by expressing itself explains nothing; the concepts of the science are mode of production, productive forces, relations of production, and in those concepts the word man does no work. To say this is to be a theoretical anti-humanist: to deny that humanism, the doctrine that history is the self-realisation of an essence of man, can serve as the theory of history. The qualifier is the doctrine. He insisted, in the essay on Marxism and humanism and repeatedly afterwards, that the denial was theoretical and not practical or moral: humanism as an ideology has real functions in the world, in struggles against oppression and indignity, and the theoretical judgement that it is not a science implies nothing about its value as a banner. The distinction was ignored by many readers in both directions, by enthusiasts who took anti-humanism as a licence for contempt toward persons, and by opponents who took it as a confession of contempt. The profile records the correction because the misreading became part of the record: the slogan travelled farther than the qualifier, and the damage to reputations, his included, was done by the slogan.

The debate that the phrase ignited was the period's central argument inside Marxism, and it is reconstructed as a debate in the series article on [humanist against structuralist Marxism](/2016/02/15/humanist-vs-structuralist-marxism/). In France the opposing position had an institutional address: Roger Garaudy, for years the party's most visible philosopher, defended a humanist Marx open to dialogue with Christians and existentialists, and the party's shifting line between the two men tracked its own hesitations in the years after the secret speech. In Britain the humanist cause was carried by socialist intellectuals for whom the young Marx grounded a moral politics, and in the socialist humanism of the historian whose attack is examined below. Althusser's counter-attack had a characteristic form: he treated the word humanism as a symptom. Its vogue, he argued, was itself an ideological fact, the form in which the failures and crimes of actually existing socialism were being processed by intellectuals who could neither defend the record nor abandon the hope, and who reached for the young Marx as for a certificate that the hope had once been pure. Whether or not that diagnosis persuaded, it set the terms on which the argument was conducted for a decade: not whether Marxism was humane, which nobody in the dispute denied wanting, but whether humane was a theoretical predicate at all. The argument's later historians have usually awarded the humanists the rhetoric and Althusser the concepts, since the field's working vocabulary, ideology, apparatus, interpellation, overdetermination, is his, while its moral self-description remained theirs. The profile notes the split verdict because it previews the pattern of his reputation in full: the apparatus conquered the vocabulary of disciplines that declined the doctrine it was built to serve.

## The Christian interlocutors

The humanist Marx had been built by many hands, but the most scholarly of them belonged to theologians, and the profile pauses on the dialogue because it formed the young Althusser before it opposed the mature one. The Jesuit Jean-Yves Calvez had published, in the nineteen fifties, a massive and respectful study of Marx's thought that took the early writings with full seriousness and found in them a doctrine of man that Christianity could engage as an equal. A generation of Catholic intellectuals in France worked the same seam: the young Marx as a witness, against the materialism of the textbooks, to the irreducibility of the human, and the dialogue between Christians and Marxists as the period's appointed conversation. Althusser had moved in that world, had written for its journals, and knew its arguments from the side of sympathy. When the break thesis classified the whole construction as ideology, the classification fell on his own teachers and friends, and the essays' peculiar combination of rigour and coldness is partly explained by the intimacy of the target: he was not describing an error he had observed but one he had inhabited, and the doctrine of the problematic, which explains how intelligent men are blinded by the questions their formation permits, was in this application autobiography generalised into method. The theologians replied with dignity and with scholarship, and the argument between the two readings of the young Marx, the anthropological and the structural, became one of the set pieces of the period's intellectual life, conducted in journals and conference halls on both sides of the confessional line. Its long-term result is a matter of record: the dialogue of Christians and Marxists faded with the century's politics, but the scholarly question it had posed, what the manuscripts of 1844 are and how they stand to Capital, remained, and it is Althusser's periodisation, not his opponents', that the scholarship still has to argue with. A man is sometimes best measured by the quality of the disagreement he leaves behind, and by that measure the quarrel with the theologians, courteous, documented, and unresolved, ranks among his most productive works.

## The rival: Sartre and the philosophy of praxis

No intervention is intelligible without its rival, and the rival in Paris was Jean-Paul Sartre. Sartre's Critique of Dialectical Reason, published in 1960 with its methodological preface on the questions of method, was the reigning attempt to refound Marxism as a philosophy of human praxis: history as the totalisation of individual projects, scarcity as the material frame of alienation, the group and the fused crowd as the forms in which men recover their sovereignty from the practico-inert matter that steals it. Sartre declared Marxism the unsurpassable philosophy of the age and existentialism a mere enclave within it, a compliment that functioned as an annexation. Althusser's entire construction can be read as the refusal of that annexation. Where Sartre founded history on the praxis of a subject, even a collective one, Althusser founded it on structures that have no subject. Where Sartre's Marx remained the philosopher of man's self-making from the manuscripts to Capital, Althusser's break cut the corpus in two and assigned the philosophy of self-making to the ideological youth. The two men never conducted the debate in person; the polemic ran through their schools, their journals, and their students, and it was unequal in an instructive way, since Sartre commanded the public stage and Althusser commanded the institution that trained the stage's next occupants. By the end of the decade the younger generation had chosen, and the choice was registered in the vocabulary of the time: structure displaced praxis, the subject became a suspect word, and Sartre, who lived to see the displacement, treated it with a mixture of contempt and curiosity that his interviews preserve.

The profile records the rivalry for two reasons. The first is accuracy of context: a reader who does not know that the break was aimed at the existential Marxism of the Critique as much as at the party's catechism will misjudge the essays' temperature, which is the temperature of a fight for the succession to Marx in the capital of Marxist philosophy. The second is that the rivalry states, in biographical form, the theoretical alternative that the strongest objection below generalises. Sartre's Marxism has a subject and no structure worth the name; Althusser's has a structure and no subject; the subsequent history of the tradition can be written as the series of attempts, none conclusive, to have both. The seminar's own heirs divided on the point, and the division is one measure of the master's failure and success: he made the antinomy unavoidable, which is what strong interventions do, and he left it exactly where he found it, sharpened. The full treatment of the existential Marxism belongs to this series' profile of Sartre himself; here it is enough that the reader see the silhouette against which the borrowed apparatus was assembled, part by part, as an anti-Sartre that never speaks its target's name in the founding texts, preferring to let the concepts do the fighting. The preference was methodological before it was tactical. A symptomatic reading does not refute a rival; it diagnoses the problematic that obliges the rival to ask his questions, and Sartre's problematic, the philosophy of consciousness and its projects, was for Althusser the ideology of the age wearing the dress of its critic.

## A seminar becomes a book: Reading Capital

The second book of 1965 was not written in the ordinary sense. In the first months of that year Althusser conducted a seminar at the school devoted to a collective reading of Marx's Capital, with a small team of his students and younger colleagues, and the volume that appeared under the title Reading Capital was assembled from the seminar's working papers: his own long essays on the object of Capital and on Marx's relation to Hegel, Étienne Balibar's essay on the basic concepts of historical materialism, and contributions from Jacques Rancière, Pierre Macherey, and Roger Establet. The French edition ran to two volumes; the later abridged edition, from which the English translation was made, dropped Rancière's and Macherey's contributions, an editorial fact that quietly rewrote the book's balance for the Anglophone world. The collective form was itself a thesis. A symptomatic reading of a text as dense as Capital was presented as a labour exceeding any individual, and the seminar enacted, in its division of tasks and its shared problematic, the theory of intellectual production that the framework preached: concepts are not the property of geniuses but the product of a practice with its own instruments and its own discipline.

### Why did the reading of Capital become a collective project at the school?

Because the method it demonstrated could not be exhibited by one reader. A symptomatic reading required the whole text held in view at once, its concepts inventoried, its lapses located, its borrowings from classical economy traced, and the seminar divided that labour among five participants whose papers were written to interlock under a shared discipline.

The seminar's most durable products came from its junior members as much as from its director. Balibar's essay constructed the concepts of mode of production and social formation with a rigour that gave the framework its working model of a structured whole, distinct periods, and transitions between them, and Balibar remained, long after others had left, the collaborator who carried the enterprise forward and revised it from inside. Rancière's contribution examined the concept of critique itself, tracing how the young Marx's critical method differed from the science founded later, and it is one of the ironies of the record that the seminar's most gifted student of the break would within a decade publish the most intimate repudiation of the master, written from the experience of the seminar's own hierarchy. The book's influence travelled along two channels that rarely met. In philosophy, it established the Parisian reading of Marx as a major event and drew the attention of theorists far outside Marxism. In the social sciences, its concepts of structure and conjuncture fed into debates about modes of production in Africa, Latin America, and Asia, where anthropologists and historians needed tools for societies in which capitalist and pre-capitalist relations coexisted, and where overdetermination and the articulation of modes became working instruments. Althusser's own essays in the volume contain the framework's most ambitious single claim: that Marx, in analysing the object of Capital, had in practice founded not only a science of history but a new continent of knowledge, as Thales had founded mathematics and Galileo physics, and that philosophy's task was to draw the consequences. The grandeur of the claim was deliberate and was received accordingly, with admiration for its audacity and with the suspicion, examined in the critics' sections, that grandeur of that order is a symptom of the very theoreticism he would later concede.

## Inside the seminar

The books give the doctrine; the seminar gave the doctrine its workforce, and the profile owes the reader a look inside the room. The teaching ran in cycles, each year taking an object and subjecting it to the collective discipline the books describe. There were years on the young Marx and on the concept of alienation, when the periodisation was being hammered out in front of the students who would carry it into print. There was the year of the Capital seminar itself, conducted as a siege: the text assigned in blocks, each participant charged with a region, the sessions devoted to the points where the assigned region resisted the reading, the lapses and the improvised concepts, and the director editing the whole toward the shared problematic with a severity that participants remembered in the vocabulary of both gratitude and grievance. There were years given to the ancestors: to Machiavelli, whose lectures ripened for decades before becoming the posthumous book; to Rousseau, whose contract was dissected for the discrepancies between its problems and its means; to Montesquieu; to Spinoza, the seminar's presiding spirit, read as the first philosopher to have thought a cause immanent in its effects and a subject decentered from its world. And there were the years of the sciences, when the seminar took up the history of mathematics, of biology, of psychoanalysis, testing the Bachelardian instruments on the disciplines that had forged them.

Two features of the practice explain its effects better than any doctrine. The first is that it was genuinely productive of other people's work: the seminar was a workshop with commissions, deadlines, and a publisher, and a student could arrive unknown and leave, within a few years, the co-author of the most discussed Marxist book of the decade. Careers in the French university did not usually begin so, and the loyalty the workshop commanded, and the resentment when the command chafed, both date from the arrangement. The second feature is the director's own position in the room, which the testimony describes with unusual consistency across the political divisions of the witnesses. Althusser taught as the theory said knowledge was made: not as a proprietor displaying conclusions but as the organiser of a practice, assigning the texts, framing the problems, and intervening at the points of blockage with the long, patient, immensely prepared expositions that the memoirs of his students describe. He was also, by the same testimony, frequently absent, and the seminar learned to run in his absence under the juniors, which is one reason the school survived his illnesses as long as it did and why its dispersal, when it came, was into competence rather than into chaos. A profile concerned with apparatuses should note the irony without savouring it: the theorist of the apparatus built, in one Parisian room, a small apparatus for the production of theorists, and it worked, for fifteen years, approximately as its theory of knowledge production said such things work, by the division of labour, the discipline of a problematic, and the subordination of individual brilliance to a collective instrument. What the instrument could not produce was a successor, and the reasons are in the sections on criticism.

The seminar's courses survive, and their posthumous publication has altered what can be said about the teaching. The course on psychoanalysis and the human sciences, delivered in the winter of 1963 and 1964 to an audience that included the young analysts of the Lacanian circle, shows the alliance of those years at work in real time, the philosopher expounding Freud's discovery as a science of the unconscious with its own object, and drawing, before an audience of clinicians, the line between that science and every philosophy that would annex it. The lectures on Rousseau, delivered nearly a decade later, show the symptomatic method in its maturity, patient and minute, the contract's text taken paragraph by paragraph until its discrepancies confess the structure beneath them. Editors and students who attended both courses have testified that the transcripts, austere as they read, omit the essential: the tempo of the room, the long preparation made visible, the sense of a text being taken seriously enough to be fought. The profile registers the testimony because the teaching, more than the books, was the medium in which the apparatus reproduced itself for twenty years, and because the loss of that medium, when the room in the rue d'Ulm fell silent, is the institutional fact beneath the dispersal that the next section records.

## The university question

The seminar's director also wrote, in the years of the intervention, about the institution that housed him, and the writings on students and on the university complete the profile's picture of the apparatus turned on its own home. The essay on student problems, published in a sociology journal in 1964, refused the two pieties of its moment: the piety that students are a class or the vanguard of one, and the piety that their discontents are a fever of youth. Students, on his analysis, are defined by their relation to knowledge: they are the apprentices of the technical and ideological division of labour, being sorted and formed for places in it, and their characteristic ideology is the ideology of that apprenticeship, a spontaneous theoreticism that mistakes the manipulation of concepts for a transformation of the world. The essay was read, four years before the explosion it seemed to predict, as an insult by the movement it analysed and as a diagnosis by those who watched the movement confirm it: the events of May raised the government of the universities and the nature of knowledge to the centre of politics exactly as the essay's categories suggested, while refusing, in the streets, the tutelage of the party whose philosopher had written it. His writings on the university's reform, and his interventions in the school's own governance, argued the same position from the other side: the university is an apparatus, its neutrality a fiction of its functioning, and the question is never whether it serves a formation but which, and with what degree of knowledge of itself. A profile assembles such facts because they show the doctrine's author applying it where he lived, at some cost to his comfort in both directions, dismissed by the student left as a mandarin of the party and by the party's custodians as a mandarin of theory. The double dismissal is a fair emblem of the position he occupied for twenty years, and the sections on criticism that follow can be read as its elaboration by other means.

## The students and the school's second life

A teacher's influence is usually measured in citations, but Althusser's ran through a building. For over thirty years the philosophy students of the École Normale Supérieure passed through his tuition, and the list of those who sat in his seminars or worked under his direction includes a remarkable share of the French intellectual generation that followed: Michel Foucault, whose early career he protected and promoted; Jacques Derrida, who studied at the school in the nineteen fifties and later taught there himself; Balibar, Rancière, and Macherey of the Capital seminar; Alain Badiou; Régis Debray; Michel Pêcheux, who built a theory of discourse on the ideology essay's foundations; Dominique Lecourt, who carried the Bachelardian inheritance into the philosophy of science; and, in the seminar's Lacanian wing, Jacques-Alain Miller and Jean-Claude Milner of the journal Cahiers pour l'Analyse, which the school's students founded in 1966 as the seminar's theoretical organ. The caïman's room functioned as a switchboard where Marxism, psychoanalysis, structural linguistics, and the philosophy of science were connected to one another, often for the first time in a young scholar's life, and the connections held even when the doctrine that had authorised them was later discarded by the connected parties.

### Who carried the seminar's habits into the next generation?

Students of the school who passed through his teaching included Foucault and Derrida from the earlier cohorts and Balibar, Rancière, Macherey, and Badiou from the seminar years, together with the circle of the journal founded by his students in the mid nineteen sixties to publish the seminar's work on science, language, and ideology.

The second life of the seminar is a study in dispersal. Almost none of the famous students remained Althusserians in any doctrinal sense, and several defined their mature work against his. Rancière's repudiation, published in 1974 under the title of a lesson administered to the master, charged the seminar with reproducing, inside the theory of emancipation, the hierarchy of those who know over those who are known about: the science of the master required the ignorance of the mass, and the students' revolt of 1968, which the master's categories could admire only from a distance, exposed the arrangement. Foucault's path led to a history of power for which the apparatus concept was a starting point to be dismantled and rebuilt. Derrida's relation to the school's Marxism remained oblique for decades before becoming, in the nineteen nineties, an explicit reckoning with Marx's spectres. Badiou kept the militant form and changed the doctrine entire. What survived the dispersal was not a school but a set of habits: the symptomatic attention to texts, the refusal of the expressive whole, the suspicion of the subject as origin, the insistence that institutions think in individuals before individuals think at all. Habits of that kind outlast the systems that teach them, and they are the reason the profile of this philosopher belongs in a series on Marxism even though much of his posterity worked outside it. The comparison with the other great teacher of the Western Marxist tradition, whose following also dispersed into disciplines he never entered, is drawn in the series article setting [Gramsci against Althusser](/2015/04/15/gramsci-vs-althusser/) on the two men's opposed accounts of intellectuals.

One institution of the seminar years deserves its own sentence of record, because later sections refer to its products. The Cahiers pour l'Analyse, founded by students in 1966 and running to ten issues, published the seminar's most technical work: on the concept of analysis in discourse, on the suture of the subject, on the history of the sciences. It was there, and in the companion series of small volumes issued by the publisher Maspero under Althusser's direction, that the framework's second generation tested the apparatus on objects its inventor had not touched: literature, in Macherey's theory of literary production, which treated the work as producing, not reflecting, its ideology; discourse, in Pêcheux's automatic analysis; mathematics and its history, in the work around Michel Serres and the young Miller. The Maspero collection also published the texts through which the wider French public met the enterprise: the Lenin lectures, the reply to John Lewis, the self-criticism. A profile concerned with sources should record the material fact that the borrowed apparatus had a publisher, a journal, a seminar, and a school, since those are the mechanisms by which borrowings become a tradition. The tradition's fragility matched its brilliance: it depended on one man's position in one institution, and when the events recorded below removed him from the school, the switchboard went quiet, and the dispersal became irreversible.

## Before the intervention: Hegel, Montesquieu, Rousseau

The Marx essays of the nineteen sixties were not the work of a beginner, and the profile pauses on the scholarship that preceded them, because the early work explains the later work's equipment better than any influence study. The diploma dissertation of 1947, written under Bachelard's supervision, examined the concept of content in Hegel, and it already contains, in embryo, the move that the mature work would generalise: a refusal to let a philosophy be summarised by its intentions, and an attention to the gap between what a system says and what its concepts actually do. Through the nineteen fifties Althusser published little, taught constantly, and worked on the French political tradition. His first book, in 1959, was a study of Montesquieu, short and exact, which read the theorist of the separation of powers as a revolutionary of method: the first thinker, on this account, to explain a society by the system of its relations rather than by the virtues of its rulers, and so a distant ancestor of the structural point of view. The essay on Rousseau's Social Contract, written for the seminar in the early nineteen sixties and widely reprinted, applied the symptomatic method before the method had its name, detecting in Rousseau's text the discrepancies, the décalages, between the problem posed and the theoretical means available to solve it, and treating those discrepancies as the text's real content.

These studies matter to the record for two reasons beyond their intrinsic quality. The first is that they show the apparatus being assembled before it was aimed at Marx: the symptomatic reading was practised on Rousseau, the structural intuition on Montesquieu, the Hegelian formation was absorbed and criticised a decade before the break was announced. Critics who present the Marx essays as an opportunist's raid on fashionable disciplines have the chronology against them; the disciplines were his formation, and the raid, if it was one, was conducted with tools he had owned for years. The second reason is the light the early work throws on the framework's strangest feature, its combination of maximal theoretical ambition with a professed indifference to system. The Montesquieu book praises its subject for having founded a science by inventing its object, the system of relations, and the Capital seminar would claim the same founding for Marx. The pattern of the claim, a science begins when a thinker constructs a new object, is Bachelard's history of physics transposed into politics, and it was in place in Althusser's mind before he had published a line on Marx's economics. When the critics below accuse the framework of imposing a French epistemology on a German economist, the early scholarship is their best evidence, and the profile places it here so that the accusation, when it comes, can be assessed against the record.

## The party's philosopher

No account of this career is honest that treats the French Communist Party as scenery. Althusser joined in 1948 and remained a member until his death, through the secret speech, the invasion of Hungary, the invasion of Czechoslovakia, the party's alliance strategies and their failures, and the long decline of its vote. He was never an official of the party and held no post in its apparatus; his position was stranger and, to his critics, more compromising than that. He was the party's most prestigious intellectual, housed in the state's most prestigious school, publishing in the party's theoretical journal, and his work functioned inside the organisation as a guarantee that the doctrine remained a living science rather than a catechism. The arrangement gave him a platform no university post could have matched and gave the party a philosopher whose international reputation reflected credit on it. It also meant that his theoretical positions were read, by friend and enemy alike, as moves in the party's internal struggles, and some of them were. The anti-humanist campaign aligned with the leadership's resistance to the reforming currents that the secret speech had licensed. The insistence on the science of Marx armed the party against both the social-democratic left and the gauchiste right of 1968, whose spontaneity the theory of the party, restated from Lenin, was mobilised to correct. When students occupied the streets in May of that year, the philosopher of the school in the rue d'Ulm was in a clinic for part of the crisis and, when present, stood with the organisation that the students were denouncing as a brake on their movement. The record of that month is the one his detractors cite most and his defenders discuss least, and the profile records the alignment without decoration: the theory of the party as the form of the class's organisation was his position before May, during May, and after it, and the events did not shake it.

His definition of philosophy belongs to this section because it was the instrument with which he tried to reconcile the two memberships, the school's and the party's. In lectures of 1967 and 1968, published as Lenin and Philosophy, he proposed that philosophy is not a science and has no object of its own; it is a practice of drawing lines, of demarcating the scientific from the ideological within the sciences and within theory itself, and in the last instance it represents, inside the field of theory, the class struggle that rages outside it. The definition did double duty. It demoted philosophy from queen of the sciences to a kind of referee with a party card, which answered the theoreticism charge in advance by subordinating the referee to a struggle he did not invent. And it dignified the party intellectual's daily work: the sorting of concepts, the policing of imports, the correction of deviations, became not scholasticism but the theoretical form of politics itself. Critics observed that the definition also immunised the practice it described, since a demarcation drawn in the name of a struggle cannot be refuted by an argument, only opposed by another demarcation; the circularity objection, that the theory certifies the politics that certifies the theory, was pressed by opponents across the spectrum and never, in the view of most later commentators, satisfactorily answered. The profile states the objection here and returns to it in the critics' sections, where it belongs to the strongest form of the case against the whole enterprise.

The reply to John Lewis shows the party philosopher at his most combative and the doctrine at its most exposed. Lewis, a British communist philosopher, had defended a humanist Marxism under the slogan that man makes history, and Althusser's reply, published in English in 1972 and 1973, restated the whole position as a series of theses: that knowledge is produced by theoretical practice and not extracted from the real by abstraction; that history is, in the phrase that became notorious, a process without a subject or goals; that men are the agents of history only as occupants of places assigned by the relations of production, supports of a structure rather than authors of a story. The exchange was read far beyond the communist movement, because it posed in the sharpest available form a question that exceeded its occasion: whether a science of society can dispense with the subject without dispensing with responsibility, blame, and hope. Althusser's answer, that the category of the subject belongs to ideology and law, which need a bearer for rights and guilt, while the science of history needs bearers of relations, was rigorous within its terms. It was also, as Lewis replied and as many neutral readers felt, an answer that saves the theory by surrendering the vocabulary in which politics is actually conducted, including by the party in whose name the reply was written, whose own discourse of heroes, traitors, and the working class as historical subject proceeded entirely undisturbed by the theses of its philosopher. The gap between the theory's rigour and the movement's rhetoric is the space in which the orthodoxy charge, stated in full below, does its work.

The last political interventions of the nineteen seventies show the party's philosopher turning the apparatus on the party itself. At the party's twenty-second congress in 1976, the leadership abandoned the doctrine of the dictatorship of the proletariat, and Althusser, who had theoretical reasons of his own to welcome the break with a formula he considered a corruption of Marx, objected publicly to the manner of the change: a doctrine discarded by administrative decision, without theoretical work, taught the membership that theory was whatever the leadership announced. In the spring of 1978 he published four long articles in a national newspaper under a title announcing that what could no longer continue in the communist party had to stop, arguing that the party's internal regime, its sealing of the leadership from the membership, its treatment of theory as property, reproduced inside the organisation of emancipation the structures of the society it opposed. The articles were the most widely read things he ever wrote for a general public, and they were written from inside the membership he never surrendered. That combination, loyalty of membership with ferocity of internal criticism, was his settled position, and assessments of the man divide on whether it represents integrity under impossible conditions or the final refinement of an apparatus for never having to choose. The profile records the facts and notes that the party, for its part, neither expelled him nor heeded him, and that the articles changed nothing in the organisation they addressed. Within three years the public career was over, for reasons that belong to a different section of the record.

## Lenin before the philosophers

In February 1968, weeks before the barricades, Althusser stood before the French Society of Philosophy, the most respectable assembly of the discipline in the country, and delivered the lecture published as Lenin and Philosophy. The scene belongs in the profile because it condenses the career's institutional drama into one evening. The society's members were the mandarinate of official philosophy, spiritualists, historians of philosophy, phenomenologists, men for whom Marxism was an opinion a colleague might hold and Lenin a name from the newspapers. The lecturer proposed to them that Lenin, the politician, had understood philosophy better than the philosophers: that Lenin's partisan materialism, his insistence that philosophy is a battlefield of tendencies dressed as eternal questions, contained the truth about their discipline that their discipline could not state about itself, namely that it has no object and lives by representing, in the field of concepts, struggles decided elsewhere. The lecture's courtesy was perfect and its content a declaration of war, and the discussion that followed, preserved in the published text's orbit, shows the assembly divided between fascination and the suspicion that it had been used as a prop in someone else's campaign. Both readings were correct. The campaign was the point: the definition of philosophy as class struggle in theory was not argued that evening so much as enacted, a demarcation drawn live in the enemy's hall, and the episode is the best illustration available of what the definition meant in practice. It also dates the confidence of the enterprise. Within months the streets had supplied a different theatre for the same questions, and the lecturer's organisation spent the spring denouncing the students whose revolt his theory of the party could neither bless nor join. The profile sets the February evening and the May streets side by side without commentary, as the record sets them: the philosopher at the height of his institutional power, expounding the primacy of practice to the professors, while the practice assembling outside belonged to a generation that had learned from his seminar to refuse every tutelage, his included.

## The party line and the philosopher

One episode of the party years complicates the orthodoxy charge so instructively that it earns its own section. In March 1966 the party's central committee met at Argenteuil, in the Paris suburbs, for a session devoted to ideological and cultural questions, and the session's resolutions marked the high point of the humanist current inside the organisation: the party committed itself to a Marxist humanism, to dialogue with Christians and with the existentialists, and to the proposition that the young Marx stood near the centre of the doctrine's self-understanding. The philosopher of the rue d'Ulm was, on the question the session decided, in the minority inside his own party, and the resolutions were widely read, by his circle and by his opponents, as an official preference for the line associated with Roger Garaudy. Althusser's response was characteristic in form: no public rupture, no resignation, and a redoubling of the theoretical work, accompanied by private correspondence of great sharpness about the difference between a political line, which a party may adopt for its reasons, and a theoretical position, which is true or false independently of any vote. The distinction was convenient, and his critics said so; it was also, in the circumstances, the only distinction available to a man who intended to remain both a communist and a philosopher, and the rest of the career can be read as its long defence.

The episode matters to the assessment because it dates the man's relation to the institution precisely. He was never the party's official philosopher in the sense of a doctrinal spokesman whose texts the organisation endorsed; the organisation endorsed, at the decisive session, his opponents. What he possessed was prestige, a journal's hospitality, and the protection that a famous name inside a famous school affords, and he used the protection to maintain, for two decades, a position the leadership tolerated as an ornament and a reproach. The orthodoxy charge, to survive this record, has to be restated in its subtler form: not that he executed the party's line in theory, but that his theory, whatever its author's quarrels with the leadership, supplied any centralised party with the epistemology it needed, a science held by specialists and a membership classified as the bearer of ideology. The subtler form is the one the critics' sections engage, and it is not answered by the Argenteuil record; it is only protected from its cruder version. The profile's judgement is that the protection is worth having on the record, since the crude version, the philosopher as the regime's hired metaphysician, is the one that circulates, and it is false to the documents.

## The Maoist moment

One current of the period ran through the seminar room without being governed by it, and the profile records it because the master's reputation became entangled with the disciples' adventure. From the middle nineteen sixties, as the Sino-Soviet split widened, a generation of the school's students looked to Mao's China for the living form of the revolution their teacher's Leninism described, and Althusser's own writings gave them materials: the essay of 1966 on the Cultural Revolution, published in the students' Marxist-Leninist journal, treated the Chinese upheaval as an event of the first theoretical importance, an attempt to revolutionise the superstructures and the ideology of a society whose economic base had already been transformed, and so a practical commentary on overdetermination from the far side of the world. After May 1968 the current broke its banks. Students of the school founded or led the Maoist organisations of the French far left; Robert Linhart, of the Capital seminar's wider circle, organised the establishment of militants in the factories and wrote the classic account of the experience; Rancière passed through the movement on his way out of the master's orbit altogether. Althusser's own position remained that of the party he would not leave, and the distance between the teacher in the party and the students in the factories and the streets became the biographical form of the period's central argument: whether the revolution required an apparatus of the kind his theory described, or whether the apparatus was the name the century gave to the revolution's defeat. He did not resolve the argument, and the profile does not pretend that his writings of those years can be read without it. The Maoist episode left a deposit in the work: the insistence, sharpening through the nineteen seventies, that ideology and the superstructures were not reflections but battlefields, and the attention to the mass line and to investigation that surfaces in the self-criticism's praise of Mao's essays on practice and contradiction. It also left a deposit in the man: the students of the adventure were, several of them, the people who remained closest to him in the years of illness and disgrace, a fact of the personal record that the biographies preserve and that this profile notes without drawing from it any moral the evidence will not carry.

## The self-criticism

In 1974 Althusser published the first instalments of a self-criticism, and the event requires careful statement, because it was received at the time, and is still described in summaries, as something between a recantation and a collapse. The texts, assembled in English as Essays in Self-Criticism, took up the framework of the nineteen sixties and indicted it on a specific charge: theoreticism. The early work, he now argued, had defined the distinction between science and ideology as a distinction internal to theory, a matter of correct concepts policed by philosophy, and had thereby produced a rationalist deviation: theory appeared as its own judge, history as the unfolding of a knowledge, and the class struggle, which the doctrine named as the motor of everything, was present in the system as a formula rather than as a force. The correction redefined philosophy as class struggle in theory, demoted the demarcation between science and ideology from an epistemological frontier to a political position taken and retaken, and conceded that the concept of the break, wielded too mechanically, had flattened the very unevenness that overdetermination had been invented to honour. He also conceded a silence: the early work had almost nothing to say about the state as an apparatus of repression, or about the mass organisations of the working class, absences he began to repair in the same years with the ideology essay's companion pieces on the state.

### What did the self-criticism actually concede?

It conceded the form, not the findings. The charge he accepted was theoreticism: that the frontier between science and ideology had been drawn inside theory alone, by philosophy acting as its own tribunal, so that the class struggle appeared as a slogan the system repeated rather than as the force that was supposed to explain the system.

The reception of the self-criticism is a small history of the framework's public in itself. Enemies read it as surrender and quoted the concessions against the early books, an easy victory that cost them nothing. Loyalists read it as a rectification that left the essentials untouched, and could quote the same texts, since Althusser was emphatic that the self-criticism corrected a deviation in his work in the name of the work's own founding insight, the primacy of class struggle, rather than abandoning the insight. A third reading, which this profile adopts as the best supported by the documents, is that the self-criticism was continuous with the method it corrected. A thinker whose central doctrine held that knowledge advances by breaks and rectifications could hardly exempt his own texts from the process, and the spectacle of the author applying his instruments to himself, detecting the symptom, naming the deviation, re-periodising his own corpus into a theoreticist phase and its correction, was the framework performing its favourite operation on the only material it had never before been turned on. The performance does not settle whether the correction went deep enough. Critics of the standing of the English historians and of his own former students judged that the theoreticism was not a deviation from the early work but its essence, and that a self-criticism which preserved the system while blaming its accent had changed the emphasis and kept the machine. The judgement is recorded here; its full statement belongs to the critics' sections, and the reader is asked to notice, in the meantime, that the self-criticism is one of the very few documents in the Marxist tradition in which a theorist of the first rank published, in his lifetime, a detailed account of his own theoretical errors, with the errors specified rather than gestured at. Whatever its sufficiency, its existence is part of the record, and summaries that report a simple retraction of the whole project are wrong about the documents.

One further document of the self-critical years matters to the profile because it shows the correction being applied to politics and not only to epistemology. The essay on the twenty-second congress, and the drafts gathered after his death under the title of Marx in his limits, pressed the argument that the Marxist tradition's own concepts of the state and of transition were underdeveloped to the point of danger: that Marx had left no theory of the state adequate to the century that would rule in his name, no theory of the party, no theory of the long transition between modes of production, and that the tradition's confidence on these points rested on a handful of texts stretched past their capacity. A philosopher is not usually found announcing the insufficiency of his own tradition's foundations in the years of his greatest fame. The announcement connects the self-criticism to the final public intervention of the decade, the declaration of a crisis, and it is the bridge to the late philosophy, where the insufficiency becomes a principle: if the foundations do not hold as a system, then perhaps history was never a system, and the concepts must be rebuilt for a world of encounters that might not have taken place.

## A crisis announced

In September 1977, at a conference in Venice devoted to the crisis of Marxism, Althusser opened his intervention by declaring that at last the crisis of Marxism had broken out and could be spoken of in the open. The phrasing was deliberate and was received as a detonation in the world that still took its bearings from the doctrine. The crisis, as he specified it, was not the work of enemies. It was internal and triple: a crisis of the revolutionary organisations and the states built in Marxism's name, whose record could no longer be treated as an accident external to the theory; a crisis of the theory itself, whose gaps on the state, on politics, on the transition, the tradition had papered over with quotations; and a crisis, therefore, of the comfortable division by which the theory stayed pure and the practice took the blame. What gave the declaration its sting was the speaker. A hostile critic announcing a crisis of Marxism was a commonplace of the century. The philosopher who had spent two decades fortifying the doctrine's scientific credentials, announcing from inside the fortress that the walls had never been finished, was an event. The Italian hosts, whose party was then exploring its own road, received the declaration as a licence; the French party received it as an embarrassment; and the essays that followed it in the Paris press, including the series on what could no longer continue in the party, extended the diagnosis from the doctrine to its principal French bearer.

### What did the announcement of a crisis claim?

It claimed that the crisis was Marxism's own and could no longer be exported onto enemies or accidents. The theory had gaps where the century most needed concepts, of the state, of the party, of transition, and the organisations ruling in its name had built their practice into those gaps, so naming the crisis was the precondition of repair.

The declaration has to be placed precisely to be understood, because it is misread in two directions. It was not a farewell to Marxism: the speaker remained a communist, framed the crisis as the possible beginning of a renewal, and spent his remaining working years on the philosophy, examined in the next sections, that he believed the crisis required. Nor was it the routine self-flagellation of a loyalist performing humility; the specified gaps were real, the historical scholarship of the following decades confirmed several of them, and the insistence that the record of the socialist states posed a theoretical problem rather than a public relations problem was a position of genuine risk inside his own organisation. The profile notes the declaration's date and its authorship because together they mark the hinge of the career: before it, the trajectory of an ascending system, each book fortifying the last; after it, the trajectory of a thinker dismantling his own fortifications in public and preparing, in private, a different philosophy altogether. The public career had three years to run. The private preparation, interrupted by the catastrophe recorded in the next section, produced the work by which the final Althusser is known, and the profile takes the sections in the order the record imposes: the catastrophe first, in its own short passage, and then the philosophy of the last years, which belongs to the account of the work and will be judged there.

## The final decade

The public career ended in November 1980. In that month, in their apartment at the school, Althusser strangled his wife, Hélène Rytmann. He was examined by psychiatrists and found to have been incapable of responsibility at the time of the act; a judicial decision was taken that he would not be brought to trial. He was committed to a psychiatric hospital, from which he was released in 1983, and he lived thereafter in seclusion, first in Paris and then in a clinic outside it, until his death in October 1990. A memoir he had written in the years of seclusion was published after his death. Those are the facts, and this profile states them once, here, without the detail that belongs to the literature of crime and without any interpretation that would make the philosophy answerable for the act or the act answerable for the philosophy.

One further fact belongs to the same passage, because the record is distorted without it. Hélène Rytmann was not a figure who exists only at the end of her husband's story. She was a sociologist and a former member of the French Resistance, a woman of intellectual standing in the milieu they shared for more than three decades, and her death is the death of a person with a life and work of her own, not an episode in the biography of a philosopher. Reference works that pass over her name, or that reach the events of that November only as a scandal in a man's career, repeat at the level of documentation the effacement that the discipline of this profile exists to refuse. She is named here as she is named in the record.

The remaining years require only a paragraph of chronology. Released from hospital in 1983, Althusser lived quietly, saw a small number of visitors, wrote the memoir and the philosophical texts that appeared after his death, and was buried following a private funeral in October 1990. The school where he had taught for three decades marked the passing of a man who had been, for a generation of its students, the centre of its intellectual life, and whose final decade it had witnessed in the same building. The profile now returns to the work, which is where the record of the last years is richest: the philosophy written before and after the catastrophe, in the seclusion and in the years of the crisis, constitutes a distinct final phase, and it is assessed in the next section as philosophy, by the same standards applied to the books of the nineteen sixties.

## The underground current: the late philosophy

The last philosophy was written largely out of sight and published after its author's death, and for a decade it was treated, when it was noticed at all, as a curiosity: the private speculation of a man whose public work was done. That judgement has not survived the texts. The writings gathered posthumously under the sign of aleatory materialism, or the materialism of the encounter, propose a philosophy of history as different from the structural Marxism of the nineteen sixties as that Marxism was from the humanism it opposed, and they do so by returning to the oldest stratum of the materialist tradition. Althusser traced an underground current running beneath the canonical history of philosophy: Epicurus and Lucretius on the atoms raining through the void and swerving, the clinamen, by an inclination no law prescribes; Machiavelli on the prince who founds a state in a conjuncture that guarantees nothing; Spinoza; Hobbes; Rousseau; and Marx himself, whose doctoral thesis had been written on Epicurus and whose mature work, on this late reading, contains the underground current surfacing under the geology of the official doctrine. The elements of the philosophy can be stated plainly. Before any world, there is a rain of elements in a void. An encounter occurs when elements take hold of one another, as atoms lock in the swerve, as a founder's virtù meets a people's materia, as a set of relations of production crystallises out of dispersed elements. Nothing guarantees the encounter, and nothing in the elements destines them for it. Once an encounter has taken hold and lasted, it generates the appearance of necessity after the fact: the accomplished structure writes its own prehistory as though it had been inevitable. Necessity, in the famous inversion, is the becoming-necessary of an encounter of contingencies.

### What survives of the project in the late philosophy?

The materialism survives and the system does not. The late work keeps the refusal of essences, origins, and guarantees, and abandons almost everything that had made the earlier framework a doctrine: the science and ideology frontier, the structural whole assigning every level its time, the last instance itself, replaced by encounters that happen or fail to happen.

The consequences for the earlier concepts are severe, and Althusser drew them without flinching. A philosophy of the encounter has no use for determination in the last instance, since there is no instance and no last: the economy itself is an encounter that took hold, feudalism an encounter that dissolved, capitalism an encounter whose dissolution is as unscripted as its birth. The category of the mode of production survives, but as the name of an accomplished fact rather than of a stage in a sequence, and the philosophy of history that the break had seemed to found, a science of modes succeeding one another by their contradictions, is quietly withdrawn. Readers have divided over what to make of the withdrawal. One party treats the late work as the truth of the early work, the contingency that overdetermination had always smuggled in, finally confessed: if every contradiction is specified by its conjuncture, then the conjuncture was never governed by a structure, and aleatory materialism is structural Marxism with its guarantees honestly surrendered. The other party treats the late work as a dissolution, the point at which the framework, pressed by its own self-criticism, gave up the claim to be a theory of history at all and became a metaphysics of chance, impressive as a testament and unusable as an instrument. The profile records both readings and observes that the dispute reproduces, at a higher level, the founding quarrel of the career: whether the power of the work lies in its concepts as tools or in its refusal of every consolation, including the consolation of a guaranteed future.

The Machiavelli writings are the late philosophy's bridge to politics and its most finished product. Althusser had lectured on Machiavelli for years, and the book published after his death reads The Prince as the theory of the encounter in political form: a founder alone with a corrupt and dispersed people, a form to be given to matter that has no vocation for it, success dependent on a conjunction of virtù and fortuna that no doctrine can schedule. Gramsci's reading of Machiavelli as the theorist of the modern party is corrected from inside the shared admiration: where Gramsci found the collective prince, Althusser found solitude, the irreducible loneliness of the beginning, and the analysis has been taken up by readers of Gramsci as a challenge the hegemony tradition must answer, which is one reason the comparison between the two thinkers, linked earlier in this profile, continues to generate work. The late philosophy also reread the tradition's sacred texts without piety: the essay on the underground current treats Marx's own materialism as double, an official philosophy of necessity laid over an unavowed philosophy of the encounter, and proposes that the tradition develop the second at the expense of the first. A thinker who began by dividing Marx into two, the ideologist and the scientist, ended by dividing him into two again, the philosopher of necessity and the philosopher of the encounter, and the symmetry is the career in miniature: each phase of the work proceeds by finding, inside the settled doctrine, an unexamined duality, and founding the next phase on the neglected term.

## The Lacan connection

One borrowing in the apparatus was also a personal and institutional alliance, and it shaped the Parisian intellectual map for a generation. Althusser's essay of 1964 on Freud and Lacan was among the first serious philosophical engagements with Jacques Lacan's teaching, and it performed a service for each man. For Althusser, Lacan supplied the theory of the subject that the framework required: a subject constituted in the symbolic order, in language and law, split from the start, an effect rather than an origin, which is exactly the subject that the theory of ideology would shortly need in order to say how individuals are made into the bearers of a social order. For Lacan, then in the crisis of his expulsion from the international psychoanalytic establishment, Althusser supplied an institution: when Lacan lost his seminar's home, the school in the rue d'Ulm received it, and the seminar that formed a generation of French analysts proceeded under the protection of the philosopher's wing. The students overlapped; the journal Cahiers pour l'Analyse was staffed by young men working in both seminars at once; and for a few years the rue d'Ulm housed a combined enterprise in which the reading of Marx and the reading of Freud were conducted as one investigation into how subjects are produced by structures they did not choose.

The alliance is recorded here because it explains both a strength and a vulnerability of the framework. The strength is the theory of the subject itself, without which interpellation is a metaphor. The mirror stage, in which the infant assumes an image and is constituted by the assumption, gave the ideology essay its model of recognition: the subject is the one who turns when hailed, and the turning is not obedience added to a pre-existing person but the person's constitution as a subject of the order that calls him. The vulnerability is the one the eclecticism charge fastens on with best evidence. The Lacanian subject is a theoretical construction internal to psychoanalysis, resting on clinical and linguistic claims that are contested inside their own discipline, and a Marxism that founds its account of ideology on that construction has mortgaged its central concept to a creditor whose solvency it cannot audit. Althusser was not unaware of the exposure; his writings on psychoanalysis are careful to treat Freud's discovery as a science with its own object, irreducible to biology or to philosophy, and to borrow its results the way one science borrows another's. Whether the borrowing protocol he imagined between sciences is coherent is a philosophical question the profile leaves where he left it, noting only that the same protocol governs every entry in the table of concepts and sources: the apparatus is a set of treaties between disciplines, and its fate is bound to the fate of the parties to each treaty.

## The Cahiers and the career of a concept

One product of the seminar's Lacanian wing had a career so improbable that the profile pauses on it as the limiting case of the apparatus's travels. The journal Cahiers pour l'Analyse, founded by the school's students in 1966, set out to construct a rigorous theory of discourse on the combined foundations of the seminar and of Lacan's teaching, and in its first issue Jacques-Alain Miller published an essay on suture, a term borrowed from Lacan's logic of the signifier, naming the operation by which a subject is stitched into a chain of discourse at the point of a lack it cannot see. The essay was an exercise in the logic of Frege's concept of number as much as in psychoanalysis, austere even by the journal's standards, and its author pursued the matter no further in that form. The concept, however, emigrated. Film theorists in the journal Cahiers du Cinéma and in the English journal Screen took up suture as the name of the operation by which classical cinema stitches the spectator into its fiction, concealing the cuts and the camera's absence so that the viewer occupies the place the film prepares; an entire school of film theory, with its accounts of the apparatus of cinema, a word whose career needs no gloss here, was built on the foundation of a student's essay in a Parisian seminar journal. The episode is recorded neither as triumph nor as farce. It demonstrates, with unusual clarity, the mechanism by which the borrowed apparatus propagated: concepts forged for one discipline's problems, stated at a level of generality that invited export, were detached by readers the inventor never addressed and set to work on materials he never considered, where they earned their keep or failed on merits he could not have judged. Suture in film theory has had its critics and its revisions, like everything else in this profile. What it never had was a proprietor able to call it home, and in that dispossession it resembles the whole estate: overdetermination in the mouths of political scientists, interpellation in the mouths of media scholars, the conjuncture in the mouths of strategists. A framework is alive, the episode suggests, exactly to the extent that its concepts can survive being borrowed in their turn, which is the fate the young philosopher of the rue d'Ulm, who built his system from other men's parts, was perhaps better placed than any thinker of his century to understand.

## The letter on art

The apparatus was also turned, in a short text of 1966 that the literature on aesthetics continues to cite, on the question of what art knows. A correspondent had asked, in the party's cultural journal, whether art could be counted a form of knowledge on the model of science, and Althusser's reply, a letter rather than an essay, staked out the position that became the school's aesthetics. Art is not science, and to promote it into a knowledge is to dissolve what is specific to it; but neither is art ideology in the raw state. What authentic art does, on the letter's account, is to make us see, in the mode of feeling and perception rather than of concepts, the ideology from which it is born and in which its audience bathes: it holds that ideology at an internal distance, makes it perceptible as an object, and so performs, in its own register, a labour akin to the break without being one. The companion essay on the painter Cremonini applied the doctrine to a contemporary: the paintings' figures, painted without the humanist face that portraiture promises, were read as a materialism in paint, the human form shown as the product of the structures that traverse it. Macherey's theory of literary production, produced in the seminar's orbit in the same years, systematised the letter's intuition into a method: the work does not reflect its ideology but works it, and in the working exposes its limits and its silences, which the critic reads symptomatically. The profile includes the aesthetics for a reason beyond completeness. The letter on art is the gentlest text in the corpus, the one in which the apparatus approaches its object without a sentence of denunciation, and it demonstrates that the instruments of the break and the symptom were built for attention before they were built for war. Assessments of the school that know only the polemics are missing a register its founder commanded, and the fact is recorded here in fairness to the record.

## The spontaneous philosophy of the scientists

A doctrine from the lectures of the late nineteen sixties deserves a section of its own, because it is the most practically influential of his ideas inside the sciences themselves and because it states, in miniature, the whole theory of philosophy's office. Every scientist, Althusser proposed in a course of lectures delivered to scientists in 1967, carries a spontaneous philosophy: an unexamined set of convictions about knowledge, matter, proof, and the scientist's own place in the world, absorbed from the practice of the discipline and from the dominant ideology that surrounds it. The spontaneous philosophy is internally divided. One element comes from the scientific practice itself and pulls toward materialism: the working conviction that the object exists independently, that concepts answer to it, that results can surprise their makers. The other element is imported from outside, from the philosophies that the surrounding society supplies ready-made, and pulls toward idealism: the conviction that the object is a construction of the subject, that truth is a convention of the community, that the science's success is a mystery to be celebrated rather than a process to be explained. Philosophical intervention, on this account, is not the delivery of a system to the sciences from above. It is the work of drawing the line, inside the spontaneous philosophy itself, between its two elements, strengthening the first against the second. The philosopher does not teach the physicist physics; he fights, on the terrain of the physicist's own unexamined convictions, the battle that those convictions are already fighting without him.

The doctrine shows the framework at its most attractive and its most exposed in a single gesture. Attractive, because it respects the autonomy it theorisess: the sciences need no permission and no foundation, and philosophy's dignity is preserved precisely by its renunciation of the throne. Exposed, because the line between the materialist element and the idealist one is drawn by the philosopher's judgement, and the judgement is certified by nothing outside the philosophy that draws it, so that the circularity objection returns at the exact point of the doctrine's modesty. The course was published in the nineteen seventies and read by the audience it addressed; the record of its reception among working scientists is thinner than its reception among philosophers, a distribution the doctrine itself would predict, since the spontaneous philosophy of scientists is, by hypothesis, the last place its holders go looking for their convictions. The profile includes the doctrine because no account of the borrowed apparatus is complete without it: it is the point where the apparatus describes its own daily use, the sorting and demarcating that the seminar practised and that the definition of philosophy as class struggle in theory elevated into a mission. It is also the point where a reader can test the whole enterprise at its smallest scale. If the description of the scientist's divided convictions rings true as phenomenology, the apparatus has earned its keep for that reader; if it rings as a licence for the philosopher to grade other disciplines' homework, the critics' case has found its smallest and clearest exhibit.

## The critics at full strength

The framework attracted criticism of unusual quality, and a profile that softened the criticism would fail its subject, who believed that a position is known by the objections it can survive. The critics fall into four companies: the historian, the former student, the party's rival philosophers, and the philosophers of science. Each is stated here in its own terms, at the strength its authors gave it.

The historian is Edward Thompson, whose book of 1978, The Poverty of Theory, contains the most celebrated polemic in English against the whole enterprise. Thompson was a historian of the English working class and a socialist humanist of the school Althusser's anti-humanism was coined against, and his attack is substantive before it is rhetorical. Its core is a charge about history. A theory in which structures determine and subjects are effects, he argued, cannot write history at all, because history is precisely the record of men and women acting within determinations they did not choose and altering them by the acting; a Marxism that dissolves agency into structural effectivity has reproduced, inside the doctrine of emancipation, the contemplative stance of the theology it despised, with the structure enthroned where providence used to sit. Thompson gave the framework a name that stuck in the English-speaking world, an orrery of errors, a clockwork model of a society turning above the real lives it claimed to explain. He charged the system with being ahistorical in its very machinery, since differential times and immanent causality supply no way to narrate the passage from one formation to another, and with being Stalinist in its epistemology, since the party of theory, the philosophers who alone can read the symptoms, stands to the class as the vanguard's general staff stood to the masses: licensed by a science the laity cannot audit. And he charged it with an evacuation of morality, arguing that a Marxism unable to say, in its theoretical voice, that exploitation is an injury done by some to others had lost the indignation that was the doctrine's original reason to exist.

### What did Thompson's attack target, exactly?

It targeted the evacuation of agency and moral judgement from history. Thompson argued that a system of structures and effects turns men and women into supports of a process they do not make, cannot narrate how one society becomes another, and divides knowers from known so as to reproduce, inside theory, the party's hierarchy over the class.

The polemic was answered, most systematically by Perry Anderson, who conceded the force of Thompson's defence of agency while defending the legitimacy of structural analysis and deploring the polemic's refusal to distinguish the framework's instruments from its excesses; the exchange remains the best single introduction to the quarrel in English. Thompson's own verdict has not carried the whole field, and his alternative, a humanist Marxism of experience and moral agency, has its own well-documented difficulties with structure and determination. What the attack accomplished beyond dispute was to fix the terms of the Anglophone reception: Althusser entered English-language debate substantially through the door Thompson built, as the defendant in a trial about agency, and the trial's terms still organise syllabuses wherever the two men are taught together. The profile notes the fact without resentment on either side: it is the normal fate of frameworks to be known by their strongest enemy's account of them, and Althusser, who taught that a text is read by its symptoms, would have recognised the process.

The former student is Jacques Rancière, and his repudiation cuts from a different angle, since it concedes the framework's brilliance and indicts its politics of knowledge. The lesson of Althusser, as the book of 1974 administered it, is that the master's science required the student's ignorance as its permanent condition: the division between the science that reads and the ideology that is read maps exactly onto the division between the party that leads and the class that is led, and a pedagogy built on that division will reproduce it indefinitely, in the seminar as in the state. Where Thompson charged the framework with being unable to write history, Rancière charged it with being unable to end its own tutelage, and the charge has proved the more durable of the two inside the tradition, since the later philosophy of emancipation in France, including Rancière's own, defines itself by the refusal of the explicator's chair. Althusser never answered the book in print. His defenders have replied that the seminar's actual practice, collective, argumentative, productive of independent work by its juniors within five years of their arrival, refutes the portrait of a hierarchy of knowledge more thoroughly than any polemic could, and that Rancière's own career, launched from the seminar's platform, is the standing counterexample to the charge that the platform prevented departure. Both documents, the charge and the career, are part of the record.

The party's rival philosophers and the humanist critics form the third company, and their charge is the one the run rules of this series require to be stated at full strength: that structural Marxism served party orthodoxy. The argument runs as follows. A doctrine that reserves science to a theoretical practice conducted by specialists, that classifies the lived experience of workers and the moral vocabulary of militants as ideology, and that locates the motor of history in structures no meeting can vote on, is a doctrine perfectly fitted to a centralised party that wishes its membership instructed rather than consulted and its leadership's line certified rather than debated. The chronology is then exhibited: the anti-humanist campaign coincided with the leadership's campaign against the reformers; the theory of the party restated Lenin at the moment when the party's own democracy was the question its intellectuals were forbidden to raise; the philosopher's prestige stood surety for an organisation whose record in the socialist states he declined, for most of his career, to examine with the instruments he applied to texts. On this account the borrowed apparatus is an ideology in his own technical sense: a practice housed in apparatuses, the school and the party journal, representing to its subjects an imaginary relation to their real conditions, the imaginary relation being that they were doing science. The charge is the strongest political objection the framework faces, and this profile does not answer it on the subject's behalf beyond recording the facts that complicate it: that the same philosopher published, in the party's years of discipline, the most damaging internal critique of the party's regime that any member in good standing produced; that the state, the party, and the transition were the subjects on which he declared his own tradition bankrupt; and that the orthodoxy he is accused of serving expelled or marginalised most of the men his seminar formed. A doctrine can serve an institution it also wounds, and the record shows both services. Readers must weigh them; the profile's duty is to ensure that the weighing is done with the charge stated whole.

The fourth company, the philosophers of science, pressed the objection on which the others silently depend: that the framework's central epistemological claim does not survive inspection. The claim is that Marx founded a science, as Galileo founded one, by an epistemological break with the ideological discourse before him. The objection is that the analogy borrows the prestige of the sciences without their discipline: a science is marked by procedures of test and correction that can, in principle, return a verdict against its own theories, while the framework's procedures, symptomatic reading above all, arrange in advance that the founding texts cannot lose. The break, on this view, is not discovered in Marx's corpus but conferred on it, by a decision that sorts passages into science and residue according to their agreement with a theory the sorting was supposed to establish. Norman Geras, the most careful of the Marxist critics working this seam, granted the framework its genuine discoveries, the critique of expressive totality and of empiricism among them, while judging the science and ideology frontier untenable and the anti-humanism an overreach that the texts of Marx do not support. Gregory Elliott's comprehensive study reached a related verdict from the documentary side: that the enterprise is best understood as a detour, theoretically brilliant and politically costly, through which French Marxism passed on its way elsewhere. These measured judgements, rather than the polemics, represent the settled assessment of the scholarly literature, and the profile adopts their balance: the apparatus survives as a set of instruments and a set of warnings, not as the science it announced itself to be.

## The elder rival: Lefebvre

One French critic of the framework deserves separate mention because he attacked it from the position of seniority inside the same tradition, and the quarrel between the two men is the French form of the argument the English conducted through Thompson. Henri Lefebvre was a generation older, a philosopher of the Resistance and of the party's heroic years, expelled from the party a decade before Althusser's ascent, and the theorist of everyday life, of the critique of the ordinary, of space and the city. His Marxism was everything the new one refused: dialectical where the new was structural, humanist where the new was anti-humanist, saturated with Hegel where the new had broken with the Hegelian consolation, and committed to the lived experience of ordinary people as the ground and test of theory. Lefebvre's counter-attack, delivered across the books of the late nineteen sixties and in a volume on the structuralist ideology at the start of the seventies, charged the seminar with having replaced history by a taxonomy, praxis by a machinery, and the revolutionary subject by a process admiring its own reflections; structuralism, in his verdict, was the ideology of the technocratic society it pretended to analyse, the world administered as a system finding its philosophy in a system without men. The Althusserian reply, conducted mostly by the school rather than by the master, treated Lefebvre as the surviving specimen of the problematic they had diagnosed: the philosophy of praxis and presence, of the total man, surviving into an age that had refuted it. The profile records the quarrel because it is the purest available statement of the choice the French tradition faced, and because time has dealt with the two legacies in a way neither party predicted: Lefebvre's concepts of everyday life and the production of space conquered urban studies and geography as completely as Althusser's conquered the theory of ideology, and the two old enemies divide the posterity of French Marxism between them like heirs who never learned they were related. A student who reads only one of them owns half the map.

## The economists' objection

A fifth company of critics has been quieter than the four above and cuts, in its way, as deep: the economists of the Marxist tradition itself. Their objection begins from a bibliographical observation. The philosopher who announced that Marx had founded a science, who organised the most famous collective reading of Capital ever conducted in a university, published almost nothing on the science's actual content: no analysis of the value form, no position on the transformation of values into prices, no engagement with the theory of accumulation, crisis, or the falling rate of profit. The seminar's great claims for Capital were claims about how to read it, about its object, its method, its symptomatic relation to classical economy, and the reading conspicuously declined the book's own terrain. Ernest Mandel, the Trotskyist economist who was the tradition's most formidable working practitioner in those decades, pressed the point into a general indictment: a Marxism re-founded as philosophy, in the capital of philosophy, by men who did not practise political economy, would reproduce in theory the division of labour it analysed in society, with the philosophers thinking and the economists, the historians, and the militants supplying the material to be thought about. The charge had a French address as well as a Belgian one; within Althusser's own camp, the economist Charles Bettelheim stood as the standing exception that proved the rule, an ally whose work on planning and on the Soviet formation carried the school's concepts into economic material the seminar itself never touched, and whose presence in the orbit only underlined the director's abstention.

The objection deserves its place in this profile because it identifies the exact sense in which the borrowed apparatus was borrowed. The creditors listed in the table of concepts and sources are philosophers, analysts, and linguists; the one creditor a Marxist framework might be expected to owe most, the economic work of Marx himself and of the tradition after him, appears in the table only as the object of a method. Althusser's defenders have two replies, and both are recorded. The first is that the division of labour was principled: the epistemological work was preliminary, the clearing of a site on which the sciences of history and economy could then be practised without philosophical interference, and the seminar's abstention from economics was the respect of a philosopher for a competence he did not claim. The second is that the abstention was not total, since the concepts of mode of production and social formation, forged in the seminar and its orbit, were built for economic history and were used there, by Bettelheim, by the anthropologists of modes of production, and by the Latin American analysts named in this profile. Readers will weigh the replies against the observation that prompted them, which stands: the most philosophical Marxism of the century is a philosophy of Marxism rather than a practice of its science, and the fact shaped everything, the triumphs of the method and the sterility that its enemies diagnosed, because a generation learned from it that the highest theoretical act was the policing of other acts. When the self-criticism named the theoreticism, it named, among other things, this abstention, and the profile's inclusion of the economists among the critics is a way of giving that concession its full address.

## The strongest objection: change without a subject

Beneath the four companies of critics stands a single objection that each of them reaches in its own vocabulary, and the conventions of this series require it to be stated at full strength and answered as fairly as the record allows. The objection is this. A framework in which structures determine, in which individuals are the supports of relations and subjects are the effects of an interpellation that precedes them, in which history is a process without a subject or goals, has built a magnificent account of how a social order reproduces itself and has no account, in principle, of how such an order is ever transformed. Reproduction is theorised to the last timetable and examination hall; transformation is gestured at with words borrowed from the older vocabulary, class struggle above all, whose agents the theory has dissolved in the act of defining them. If the subject is an effect, who rebels? If ideology constitutes the rebel as surely as the conformist, from what position is the call refused? The framework seems to describe a world in which nothing can happen except what the structure permits, punctuated by events, revolutions, collapses, that it can narrate afterwards in the language of overdetermination but could never have led anyone to expect, still less to make. A theory of history that cannot house the making of history has failed at the task for which Marxism exists, and its failure is not an omission but a consequence: it follows from the founding decisions, the borrowed causality, the theoretical anti-humanism, the dissolution of the subject, each of which this profile has recorded in its place.

### Can a theory of structures explain how anything changes?

Only with difficulty, and the difficulty is the framework's acknowledged frontier. The resources are real: contradictions specified by their conjuncture can fuse into rupture, and the apparatuses reproducing an order are also stakes of struggle inside it. But the theory is markedly stronger on why orders persist than on how they fall, and its author never closed the gap.

The answer the framework gives has three layers, and fairness requires all three. The first is overdetermination itself: a structure in Althusser's sense is not a monolith but an uneven articulation of instances, each with its own time and its own contradictions, and a formation so composed is not stable in the way the objection supposes; its unity is a unity of displacements, and the ruptural fusion of contradictions in a conjuncture is a mechanism of transformation internal to the theory, instanced by the analysis of 1917. The second layer is the doctrine, appended to the ideology essay and developed by his school, that the apparatuses are not only the instruments of reproduction but the site and stake of class struggle: schools, churches, parties, and media are fought over, and the fighting is not decoration on the structure but one of the forms its contradictions take. The third layer is the testimony of the late philosophy, in which the objection is not answered but absorbed: the materialism of the encounter abandons the guarantee that transformation has a direction or a subject, and offers instead a world in which orders crystallise and dissolve without a script, so that politics becomes the art of the conjuncture in a sense stricter than any strategist intended. The profile's assessment, standing back from the three layers, is the one the H3 answer above is obliged to give in brief. The resources are genuine and the gap is genuine too. No reader of the corpus can honestly say that its account of transformation has the density, the worked examples, and the conceptual finish of its account of reproduction; the ratio of pages is itself evidence. The framework's defenders reply, with some justice, that a theory which explained transformation as confidently as economism explained reproduction would have purchased symmetry at the price of truth, and that the honest marking of a frontier is a scientific virtue rather than a confession. The dispute is left, as the conventions require, with both sides standing.

## The research programmes: the state, the school, the colony

A framework's life is measured by the work it enables in other hands, and three programmes that ran on Althusserian instruments deserve a place in the profile because they carried the concepts into disciplines the master never practised. The first is the theory of the capitalist state associated with Nicos Poulantzas, who worked in Paris in Althusser's orbit and whose book of 1968 on political power and social classes built the first systematic Marxist account of the state as a structure with its own effectivity, neither a tool in the hands of a class nor a subject with a will of its own. The famous debate that followed, with Ralph Miliband defending an instrumental account from the evidence of personnel and connection, set the terms of Anglophone state theory for a decade and exported the apparatus, in the act of disputing it, into political science departments that had never heard the name of the rue d'Ulm. The second programme is the sociology of education. The ideology essay's identification of the school as the dominant apparatus of a modern formation, reproducing not only skills but submission and command in their graded forms, converged with the empirical sociology of Pierre Bourdieu and Jean-Claude Passeron on cultural inheritance and scholastic verdicts, and between them the two bodies of work transformed the study of education from a technology of improvement into an analysis of reproduction, a transformation whose vocabulary, reproduction itself, is the period's coinage. The third programme ran furthest from Paris. In Latin America, where the Chilean Marta Harnecker, who had studied in Althusser's circle, produced the elementary manual through which several generations of militants learned the concepts of mode of production, social formation, and conjuncture, the apparatus became a vernacular: the analysis of formations in which capitalist, feudal, and communal relations coexisted, the articulation of modes of production, was not an academic exercise but the working theory of movements confronting societies that the classical sequence of stages described nowhere. The dependency debates and the anthropology of peasantries absorbed the same instruments. A profile of the philosopher should register the geography without romance: the concepts travelled because they solved problems of analysis in places their inventor never visited, and they were altered, sometimes past his recognition, by the journeys. He is reported to have regarded the manual literature written in his name with the ambivalence of a man watching his instruments used by hands he could not train, which is the ordinary fate of instruments and no discredit to either party.

A smaller programme in the law deserves its sentence for the completeness of the map. Bernard Edelman, a lawyer formed in the same Parisian orbit, applied the apparatus to the juridical forms themselves, arguing in a study of photography and copyright published in the nineteen seventies that the legal subject, the author-owner whom the law of images conjures into being, is an interpellated subject in the strict sense: a place prepared by an institution, into which individuals are hailed and from which the work's real production, collective and material, is made invisible. Legal theory had not previously possessed a Marxist account of the form of law to set beside the sociology of its contents, and Edelman's work, with the wider current of critical legal studies that drew on the same sources in Britain and America, carried the concept of the subject of law out of the seminar and into the law reviews. The example is minor beside Poulantzas and the educators, and it is included because it completes the pattern the profile has been tracing across four disciplines: wherever an institution's self-description concealed its mode of operation, the instruments of the rue d'Ulm, the distinction between the lived relation and the real conditions, the subject as an effect of the apparatus that addresses it, found work to do.

The programmes also register the framework's limits from the productive side. Each of them succeeded by relaxing the doctrine it inherited: Poulantzas, by his final work, was writing of the state as a relation and a condensation of forces, language closer to the historians than to the seminar; the sociology of education accumulated its evidence by methods the epistemology of the break classified as empiricist; the Latin American analyses were driven by political urgencies that the theory of theoretical practice would have had to call, in its strict voice, ideological. The pattern is the one this profile has met at every level: the apparatus works best as a set of strong questions and partial concepts in the hands of investigators who refuse its system, and worst as a system in the hands of believers. That pattern is not a refutation. It is the biography of most frameworks that matter, and stating it is the beginning of the assessment with which the profile closes.

## The anthropologists

A fourth research programme ran on the apparatus in a field its inventor visited only in books, and its results are among the framework's most concrete achievements. French anthropology in the nineteen seventies took up the concepts of mode of production and articulation to analyse societies that the classical sequence of stages, slavery, feudalism, capitalism, had always embarrassed: the lineage societies of Africa, the tributary empires, the peasantries inserted into world markets they did not control. Emmanuel Terray, who had worked in the seminar's orbit, opened the programme with a study that read the classic ethnographies symptomatically, extracting from their data a theory their authors had not possessed; Pierre-Philippe Rey analysed the alliances of classes in the Congo and the articulation of the capitalist mode with the formations it subordinated without dissolving; Claude Meillassoux reconstructed the economics of the domestic community and the uses of its labour by the capitalism that fed on it. The programme mattered beyond its field because it submitted the apparatus to the discipline of evidence at its most unforgiving: a concept of articulation that could not organise the facts of a lineage society's bridewealth, labour migration, and chiefly extraction was worth nothing, and the concepts, suitably bent, organised them. The bending is the point the profile keeps making. Meillassoux's domestic community is not in Althusser; Rey's alliances are not in Althusser; what is in Althusser is the licence and the instruments, the permission to treat a formation as an articulated whole of instances with distinct times, and the demand that the analysis descend to the conjuncture. The anthropologists' Marxism faded with the field's turn away from political economy, and its monographs are read now mostly by specialists, but the episode remains the strongest exhibit for the defence in the trial this profile has been conducting: set to work on recalcitrant material by investigators free to amend it, the borrowed apparatus produced knowledge that no rival framework on offer produced as well.

## Misreadings this profile corrects

The conventions of this series require the correction of the standing misreadings attached to each thinker, and Althusser's are unusually well defined, since the brief for this profile lists five and the record supports all five corrections.

The first misreading makes him a structuralist in the same sense as the linguists and anthropologists whose prestige filled the Paris of his decade. He denied the label repeatedly and with precision: structuralism, as he used the word for others, named the ideology of a structure without contradiction, a combinatory of elements governed by a formal model, and he held that Marx's structures are of a different kind, articulated, uneven, and driven by the contradictions they contain. The denial was not coyness. Lévi-Strauss's kinship systems do not contain their own transformation; Althusser's social formations are supposed to contain nothing else. That the two enterprises shared a vocabulary and a city, and that his own rhetoric of the sixties invited the confusion he later protested, is part of the record; the correction stands that the identification of the two structuralisms is an error about both.

The second misreading takes the rejection of humanism as a moral position, a contempt for human beings or for the movements that march under humanist banners. The correction has been given in its place above and is repeated here because the misreading is the one with political consequences: the anti-humanism was theoretical, a judgement about what can serve as the foundation of a science of history, and its author distinguished, explicitly and repeatedly, between humanism as a theory, which he rejected, and humanism as an ideology of protest, whose functions in the world he recognised and whose banners he did not propose to tear down. Readers who quote the slogan without the qualifier are quoting half a doctrine, and the half travels badly.

### Why is the ideology essay, and not the break, the consequential piece?

Because the break sorted one author's corpus while the essay equipped a dozen disciplines. The essay on ideology and the apparatuses gave education, media studies, literary criticism, and political theory a mechanism they could use on their own materials, the interpellation of subjects by institutions, while the break remained a thesis inside Marxology, necessary there and inert elsewhere.

The third misreading, corrected by implication in that answer, enthrones the break as the contribution: the thesis that examiners test and summaries lead with. The break matters, and this profile has given it its section. But measured by consequences in the world of inquiry, the ideology essay outruns it by an order of magnitude, and Althusser's name in a bibliography of education, of film studies, of the theory of the state, is the name of the essay's author. The fourth misreading reports the self-criticism as a retraction of the whole project, a deathbed conversion spread over several years. The documents refute it: the self-criticism named a deviation, theoreticism, corrected the definition of philosophy, and reaffirmed the founding claims in the act of correcting their presentation, and the late work that followed is unintelligible as a retraction, since it radicalises the early work's deepest tendency, the refusal of guarantees, rather than retreating from it. The fifth misreading dismisses the late philosophy as a curiosity, the private metaphysics of a recluse. The correction is a matter of reading the texts and their reception: the materialism of the encounter has generated a literature of its own, has been taken up in political theory as a resource for thinking foundation and contingency, and states, in systematic form, positions toward which the whole career had been travelling. A curiosity does not reorganise a field's sense of its subject's development; the late philosophy did, by making it impossible any longer to teach the career as the rise and fall of a system, and necessary to teach it as the record of a mind that dismantled its own constructions faster than its critics could.

## The English school and its self-dissolution

The most instructive episode in the framework's travels happened in England, where the apparatus acquired, for a few years, something it never had in France: a school that believed it whole. The journal Theoretical Practice, founded in 1971, announced a programme of reconstructing historical materialism on Althusserian foundations, and its most gifted contributors, Barry Hindess and Paul Quentin Hirst, produced in 1975 a large work on pre-capitalist modes of production that carried the paired concepts of the earlier seminar across a range of historical material its inventor had never attempted. Within two years the same authors published a reconsideration that dismantled the enterprise's epistemology root and branch: the theory of knowledge as production, the distinction between the real object and the object of knowledge, the guarantee that a discourse could certify itself as science, were subjected to a critique from inside the problematic that concluded they could not be sustained, and the book's authors withdrew the foundations from under the school they had built. The episode is the cleanest natural experiment the record affords on the question this profile keeps asking. Given intelligence, discipline, and complete commitment, the apparatus could generate formidable work; given the same qualities turned on itself, it generated the demonstration that its epistemology would not bear weight. Althusser's own self-criticism had reached a milder version of the verdict by a different road, and the English school's version, arriving independently, corroborates the judgement this profile has adopted: the instruments survive their system. Hindess and Hirst went on to other work; the concepts they had sharpened, of articulation, of the specificity of instances, of the critique of empiricism in historiography, passed into the common stock of the social sciences in Britain, where they remain in use by scholars who would not describe themselves by the school's name. The dispersal is the pattern of the whole career repeated in one national miniature: a seminar, a journal, a system, a self-criticism, a diaspora of tools.

## The feminist engagement

A further reception deserves record because it tested the apparatus on the question the epoch came to regard as decisive. Feminist theorists of the nineteen seventies and eighties found in the framework both an instrument and an obstacle, and the double finding produced some of the most exact criticism the work received. The instrument was the theory of ideology: an account in which subjects are constituted by practices and rituals, in which the family figures as an ideological apparatus in its own right, and in which identity is an effect rather than an origin, offered a feminism then fighting its way out of both liberal individualism and biological essentialism a machinery for thinking how women are made, in their own self-understanding, by the order that subordinates them. Michèle Barrett's study of the ideology of women's oppression, published in 1980 and revised by its author a decade later with a candour unusual in the literature, built its first edition substantially on these instruments and recorded, in the revision, both their yield and their limits. The obstacle was symmetrical to the instrument. A theory whose subject is constituted by interpellation in general has no native account of why the subject is sexed, of why the apparatuses hail women and men into different places, and the framework's own texts are, on the division of labour inside the family and on the specificity of women's subordination, nearly silent: the family appears in the famous list of apparatuses and is analysed hardly at all. Critics pressed the point as an instance of the general objection, that the structure's places are assigned without a theory of who is assigned to them, and defenders replied that the instruments were built to be completed by the analysis of each formation's concrete arrangements. The exchange left the framework marked in a way the profile should not minimise: on the question that reorganised the left's thought in the decades after his death, the apparatus is a resource its author never used, and the feminist literature that used it did so by amending it past the point its orthodoxy could bless. That is, once more, the pattern of the instruments outliving the system, with the amendment counted, this time, as the amendment's authors insisted, against the system as well as for it.

## The cultural studies inheritance

The most populous of the framework's afterlives grew in Birmingham rather than Paris. Stuart Hall, directing the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies through the nineteen seventies, conducted the most sustained experiment in running Althusserian instruments on materials their inventor never touched: television, policing, the popular press, the politics of race in a declining imperial power. Hall's famous formulation of a Marxism without guarantees, delivered as a lecture on the problem of ideology in the nineteen eighties, is the experiment's theoretical report: from Althusser he took the critique of economism, the specificity of instances, ideology as the production of subjects, and the refusal of expressive totalities; from Gramsci, by the road the comparison article in this series describes, he took hegemony and the conjuncture; and the synthesis discarded, with thanks, the master's science and ideology frontier and the last instance alike. The concept of articulation, developed in the same milieu and in the work of Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe on hegemony, whose book of 1985 built its post-Marxism substantially on a radicalised overdetermination, carried the instruments into the theory of politics itself, where they remain active in debates their originator could not have foreseen. The inheritance is the largest single counterexample to the verdict that the framework died with its institutions. In the seminar's own city the school dispersed; abroad, in disciplines unborn when the essays were written, the instruments went on working in hands that amended them freely, which is the form of survival that the profile's assessment has already identified as the career's pattern. Hall's own judgement of the transaction, recorded in interviews, is the balanced one this profile adopts: that the encounter with Althusser was the condition of his generation's best work and an encounter to be struggled with, not a doctrine to be applied, and that the struggle, conducted in good faith on both sides of the Channel, is what a living tradition looks like from inside.

The Birmingham experiment produced, in the collective study of policing and the panic over mugging published in 1978, the single most accomplished application of the combined instruments to a living conjuncture: a crime wave and its amplification traced through the media, the courts, the police, and the state, each instance granted its own effectivity, the whole read as the preparation of an authoritarian turn in a society whose economic order was failing. Hall's name for the settlement then being assembled by the British right, authoritarian populism, entered the language, and the analysis behind it is unthinkable without the school's lessons in the specificity of instances and the refusal of the expressive whole, even where its authors corrected those lessons by Gramsci's. The profile counts the book as the framework's most successful grandchild: a work of empirical sociology and political intervention at once, written by a collective, in a conjuncture, against a deadline, which is to say, under exactly the conditions the seminar's epistemology had declared impossible for knowledge and the seminar's practice had quietly observed. The irony is benign and is left standing, as the best findings about this school usually are.

## Translations and the wider world

The framework's conquest of the world's universities was a material process with dates, publishers, and translators, and a profile that tracks the borrowed apparatus should track its distribution with the same care. The English translations came through London: For Marx in 1969 and Reading Capital in 1970, in versions by Ben Brewster whose renderings of the key terms, problematic, overdetermination, interpellation in due course, set the vocabulary of the Anglophone debate; the Lenin lectures and the essays on ideology followed, and the journal New Left Review served as the transmission belt, publishing translations, interviews, and the first rounds of the English argument. In Italy the reception was older and more combative, since Italian Marxism possessed its own philosophical traditions, the historicism descending from Gramsci through the party's intellectuals and the rival school of Galvano Della Volpe, whose anti-Hegelian, Kantian Marxism had anticipated several of Althusser's moves by a different road; the Italian debate between the two anti-historicisms, conducted by Lucio Colletti among others with a ferocity the French found bracing, remains the most philosophically literate of the national receptions. In the Spanish-speaking world the translations issued from Mexico and Buenos Aires and Madrid in a stream that the dictatorships of the period repeatedly interrupted and never stopped, and the concepts entered the Latin American debates on modes of production and dependency in the forms described above. In Germany the reception was delayed and partial, filtered through a Social Democratic and a Frankfurt tradition that each had reasons to resist a French Leninism arriving with Spinoza in its luggage. The pattern of the whole is worth a sentence: the framework travelled best where a strong communist culture existed to argue with it and a university system existed to house the argument, and it faded fastest where either condition failed, which is a materialist footnote its author would have been obliged to appreciate.

The translations also fixed certain fortunes. Brewster's English made overdetermination a word of the language; the French surdétermination had carried the Freudian echo more audibly, and the Anglophone debate about the concept was partly a debate about a translation. The abridged English Reading Capital, missing two of the seminar's contributions, presented a more purely Althusserian book than the French two volumes had been, and the Anglophone image of the enterprise as one man's system owes something to the cut. A profile is not the place to prosecute these bibliographical matters, but it is the place to record that the object the world argued about was, in each language, a slightly different object, and that the unity of the international Althusser debate is partly an artefact of footnotes. The archive, to which the profile now turns, has made the fuller object available to a later generation of readers, with consequences still unfolding.

## The archive and the posthumous books

Althusser wrote far more than he published, and the proportion inverted after his death. The papers deposited with the institute that administers his literary estate contain the seminar lectures, the correspondence, the drafts of books abandoned at every stage, and the autobiographical writings, and from the early nineteen nineties the editors of the archive, François Matheron chief among them, issued the unpublished work in a sequence that amounted to a second career conducted posthumously: the writings on psychoanalysis, the book on reproduction, the Machiavelli, the texts of the materialism of the encounter, the lectures on Rousseau and on the philosophy of the Enlightenment, the treatise on how to be a Marxist in philosophy, written in 1976 and published four decades later, in which the definition of philosophy as class struggle in theory receives its most systematic and most personal statement. The memoir appeared in the same years and is governed, for this profile, by the rule stated in the passage above: it is a document of the life, mined by biographers, and no part of its account of the central catastrophe is quoted or paraphrased here.

The posthumous career changed the scholarship in ways a profile should register. The early image of the work, a system announced in 1965 and abandoned in confusion, has been replaced in the specialist literature by a longer and stranger curve: a philosopher whose unpublished production was continuous, whose published interventions were the visible fraction of a labour mostly conducted in lectures, letters, and drafts, and whose last philosophy had roots his contemporaries could not have suspected, in the Machiavelli lectures of the nineteen sixties and the Rousseau seminar. The editors' labours also settled, as far as documents can, several disputes of fact that the polemical literature had traded in: the dating of the self-criticism's components, the extent of the party's interventions in his publishing, the chronology of the illnesses and the teaching. A reference profile written from the current state of the archive differs from one written in 1985 as an aeroplane differs from a kite, and the difference is a debt this article acknowledges on behalf of its readers: the facts assembled above about the life and the bibliography rest on the archival scholarship of the last three decades, and the interpretations remain, as interpretations must, the responsibility of the profile and of the critics it quotes.

## The backlash at the decade's end

The climate in which the career ended was not the climate in which it had risen, and the profile closes its record of the public years with the reversal, because the reversal explains the silence that greeted much of the late work. In the middle nineteen seventies the French intellectual field turned against Marxism with a speed that startled the participants on every side. The translation of Solzhenitsyn's account of the Soviet camps had detonated in Paris in 1974, and a generation of younger intellectuals, many of them formed in the Maoist organisations that the seminar's penumbra had supplied, concluded that the problem was not this or that deviation of the doctrine but the doctrine's will to totality itself. The new philosophers, as the media christened them, made the case in bestselling books and television studios: that every Marxism carried the camp inside its concepts, that the master thinkers of the rue d'Ulm were the camp's metaphysicians, and that the ethics of the dissident, the single voice against the system, was the only politics the century had left intact. Althusser's name stood near the centre of the indictment, as the system's most sophisticated French form. His replies were made in the registers this profile has recorded: the internal critique of the party, the declaration of the crisis, the insistence that the record of the socialist states was a theoretical problem to be worked on rather than a verdict to be fled from. The media verdict went the other way, and by the end of the decade the climate of French intellectual life had changed so completely that the questions of 1965 sounded, to the new common sense, like dispatches from a buried civilisation. The profile records the backlash without adopting its terms, which were journalism's terms, and without dismissing its cause, which was the record of the states. It also records the asymmetry that the backlash created in the reputation: the framework's fall from fashion was total in the country of its birth and partial everywhere else, so that the afterlives documented in this profile, in cultural studies, in state theory, in Latin America, proceeded abroad while the name was being interred at home. A thinker's reception is part of his record, and the record here is of a reputation that died in Paris in the late nineteen seventies and went on working, under its own name and under others', in places the Parisian obituaries did not reach.

## Whether he is still read

The question that closes most searches on this name deserves a documented answer rather than a gesture. The evidence of continued life is of four kinds. The texts remain in print and in translation, in the major languages of the Marxist tradition and beyond it, with the posthumous works still appearing in new editions prepared from the archive deposited after his death, an archive whose catalogue of manuscripts, lectures, and correspondence continues to yield volumes. The concepts remain in working use, often detached from their source in the way of successful instruments: interpellation is a term of art in media and cultural studies whose users could not all name its author, overdetermination circulates in political analysis, symptomatic reading is a method taught in literature departments under several flags. The tradition that descends from the seminar continues to argue with him in public: Balibar's work on citizenship and on Spinoza carries the enterprise forward by revising it, and the philosophers who formed themselves against the master, Rancière above all, define their positions by the distance, which is a form of presence no syllabus can confer. And the quarrels remain open in the form this profile has recorded them: agency against structure, science against ideology, the subject against its dissolution, so that a student meeting the questions for the first time meets them, whether the syllabus says so or not, in the shapes Althusser gave them.

Against that evidence stands the honest counterweight. The school as a school is gone; there is no Althusserian orthodoxy, no journal, no party of theory, and the Marxism that survives in the universities speaks other dialects, historical, analytical, ecological, feminist, each of which took from the apparatus what it needed and left the rest. The man's name carries, for the general public that knows it at all, the association recorded in the short passage above, and that association has measurably thinned the readership that the work's quality, judged by the standards applied to his contemporaries, would predict. The profile's closing assessment refuses to resolve these two columns into a verdict of survival or oblivion, because the record supports a more exact statement. The system failed: there is no science of history of the kind announced in 1965, and its author was the first competent witness to the failure. The instruments succeeded: a reader equipped with the table of concepts and sources at the centre of this profile can do work, on texts, on institutions, on conjunctures, that cannot be done as well without them. The life is documented, in full, in the sections above, and the work is documented beside it, and the discipline with which the two accounts have been kept apart is the condition on which a reference work can offer both. What a reader does with the separation, whether it reads as integrity or as an evasion of the oldest question about thinkers, whether the work can be honoured while the act is condemned, is a judgement the profile has equipped and deliberately not made. The tradition he served has a formula for such cases, that men make their own history but not in circumstances of their choosing. His circumstances included a school, a party, an illness, and an act. The history he made includes the concepts in the table. Both sentences are in the record, and neither is permitted, here, to swallow the other.

## A route through the works

The profile ends its exposition with a practical question that readers of a reference article are entitled to have answered: in what order should the works themselves be read? The corpus resists the chronological route, since the famous books are the hardest and the easiest text is an essay of a few dozen pages. The route recommended here follows the order of intelligibility rather than of composition, and it assumes the table of concepts and sources as the reader's companion throughout. Begin with the essay on ideology and the state apparatuses, which is short, concrete, and complete in itself, and which contains, in the theses on ideology's material existence and on interpellation, the doctrine at its most portable. Read next the two programmatic essays of For Marx that carry the politics and the method, the essay on contradiction and overdetermination and the essay on the young Marx, in that order, so that the break is met after the concept it was built to serve. Then take the essay on Marxism and humanism together with the reply to John Lewis, which between them stage the anti-humanist quarrel in its original voices and at its original temperature. Only then attempt Reading Capital, and within it the director's own two essays before Balibar's, since the concepts of structural causality and of the problematic are forged in the former and applied in the latter. The self-criticism should be read immediately afterwards, while the system it corrects is still warm in the reader's mind, and the Lenin lectures with it, since the redefinition of philosophy is unintelligible except as the correction's positive face. The late philosophy comes last and gains everything from coming last: the materialism of the encounter reads as a testament when its predecessors are known and as a riddle when they are not, and the Machiavelli book, the most finished of the posthumous works, makes the best door into it. Two warnings belong to the route. The memoir is not on it: it is a document for biographers and for readers who have finished the works, not an introduction to them, and the rule of this profile, which quotes none of it, is recommended to students as a rule of method rather than of taste. And the secondary literature, copious and quarrelsome, should be rationed until the primary circuit is complete, since the critics named in this profile are most valuable, Thompson included, when the reader can check their indictments against the texts at first hand. The whole circuit, taken at seminar pace, is a term's work, and it returns a reader who can say, with the documents open, what the borrowed apparatus was, what it did, what it cost, and why a discipline of keeping accounts, the man's from the work's and the work's from its enemies', is the price of knowing any of it exactly.

## For students and exam candidates

Begin with the table of concepts and sources in this profile and learn it as a map before attempting the books, because examination questions on this thinker punish the candidate who has absorbed a portrait and reward the one who can assign each concept its job and its origin. The examinable core is the break, overdetermination, and the ideology essay; the series article devoted to that essay and the state apparatuses should be your second stop after this profile. Build a study card for each concept with four faces, claim, source, problem, objection, and file the cards in a durable system such as the [Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) companion, so that the table's rows become retrievable under pressure. In answers, separate the two breaks this profile distinguishes, present Thompson's objection before your marker raises it, and never let the biography do the work of an argument, in either direction: scripts that dismiss the theory by the life, and scripts that excuse the life by the theory, are marked down by the same examiners for the same reason.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The profile is designed to be taught against itself. Set the concepts table as the first week's exercise and require students to defend one row's source attribution against the class, since the borrowed apparatus becomes real to a seminar when the borrowings are argued rather than recited. The debate your syllabus needs next is staged in the series article devoted to the humanist against the structuralist controversy, where the opposing case is given in its own terms; pair it with a session on the self-criticism, in which the author marks his own work more severely than most of his critics managed, and a session on the strongest objection, the problem of change, where the students should be required to construct the answer from overdetermination before being shown its limits. Handle the biographical record as this profile does: state it once, in its place, with the victim named and her standing noted, and treat any attempt, prurient or exculpatory, to make the record interpret the theory as the seminar's first correctable error. The discipline is itself the lesson's transferable content.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three cautions govern research use of this material. First, distinguish the published interventions from the archive: the positions of the books of 1965 were revised in print by their author within a decade and supplemented after his death by a posthumous corpus that changes the curve of the career, so cite the stratum, not the name. Second, for the central concept in its technical form, work from the dedicated treatment of overdetermination and the analysis of conjunctures rather than from summaries, and for the holdings, editions, and reading routes through the whole tradition, use the series' [guide to reading Western Marxism and its archives](/2016/03/15/western-marxism-reading-guide/). Third, on the events of November 1980, the documented facts are those stated in this profile's short passage: the date, the judicial finding that he was incapable of responsibility, the decision against a trial, the hospital years, the death in 1990. Report them at that weight. The literature of speculation about motive is large and evidentially worthless, and the memoir, which journalists reach for first, is a party to the case rather than a witness to it; this profile quotes none of it, and the practice is recommended.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Louis Althusser?

A French philosopher, born in Algeria in 1918 and dead in 1990, who spent more than thirty years teaching at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris and belonged throughout his adult life to the French Communist Party. From that double position he rebuilt the interpretation of Marx around a set of imported concepts: a rupture within Marx's own development, the overdetermination of contradictions, an immanent causality of structures, and ideology understood as institutional practice. His seminar formed a generation of French thinkers, and his later years were marked by a published self-criticism, a declaration that Marxism was in crisis, and a final philosophy published after his death.

### Q: What did Althusser contribute to Marxist theory?

Four contributions outlast the system they were built to serve. He redated Marx's intellectual development, placing a rupture between the humanist writings of the eighteen forties and the science of history founded afterwards. He gave the analysis of revolutions a concept for contradictions that act only in company, never singly. He supplied a way of thinking a social whole that is present solely through its effects rather than expressed by an essence. And he relocated ideology from false belief to institutional routine, arguing that schools, churches, and families form the subjects who then experience that formation as freedom.

### Q: What is symptomatic reading?

It is a discipline of attention to what a text fails to say. The reader inventories a work's concepts, then hunts the points where the argument leans on a term it nowhere defines, answers a question it never posed, or breaks off where a notion is required and missing. Such lapses are treated as symptoms, evidence of the unspoken framework that governed what the author could formulate. He modelled the practice on the dream interpretation of Freud and on the blind spot Marx had exposed in Adam Smith, then applied it to Capital itself, claiming to recover the theory Marx practised without stating.

### Q: What is structural causality?

It names a kind of causation in which the cause cannot be separated from its results. A social structure, on this account, is not a hidden engine behind events, nor a spirit that each event expresses; it exists only as the arrangement of relations among institutions, practices, and classes, and it is effective precisely as that arrangement. Althusser took the model from Spinoza's doctrine of an immanent cause and set it against two rivals: the mechanical picture of one body striking another, and the expressive picture in which every part of a society mirrors a single inner principle.

### Q: Who did Althusser teach?

His classroom at the school in the rue d'Ulm was a junction of postwar French thought. Foucault and Derrida passed through his tuition in the earlier cohorts; the seminar on Capital was staffed by Pierre Macherey, Roger Establet, Jacques Rancière, and Étienne Balibar; Alain Badiou and Régis Debray were among his students; Michel Pêcheux built a theory of discourse under his influence; and the analysts around Jacques-Alain Miller shared his corridors during the years when Lacan's seminar also sat in the building. Almost none remained his disciples, which the record suggests he counted a success of teaching rather than a failure of doctrine.

### Q: What is a problematic?

The word denotes the invisible architecture beneath a body of thought: the connected set of questions, concepts, and exclusions that decides in advance what its holder can perceive as a problem and what will never come into view. A problematic is not an opinion and cannot be corrected by information, since it governs which information registers as evidence. Althusser borrowed the notion from the French philosophy of science, crediting his student Jacques Martin with its sharpening, and wielded it to explain how the young Marx could be simultaneously brilliant and blind, sighted wherever his framework had eyes and blind wherever it lacked them.

### Q: Did Althusser criticise his own work?

Yes, in print, in detail, and at the height of his fame. In texts of the nineteen seventies he charged his earlier system with theoreticism, meaning that the line between science and ideology had been policed by philosophy acting as its own supreme court, with class struggle present as a formula rather than a force. He redefined philosophy accordingly, as the class struggle continued inside theory, and conceded ground on the mechanical use of the break. The correction was not a withdrawal: the founding claims were restated in the same breath, which is why readers still argue over whether the episode was a rectification or a relaunch.

### Q: What happened to Althusser's wife?

Hélène Rytmann, a sociologist and a veteran of the French Resistance who had shared his life for over thirty years, was killed by her husband in November 1980 in their rooms at the school. Psychiatrists who examined him judged him incapable of responsibility; the courts ordered that no trial be held, and he was placed in hospital care, from which he was released three years later. This profile sets out the facts once, in the section on his final decade, and holds to the rule that neither the philosophy nor the crime may be used to interpret the other.

### Q: What is aleatory materialism?

It is the name given to the philosophy of his last years, published after his death, in which history is stripped of every guarantee. Elements drift, meet, and sometimes lock together into durable forms; a form that lasts then rewrites its own past as though it had been destined. His emblem was the swerve of atoms in Epicurus and Lucretius, and his reader was Machiavelli, whose founder succeeds or fails by a conjunction no doctrine can schedule. Against the earlier system, there is no last instance and no appointed direction: necessity is only the name we give, afterwards, to encounters that happened to hold.

### Q: Why did Thompson attack Althusser so fiercely?

Because he believed the system abolished the things his own Marxism existed to defend. Edward Thompson, historian of the English working class, argued that a doctrine of structures and their supports leaves men and women as carriers of a process they do not make, that it cannot narrate the passage from one society to another, and that its division between those licensed to read symptoms and those condemned to live them reproduced, inside theory, the hierarchy of a party over its class. His book of 1978 pressed the case with unmatched polemical force, and the exchange that followed remains the standard English-language introduction to the quarrel.

### Q: How does the borrowed apparatus explain the eclecticism charge?

The charge arises directly from the inventory. When a thinker's developmental concept comes from the philosophy of science, his master term for contradiction from dream interpretation, his causality from a seventeenth-century metaphysician, and his subject from psychoanalysis, critics ask what remains that is Marx's. The apparatus explains both the accusation and its partial answer: each import was hired for a repair the home tradition had failed to perform, and each changed meaning in the hiring. Whether the result is creative reconstruction or a foreign system wearing a Marxist badge is the question the critics' sections of this profile leave deliberately open.

### Q: What should a student separate between the two breaks in his career?

Two ruptures share one word and are constantly confused. The first is the break he located inside Marx's writings, dated to the middle of the eighteen forties, between an ideological youth organised around man's essence and a maturity organised around modes of production. The second is the break inside his own work: the self-criticism of the nineteen seventies, which indicted his theoreticism and recast philosophy as struggle within theory, followed by the late philosophy of the encounter. An answer that merges them, crediting Marx with the son's corrections or the father with the son's doubts, loses marks and the thread of the career alike.

### Q: Why did the reading of Capital become a collective project at the ENS?

Because the undertaking exceeded one reader's span and the school was organised to prove it. The seminar of 1965 divided Marx's book among five participants, each charged with a territory, its concepts, its borrowings from classical economy, its silences, and set their papers to interlock under a shared discipline of reading. The collective form was also an argument: against the cult of the solitary interpreter, it exhibited knowledge as a workshop product, made by division of labour and mutual correction. The volume that resulted carried five names because the method, honestly practised, required them.

### Q: How does the profile keep a life and a theory in separate accounts?

By assigning each order of fact its own evidence and forbidding transfers between them. The theory is reconstructed from published texts, from the revisions their author printed, and from named opponents who answered them; it stands or falls by those documents. The biography is reconstructed from dates, posts, publications, and court records, and it too is stated in full, including the killing of Hélène Rytmann, which is recorded once and without embroidery. No passage permits the man's record to grade the arguments, and none permits the arguments to mitigate the record; the separation is the profile's method, stated openly so readers can audit it.

### Q: What separates his structuralism from the structuralism of the linguists?

A single word: contradiction. The structuralism of the linguists and anthropologists models systems as combinations of elements under formal rules, elegant and static, in which change arrives from outside the model or not at all. His structures are articulated wholes whose levels develop unevenly, rub against one another, and carry the sources of their transformation inside them; the whole point of overdetermination is that a structure specifies its contradictions rather than abolishing them. He therefore refused the label throughout his career, allowing only that he had shared a city, a decade, and a vocabulary with the movement the label properly names.
