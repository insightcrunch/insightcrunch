## The Argument in Brief: What Counting the Play's Images Proves, and What It Cannot

Caroline Spurgeon's imagery clusters remain the most consequential single idea in the study of this play's language, and the most contested. In Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us, published in 1935, Spurgeon reported the result of counting and classifying every image in Romeo and Juliet: the play's dominant cluster yokes light against darkness, sun, moon, stars, torches, lightning and dawn ranged against night, gloom and the tomb, and the lovers burn at the centre of that cluster as brief brightness inside a dark and violent city. The finding rebuilt the way the play is read. Before Spurgeon, imagery in criticism of the play meant the famous single lines, quoted and admired one at a time. After Spurgeon, imagery meant a system: hundreds of small pictures accumulating into an atmosphere, a design that a reader feels before being able to name it. The cliche quotes the single image. The count reveals the weather system the single image belongs to.

The verdict argued here has two halves, and both halves matter. The first half is that Spurgeon's central finding for this play survives every objection raised against her method. Light against darkness is not a pattern a counter projected onto the text. The cluster concentrates in the lovers' mouths, strikes at the play's hinges, and thins after Act 3 in step with the catastrophe, and a pattern that knows where the turning points are is a pattern the play itself maintains. The second half is that the count alone never proves any of this. A tally cannot distinguish a structuring symbol from a repeated habit, cannot weigh one image placed at a death against twenty decorative ones, and cannot keep its own boundaries honest, because the counter decides what counts as one image and which cluster receives it. Spurgeon's method is an instrument, not a verdict machine. Used as an instrument, audited by speaker, position and function, it tells the truth about this play more reliably than any rival instrument of its generation. Used as a verdict machine, it flattens the very contexts that give images their force.

That two-part verdict shapes everything that follows. The method gets a full hearing first: who Spurgeon was, how the counting worked in practice, what it found in this play, and what the play's clusters look like when set out in one table. Then the case against counting gets its full hearing: Wolfgang Clemen's demand that images be studied in their development, L. C. Knights's attack on documentary reading, the weighting problem, and the embarrassing dependence of every total on the edition counted and the counter's filing decisions. Then the descendants: Edward A. Armstrong's refinement of the cluster idea, the method's mid-century eclipse, and its revival in the age of the searchable text. The closing sections propose a working protocol for honest cluster analysis and test Spurgeon's light finding against it, test by test.

## Who Was Caroline Spurgeon, and Why Did She Start Counting Images?

![Caroline Spurgeon and the imagery clusters of Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Caroline Frances Eleanor Spurgeon (1869-1942) held the chair of English literature at Bedford College, University of London, from 1913, among the first women in England to hold a university chair in the subject, and she built her reputation in a profession that still treated systematic evidence as a masculine habit and appreciation as the critic's whole duty. Her road to the imagery work ran through comparison. In Shakespeare and the Italian Tradition (1931), she examined the imagery of Shakespeare alongside that of his fellow dramatists and argued from its Italian colouring, its sports, its gardens and its sea, about the mind behind the plays. The comparison required a technique that appreciation could not supply: if one dramatist's plays were to be set against another's, the pictures inside them had to be extracted, sorted and counted. The same year, 1931, her British Academy Shakespeare Lecture, Shakespeare's Iterative Imagery, presented the core idea to the profession: that a play's imagery is iterative, repeated in groups, and that the groups, not the isolated beauties, carry the play's atmosphere and much of its meaning.

The full statement came four years later. Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us (1935) reported a labour of years: every image in the plays read out, recorded, classified by subject, and charted, so that each play could be described by the image groups that dominated it. The title carries the whole ambition in its two halves. The first half, the imagery, is the evidence: the counted clusters. The second half, what it tells us, is the claim built on the evidence: that the clusters reveal the themes of individual plays and, beyond them, the tastes, sympathies and experience of the man who wrote them. Her wider influence on the profession is traced in the companion study of [Caroline Spurgeon and the Imagery Revolution](/2027/03/05/caroline-spurgeon-imagery-revolution/), which follows the institutional side of the story, her students, her charts pinned up in lecture rooms, and the generation of women scholars who took the method into schools and universities.

The context explains both the boldness and the blind spots. Spurgeon worked against a criticism of impression: the eminent lecture that praised Shakespeare's magic in general terms and quoted three lines. Her count was a discipline imposed on that vagueness, a demand that claims about a play's atmosphere produce their evidence. At the same time she inherited from nineteenth-century biographical criticism the assumption that a writer's work is a document of the writer's life, and the second half of her title spends the count's earnings on that assumption. The two inheritances pull in opposite directions through the whole book, and every later quarrel with Spurgeon is, at bottom, a quarrel about which inheritance should govern.

### What exactly did Spurgeon mean by an image?

Spurgeon used the word broadly and deliberately: any picture or word-picture by which an abstract idea or a direct statement is given sensory body. Similes and metaphors qualify first, then comparisons implied rather than stated, personifications, and the compressed pictures inside single charged words when a speech leans on them.

The breadth was a choice with consequences audited later in this article: the wider the unit, the larger the count and the more the counter's judgement decides the result. It also separated her unit from the grammarian's trope and from the rhetorician's figure, both narrower, both cleaner, and both too small to hold the weather she was trying to measure.

Her explanation of why repetition matters deserves precise statement, because later critics often attacked a cruder version of her claim than the one she made. She did not argue that Shakespeare sat down with a scheme of symbols and distributed them by plan. She argued that the mind of a poet works by association and recurrence, that certain pictures rise again and again in a given play because they are bound to the play's central feeling, and that a reader who gathers the recurrences can recover the feeling's shape. The cluster, in her handling, is closer to a climate than to a code. A climate is real, measurable in its effects, and nobody supposes a climate was designed cloud by cloud. Whether the climate metaphor can carry all the weight she loaded onto it is one of the questions the critique section takes up.

Her standing in the profession when the book appeared also shaped how the findings were received. She was not an outsider with a pamphlet. She was a senior professor, a founder figure in the academic study of English, and the book arrived with the authority of an institution behind it. That authority protected the method in its first decade and made the eventual reaction against it sharper, because the reaction was aimed not only at a technique but at the establishment that had certified it.

## How the Counting Worked: Slips, Classes, Charts and Leading Images

The mechanics matter, because the critique lands on the mechanics. Spurgeon's procedure, carried out with assistants over years of reading, had four stages. First, extraction: the play was read through slowly and every image was copied out. Second, classification: each image was filed under a subject heading, the headings covering the great provinces of early modern experience, nature and the seasons, animals and birds, the sea and weather, the body and sickness, food and household life, war and weapons, sport and hunting, light and darkness, and the rest. Third, aggregation: the files for a single play were totalled, and the totals compared within the play, to find which groups dominated, and across plays, to find what distinguished one play's stock from another's. Fourth, interpretation: the dominant groups, which she called the play's leading images, were read as the key to its atmosphere and theme, and the whole stock was mined for what it told about the author.

The charts were the famous visible product. On large diagrammatic sheets she plotted the image groups of play against play, so that a viewer could see at a glance that one tragedy ran on sickness, another on clothing, another on light. The chart did rhetorical work that prose could not: it made the pattern look like a fact of nature, the way a graph of rainfall makes a climate look settled. Defenders and attackers both understood the chart's power. The defence said the chart merely displayed what the reading had found. The attack said the chart laundered judgement into data, because every decision about what counted and where it filed had been taken before the chart was drawn, invisibly, by hand.

### Could two counters, given the same play, ever reach the same total?

Almost certainly not, and Spurgeon's own book concedes the pressure points. An image can span a word, a phrase or a whole speech. A star can file under light, under the heavens, or under fate. A torch can file under light or under household goods. Two honest counters will therefore differ.

The totals will drift furthest apart exactly where the material is richest, because rich passages generate borderline cases fastest. Totals of this kind are therefore best understood as measurements carrying an unstated margin of error, a point the protocol section turns into a rule, and any critic quoting a Spurgeon figure without its margin is quoting half a measurement.

Three features of the procedure deserve emphasis before the findings are examined, because the findings inherit all three. The count is comprehensive in intention: nothing was sampled, the whole play was read, which separates Spurgeon's method from the quotation-hunting it replaced. The count is indifferent to position: an image in the Prologue and an image at a death weigh the same in the file, a blindness the critique exploits. And the count is indifferent to speaker: the file records that a play contains so many images of disease, not who speaks them, although the meaning of a disease image changes entirely between a lover's metaphor and a dying man's curse. Those two indifferences are the price of the method's simplicity, and the verdict sections will ask what must be added back before the method's results can be trusted.

One more feature of the original work is often forgotten in the quarrels. Spurgeon counted comparatively from the start. Her charts set playwright against playwright and play against play, and many of her sharpest findings are relative: not that a play contains light images, since every Elizabethan play does, but that this play contains them in a proportion its fellows do not match. Relative claims survive classification disputes better than absolute ones, because two counters who file differently will still usually agree about which of two plays leans harder on a given stock. A good deal of the adjudication below turns on that distinction.

## Dead Metaphors and the Grey Band: What Counts as One Image

Every difficulty in Spurgeon's method can be traced back to a single unglamorous question that her book never quite settles: what, exactly, is one image? The question sounds like bookkeeping. It is actually the whole philosophy of the method in miniature, because the totals that made her reputation are sums of units, and the units are not found in nature. They are cut out of the verse by a reader's decision, and different readers cut at different joints. This section works through the cutting problem with examples from the play, because the problem is easier to see in instances than in definitions, and because a reader who understands the unit problem understands both why Spurgeon's findings persuaded and why they could never be final.

At the clear end of the scale stand the formal comparisons, the similes with their joints showing. When Juliet says "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, My love as deep" (2.2.133-134), no counter hesitates: here is an image, countable, classifiable under the sea. Metaphors of the copula kind stand nearly as clear: "Juliet is the sun" (2.2.3) announces itself. Personifications stand clear too, when Death is wooing or Fortune is turning her wheel. A method built only of such instances would be tidy, and its totals would be small, and it would miss most of the play's pictorial life, because Shakespeare's imagery more often works by implication, compression and single charged words than by formal comparison.

The grey band begins where implication begins. Consider the Prologue's most famous phrase: the lovers are "star-crossed" (Prologue, line 6). Is that one image? It is a single compound word carrying a whole astrology inside it, destiny figured as an adverse sky. File it, and the fate cluster gains a unit from the play's sixth line; a counter who demands a developed picture passes it by, and the fate cluster starts empty. Consider Romeo's cry over Tybalt's body and his own act: "O, I am fortune's fool" (3.1.131). Fortune here is personified by grammar alone, a possessor with a fool for property. Consider Juliet's "mask of night" (2.2.85): night is not compared to a mask, it is handed the mask's function, concealment of a blush, in two words. Each of these does pictorial work in performance. Each sits in the grey band where one counter's image is another counter's idiom.

Dead metaphors widen the band further. Verona speaks a commercial language of worth, price and debt so habitually that its metaphors arrive pre-spent: love is reckoned, beauty is treasure, a life is forfeit. Some of these are dead in the strict sense, figures no hearer pictures. Some are resurrected by context, as when the Prince prices the feud's damage and the dead figure of the account book stands up and walks. A counter must either file every commercial word, inflating the money cluster with cliches of the language itself, or file only the resurrected ones, exercising in every instance the interpretive judgement the count was supposed to suspend. Spurgeon's practice leaned inclusive, and her clusters are correspondingly broad. Armstrong's word-anchored revision leaned the other way. The choice between them is not a technicality; it is a decision about whether a cluster measures a play's imagination or a period's vocabulary, and honest work states the choice and its direction.

The unit problem has one more twist, unique to drama. A stage image is not always verbal. Torches enter with the masquers in Act 1 and with Paris and Romeo in Act 5; they are carried, lit, visible, and they do the light cluster's work in the theatre without a word being spoken. Spurgeon counted the words, as a reader must. A performance count would add the carried flames, the window's practised glow, the dawn that the staging asserts while the lovers deny it. The two counts measure different objects, the text's stock and the event's stock, and neither is the truer one; but a critic who moves between them without marking the crossing will quote the text count as evidence about the stage effect, a quiet equivocation that runs through a good deal of imagery criticism. The protocol's first rule, declare the unit, is meant to catch exactly this: to force the counter to say whether the file records words, pictures, or properties, and to live with the consequences of the answer.

It is worth pausing on why Spurgeon, an exact scholar in other respects, left the unit loose. The looseness was the price of the harvest. A strict unit, formal comparisons only, would have produced counts too small to chart and a method too weak to found a school. Her inclusive unit gathered the play's whole pictorial weather, at the cost of totals no successor could reproduce. Founders regularly make that trade, breadth now against rigour later, and the fair historical judgement is that the trade was worth making in 1935 and indefensible by 1955, when the school existed and the protocols were due. Her critics were right about the unit and wrong to let the unit's looseness bury the harvest, which, as the verdict section's audits show, survives stricter counting in its main lines.

## A Worked Example: Filing the Feast Scene

Abstract accounts of method persuade less than one scene filed in the open, so this section runs Spurgeon's procedure over the feast in Act 1 Scene 5, slowly, showing every decision the counter must take and what each decision costs. The scene is a fair test because it is short, famous, and dense: the lovers meet inside it, the feud looks on inside it, and its master speech is the light cluster's founding document. A counter approaching the scene with slips in hand begins, as Spurgeon's assistants began, by extraction, reading for pictures and copying them out in order.

The extractions accumulate fast. Capulet's hall is dressed with light before anyone speaks of love: torches are carried in with the masquers, and the room's brightness is a staged fact the words keep touching. Romeo's first speech at the sight of Juliet yields, in Weis's text, a run of extractions in six lines: "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night Like a rich jewel in an Ethiope's ear; Beauty too rich for use, for earth too dear. So shows a snowy dove trooping with crows As yonder lady o'er her fellows shows" (1.5.43-48). The speech closes with a vow and a renunciation built from sight itself: "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight! For I ne'er saw true beauty till this night" (1.5.51-52). Tybalt, recognising the voice, contributes the scene's counter-pictures, the recognised enemy, the fetched rapier, the scorn he is ordered to swallow. The sonnet the lovers then speak adds the pilgrim and the saint, palms and prayer and sin purged by lips, a whole sacred file inside fourteen lines.

Now the filing, and with it the method's character laid bare. The torches file under light, surely; but they are also household goods, a rich man's furniture, carried by servants, and Capulet's magnificence is part of their meaning. The jewel files under light as a shining thing, or under wealth as a precious thing, or under the body as an earring worn; each filing is defensible and each changes three clusters' totals. The snowy dove files under birds, where it will meet the wedding-night raven and the dawn lark; but its work in the line is chromatic, white against the crows' black, and a counter filing by effect would put it in the light file and defend the choice. The cheek of night files under darkness, or under the body. The pilgrim and saint file under the sacred, although their function is erotic negotiation conducted in liturgical vocabulary, which is precisely the fusion the play is famous for and precisely what a subject file cannot represent: the file records a saint, the scene performs a flirtation in saint's clothing.

Even the count of units wobbles under the hand. Is the torch speech one sustained image of radiance, or four images, torches, night-cheek, jewel, dove? Spurgeon's inclusive practice scores the parts; a stricter practice scores the sweep. The scene's file can therefore be made to show the light cluster modestly present or overwhelmingly dominant, by counters acting in good faith under different stated rules. What no good-faith filing can do is make the scene's pattern disappear. Under every defensible rule, the extractions crowd toward brightness set in darkness, spoken by the lover, at the moment of first sight, inside the enemy's house. The worked example thus lands exactly where the article's verdict lands: the totals are negotiable, the shape is not, and criticism goes wrong when it quotes the totals as if they were the shape.

One further lesson falls out of the exercise, about scale. A single scene, filed honestly, generates dozens of slips and a page of borderline calls. A whole play generates thousands of decisions, each small, each arguable, and the sum of the small decisions is the chart. Spurgeon's achievement was to sustain that labour across the canon without the decisions collapsing into whim; her book's weakness was to publish the sums without the decision log. A reader who has filed one scene by hand will never again quote a cluster total without asking for the log, and will never again dismiss the totals either, having felt how the pattern asserts itself through the borderline cases like weather through a badly sealed window.

## A Second Worked Example: Filing the Tomb

The feast showed the method founding a cluster. The tomb shows the method at the cluster's deathbed, and the filing of Act 5 Scene 3 tests every claim the findings made about trajectory. If the light cluster were a verbal habit, its density in the tomb should resemble its density anywhere else, since habits do not consult the plot. If it were an instrument of the design, the tomb should show the cluster transformed: still present, because the design completes its patterns, but present in the modes of negation, inversion and refusal. Filing the scene in the open, as the feast was filed, lets the distinction be judged on evidence rather than on the article's say-so.

Extraction begins with the scene's carried light. Paris enters with flowers and a torch borne by his page, and Romeo enters with a torch of his own, so the vault scene is played, in the mind's eye the words construct, by torchlight from first to last. Paris's errand yields the nature file's saddest slip: "Sweet flower, with flowers thy bridal bed I strew" (5.3.12), the garden cluster arriving at the wrong ceremony for the last time, bridal flowers spent on a grave. Romeo's address to the vault yields the tomb and womb file's defining slip: "Thou detestable maw, thou womb of death, Gorged with the dearest morsel of the earth" (5.3.45-46), in which the place is figured as a digestive organ, a womb that gestates nothing and a mouth that only swallows. Then the light file receives its final and strangest deposits. Romeo, looking at Juliet's face, corrects his own noun in mid-line: the place is "A grave? O no, a lantern, slaughtered youth, For here lies Juliet, and her beauty makes This vault a feasting presence full of light" (5.3.84-86).

File those deposits and the trajectory claim stands up in the counter's hands. The light is still there, and the counter who stopped at totals would record the cluster healthy in Act 5. But every term of the founding pattern has been inverted. At the feast, the light was real, social and prospective: torches burning in a hall, a face outshining them, a future implied. In the tomb, the light is asserted against the evidence of the eyes, a property Romeo assigns to a body he believes to be a corpse, in a place he has just called death's womb and maw. The lantern is a lantern because he says so; the feasting presence feasts nobody. The cluster has passed from description to defiance, the same passage the article traced in miniature at Mantua, where the light language shrank to a single refusal addressed to the stars. A habit does not invert itself on schedule. A design does, because inversion is how designs announce their completion.

The scene's last light deposit makes the point beyond rescue. The Prince closes the play by closing the cluster: "A glooming peace this morning with it brings. The sun, for sorrow, will not show his head" (5.3.304-305). Across five acts the sun has been the lovers' property: Juliet is the sun, the sun's lodging is where the fiery steeds run, the sun is what the dawn keeps threatening to become. At the end the sun is reassigned to the civic voice, and its act is a refusal to shine. The founding cluster of the play's imagery completes its arc in a withheld sunrise, spoken by the one principal who never shared the cluster while it flourished. No filing rule in dispute touches this datum. File the sun under light, under nature, under the heavens; under every heading the trajectory reads the same, a rising line broken at the catastrophe and formally closed, by negation, in the play's last speech about light.

The tomb filing also returns a dividend to the smaller clusters, worth recording before the worked examples close. The blood file, modest through the middle acts, fills at the end with the scene's literal contents, two bodies bleeding in a vault full of older blood, until the feud's ruling metaphor has become stage property. The hands file closes with the fathers' clasp, the first handclasp of the play that is not a bargain, a blow or a lover's palm. The book file closes with the Prince's promise of a story, the lovers converted into a tale Verona will tell, the final misreading corrected into a narrative at last. None of these closures is visible to a count, because a count has no column for closure. All of them are visible to the count's reader, slips in hand, watching the files fill in the order the play fills them. That division of labour, the count assembling what only reading can finish, is the method's mature form, and the tomb is its best classroom.

## What the Method Found in Romeo and Juliet: Light Locked Against Darkness

Spurgeon's report on this play is the best known of all her play-by-play findings, and its core claim is simple to state. The dominant imagery of Romeo and Juliet is light set against darkness. The lovers are figured, again and again, as sources or vessels of light inside a dark world: the sun, the moon and stars, torches and lightning, dawn breaking and radiance in a vault. Around that radiant centre the darkness gathers: night that hides and night that kills, the gloom of the feud's Verona, and at the last the tomb, which the play insists on calling a lantern and a feasting presence full of light, as if the cluster refused to surrender even there. Her reading of the pattern was equally direct. The light images are the lovers; the darkness is everything that surrounds and finally extinguishes them; and the brevity built into the chosen vehicles, torches that burn down, lightning that is gone as it is seen, stars that fall, is the tragedy's meaning carried in its pictures.

The primary text, quoted here from René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, supports the shape of the claim at every hinge of the action. At the first sight of Juliet, before Romeo knows her name, her house or her voice, the language he finds for her is light language: "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night" (1.5.43-44). The comparison defines her by what she does to other lights and by the darkness she hangs against. A few lines later the cluster adds its precious-object register: she is "a rich jewel in an Ethiope's ear" (1.5.52), brightness framed by darkness again, the image unintelligible without the dark ground. In the orchard the pattern becomes cosmology: "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3), and within the same speech her eyes are promoted to stars: "Two of the fairest stars in all the heaven, Having some business, do entreat her eyes To twinkle in their spheres till they return" (2.2.15-17). The dedicated study of [Light and Dark Imagery in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/01/26/light-and-dark-imagery-analysis/) traces that system scene by scene at a length the present argument, whose subject is the method rather than the system itself, deliberately compresses.

Juliet speaks the cluster as fluently as Romeo, and with a darker edge that the count alone would never register. Her wedding-night soliloquy opens in fire and speed: "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2.1-2). When she imagines Romeo dead and transfigured, the vehicle is again stellar: "when I shall die, Take him and cut him out in little stars, And he will make the face of heaven so fine" (3.2.21-23). The aubade at dawn turns the cluster into an argument about evidence, the lovers disputing which bird sang and therefore whether the light is the dangerous daylight: "It was the nightingale, and not the lark, That pierced the fearful hollow of thine ear" (3.5.2-3). Even the Friar, standing outside the love, frames his first appearance in the same opposition: "The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night, Check'ring the eastern clouds with streaks of light" (2.3.1-2), a dawn that smiles and frowns at once, the cluster already carrying the play's doubleness inside its most serene instance. The celestial branch of the system, the sun and star conceits as formal love-poetry, receives its own treatment in the study of the [Conceits of Light: The Lovers as Stars and Sun](/2025/08/15/conceits-of-light-celestial-imagery/), and the concentration of the whole radiance on Juliet herself is mapped in [Light as Holiness: Juliet as Sun and Saint](/2024/08/09/juliet-as-light-sun-saint-imagery/).

The subsidiary clusters matter to the method's credit because they show the count finding more than the one pattern everyone quotes. Fire runs beside light but behaves differently: where light reveals, fire consumes, and the play's most famous fire image is a warning delivered at a wedding, the Friar's "These violent delights have violent ends And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, Which as they kiss consume" (2.6.9-11). Disease runs under the action as an undertow and twice breaks the surface as literal plot: Mercutio's dying curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1.99), and the quarantine that seals Friar John in a house "Where the infectious pestilence did reign" (5.2.10), so that the metaphor the lovers lived by becomes the mechanism that kills them. The poison and medicine group is stated as doctrine in the Friar's herb speech, "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied, And vice sometime by action dignified" (2.3.21-22), and is then enacted by vial and apothecary. Books, hands, birds, blood, garden growth, the tomb and the womb: each forms a countable group with its own behaviour, and the cluster table in the next section sets the whole stock out in one view.

### Why does the darkness have to be counted as part of the light cluster?

Because in this play light is almost never predicated alone. Juliet teaches torches to burn bright against the cheek of night; the jewel shines in the Ethiope's ear; the sun rises in a speech about a window in the dark. Spurgeon's instinct to count the pair as one cluster is her single best filing decision.

Strip the darkness out of the file and the residue is a set of compliments; keep the opposition and the file describes a world, a small radiance besieged. The decision also matches the play's own staging of the cluster: radiance in Verona is always local, a window, a torch, a face, held inside a general dark that the light never dispels for longer than a scene.

One finding of the count is so regularly undervalued that it deserves a paragraph of its own. The light cluster is not evenly shared among the play's speakers. It belongs overwhelmingly to the two lovers, secondarily to the Friar in his dawn and herb speeches, and it is withheld almost entirely from the feud's principals in their public capacities: Tybalt receives no light image to speak, the Prince speaks law rather than radiance, and old Capulet's light language is the borrowed brilliance of his feast. Spurgeon noticed the concentration but did not build her interpretation on it, because her files were not organised by speaker. The protocol proposed later in this article treats that distribution, invisible to the raw count, as the strongest corroboration her finding has.

A second undervalued point concerns the cluster's behaviour across time. Counted in bulk, the light images look like a constant of the play. Distributed act by act, they are front-loaded: dense in the feast, the orchard and the wedding, present but embattled in the parting at dawn, and thereafter surviving mainly as desperate assertions, Romeo's defiance of the stars in Mantua, "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), and his last vision of the vault as "a feasting presence full of light" (5.3.85-86), spoken over a body he believes dead. A cluster that fades on schedule with the hope it figures is behaving like an instrument of the design, not like a verbal tic. Spurgeon's method, as she left it, could not show the fading, because her totals abolished sequence. Her successors' methods could, and the tension between the total and the trajectory is the heart of the dispute examined after the table.

## The InsightCrunch Cluster Table: The Play's Image Clusters by Frequency and Effect

The artifact of this article is the InsightCrunch cluster table, which sets the play's main image clusters side by side with their chief vehicles, their frequency standing and their effect on the whole. One feature of the table needs explanation before it is read, because it embodies a finding of the whole investigation. The frequency column reports standing, not raw tallies: dominant, major, persistent, concentrated, late-gathering. Raw tallies are deliberately withheld, for a reason Spurgeon's own history teaches. Published counts of the same cluster differ between counters because the unit and the filing rules differ, and they differ between editions because the text itself differs. A table that printed precise totals would be claiming a precision the method cannot deliver. A table that reports the ranking, which independent counters and the distribution tests broadly agree on, claims only what the evidence can carry. The table is a map of the play's imaginative economy, not an audited account, and its prose discussion says plainly where its rankings come from: Spurgeon's published findings first, checked against the distribution of each cluster across speakers, acts and turning points.

Read down the standing column and the table tells the story of the method's strength and weakness in one view. The strength stands at the top: the light cluster's dominance is the kind of large fact that quotation-hunting could never establish, because no reader quoting favourite lines knows what the whole stock contains. The weakness stands in the middle rows: clusters like books and reading, tiny by count, carry weight out of all proportion to their standing, because their few instances are placed at hinges, the marriage pitch in Act 1, the sonnet at the feast, the misread face in the tomb. The table's two right-hand columns are therefore in permanent tension. Frequency says what a play is saturated with. Effect says what a play is governed by. Spurgeon's method measures the first with real authority and can only gesture at the second, and much of the later quarrel is a quarrel about which column deserves the critic's trust.

The table also exposes a classification decision that every counter of this play must make and few declare. Where does a star belong? As a shining body it files under light; as an instrument of destiny it files under the heavens and fate; in the Prologue's "star-crossed lovers" (Prologue, line 6) it is doing fate's work in light's clothing. Spurgeon's charts filed the stars with the light, swelling her dominant cluster. A counter who files them with fate produces a smaller light cluster and a larger fate cluster from the identical text. Neither filing is dishonest. They are different theories of what an image is: a picture of a thing, or a piece of an argument. The cluster table hedges the dispute by giving the stars a row of their own, and the FAQ section returns to the filing problem, because it is the single clearest proof that counting is interpretation conducted by other means.

A word about the rows that did not make the table. Verona's image stock contains smaller groupings that recur often enough to notice and too seldom to govern: sea and ships, music and dance, money and gold, sleep and dreams, clothing and the body dressed. A complete census would list them, and a critic building an argument on one of them would need them. They are omitted here because the table's purpose is the play's governing economy, the clusters whose behaviour a general reader must know to judge Spurgeon's claim. Their omission is itself a cluster-method decision, declared rather than hidden: the table ranks, and ranking means some real patterns are set below the line of attention. Every published cluster study makes the same cut, usually without saying so.

## The Daylight Stage: Why a Census of Words Is the Right Census for This Playhouse

A standing objection to counting words, rather than weighing performances, dissolves when the original conditions of performance are remembered. Shakespeare's company played in the afternoon, in open air or in halls lit for the occasion, on a stage that could not darken. Night, in that theatre, was not a lighting state; it was a verbal achievement. When Verona needed to be dark, the play said so, in the mouths of its people: night's cloak, the mask of night, torches announced and carried, a lark mistaken for a nightingale because the light could not be shown and had to be argued. The radiance of the feast and the gloom of the tomb were built out of the same material, language, because language was the only material the playhouse had for light.

This fact quietly vindicates the unit Spurgeon chose to count. Her files record the word-stock rather than the stage picture, and critics have sometimes treated the choice as a reader's impoverishment, the substitution of a text for an event. For this playhouse the substitution is close to an identity: the event's light was the text's light, spoken. A census of the words is therefore not a proxy for the theatrical fact; for the visual dimension of the imagery, it very nearly is the theatrical fact, since the audience's eyes received what the characters' mouths declared. The torches carried on stage are the honest exception, real flame doing real work, and the protocol's demand that a counter declare whether properties are in or out of the unit covers them.

The daylight condition also explains a feature of the clusters that pure reading can miss: their redundancy. The play says its darkness and its brightness over and over, in scene after scene, because each new stretch of action had to repaint the light for an audience sitting in sunshine. What feels, on the page, like saturation is partly the technology of an unlit stage, a dramatist restocking the visible world every few minutes. A count cannot separate the technological redundancy from the thematic insistence, and the confession should be entered plainly in the method's ledger. But the separation matters less than it first appears, because the dramatist chose where to spend the redundancy: the repainting is lavished on the lovers' meetings and partings, and spent thriftily on the feud's councils. Technology explains the repetition. It does not explain the distribution, and the distribution is where the meaning lives.

## What Comparison Added: This Play Set Among the Plays

Spurgeon's findings for Romeo and Juliet gain half their meaning from the comparative frame she built around them, and the frame deserves a hearing of its own, because her strongest evidence was never this play's totals alone. In Shakespeare and the Italian Tradition and then in the 1935 book, she set the plays side by side and let each play's dominant groups define it against its fellows. Hamlet, in her count, runs on images of sickness and disease, an ulcerous hidden corruption working through the body of the state and the prince alike. Macbeth runs on images of clothing, of garments borrowed, loose and ill-fitting, a usurper dressed in robes that hang on him like a giant's robe on a dwarfish thief, alongside its famous darkness. Against those neighbours, Romeo and Juliet's profile is startling: the sunniest stock in the canon, a play saturated with light language, whose darkness exists mainly as the ground its light is seen against.

The comparison does argumentative work that no single-play count can do. It answers the sceptic's first move, the claim that any play ransacked for a topic will yield a pile of instances. Of course a ransack yields instances; the comparative chart shows the piles are different sizes in different plays, and different in kind, and that the differences track the plays' felt characters with embarrassing fidelity. The tragedy of a diseased court runs on sickness. The tragedy of a usurper runs on misfit clothing. The tragedy of young love runs on light against dark. A method whose findings keep coinciding with three centuries of uncounted reading is measuring something that was there before the measuring began.

Comparison also exposes the method's limits from a new angle, because comparative totals are only as stable as the classifications beneath them, and classifications wobble most at the borders between plays' favourite stocks. Is the darkness of Macbeth, its unnatural night and thickening light, the same variable as the darkness of Verona? Filing says yes, and the chart obliges. Reading says the darknesses differ in kind: one is moral weather, the other is the absence the lovers shine against. The chart cannot express that difference, and a critic who trusts the chart over the reading will say the two plays share a cluster when what they share is a filing heading. Spurgeon's comparative frame is thus both her best evidence and her best warning. It shows the method discovering real large-scale facts about the canon's shape, and it shows those facts arriving pre-flattened, their kinds smoothed into categories coarse enough to count.

## The Case Against the Count: Clemen, Knights and the Flattening Charge

The critique of Spurgeon's method arrived in layers, and the layers attack different storeys of her building. The ground floor, the existence of the clusters, was never seriously denied: the plays do run on repeated image groups, and nobody who has filed the slips unfinds them. The first floor, the interpretation of clusters as a play's theme made visible, drew the sharpest methodological fire. The second floor, the biographical claim of the title's second half, drew the sharpest philosophical fire. Keeping the layers separate is essential to a fair adjudication, because a critic can demolish the second floor and leave the ground floor untouched, and much confused polemic has treated a hit on the biography as a hit on the counting.

The most consequential answer came from Wolfgang Clemen, whose The Development of Shakespeare's Imagery (1951) rebuilt the study of Shakespearean imagery on a different axiom. Clemen granted the recurrences. What he denied was that recurrence, as such, is the meaning. An image, in Clemen's handling, means by its place in the action: by who speaks it, at what pressure, answering what earlier image, and preparing what later one. The same torch image at a feast and in a tomb are not two units of one currency; they are two events in an argument, the second quoting and reversing the first. Clemen therefore replaced the aggregate with the trajectory. His readings trace single image lines through a play as they gather charge, change hands and discharge at the catastrophe. Against the chart, he set the curve. The charge against counting in its strictest form follows from his axiom: a method that adds images without regard to their dramatic position systematically destroys the very information that makes them significant. The count flattens context not by accident but by design, since flattening is what counting is.

L. C. Knights had fired on the second floor two years before Spurgeon's book even appeared, in the 1933 essay How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth?, and the essay's logic was waiting for her when she published. Knights attacked the habit of treating a play as a document from which facts about real or imagined lives could be extracted, the habit that asked after Lady Macbeth's children as if she had a biography outside her lines. His positive doctrine was that a Shakespeare play is a poem, a structure of words whose meaning lives in the words' arrangement and nowhere else. Spurgeon's second claim, that the image stock tells us about Shakespeare the man, his gardens, his sports, his sympathies, his supposed experiences, is the documentary habit in statistical dress. The count may be exact and the inference still illicit, because the route from a poet's picture stock to a person's biography runs through every convention of the period's rhetoric, every source the poet plundered, and every character the poet ventriloquised. Knights did not review Spurgeon's book, and she is not his target. His essay matters here because it named, in advance, the fallacy her second floor committed, and because the profession's later desertion of her biographical claims followed his map almost exactly: the imagery stayed, the biography went.

The third line of attack is quieter and, for a working critic, the most damaging. It concerns weight. A count treats instances as interchangeable, but plays do not. One image, placed at a hinge and taken up by the action, can outweigh a score of casual ones. This play supplies the test case in its smallest governing cluster. The book images are few: Lady Capulet's pitch that Juliet should "Read o'er the volume of young Paris' face" (1.3.81), the sonnet the lovers speak as a text at the feast, the Friar's verdict on Romeo's first love, that it "did read by rote, that could not spell" (2.3.87-88). Filed in a chart, the group is a minor row. Placed in the action, the group is a theory of the whole tragedy: people in Verona are continually read, as faces, as books, as letters, and the catastrophe is a misreading, a face in a tomb read as dead that is alive. A counter weighing instances equally must rank the book cluster below the household imagery it dwarfs in significance. Any method that produces that ranking has measured something real and judged it wrongly.

### Can twenty casual images outweigh one image placed at a death?

On the evidence of this play, no, and the reason exposes the count's deepest structural limit. Images derive force from position and uptake: from arriving at a hinge, from being answered, repeated or reversed by the action, from passing into the audience's memory as the scene's emblem.

Twenty instances scattered through low-pressure scenes build atmosphere, and atmosphere is a genuine good, but one instance at a death can organise a whole act around itself. Counting registers the first kind of power accurately and the second kind not at all, which is why the protocol's weight audit insists that an interpretation rest on the hinges and say so.

The fourth line of attack concerns the text itself, and its champion is properly an editor. René Weis, whose Arden Third Series edition supplies this article's quotations, prints a text based on the Second Quarto of 1599, the full early text of the play. The First Quarto of 1597 is a much shorter text, differing in thousands of readings and lacking whole stretches of the verse. An imagery count is a count of a particular edited text, not of an abstraction called the play. Run on the 1597 text, every cluster in the play shrinks; run on a modern edition, the totals silently include the editor's choices among variants, including choices at exactly the lyrical passages where clusters are densest. The point is not a pedant's quibble. It means the count's authority is borrowed from the edition, and the edition's authority comes from arguments the count cannot make. Cluster analysis floats on textual scholarship it rarely acknowledges.

The fifth line of attack concerns the counter. Classification is interpretation, as the star problem in the table section showed, and classification is also vulnerable to the counter's thesis. A critic convinced in advance that a play is about light will file generously toward light, counting marginal glows that a sceptic would file under weather or omit as dead metaphors. No malice is required; the unit problem guarantees drift, because between the living image and the dead metaphor there is a wide grey band, and every counter draws its border in a different place. Spurgeon's generation had no protocol for the grey band, no second counter, no published filing rules against which her chart could be checked. The totals entered the literature as facts and were quoted for decades by critics who had never asked how a torch was filed.

Set the five attacks in a row and their combined force is considerable. Position blindness from Clemen. Documentary fallacy from Knights. Weight blindness from the book cluster's example. Edition dependence from the textual evidence. Counter drift from the unit problem. A fair adjudication must also record what the attacks do not touch. None of them dissolves the clusters themselves. None of them explains the distribution facts: that the light images belong to the lovers, that they crest before the catastrophe, that the feud's principals are starved of them. None of them supplies a rival instrument that could have discovered those facts in the first place. The critique disciplines the method. Whether it destroys it depends on how much of Spurgeon's claim the method is asked to carry, which is the question the descendants and the verdict take up.

One more critic belongs in this section because his objection is the one students meet first and phrase worst. The flattening charge, in its common form, says that counting treats poetry as data and thereby insults it. In its strong form, the charge is Clemen's and has been answered by engagement. In its weak form it is not an argument at all but a distaste for arithmetic, and it deserves the reply Spurgeon's defenders gave: that a critic who refuses to count is not protecting the poetry but protecting a first impression from evidence. The history of the light cluster vindicates the reply. Readers had felt the play's radiance for three centuries before 1935. What they could not do, without the count, was show that the radiance was systematic rather than a handful of famous lines, or that it belonged to the lovers rather than to Verona at large. Feeling located the phenomenon. Counting mapped it. Criticism needs both operations, in that order or the reverse, but it needs both.

## Two Confounds the Count Cannot See: Irony and Inheritance

Two further confounds deserve their own section because neither Clemen's functionalism nor the weighting argument quite covers them, and both bear directly on how much of the light finding a sceptic must grant. The first is irony: the count records an image's presence but not its truth value inside the play, and this play puts some of its most concentrated image-work into speeches it marks as false. The second is inheritance: the count records a play's stock but not the stock's provenance, and some of Verona's pictures were inherited from the sources, where they carried different weights.

The irony confound is sharpest in Act 1. Romeo enters the play speaking the light and dark language in its most concentrated early form, the oxymoron chain: "O brawling love, O loving hate, O anything of nothing first create! O heavy lightness, serious vanity" (1.1.176-178), with "bright smoke, cold fire, sick health" in the same breath. File those lines and the light cluster gains an early deposit, the sickness cluster likewise, and the chart registers a play already saturated with its dominant patterns before the lovers meet. But the play itself files the speech differently. The oxymorons are Petrarchan counters, the standard currency of the fashionable sonneteer, and Romeo spends them on Rosaline, a woman the audience never sees and the Friar later mocks him for doting on. The play's whole design requires the audience to hear Act 1's imagery as borrowed plumage, which the real thing, arriving at the feast, will expose. A count cannot hear the exposure. It banks the false coin at face value, and in doing so it actually flattens the play's most deliberate piece of imagery criticism, its own distinction between performed feeling and felt feeling. The speaker audit in the protocol mitigates the confound without curing it: the audit can note that the early deposit sits in the mouth of a Romeo the play is correcting, but the correction is a reading, not a tally, and the honest counter must write it in prose beside the number.

Old Capulet's feast supplies the confound's second instance. The light of the feast, the torches, the bright room, is real in the file and real on the stage, yet within the scene the light belongs to hospitality, to a host performing magnificence for his guests, and Romeo's great light speech is spoken inside that borrowed brightness. When he says Juliet teaches the torches to burn bright, the image's force depends on the torches being Capulet's, the enemy's household goods outshone by the enemy's daughter. The cluster reading survives this complication, indeed it deepens, since the feud's own house furnishes the darkness and the lamps against which the light is measured. But the survival is again the work of reading, not counting. The file alone would credit the feast's radiance to the play's light saturation in general, missing that the scene has staged a small allegory of the cluster's whole claim: brightness kindled inside the dark house, by the house's own heir, against the house's own torches.

Inheritance is the colder confound. Shakespeare built the play on Arthur Brooke's poem The Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet and on the prose tradition behind it, and the sources brought pictures with them. Where an image stands in Brooke, its presence in the play records transmission as well as choice; where Shakespeare adds, sharpens or redistributes, the design claim bites. A raw count cannot tell the inherited from the invented, and Spurgeon's book, for all its learning, did not sort her files by provenance. The mitigation available to her defenders is real but partial: retention is also a choice, since a dramatist who keeps a source's picture, moves it to a hinge, and builds a scene on it has designed with it as surely as its inventor did. The honest formulation is that a cluster's mass proves saturation, its placement proves intention, and its provenance merely discounts the portion of the mass that arrived ready-made. For the light cluster the discount is small, because the feast, orchard and tomb radiances in their dramatic placement are the play's own work whatever their ancestry. For smaller clusters the discount can be decisive, one more reason the protocol weights hinges over totals.

Taken together, the two confounds refine the verdict rather than reverse it. Irony means the count overstates the early light deposit's value while understating its function as a foil. Inheritance means the count overstates the play's inventiveness while understating nothing about its saturation. Both corrections are the kind that reading supplies to counting, and both were supplied, in the decades after 1935, by the very critics the counting had provoked. The method's history is therefore not a simple arc of claim and refutation. It is a collaboration across a quarrel, in which the counters found the facts and the readers found the facts' values, and the play's imagery emerged better understood than either party could have managed alone.

## The Objection from Context: The New Criticism and the Price of Extraction

Between Clemen's functionalism and the method's eclipse stands a broader doctrine that deserves separate statement, because it changed what counted as a respectable sentence about a poem. The New Criticism, whose classic statement is Cleanth Brooks's The Well Wrought Urn (1947), held that a poem's meaning is inseparable from its total verbal structure, that paraphrase is a heresy and extraction a subtler one. On that doctrine, an image lifted from its scene into a subject file has been deprived of the very thing that made it mean: its pressure against the words around it, its speaker's unreliability, its irony, its place in a rhythm of statement and qualification. The file says the play contains a picture of a torch. The poem says a frightened boy carries light into a vault where the light will find two bodies. Between the file and the poem, on the strict doctrine, everything that matters has been lost.

The doctrine's force against Spurgeon is real, and it lands hardest on her weaker followers, the recitationists of the textbook era, for whom the cluster label genuinely did replace the scene. A critic who knows that the play runs on light and has never watched the light change its meaning between the feast and the tomb knows less than a reader who has watched one scene closely and never heard of clusters. The protocol in this article is built to honour the doctrine's demand: every general claim must be re-earned at the hinges, in context, with the local work of the image stated in prose. Where that discipline is kept, extraction is a stage in reading rather than a substitute for it, a census taken so that the return to the scenes can be informed about what the scenes collectively contain.

But the doctrine, pressed to its limit, proves too much, and its limit case is worth stating because it marks the boundary of the whole dispute. If no element of a poem may be considered in aggregate, then no claim about a whole play's diction, verse, structure or imagery can ever be made, since every such claim aggregates. The New Critics themselves aggregated constantly, about paradox, tension and irony, patterns they traced across whole poems without filing a single slip. Their objection, fairly read, was never to pattern-finding. It was to pattern-finding that mistook its patterns for the poem. Spurgeon's book is vulnerable to the fair reading at its biographical floor and at its moments of chart-drunk confidence, and invulnerable to it at its core finding, because the light pattern was offered as a fact about the play's furniture, and furniture facts are exactly the kind that aggregation establishes. The mature criticism of the play keeps both inheritances in view: Brooks's warning pinned above the desk, Spurgeon's files open on it.

## The Defence and the Descendants: Armstrong, the Eclipse, and the Machine Count

The method's most important defender was also its most important reviser. Edward A. Armstrong, in Shakespeare's Imagination (1946), took over the cluster idea and relocated its mechanism. Where Spurgeon had spoken of leading images revealing a play's theme, Armstrong spoke of image clusters as chains of association in the creative mind: words and pictures linked by sound, by contiguity, by buried personal association, surfacing together in passage after passage. His clusters were psychological rather than thematic, evidence about how the imagination worked rather than about what a play meant. The relocation was a rescue operation with a price. It saved the phenomenon, the recurrences are real, by surrendering the largest claim, that the recurrences encode the play's design. On Armstrong's account, a cluster may be a habit of the poet's mind leaking into every play it touches, in which case its presence in this play tells us about Shakespeare's imagination in general and about Romeo and Juliet only weakly. The adjudication below returns to Armstrong's fork, because the fork is genuine: the same tally can be read as design or as habit, and only the distribution evidence can decide between them.

Armstrong's version also repaired one of Spurgeon's mechanical weaknesses, and the repair is worth naming because it shows the method improving itself. His clusters were built from verbal links, from repeated words and their associates, rather than from subject headings imposed in advance. A chain that begins in an actual repeated word has a checkable spine: another scholar can test whether the links hold. The subject-heading files of 1935 had no comparable spine, since the headings lived in the counter's judgement. Descendants of the method ever since have tended to follow Armstrong's instinct, anchoring clusters to countable verbal facts and letting the subject groupings emerge from the links, rather than decreeing the groupings first.

The middle decades of the twentieth century were the method's eclipse, and the reasons were only partly intellectual. Clemen's functional approach won the seminar room because it produced better close readings, and a method is judged in practice by the readings it generates. The New Criticism's doctrine of context, the insistence that a word means within its poem and nowhere else, made the extraction of images into files look like a category error, whatever its statistical charms. Later movements wanted the play's politics, its bodies, its economics, and the cluster files seemed to belong to a gentler era's questions. Spurgeon's name survived in footnotes as a pioneer, a word professions use for ancestors they have stopped reading. Her biographical claim collapsed first and hardest, as Knights's logic predicted it would. The thematic claim survived in diluted form, absorbed into the general practice of tracing imagery, which nearly every critic did and few credited to her.

The revival, when it came, arrived from an unexpected direction: the machine. Searchable electronic texts turned every reader into a potential counter, and the questions Spurgeon had asked by hand became askable in seconds, at scales her slips could never reach. Concordance software could list every occurrence of a word and its collocates across the canon. Stylometry could compare the picture stocks of plays, authors and periods with the filing rules stated in code, checkable and repeatable by any sceptic. The revival vindicated some of her instincts and corrected others. It vindicated the premise that large patterns of recurrence exist and carry meaning, and that unaided reading cannot map them. It corrected her unit: machine counts run on words and phrases, the Armstrong spine, not on impressionistic word-pictures. And it corrected her epistemology: a reproducible count publishes its rules, invites recounts, and treats its totals as arguments rather than facts. The instrument she founded came back wearing the protocols she had lacked.

### Where does the counting hand stop and the interpreting mind begin?

At the classification desk, and the honest answer is that it never stops at all. Deciding that this torch files under light and that star under fate is already interpretation, done early, done quickly, and done where later readers cannot see it.

The machine count did not abolish the desk; it moved the desk into the code, where the decisions sit in the open, written down, arguable line by line. That relocation, from private judgement to published rule, is the real distance between the counting of 1935 and its descendants.

One road not taken in 1935 deserves a paragraph, because it clarifies what the count was for. G. Wilson Knight, Spurgeon's contemporary, approached the same phenomenon, the play's saturated atmosphere, from the opposite pole: no extraction, no files, the play apprehended whole as a spatial symbol, its light and music contemplated rather than tabulated. Knight's readings of this play's radiance are treated in the light and dark study linked earlier, and the weighing of his approach against hers belongs there. What belongs here is the taxonomy. Knight and Spurgeon agreed that the play's imagery formed a unity that quotation could not capture. They disagreed about whether the unity could survive being taken apart. The subsequent history suggests a division of honours: her method produced the evidence, his produced the evocations, and the criticism that has lasted tends to use her maps with his eyes.

## The Working Life of the Method: Lecture Rooms, Textbooks and the Long Middle of the Century

A method's history is written not only in its famous books but in its working life, the decades in which it is taught to students who never learn its founder's name. Spurgeon's counting had such a life, and tracing it explains both the profession's debt and the sharpness of the eventual reaction. Through the middle of the twentieth century, the standard way to teach a Shakespeare play's imagery in universities and schools was Spurgeonian in everything but citation: the lecture that gathered the play's pictures into groups, named the dominant group, and read the play through it. Generations of students met Romeo and Juliet first as a tissue of light and darkness, Hamlet as a tissue of disease, Macbeth as a tissue of darkness and unnatural deeds, and the groupings in their notes descended, often at several removes, from the charts of 1935. Textbook introductions absorbed the findings as facts about the plays, detached from the counting that produced them, which is the surest sign a method has won: its results become the furniture of the subject.

The working life had a cost that the reaction eventually itemised. Detached from the files, the findings hardened into formulae, and the formula for this play, light against dark, lovers bright, world dark, became a recitation that could be produced without reading a scene. The method that had been founded to replace vague appreciation with evidence ended by supplying a new vagueness, numerical in origin and incantatory in use. Students taught the cluster as a conclusion had no access to it as a question, and the backlash of the Clemen generation was partly a teachers' revolt against the recitation, a demand that imagery be returned to the scenes where it lives. The revolt was just, and its justice is compatible with the priority claim: the recitation existed because the finding was strong enough to survive being simplified, which weak findings never are.

The method's working life also carried a quiet institutional significance that the history of criticism has been slow to credit. Spurgeon's operation, a senior woman professor directing a team of assistants through years of systematic drudgery in the service of a checkable claim, modelled a kind of scholarship the profession's gentlemanly wing did not practise: collaborative, cumulative, and falsifiable in principle. The research seminar and the funded project, the normal machinery of later literary studies, resemble her workshop more than they resemble the solitary appreciator she displaced. When the machine counters of the searchable-text era rebuilt her questions with stated rules and repeatable procedures, they were not reviving a curiosity. They were completing the institutional form she had sketched, in which a claim about a play's language is a thing a team can test, a sceptic can rerun, and a student can inherit as a problem rather than as a password. That form, as much as any single finding about light, is her bequest to the study of this play.

## Running the Method Fairly: A Protocol for Honest Cluster Analysis

The adjudication of a ninety-year quarrel should end in something usable, so this section states the protocol that the critique implies, as six rules a counter of this play can actually follow. The rules accept Spurgeon's premise, that recurrence at scale is evidence, and accept her critics' conditions, that evidence becomes proof only after audits the count cannot perform on itself. Readers assembling material for teaching can lay the resulting table out in slides with the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), since a cluster table argued in the open, its rules attached, is exactly the kind of exhibit a presentation ought to carry.

The first rule is to declare the unit. Before counting begins, the counter states what will count as one image: which of simile, metaphor, personification and charged single word qualify, how a sustained comparison spread over six lines is scored, and where the border against dead metaphor is drawn. The declaration will be arguable, and that is the point. A published unit definition turns a private judgement into a public commitment, and lets a sceptic recount the grey band differently and show how much of the result survives.

The second rule is to publish the filing system with the totals. Every cluster claim must travel with its classification rules, including the hard cases: the star filed under light rather than fate, the torch under light rather than household goods, the plague filed under disease rather than under fortune. Totals without filing rules are anecdotes wearing numbers. Spurgeon's charts failed this rule, through no fault of her era, and the failure is why her figures were quoted for decades at a strength their foundations could not bear.

The third rule is the speaker audit. After the count, the counter breaks the cluster down by who speaks it, and treats a skewed distribution as a finding and an even one as a warning. A cluster spoken by everyone in Verona is civic wallpaper. A cluster concentrated in two mouths is a position, a way of seeing held against the world. The audit converts the raw total's weakest feature, its indifference to voice, into the interpretation's strongest evidence.

The fourth rule is the position map. The counter plots the cluster across the acts, marks its appearances at the play's hinges, its first sight, its vow, its catastrophe, its reconciliation, and reports the trajectory beside the total. A cluster that crests and fades with the action is behaving like an instrument of the design. A cluster spread evenly, indifferent to pressure, is behaving like diction. Clemen's whole critique is honoured by this rule, at the cost of one diagram.

The fifth rule is the weight audit. The counter lists the cluster's three most consequential instances, the ones the action answers, repeats or reverses, and checks that the interpretation rests on them rather than on the mass. If the mass and the hinges tell different stories, the hinges govern, and the counter says so. This rule exists because of the book cluster, whose handful of instances run the play's epistemology while greater stocks decorate it.

The sixth rule is the cross-text check. The counter states which edition was counted, and where the early texts diverge at cluster-dense passages, reports the sensitivity of the result. A finding that collapses when the 1597 quarto is substituted for the 1599 was never a finding about the play; it was a finding about an edition. Weis's Arden text is the basis for the quotations throughout this article, and the light finding, as the verdict section shows, survives substitution, because its evidence lies in speeches no early text lacks.

### What would a fair recount of this play have to publish alongside its totals?

Five things, and a recount that omits any of them has not finished its work: the unit definition; the filing rules with the hard cases argued; the speaker breakdown; the act-by-act trajectory with the hinges marked; and the edition counted, with the result's sensitivity to the early-text variants.

A recount carrying those five attachments could be believed, disputed and improved. A recount carrying none of them is a horoscope, however many slips were filled in to make it.

## The Verdict Tested: Spurgeon's Three Claims Against the Protocol

Spurgeon made three claims for this play, of descending strength, and the protocol returns a different verdict on each. The exercise matters because the literature's habit has been to accept or reject the three as a package, and the package deal has served both her reputation and her critics badly.

The first claim is that the play's imagery is dominated by a light-against-darkness cluster. Verdict: proven, on evidence stronger than the count that proposed it. The count found the saturation. The speaker audit finds the concentration: the lovers speak the cluster, the Friar frames it, and the feud's principals are starved of it, so the pattern is a held position rather than a civic habit. The position map finds the trajectory: dense at the feast and the orchard, embattled at the dawn parting, thinning after the street fight, and surviving into Act 5 only as defiance and as a last false reading of the vault's light. The cross-text check finds the evidence stable, since the feast speech, the orchard speeches, the epithalamium, the aubade and the tomb speech stand in both early texts. A cluster that is saturated, concentrated, well-timed and textually stable is a designed feature of the play by any standard criticism can apply, Armstrong's habit hypothesis included, because a mere habit of the poet's mind would spread across speakers and acts without respect for the story's pressure points, and this one respects them exactly.

The second claim is that the dominant cluster is the play's meaning in pictorial form: the lovers as brief brightness in a dark world, their light's brevity built into its vehicles, torches, lightning, falling stars. Verdict: sustained, with one qualification drawn from Clemen. The claim holds wherever the trajectory and the sense agree, which in this play is nearly everywhere the cluster matters. The qualification is that the cluster does not exhaust the meaning it serves. The book cluster's small, perfectly placed group states the tragedy's epistemology in a way the light cluster cannot, and the disease cluster converts metaphor into mechanism at the plot's hinge. Spurgeon's reading is the play's largest truth about itself, not its only one, and her book's confidence that the leading images unlock the whole play should be read as the enthusiasm of a founder, corrected by her own table's middle rows.

The third claim is the title's second half: that the image stock tells us about Shakespeare himself, his experience, his pleasures, the man behind the work. Verdict: not proven, and not provable by this instrument. Knights's objection stands untouched by any refinement of counting, because the inference crosses a gap that no tally can bridge, the gap between a professional dramatist's stocked imagination, fed by sources, conventions and thirty speaking parts, and a biographer's subject. The count can show what the plays are saturated with. Saturation is a fact about texts. The step from the texts to the man's gardens and sports and sympathies is a step into vacancy, and the history of the claim, three generations of confident portraits built on it and quietly abandoned, is the empirical proof of Knights's logic. The instrument survives the loss easily. Its best findings never needed the biography.

The general verdict on the method follows from the three. Cluster analysis is a genuine instrument of knowledge about this play, the only instrument that could have established its largest finding, and it is an instrument that reads falsely when its outputs are consumed raw. Count first, because the unaided eye cannot hold hundreds of images in view at once. Then audit, by speaker, by position, by weight and by text, because the count cannot tell a design from a habit or a hinge from a habit's echo. Spurgeon performed the first operation with a pioneer's genius and sketched the second only fitfully. Her critics performed the second and sometimes forgot the first had been necessary. The play, which is about two people destroyed by a failure to check a message, rewards the critic who does both.

A verdict should also state what would overturn it, and this one can. The light finding would fall if a declared-unit recount showed the cluster spread evenly across the play's speakers, or holding its density through the tomb scenes, or resting on passages that stand in only one early text. None of those results has appeared in ninety years of hostile and friendly scrutiny, although the scrutiny has overturned plenty else in the book that proposed the finding. That asymmetry is the final measure of the method's worth for this play: its largest claim has been exposed to every incentive to refute it, by critics whose reputations were built on refuting its author, and the claim is standing. Instruments are not judged by the company they keep or the century that made them. They are judged by whether their readings survive their enemies. Spurgeon's reading of this play's imagery has survived hers.

There is a last, practical sense in which the verdict matters beyond the history of criticism. A reader who takes the light finding seriously, with its audits attached, reads the play differently at the level of the scene. The feast is no longer a meeting that happens to be lyrical; it is the cluster's founding deposit, placed where the design needs its capital. The orchard is no longer a window with famous lines; it is the capital invested, the sun and stars spent by two speakers building a private cosmology against the city's dark. The tomb is no longer a gothic ending; it is the audit's final page, where every deposit is called in and the account is closed by a sun that will not rise. Reading with the cluster in mind is not reading with a chart in hand. It is reading with a memory trained to notice recurrence, and to ask of every recurrence the protocol's questions: whose mouth, which hinge, what weight, what text.

That habit of attention is, in the end, the strongest argument for the method, stronger than any single finding it produced. Before 1935, the play's imagery was admired. After 1935, it could be investigated, and investigation is a sterner and more durable form of attention than admiration. The investigation corrected its founder on the biography, disciplined her totals, rebuilt her unit, and routed her chart through Clemen's trajectories and the editor's variants. What it never did was dissolve the pattern she found at the centre of this play: two brief lights, counted and recounted, burning against a darkness that the counting taught criticism to see as a system rather than a backdrop. The cliche quotes the single image and calls the play a romance. The count, audited and answered for, shows the romance's machinery, and on the evidence assembled here the machinery is a large part of the meaning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch cluster table, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch cluster table is this article's map of the play's main image clusters, setting each cluster's chief vehicles beside its frequency standing and its effect on the whole. The pattern it exposes is a ranked economy rather than a miscellany: light against darkness dominates, a second tier of day and night, stars, fire, sacred and garden imagery sustains the action, and a set of small but heavily placed clusters, books above all, punches far above its numerical weight. The table withholds raw tallies deliberately, because counters and editions disagree about totals while agreeing about the ranking, and the ranking is the finding a reader can build on.

### Q: Who was Caroline Spurgeon, and what did Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us set out to prove?

Caroline Spurgeon (1869-1942) was professor of English literature at Bedford College, University of London, among the first women to hold such a chair in England, and her 1935 book set out to prove that a play's imagery, counted and classified in full, reveals both the play's governing atmosphere and the mind of its author. The first half of the title names the evidence, the imagery itself, gathered image by image from every play. The second half names the ambition: that the gathered stock tells us what the plays mean and who Shakespeare was. The evidence half founded a method. The ambition half drew the fire of Knights and his successors, and the two halves have had separate fortunes ever since.

### Q: How did Spurgeon carry out her count of a play's images in practice?

Spurgeon's count proceeded in four stages: extraction, classification, aggregation and interpretation. Assistants read each play slowly and copied out every image. Each image was then filed under a subject heading, nature, animals, the sea, the body and sickness, household life, war, sport, light and darkness, and the rest of her provinces of experience. The files for one play were totalled to find its dominant groups, and the totals were charted against other plays and other dramatists. Only then did interpretation begin, with the dominant groups, her leading images, read as the key to the play's atmosphere. The procedure's strength was its completeness: nothing was sampled. Its weakness was that the filing decisions, taken by hand and never published as rules, decided the results before the chart displayed them.

### Q: What did Spurgeon mean by iterative imagery, the subject of her 1931 British Academy lecture?

By iterative imagery Spurgeon meant imagery that repeats, pictures returning in groups throughout a play until the group, rather than any single instance, carries the meaning. Her 1931 British Academy Shakespeare Lecture, Shakespeare's Iterative Imagery, argued that Shakespeare's imagination worked by recurrence, that certain images rise again and again within one play because they are bound to its central feeling, and that gathering the recurrences lets a reader recover the feeling's shape. The idea cut against the quotation culture of the criticism around her, which prized the single brilliant line. On her account, the brilliant line is a sample of a climate, and criticism that never maps the climate mistakes the sample for the play's imaginative life.

### Q: What did the second half of her title, "and What It Tells Us", claim imagery could reveal about Shakespeare himself?

The second half of the title claimed that the image stock of the plays is a document of their author's own experience, tastes and sympathies, a record of what Shakespeare had noticed, loved and practised, from gardens and music to sports and the sea. Spurgeon drew biographical conclusions from the stock: the pictures a man reaches for ten thousand times, she reasoned, betray the life that stocked them. The claim made the book famous and then unmade its reputation. Knights's doctrine, that a play is a poem and not a document, denies the inference at its root, because a professional dramatist's stock is fed by sources, conventions and the needs of thirty speaking parts. The plays' saturation is a fact about texts. The portrait built on it remains unproven.

### Q: What did Spurgeon compare in Shakespeare and the Italian Tradition, and how did it prepare the play-by-play method?

In Shakespeare and the Italian Tradition (1931), Spurgeon compared the imagery of Shakespeare with the imagery of his fellow dramatists, arguing from its Italian colouring and its characteristic stocks about the imagination behind the plays. The comparison forced the technique that the 1935 book would perfect: pictures had to be extracted, sorted and counted before two dramatists' stocks could be set side by side at all. It also established the comparative instinct that distinguishes her best findings. A cluster's meaning in her work is rarely a bare total; it is a proportion, this play's leaning toward light measured against the canon's other leanings, a tragedy's sickness stock against a comedy's gardens. The play-by-play method of 1935 is the 1931 comparison turned inward, one play's stock charted against its fellows.

### Q: What did Spurgeon find when she ran the method on Hamlet and Macbeth, and what do the two results show about the instrument?

Running the method on Hamlet, Spurgeon found a stock dominated by images of sickness and disease, corruption festering in the body of the state and the prince. Running it on Macbeth, she found the famous clothing images, garments borrowed, loose and ill-fitting on the usurper who wears them, together with a saturating darkness. The two results show the instrument at its best, because each finding is a large pattern that unaided quotation had never established, and each coincides with the felt character of its play: the diseased court, the misfit regicide. They also show the instrument's preferred terrain. It excels on plays whose meaning is carried in atmosphere and recurrence, and it has less to say about plays built from argument, intrigue or character in prose.

### Q: Did Spurgeon believe the image patterns were designed on purpose, and what did she take them to be instead?

Spurgeon did not believe the patterns were a plotted code, distributed by deliberate plan. She took them to be the natural recurrence of an imagination thinking in pictures: the poet's mind, seized by a play's central feeling, reaching again and again for the same family of images without needing to scheme the repetitions. The distinction mattered to her and matters to the adjudication of her work, because it locates her claim between design and accident. Armstrong later pressed the patterns further toward the unconscious, treating clusters as associative habits of the creative mind. The distribution evidence in this play resists both soft readings at their weakest point: a habit or an unplanned recurrence should spread evenly across speakers and acts, and the light cluster instead concentrates in the lovers and fades on the catastrophe's schedule.

### Q: What did Wolfgang Clemen mean by the development of an image, and why did the idea challenge a bare count?

By the development of an image, Wolfgang Clemen meant the life an image lives across a play: its first appearance, its repetitions in altered mouths and altered pressure, its reversals, and its final discharge at the catastrophe. In The Development of Shakespeare's Imagery (1951), he argued that an image means by its place in that trajectory, so that a torch at a feast and a torch in a tomb are not two units of one currency but two events in an argument, the second quoting the first. The idea challenges a bare count at its foundation, because counting must treat instances as interchangeable, and development is precisely the order in which they are not. Clemen did not deny the recurrences. He denied that recurrence, added up, is the meaning.

### Q: What did Edward Armstrong add to cluster thinking in Shakespeare's Imagination?

Edward Armstrong added a mechanism and a spine. In Shakespeare's Imagination (1946), he treated image clusters as chains of association in the creative mind, groups of words and pictures linked by sound, contiguity and buried association, surfacing together across a play. The mechanism relocated Spurgeon's finding: clusters became evidence about how the imagination works, not, in the first instance, about what a play means. The spine was methodological. Armstrong built his chains from actual repeated words and their verifiable associates, rather than from subject headings assigned by a counter's judgement, so another scholar could test whether a chain held. Later cluster work has generally followed his instinct, anchoring the pattern to countable verbal facts and letting subject groupings emerge from the links.

### Q: How did L. C. Knights's 1933 attack on documentary reading bear on Spurgeon's biographical claim?

Knights's essay How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth? (1933) attacked the habit of treating a play as a document from which facts about lives could be extracted, insisting that a Shakespeare play is a poem whose meaning lives in its words' arrangement. The bearing on Spurgeon is direct, although Knights did not name her book, which appeared two years after his essay: her biographical claim is the documentary habit in statistical dress. Counting the image stock exactly does not license inferring the author's gardens, sports and sympathies from it, because the stock is fed by sources, rhetorical convention and characters' needs. The profession's eventual settlement followed Knights's map. The imagery findings were kept, absorbed into general practice. The biography built on them was quietly set aside.

### Q: Why does an imagery count change with the edition counted, when the 1597 and 1599 quartos differ so widely?

An imagery count changes with the edition because the underlying text changes: the First Quarto of 1597 is a far shorter version of the play than the Second Quarto of 1599, differing in thousands of readings and lacking whole stretches of verse, so every cluster total computed on one text must shrink or shift on the other. A modern edition adds a second layer of dependence, since its text embodies an editor's choices among variants, and the variants cluster at lyrical passages where images are densest. René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, the source of this article's quotations, prints a 1599-based text with the early variants recorded. The general rule follows: a cluster finding is a finding about an edition until its evidence is shown to stand in every early text, as the light cluster's evidence does.

### Q: What separates a cluster, in the method's strict sense, from a motif or a theme?

A cluster is a countable set of images grouped by their vehicle, the recurring sun, stars, torches and dawn of the light group, whatever those images are doing in any given line. A motif is a recurring element defined by its function, an omen, a warning, a recognition, which may travel in many vehicles. A theme is a proposition the play argues, that haste destroys, that private love and public duty collide, which images may serve but cannot be. The separations matter because the method's findings are claims about clusters only. Saturation of a vehicle family is measurable. Inferring a theme from the saturation is a further step, defensible when distribution and placement corroborate it, as they do for light in this play, and illicit when the tally is asked to speak alone.

### Q: What decides whether an image of a star is filed under light or under fate, and why does the filing change the finding?

The filing is decided by the counter's governing theory of what an image is: a picture of a thing, or a move in an argument. Filed as a picture, every star joins the light cluster, and the Prologue's "star-crossed lovers" (Prologue, line 6) swells the radiant stock by one. Filed as an argument, the same phrase joins the fate stock, since the stars there are destiny's instruments and shine on nobody. The finding changes because the play's two most famous stocks share their border: move the stars and the light cluster shrinks while the fate cluster grows, from the identical text. Spurgeon's charts filed the stars with light. This article's cluster table gives the stars a row of their own and declares the decision, which is the only honest settlement available: the filing rule published, the reader warned.

### Q: Why did the counting method fall out of fashion in the middle decades of the twentieth century?

The counting method fell out of fashion for intellectual and institutional reasons together. Intellectually, Clemen's functional readings produced richer accounts of individual passages, and the New Criticism's doctrine of context made extracting images into files look like a category error, while Knights's argument had already discredited the biographical use that gave the counting its glamour. Institutionally, the next generations defined themselves against the establishment Spurgeon represented, and her findings, absorbed into textbooks as unargued facts, hardened into formulae that students recited without the evidence. A method judged by its recitations looks dead. The eclipse was therefore partly a misunderstanding: the seminar room abandoned the founder while continuing, daily, to trace the image patterns her files had first mapped.

### Q: How has the arrival of searchable electronic texts revived the counting Spurgeon did by hand?

Searchable electronic texts revived the counting by removing its two practical barriers, labour and unverifiability. Spurgeon's files cost years of assisted reading, and no sceptic could afford to recount a play to check her. A searchable text lets any reader list every occurrence of a word and its company across the canon in moments, test a claimed cluster against the whole stock, and rerun a count under different filing rules to see how much of the finding survives. The revival has corrected her method while vindicating her premise: machine counts run on words and phrases rather than impressionistic word-pictures, publish their rules in code, and treat totals as arguments to be disputed. The question she founded, what a play's recurrences mean, is asked with her instinct and her successors' protocols.

### Q: Why is a count blind to who speaks an image, and what does the blindness hide in this play?

A count is blind to speakers because its files are organised by subject, not by voice: the tally records that the play contains so many light images, not that two speakers hold nearly all of them. The blindness hides this play's sharpest distribution fact. The light cluster belongs overwhelmingly to Romeo and Juliet, secondarily to the Friar at dawn, and is withheld almost entirely from the feud's principals in their public capacities: Tybalt speaks no light, the Prince speaks law, old Capulet borrows his feast's brightness. That concentration converts a saturation into a position, a way of seeing held by the lovers against Verona. Spurgeon's files could not show it, because the files abolished the speaker. The speaker audit in this article's protocol exists to restore what the filing removed.

### Q: Why does cluster analysis suit a play built from atmosphere better than a play built from argument?

Cluster analysis suits an atmospheric play because such a play genuinely stores its meaning in recurrence: the feeling a scene leaves behind is built from the accumulated pictures, and the count maps the accumulation. A play built from argument stores its meaning in propositions, rebuttals and decisions, where a picture's force depends on the dialectical moment it serves, and a tally of vehicles misses the reasoning that gives the pictures point. Romeo and Juliet is unusually atmospheric even among the tragedies, a love story told as weather, radiance gathering and failing over five days. The method's triumph here is therefore partly a property of the patient. Spurgeon's instrument found its ideal subject early, and some of its reputation is the record of that fortunate match.

### Q: What single test should a reader apply to any cluster claim before trusting it?

The single test is the distribution test: ask where the cluster lives, in whose mouths, at which moments, and how its density moves as the action tightens. A designed pattern answers those questions with a shape, concentrating in the speakers the pattern concerns, cresting at the hinges, thinning or darkening as the catastrophe approaches. A verbal habit answers them with a flat line, spread evenly over speakers, acts and pressure alike. The test needs no software and no faith in any counter's totals; it can be run on a claimed cluster from the text in an hour. Applied to the light cluster, it returns the strongest corroboration Spurgeon's finding has: concentration in the lovers, a crest before the wedding, and a fading that keeps the catastrophe's time.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch cluster table without letting the table do the arguing?

An essay should use the cluster table as a map of where to look, never as a substitute for looking. The table's standing column can justify a claim of saturation, and its effect column can frame a hypothesis, but the essay's evidence must be the instances themselves, quoted, placed and weighed: the feast speech against Capulet's torches, the orchard cosmology, the vault's false light, with the distribution facts argued from the scenes. The strongest structure runs the table's claim through the protocol's audits in the essay's own order, count, speakers, positions, weight, and lets the verdict emerge from the audits rather than from the founder's authority. An essay that cites Spurgeon's dominance finding without re-performing the checks has borrowed a conclusion. An essay that performs them owns one.
