The National Company Law Tribunal is the clearest case in Indian legislation of a reform whose fate was decided entirely by the forum question. Parliament decided in 2002 that company disputes should leave the High Courts, the Company Law Board and the sickness machinery of the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction, and gather in one specialist tribunal with an appellate tribunal above it. The substantive law was never the obstacle. What consumed the next fourteen years was a constitutional argument about who may staff such a body, who may choose its members, and on what tenure, and that argument had to be fought twice, through two statutes, before a single bench could sit. When the Tribunal was finally constituted on 1 June 2016, it inherited the combined dockets of the bodies it replaced, and within six months the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code handed it a second, larger jurisdiction on top of the first. The institution that company law reform waited fourteen years for became, in the same year, the institution on which insolvency reform depends for its timelines.

![The National Company Law Tribunal building its benches, showing the constitution of the NCLT and NCLAT and the fourteen-year litigation that preceded it - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-38.webp)

That double history is what an institution profile of the Tribunal has to explain. The design question comes first: what was this body built to do, and why did its designers believe a tribunal could do it better than the courts it displaced? The record question comes second: what has the body actually been able to do, given the strength at which it has been staffed and the volume that has been routed to it? The two questions have the same answer at different dates. In 2002 the constraint was that the appointment provisions could not survive constitutional scrutiny. After 2016 the constraint is that the benches sanctioned for the work have never matched the work assigned. The argument of this article is the claim the brief for it names: the Tribunal was the bottleneck twice, first by its absence and then by its capacity, and every reform that adds jurisdiction to it without adding capacity repeats the original error in a new form.

## What the two tribunals were designed to be

The National Company Law Tribunal and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal are creatures of Chapter XXVII of the Companies Act, 2013. Section 408 empowers the Central Government to constitute the Tribunal by notification, and Section 410 does the same for the Appellate Tribunal. The chapter then supplies the full institutional skeleton: definitions in Section 407, the qualifications for the President, the Chairperson and the members in Sections 409 and 411, selection in Section 412, terms of office in Section 413, salary and service conditions in Section 414, removal in Section 417, staff in Section 418, benches in Section 419, procedure and powers in Section 424, and appeals in Sections 421 and 423. The design is a two-tier adjudicatory system standing in place of a court for a defined field. The Tribunal is the forum of first instance for company law. The Appellate Tribunal hears appeals from it. Above the Appellate Tribunal sits the Supreme Court, on questions of law only, a limitation that is widely misunderstood and examined later in this article.

The field assigned to the first tier was deliberately comprehensive. Under the Companies Act the Tribunal decides applications on oppression and mismanagement, class actions, compromises and arrangements, mergers and amalgamations, reduction of share capital, conversion of public companies into private ones, restoration of companies struck off the register, compounding of offences, and the winding up of companies on grounds other than inability to pay debts. Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code it is the Adjudicating Authority for corporate insolvency resolution and liquidation, and for the insolvency of personal guarantors to corporate debtors in the linked situations the Code specifies. The Appellate Tribunal, in addition to its Companies Act appeals, hears insolvency appeals under the Code, appeals from the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India in the cases the Code provides, appeals against orders of the National Financial Reporting Authority, and, since an amendment carried by the Finance Act of 2017 took effect in May 2017, appeals from the Competition Commission of India in place of the former Competition Appellate Tribunal.

### When did the National Company Law Tribunal start functioning?

The Tribunal and the Appellate Tribunal were constituted with effect from 1 June 2016, by notifications issued under Sections 408 and 410 of the Companies Act, 2013. The Company Law Board stood dissolved the same day, and the Tribunal began with eleven benches, two at New Delhi and one each in nine other cities.

The tribunal form itself had a lineage the designers were consciously extending. The Constitution's Forty-second Amendment had inserted Articles 323A and 323B, empowering legislatures to create tribunals for specified subjects and to exclude the jurisdiction of other courts over them, and the administrative tribunals of the nineteen eighties had tested the model for service matters, with the Supreme Court in L. Chandra Kumar settling the enduring compromise: tribunals may supplement the courts, their decisions remain subject to the scrutiny of a division bench of the High Court, and the judicial review power itself cannot be excluded. Company law was a tempting next field for the model because the existing distribution of its work was visibly failing on every side. The High Courts' company benches carried the arrears described above. The Company Law Board, created in its independent form in 1991, was an administrative body exercising delegated governmental power, acceptable for approvals and filings but an awkward holder of adversarial jurisdiction between private parties. The sickness apparatus under the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction had fallen into disrepute for a worse reason: its statutory shield against creditors, designed to protect ailing companies during revival attempts, had become a debtor's refuge that froze recovery for years while assets decayed. Consolidation into one judicialised tribunal was therefore not an efficiency preference chosen from a menu. It was the proposed cure for three distinct failures, delay in the courts, delegation in the Board, and capture in the sickness regime, and the cure's ambition explains both its comprehensive jurisdiction and the cost of its fourteen-year absence: every one of the three failures continued, untreated, in the forums the Tribunal was built to retire.

The design premise deserves a fair statement before the criticism begins, because the case for a specialist tribunal was and remains strong. Company disputes in the High Courts competed for judicial time with every other species of litigation, and company benches in several High Courts carried arrears measured in years. A specialist forum promised members who did nothing else, procedures written for corporate disputes rather than borrowed from the civil courts, and a single national appellate tier to settle conflicting approaches between regions. The counter-premise, which the Supreme Court substantially accepted across two rounds of litigation, is that a body exercising the judicial power of the state is a court in substance whatever it is called, and that its independence cannot be left to the goodwill of the executive that appoints its members, fixes their tenure and controls its budget, least of all where the state and its instrumentalities are frequent litigants before it. Both premises are true, and the fourteen-year gap is the price of reconciling them.

## A tribunal enacted in 2002 and blocked for fourteen years

The recommendation that produced the Tribunal came from the High Level Committee on Law Relating to Insolvency and Winding Up of Companies, chaired by Justice V. Balakrishna Eradi, a former judge of the Supreme Court. The Eradi Committee's work at the end of the nineteen nineties proposed a single tribunal to take over the company jurisdiction scattered across the High Courts, the Company Law Board and the sickness apparatus, on the reasoning that fragmented forums produced fragmented and slow outcomes. Parliament acted on the recommendation in the Companies (Second Amendment) Act, 2002, which inserted new parts into the Companies Act, 1956 to create the Tribunal and the Appellate Tribunal, dissolve the Company Law Board, and transfer the relevant jurisdictions. The story of that amendment, and of why the 1956 Act's own tribunal provisions could never be commenced, belongs to the account of [how the 1956 Act came to be replaced](/2012/02/15/companies-act-1956-replacement/); what matters here is what happened to the provisions once enacted.

What happened was a constitutional challenge that the government could not outrun. The 2002 provisions were attacked on the ground that Parliament could create a tribunal, but could not staff it with persons lacking judicial qualifications, on short tenure, chosen through a selection process in which the executive predominated. The challenge travelled from the Madras High Court to the Supreme Court, and in Union of India v. R. Gandhi, President, Madras Bar Association, decided in 2010, a Constitution Bench gave the answer that has governed tribunal design since. The creation of the Tribunal and the transfer of jurisdiction from the High Courts were constitutionally permissible. The provisions on qualifications, composition and selection were not, in their enacted form. The Court directed the corrections it considered indispensable: tenure too short to attract or protect independent members had to be lengthened from the three years the 2002 Act provided; the selection committee had to be reconstituted so that the judiciary held the decisive voice, chaired by the Chief Justice of India or a nominee with a casting vote; and the qualifications for technical members had to be set at a level of seniority and standing comparable to the judicial work they would perform.

The litigant behind both rounds deserves a place in the institutional record. The challenge was carried by the Madras Bar Association, a professional body of advocates, first against the 1956 Act scheme and then against the 2013 Act, and the same association's name runs through the tribunal litigation of the following decade, on the tax tribunal, on tribunal rules, on the 2021 legislation. The National Tax Tribunal judgment of 2014, in which a Constitution Bench struck down a tribunal statute outright for compromising the judicial function it displaced, belongs to the same doctrinal family and shows the direction of travel between the Tribunal's two rounds: by the time the Companies Act scheme was examined in 2015, the principles were not new law being made for the occasion but settled doctrine being applied to a legislature that had been told, in terms, what compliance required. An institutional history of Indian tribunals is, in considerable part, a history of this repeated pattern, in which a bar association litigates, a Constitution Bench prescribes, a legislature partially complies, and the cycle repeats on the points of non-compliance. The Tribunal's fourteen years sit inside that pattern as its clearest single example, because the stakes were visible: a whole jurisdiction waited, in abeyance, for the arithmetic of one committee.

The judgment is sometimes summarised as the Court blocking the Tribunal, and that summary inverts the holding. The Court upheld the institution and invalidated the staffing model. It accepted the specialist-tribunal premise in full, including the transfer of the High Courts' company jurisdiction, and it insisted that a body receiving that jurisdiction must be a real substitute for the court it replaces, independent in the same measure. The distinction matters for everything that followed, because the second round of litigation was not about whether the Tribunal should exist. It was about whether Parliament, when it redrafted the provisions, had actually made the corrections the Court had directed, or had reproduced the defects under new section numbers.

## The second round: redrafting, fresh defects and constitution in 2016

Parliament's redraft came inside the Companies Act, 2013, whose Chapter XXVII re-enacted the tribunal scheme with corrected text. The corrections were real but incomplete. When the Madras Bar Association challenged the new provisions, the Supreme Court in Madras Bar Association v. Union of India, decided in May 2015, upheld Section 408 and the constitution of the Tribunal, and then held that parts of the scheme still deviated from the 2010 directions. Clauses of Section 409 dealing with the qualifications of technical members, and Section 411(3) on the same subject for the Appellate Tribunal, were held invalid because they diluted the seniority the Court had required. Section 412(2), which constituted a five-member selection committee in which three members came from the executive, was struck down because a body so composed gave the administration the predominant say in choosing the members of a judicial forum; the Court directed a four-member committee chaired by the Chief Justice of India or a nominee, holding a casting vote, on the lines laid down in 2010.

With those defects identified, the path to constitution finally opened. The Central Government issued notifications S.O. 1932(E) and S.O. 1933(E) dated 1 June 2016, constituting the Tribunal under Section 408 and the Appellate Tribunal under Section 410 with effect from that day, and a companion notification brought the tribunal chapters of the 2013 Act into force, including Section 245 on class actions, whose commencement had waited for a forum to exist. Justice M. M. Kumar, a former Chief Justice of the Jammu and Kashmir High Court who had chaired the Company Law Board, became the first President of the Tribunal. Justice S. J. Mukhopadhaya, a former judge of the Supreme Court, became the first Chairperson of the Appellate Tribunal. The Tribunal opened with eleven benches: the Principal Bench and a second bench at New Delhi, and one bench each at Ahmedabad, Allahabad, Bengaluru, Chandigarh, Chennai, Guwahati, Hyderabad, Kolkata and Mumbai. Fourteen years after enactment, the forum existed.

## Constitution day and the first year

The commencement machinery of 1 June 2016 repays attention, because it shows constitution as an act of administration rather than a single signature. Three notifications did the work. Two constituted the tribunals. The third commenced the provisions of the Companies Act that presuppose a tribunal: the chapter on the tribunals themselves, the class action in Section 245, the restoration jurisdiction, the approval and adjudication provisions threaded through the Act that name the Tribunal as the deciding authority, and the first stage of Section 434, which carried the Company Law Board's pending proceedings across on the day the Board died. The Board did not fade; it stood dissolved by the same instrument that created its successor, and its members and staff watched a decade of pendency change letterheads overnight. The sequencing inside Section 434 tells the government's own risk assessment: the Board's matters moved at once because they were already tribunal-shaped, the High Courts' matters waited for the transfer rules of December because they were not, and the sickness matters were extinguished rather than moved because the regime that generated them was being repealed outright.

The eleven benches that opened were placed in the cities where the company courts had sat, and the pattern was not accidental: the Principal Bench and a second bench at New Delhi, and benches at Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Ahmedabad, Allahabad, Chandigarh and Guwahati. Within six months the forum was asked to begin its second life. The corporate insolvency provisions of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code were brought into force on 1 December 2016, the same day the sickness repeal took effect, so the Tribunal's first December carried admissions under a brand new code, the first files of the sickness migration, and the tail of the Company Law Board's queue simultaneously. The following year brought the Reserve Bank of India's first directed referrals, a list of twelve large defaulting accounts sent to the Tribunal for resolution, which placed the institution at the centre of the banking system's bad loan reckoning before it had completed a full year of insolvency work. No serious account of the institution's later queues can begin later than this: the docket that defines the Tribunal's reputation was substantially assembled in its first twelve months, by three separate statutes and one regulator, against a bench strength designed in an earlier world for one of them.

The final column is the point of the table. At every stage something remained pending or unadjudicated, and the thing left pending was never the policy of having a tribunal. It was always the question of who would sit on it and how they would be chosen.

## Who may sit: qualifications, selection and tenure

The composition rules are where the constitutional litigation left its permanent mark, and they are also the rules an institution profile cannot treat as boilerplate, because in this institution the composition is the history. Section 407 defines the vocabulary: a Judicial Member is a person who is or has been a judge of a High Court, or a district judge for at least five years, or an advocate with at least ten years of practice; a Technical Member is drawn from senior levels of the Indian Corporate Law Service or the Indian Legal Service, from chartered accountancy, cost accountancy and company secretaryship in practice for at least fifteen years, from senior industrial and corporate management, and from the presiding officers of labour courts and tribunals with prescribed experience. Section 409 prescribes the President's qualifications: a person who is or has been a judge of a High Court for five years. Section 411 prescribes for the Appellate Tribunal that the Chairperson be a person who is or has been a judge of the Supreme Court or a Chief Justice of a High Court, and that its Judicial Members be High Court judges or members of the Tribunal of five years' standing.

The appointment machinery runs through Section 412. The President, the Chairperson and the Judicial Members are appointed after consultation with the Chief Justice of India. Other members are appointed on the recommendation of a Search-cum-Selection Committee, a body whose composition was itself litigated: the enacted text gave the executive three of five places, the Supreme Court struck that down in 2015, and the operative composition follows the Court's directions, with the Chief Justice of India or a nominee in the chair holding a casting vote, a senior judge beside the chair, and the secretaries for corporate affairs and for law and justice completing the committee. Tenure is governed by Section 413. The President and the members hold office for five years and are eligible for reappointment for a further term of five years; the President may serve until sixty-seven and the members until sixty-five, while for the Appellate Tribunal the corresponding ceilings are seventy for the Chairperson and sixty-seven for members. A person below fifty is not eligible for appointment as a member at all, a floor that codifies the judicial insistence that the tribunal's members arrive with a substantial career behind them rather than ahead of them.

Two features of this scheme deserve emphasis because they explain the litigation that produced it. The first is that tenure length was never a detail. Three years, the original offer, was condemned in 2010 as too short to attract persons of standing or to give them independence in office, and the same instinct ran through every later round of tribunal litigation in India, including the striking down of four-year terms in rules and ordinances made years afterwards. A member who must seek reappointment or post-retirement preferment from the executive within a short cycle is not independent of it in any sense that survives contact with a hard case. The second feature is that selection, not qualification, was the decisive defect twice running. The qualification clauses were corrected and re-corrected, but the reason the Tribunal could not be constituted between 2010 and 2015 was that the executive would not surrender a majority on the body that chose the members, and the Court would not accept a body without surrendering it. Independence, in the end, was a question of arithmetic on a committee.

### Is the National Company Law Tribunal a court?

The Tribunal is a statutory tribunal and not a court, but it exercises judicial power. Section 424 gives it the powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure for summoning witnesses, discovery, receiving evidence and enforcing attendance, its orders are enforceable as decrees, and its decisions remain subject to the supervisory jurisdiction of the High Courts.

## Why the technical member became the contested office

The single most litigated figure in the institution's design is not the President but the technical member, and understanding why explains the shape of both judgments. The tribunal idea promises mixed adjudication: a judicial member for law and a technical member for the commercial, accounting and managerial substance in which company disputes are embedded. The constitutional anxiety is equally plain: if the technical member is a serving officer of the very department that administers company law, or one barely retired from it, drawn from the Indian Corporate Law Service or the legal service at a rank below real independence, sitting beside a judge and voting equally on questions of law, then the transfer of jurisdiction from the High Court is not a transfer to a specialist court but a transfer, in part, to the administration. The 2010 judgment answered that anxiety with a standard: members of a tribunal that replaces a High Court must have the qualifications, seniority and independence the replaced office demands, technical members must be genuinely senior experts in their fields, and questions involving substantial questions of law belong with judicial members. The 2002 Act failed the standard in both directions, admitting officers of comparatively junior rank and contemplating long-service administrators deciding the whole range of the Tribunal's work.

The 2013 Act narrowed the gap without closing it, which is why the 2015 judgment invalidated the clauses it did. The enacted text of Section 409(3) admitted, among others, officers of the Indian Corporate Law Service and the Indian Legal Service who had held posts at a level the Supreme Court read as below the seniority its 2010 directions required, and Section 411(3) carried the same dilution upward into the Appellate Tribunal, where the defect compounded, since the appellate tier is the system's court of record in everything but name. The Court's insistence on seniority is sometimes caricatured as judicial protectionism, the guild defending its turf. Read closely, it is a claim about litigant perception and therefore about legitimacy: a promoter whose company is being wound up, or a bank whose resolution plan is being examined, accepts the decision of a bench whose members visibly owe their places to merit and seniority rather than to departmental nomination, and acceptance, not merely correctness, is what makes a first-instance system final. The eventual settlement, five-year terms, an age floor of fifty, seniority thresholds in the qualifications and a judiciary-led selection, is the institutional form of that claim, and every later attempt to dilute it by rule or ordinance, across the whole tribunal system, has met the same doctrine and the same result.

## The working shape: benches, the Principal Bench and removal

Section 419 governs how the institution arranges itself to sit. The powers of the Tribunal are exercised by benches, and the default bench is a division bench of two members, one judicial and one technical, a pairing that embodies the design theory of the whole tribunal experiment: questions of law and questions of commercial and accounting fact are decided together, in one order, by one bench carrying both kinds of competence. Specified classes of matters may be assigned to single-member benches under the rules, and the President may constitute larger or special benches for matters that warrant them, allocate work among benches, and transfer matters between them. The Principal Bench sits at New Delhi and is presided over by the President. In the wider system, the Appellate Tribunal likewise works through benches, its principal seat being New Delhi, where the Chairperson sits. The bench structure matters beyond administration, because in a two-member bench a disagreement between the judicial and technical member, or an absence that breaks the pairing, does not merely slow a case: it removes the forum's capacity to decide that class of case at all until the bench is reconstituted. The institution's working unit is not the member but the pair. The attrition arithmetic of pairs is harsher than the arithmetic of single judges: a bench of two loses half its sanctioned capacity when one member is missing and the whole of its capacity when the missing member's pairing cannot be reconstituted from the bench's remaining strength, so a vacancy rate that would slow a court system by a fifth can disable a tribunal's benches out of proportion to the number. Headship vacancies carry the same multiplying effect in the administrative dimension. The Appellate Tribunal has functioned under acting headship for a period following a Chairperson's retirement, with the head of the first tribunal holding charge of the appellate office, an arrangement the statute permits for continuity and one that no one describes as a design goal. Succession at the top is where the pipeline failure is most visible, because a Chairperson's retirement date is fixed by age and knowable years ahead; an institution that cannot replace a foreseeable vacancy at its summit on time has disclosed something about the priority of the whole appointment system, and the disclosure is not lost on the members watching from below, whose own tenure security and post-retirement prospects are administered by the same machinery.

The offices at the head of the two tribunals carry their own detailed discipline in Sections 415 to 418. When the office of President or Chairperson falls vacant, or its holder is absent or unable to act, the senior-most member discharges the functions of the office until a successor enters upon it, an arrangement that keeps the institution formally headed but cannot supply the administrative drive, the roster planning, the allocation of sensitive matters, that a substantive head provides, so long vacancies at the top compound vacancies in the ranks. Members may resign by writing to the Central Government. Removal, under Section 417, is deliberately hard: a member may be removed only on proved misbehaviour or incapacity after an inquiry, conducted for the President and the Chairperson by a judge of the Supreme Court and for other members by a judge of a High Court, in which the member has been informed of the charges and heard, and the grounds extend to insolvency, conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude, physical or mental incapacity, acquisition of prejudicial financial interest, and abuse of position. The salaries, allowances and conditions of service of members cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment. Staff of the tribunals serve under the general superintendence of the President or the Chairperson as the case may be, and the institution's registry, its officers who scrutinise filings, issue notices, maintain cause lists and issue certified copies, is the layer at which the vacancy figures for officers and staff, recorded in the government's own answers, do their quiet damage: a fully staffed bench with an understaffed registry still lists fewer matters and issues orders more slowly.

The administrative placement of the institution completes the structural picture, and it is the feature critics of tribunalisation press hardest. The Tribunal is administered through the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, the very ministry whose regulatory arms, the Registrar of Companies, the Regional Directors, the Serious Fraud Investigation Office, appear before it as parties and whose parent government is the largest single shareholder in the public sector companies whose disputes it decides. The design defence is that some ministry must hold the budget and that adjudicatory independence is secured by the selection and removal disciplines rather than by the letterhead on the budget line. The critics' answer is that infrastructure is adjudication by other means: courtrooms, registries, staff and technology arrive through the ministry, and an institution that must ask a litigant department for its working tools is independent on a shorter leash than the appointment provisions suggest. The Supreme Court's direction of a National Tribunals Commission, an umbrella body to oversee appointments, administration and conditions across tribunals, is in large part an attempt to cut exactly this knot, by interposing a specialised institution between the tribunals and the line ministries.

## What the Tribunal decides under the Companies Act

The Companies Act jurisdiction is best read as four groups of business. The first group is protective: proceedings for oppression and mismanagement under Sections 241 and 242, in which the Tribunal holds some of the widest remedial powers in Indian civil law, able to regulate the conduct of a company's affairs, supersede boards, set aside agreements and order the purchase of shares; and class actions under Section 245, which allow prescribed numbers of members or depositors to sue on behalf of the class for wrongs done to the company, a remedy that slept in the statute book until the Tribunal existed to receive it. The second group is transactional: compromises, arrangements, mergers and amalgamations under Sections 230 to 232, approval of reduction of share capital under Section 66, conversion of a public company into a private company, approval connected with redeemable preference shares and debentures, and the adjudication of applications to restore companies that the Registrar has struck off the register under Section 252. The third group is disciplinary within the corporate form: compounding of offences under Section 441, orders on incorporation obtained by false information, and freezing or investigation-adjacent orders that the Act routes to it. The fourth group is exit: winding up on grounds other than inability to pay debts, including the just and equitable ground, since insolvency-based winding up moved to the Code.

The pattern across the four groups is that the Tribunal is not a court of general jurisdiction wearing a specialist label. Each head of business arrives by an express grant, and matters the Act does not grant, it does not have. Prosecution for offences under the Act, for example, is not its business: the criminal side of company law runs through the Special Courts the Act designates, and the Tribunal's compounding power, which closes certain offences on payment, is an adjudicatory power over the compounding application, not a trial. Equally, the Tribunal has no roving commission over corporate wrongdoing at large. Where a grievance does not fit a granted head, the suit or prosecution must go where the general law puts it. This architecture of enumerated grants is the standard Indian tribunal design, and it is why jurisdictional skirmishes, whether a matter falls inside or outside a granted head, occupy so large a share of the appellate record.

### Does the National Company Law Tribunal hear criminal cases?

The Tribunal holds no criminal trial jurisdiction. Offences under the Companies Act are prosecuted before the Special Courts designated under the Act, and fraud investigation belongs to the Serious Fraud Investigation Office and the courts that try its complaints. The Tribunal's compounding power under Section 441 disposes of compoundable offences by adjudication, which is not a trial.

One further boundary defines the forum against the rest of the legal system. Section 430 bars any civil court from entertaining a suit or proceeding in respect of any matter which the Tribunal or the Appellate Tribunal is empowered to determine, and bars injunctions against action taken under the Act. The bar is what makes the enumerated grants exclusive rather than concurrent. But the bar on civil courts is not a bar on constitutional review: the Supreme Court's jurisprudence on tribunals, running back to L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India, preserves the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 over tribunal decisions, and the parliamentary design itself supplies the statutory appeal to the Appellate Tribunal and onward to the Supreme Court. The Tribunal replaces the court of first instance; it does not float free of the judicial system above it.

## The Companies Act heads in detail

The protective heads are the oldest part of the Tribunal's inheritance, and the most litigated. Proceedings for oppression and mismanagement descend from the 1956 Act, under which they lay before the High Courts and then the Company Law Board, and Sections 241 and 242 re-enact them with the Tribunal as the forum. Section 244 sets the threshold for who may apply: members numbering at least one hundred or one-tenth of the total membership, whichever is less, or members holding at least one-tenth of the issued share capital, with the Tribunal empowered to waive the threshold where the case warrants. The breadth of the relief under Section 242 shows why the jurisdiction was worth fighting over: the Tribunal may regulate the conduct of the company's affairs in future, direct the purchase of shares of some members by others or by the company, terminate or set aside agreements between the company and its management or third parties, remove directors, appoint administrators, and pass continuing directions for years. No decree of a civil court in a shareholder suit carries a comparable toolkit, because the jurisdiction is not compensatory at its core; it is supervisory over the governance of a going concern, and it assumes a forum that can keep a matter under review rather than dispose of it once.

The class action under Section 245 is the head that best illustrates the forum-dependence of rights. The provision lets prescribed numbers of members, depositors or classes of them apply to the Tribunal for orders against a company and its directors, auditors and advisors for fraudulent, unlawful or wrongful conduct, claiming damages and other relief on behalf of the class. It was modelled on the derivative and class remedies of other jurisdictions and was enacted in response to a widely felt gap in Indian law, exposed by large corporate failures in which small investors had no collective remedy at all. Yet a remedy that lies only before a tribunal can be commenced only when the tribunal exists: Section 245 waited in the enacted statute from 2013 until the commencement notification of 1 June 2016, and its early career before the Tribunal has been sparse, a mixture of the threshold requirements, the availability of the insolvency route for the largest failures, and the simple unfamiliarity of a new remedy. The pattern recurs across the institution's history: Parliament writes the right, the right waits on the forum.

The transactional heads are the institution's daily bread and the part of its work least visible in the appellate reports. Under Sections 230 to 232, every compromise or arrangement between a company and its creditors or members, and every merger or amalgamation, passes through the Tribunal: meetings are ordered or dispensed with, approval by a majority representing three-fourths in value of those voting is recorded, notices go to the regulators and the income tax authorities, and sanction follows, with the Tribunal able to supervise implementation and to order the transferor's dissolution in a merger. Reduction of share capital under Section 66, a transaction that touches creditor protection directly, requires the Tribunal's confirmation on notice to creditors and regulators. Conversion of a public company into a private company, which changes the governance regime applicable to it and historically offered an escape from regulation, requires the Tribunal's approval. Restoration under Section 252 brings companies back from the Registrar's strike-off register, on appeal by persons aggrieved against the Registrar's order and on application by the company, its members, creditors or workmen within the long window the section allows, a jurisdiction that acquired heavy volume when strike-off drives removed large numbers of companies from the register in a short period and the affected, the dormant and the merely careless all arrived at the same forum to be sorted.

The exit heads, after the Code, are residual but real. Winding up under Section 271 remains with the Tribunal for grounds other than inability to pay debts: where the company has acted against the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the state, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, where its affairs have been conducted fraudulently or it was formed for a fraudulent or unlawful purpose, where it has defaulted in statutory filings for five consecutive financial years, and where the Tribunal considers it just and equitable that the company be wound up. The just and equitable ground imports a century of company jurisprudence into the Tribunal's hands. Compounding under Section 441 occupies the opposite temper: offences punishable with fine alone may be compounded by the Tribunal, or by the Regional Director within the monetary limits prescribed, closing prosecutions without trial where the default is technical and cured. Between these two, the Tribunal also decides such matters as applications against refusal to register transfers, orders on investigations and the freezing of assets in the circumstances the Act specifies, and approvals threaded through the statute wherever Parliament preferred an adjudicatory to an administrative decision.

## The second jurisdiction: insolvency and the Adjudicating Authority

No sooner had the Tribunal been constituted to settle company law than the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 made it the Adjudicating Authority for the corporate insolvency resolution process, assigning to the same benches the admission of insolvency applications, the approval of resolution plans, and orders for liquidation. The Code's own design, its timelines and its contested questions belong to the pillar account of the Code and to the separate record of what the Code has done in practice; the institutional point for this profile is narrower and heavier. Parliament had built one forum, sized it for company law, and then, before the forum had completed a year of work, routed through it a high-volume, time-critical jurisdiction in which statutory clocks run in days and every adjournment consumes the value the process exists to preserve. Section 60 fixes the forum: the Adjudicating Authority for a corporate debtor is the bench of the Tribunal with territorial jurisdiction over the place of the corporate debtor's registered office. There is no discretion to spread the load elsewhere, and no alternative forum when a bench is short of members.

The insolvency jurisdiction also carried a defined and deliberately narrow appellate discipline inside the Tribunal itself. The Code makes the Committee of Creditors the commercial decision-maker on a resolution plan, and the Tribunal's role is supervisory in a limited sense: it examines whether a plan approved by the committee complies with the Code, including the requirements of Section 30(2), and it does not sit in appeal over the commercial merits. The Supreme Court enforced that boundary in K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank and in the Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Limited v. Satish Kumar Gupta litigation, holding that the commercial wisdom of the committee is not justiciable beyond the Code's own parameters and that neither the Tribunal nor the Appellate Tribunal may modify a plan the committee has approved. The discipline cuts both ways for the institution. It protects the Tribunal from being asked to redo every bargain, which its capacity could never sustain; and it confines the Tribunal's real contribution to the gatekeeping and approval stages, where its own speed, not the law, is usually the variable in dispute.

The constitutional settlement of the Code itself belongs to the Tribunal's story, because the forum was the settlement's delivery mechanism. The Code was challenged at its foundations, on the classification of creditors, on the withdrawal and moratorium provisions, and on the position of the Adjudicating Authority within the separation of powers, and in Swiss Ribbons Private Limited v. Union of India the Supreme Court upheld the Code in substance, observing in the course of the judgment that the experiment, judged by the shift of control from defaulting managements and the volume of resolutions it produced, was working. Judgments of that kind are delivered about statutes, but they are executed by institutions: every proposition in the Code's defence, that resolution would be faster than the sickness and winding-up regimes it replaced, that a committee of creditors would decide commercially, that an Adjudicating Authority would police the process without owning it, depended for its truth on the Tribunal doing its stages on time. The later qualification of the experiment, in the public discourse and in the Court's own suo motu observations, has likewise been executed by the institution, in the opposite direction: the doctrine survived every challenge, and the delivery mechanism accumulated the delays. The pandemic period supplied a stress test of the forum's dependence on policy: Section 10A, inserted into the Code in 2020, suspended the filing of new insolvency applications for defaults arising in the protected window of that emergency, and the Tribunal's insolvency intake fell by legislative command, a reminder that the queue outside its doors is set by Parliament and the economy jointly, while the queue inside them is set by benches.

A third insolvency stream arrived in December 2019, when the Code's provisions on insolvency resolution for personal guarantors to corporate debtors were brought into force and placed before the same Adjudicating Authority where the connected corporate process sits, a routing the Supreme Court upheld in Lalit Kumar Jain v. Union of India. The logic of the routing is coherence: a guarantor's estate and the corporate debtor's process interlock, and one forum can see both. The cost of the routing is arithmetic: guarantees are given across the whole lending market, and every stream added to the same benches draws on the same pool of sitting members. Nothing in the institution's sanctioned strength changed when the stream was added.

## The boundaries of the forum: residuary jurisdiction, arbitration and the writ courts

Every tribunal lives at its boundaries, and the Tribunal's boundaries have generated a jurisprudence of their own. The broadest grant in its insolvency work is Section 60(5) of the Code, which gives the Adjudicating Authority jurisdiction over any application or proceeding by or against the corporate debtor, any claim by or against it, and any question of priorities or any question of law or facts arising out of or in relation to the insolvency resolution or liquidation. That residuary clause is what allows the Tribunal to decide, within a single process, disputes that would otherwise scatter across forums: the status of a disputed contract, the recovery of the debtor's assets, the treatment of a guarantee, the validity of a termination. The Supreme Court in Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Limited v. Amit Gupta confirmed the breadth of the clause while marking its limit: the jurisdiction is anchored to the insolvency, and the Tribunal is not to wander into public law disputes that have no bearing on the resolution, a caution later cases have repeated for disputes dressed in insolvency clothing to capture the forum. The boundary matters institutionally because breadth at the centre is tolerable only if the edges hold; a residuary jurisdiction that swallowed every commercial dispute touching a distressed company would convert the Adjudicating Authority into a general commercial court, which the design, and the benches available, cannot sustain.

The arbitration boundary runs on a parallel logic. A corporate insolvency proceeding is a proceeding in rem, effective against the world, where an arbitration is a private proceeding in personam between the parties to the agreement. The Supreme Court's treatment in Indus Biotech Private Limited v. Kotak India Venture Fund settled the working rule: an application under Section 7 of the Code, once it crosses the admission threshold, displaces the arbitral route for the same subject matter, because the collective process cannot be subordinated to a bilateral one; before admission, a genuine and substantial dispute about the existence of the default itself can still be examined. The same hierarchy appears in the Tribunal's Companies Act work, where applications under Section 8 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act to refer oppression or scheme matters to arbitration have been refused on the ground that the statutory reliefs of Sections 242 and 232 are in rem in character and beyond an arbitral tribunal's competence. Practitioners test these boundaries constantly, because forum choice is often outcome choice, and the appellate line that polices them is one of the clearest bodies of doctrine the two tribunals have produced.

The third boundary is constitutional rather than statutory. The Companies Act bars civil courts, through Section 430, and the Code bars courts and authorities, through Section 63, and overrides inconsistent law through Section 238, but neither statute can bar the High Courts' writ jurisdiction, which the tribunal jurisprudence from L. Chandra Kumar onwards has preserved as part of the basic structure of judicial review. In practice the High Courts exercise that jurisdiction with self-imposed restraint where the statutory appeal to the Appellate Tribunal offers an effective remedy, intervening where the Tribunal has acted without jurisdiction, in breach of natural justice, or where the appellate route is illusory in the circumstances. The result is a layered system that the design does not advertise but the record shows plainly: the Tribunal is final for most purposes, the Appellate Tribunal corrects it on fact and law, the Supreme Court corrects the Appellate Tribunal on law, and the High Courts stand behind the whole structure as a constitutional reserve, used sparingly because the statutory chain exists. Understanding the institution requires seeing all four layers at once, because litigant behaviour at each layer is shaped by the layer above it: the knowledge that facts close at the Appellate Tribunal concentrates advocacy at the first two tiers, and the knowledge that a writ remains available disciplines the tribunals against the jurisdictional excess that would invite it.

## The gatekeeping stages under the Code

The stages at which the Tribunal handles insolvency work explain why its capacity question is an insolvency question and not merely a company law question. The first stage is admission. A financial creditor under Section 7, an operational creditor under Section 9 after a demand notice, or the corporate debtor itself under Section 10, applies to the bench with territorial jurisdiction, and the Tribunal must decide whether a default exists and whether the application is complete. In Innoventive Industries Limited v. ICICI Bank, the first great judgment under the Code, the Supreme Court described an admission inquiry that is deliberately summary: on proof of debt and default, the application is to be admitted, and the moratorium under Section 14 follows, freezing suits, enforcement and recovery against the corporate debtor while the resolution process runs. The design intends admission to be measured in days, and the statutory outer frame for completing the whole process, extended by later amendment and judicial reading to three hundred and thirty days in the longest cases, assumes that the gate opens on schedule. Every week an admission application waits for a sitting bench, the value the Code exists to protect is exposed outside the moratorium, and the delay is not neutral between the parties: it is a transfer from the creditors and the enterprise to whoever benefits from the status quo.

The middle stage belongs to the resolution professional and the committee of creditors, and the Tribunal's appearances within it are episodic but consequential: replacing a resolution professional, deciding applications against avoidance transactions such as preferential, undervalued and fraudulent transfers under Sections 43, 45 and 66, ruling on disputes over claims and on the conduct of the process, and extending time where the Code permits. The final stage returns to the Tribunal in full: approval of the resolution plan under Section 31, which makes the plan binding on all stakeholders including the state authorities, or, where no compliant plan arrives or the committee votes for it, an order of liquidation under Section 33, followed by the supervision of the liquidation and eventually dissolution. Plan approval is the stage at which the queue bites hardest, for a commercial reason that distinguishes it from ordinary arrears. A resolution plan is a priced bargain about a wasting asset, struck on assumptions about the debtor's business, the sector and the cost of money, and it does not keep. A plan approved after long delay may be performed reluctantly, renegotiated under pressure, or abandoned, and the Supreme Court's suo motu attention to the approval queue at the Principal Bench, described in the record section of this article, reflects judicial recognition that delay at this stage consumes the Code's central achievement.

Beyond the resolution track, the liquidation and exit work of the Code also runs through the same benches: the supervision of liquidations it has ordered, applications by liquidators for directions and for the dissolution of corporate debtors at the end of the process, the approval jurisdiction at the close of voluntary liquidations under Section 59, and the prosecution-adjacent work of deciding avoidance and wrongful trading applications that arise within liquidations as they do within resolution. Exit work is chronically under-discussed in the institution's public profile, which fixates on admissions and plan approvals, but it is neither small nor simple: liquidation estates generate disputes over assets, claims and distributions for years, and every such dispute is a bench-day taken from the same pool. A realistic accounting of the Tribunal's insolvency burden counts the tail as well as the intake, and the tail is long.

Superimposed on all three stages is the Tribunal's position at the junction of two procedural cultures. Company petitions arrive with the habits of civil litigation: elaborate pleadings, interlocutory skirmishing, evidence by affidavit stretched across many hearings. Insolvency applications arrive with a statutory clock and an expectation of summary disposal. The same bench, on the same day, may be asked to move at both speeds, and the roster cannot be optimised for both at once. This is the operational form of the separation debate recorded later in this article, and it is also why comparisons of raw disposal numbers between the Tribunal and the courts it replaced mislead unless they are read stage by stage. The forum's problem is concentrated: it is worst where the statute demands speed, and least damaging where the parties' own pace governs.

## Procedure, powers and the three-month endeavour

The Tribunal is not bound by the procedure of the Code of Civil Procedure. Section 424 directs it to be guided by the principles of natural justice and the provisions of its governing statutes, and equips it, for the purposes of its work, with the powers of a civil court: summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses, requiring discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavit, issuing commissions, and reviewing, dismissing for default and deciding ex parte within the section's terms. Its orders carry the enforceability of decrees, so that a direction of the Tribunal is not a recommendation awaiting another forum's muscle. Section 425 gives it the power to punish for contempt of itself, on the footing of a High Court under the Contempt of Courts Act. Section 420 permits it to amend an order to rectify a mistake apparent from the record within two years, which is a power of correction, not a general power to rehear its own decisions on the merits; parties who want the merits reopened must appeal. The Tribunal and the Appellate Tribunal also hold the power to punish, through the penalty jurisdiction the Act and the Code confer at various points, and to proceed against conduct that obstructs their processes.

The Tribunal's rules preserve, in addition, its inherent power to make such orders as the ends of justice require, the standard residuary clause of Indian tribunal procedure, and it is under the combined authority of the statutes, the rules and that residuary power that the benches conduct the interlocutory life of their cases: interim protection of assets and of the status quo, directions for meetings and for the conduct of processes, orders on discovery and on the production of records, and the day-to-day discipline of parties who might otherwise profit from the queue. Interim power in a slow system carries a weight the drafters of faster systems need not consider: where a final hearing is distant, the interim order is, for long stretches, the effective law between the parties, and the benches' interim jurisprudence, on share transfers during oppression petitions, on asset dissipation during insolvency admission, on meeting directions during schemes, is a body of practice that practitioners study as carefully as the final-order case law.

Against this equipment stands Section 422, the statute's own statement of expected speed: the Tribunal and the Appellate Tribunal shall endeavour to dispose of every application, petition and appeal before them within three months, and where they cannot, the President or the Chairperson may extend the period by up to ninety days after recording the reasons for the delay. The provision is an endeavour clause, not a limitation period: a matter does not lapse, and an order does not become void, because the three months ran out. Its significance is diagnostic rather than legal. It is the benchmark Parliament wrote against which the institution's performance is measured, and the extension machinery, reasons recorded and ninety days added, was designed for the exceptional file. When the exceptional became the ordinary, the provision remained on the page as a measure of the distance between the design and the record.

### Can the National Company Law Tribunal review its own orders?

The Tribunal holds only a narrow power over its own orders. Section 420(2) of the Companies Act permits rectification of a mistake apparent from the record within two years of the order, and no general power of review on the merits exists. A party dissatisfied with the substance must appeal to the Appellate Tribunal.

## Appeals: the route, the clocks and the question-of-law limit

The appellate design is tight, and its clocks differ between the two statutes, which is a frequent source of error in practice. Under the Companies Act, an appeal from an order of the Tribunal lies to the Appellate Tribunal under Section 421, to be filed within forty-five days from the date on which a copy of the order is made available to the appellant; the Appellate Tribunal may condone delay for sufficient cause, but by a further period not exceeding forty-five days, so ninety days is the outer bound. From the Appellate Tribunal, an appeal lies to the Supreme Court under Section 423 within sixty days of the order being received, condonable by a further period not exceeding sixty days, and, critically, on a question of law arising out of the order. Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code the clocks are shorter and the condonation narrower: Section 61 gives thirty days to appeal to the Appellate Tribunal, with a condonable extension of fifteen days and no jurisdiction in the Appellate Tribunal to entertain an appeal filed later, a hard cap the Supreme Court applied in National Spot Exchange Limited v. Anil Kohli; Section 62 gives forty-five days to the Supreme Court, on a question of law, with a condonable extension capped at fifteen days.

Standing and condonation practice complete the appellate picture, and both reward close reading because the two statutes answer them differently. Each statute opens its appeal to a person aggrieved by the order, a formulation the courts have read with some generosity in the insolvency context, where promoters, guarantors, dissenting creditors, unsuccessful resolution applicants and occasionally employees have all carried grievances to the Appellate Tribunal, while policing the boundary against strangers to the process who seek to reopen concluded bargains. On condonation, the Companies Act route behaves like ordinary appellate law: forty-five days to file, delay condonable for sufficient cause within the further forty-five, and a body of discretionary case law applying the familiar tests of explanation and diligence. The Code route is built to a different philosophy. Its thirty days reflect the perishability of the underlying bargain, its fifteen-day condonation cap is jurisdictional rather than discretionary, and the Supreme Court's insistence, in the National Spot Exchange case and in the line that follows it, that the Appellate Tribunal simply has no power beyond the cap, converts what looks like a procedural detail into a substantive feature of the Code: rights in an insolvency are time-stamped, and a creditor or promoter who sleeps past the forty-fifth day loses the appeal outright, whatever the merits. Practitioners regard the two clocks as the single most dangerous trap in the whole appellate design, precisely because they coexist: the same lawyer may carry a Companies Act appeal governed by a forgiving regime and a Code appeal governed by an unforgiving one, out of the same building, in the same week.

### What does the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal do?

The Appellate Tribunal is the single appellate forum above the Tribunal. It hears appeals under the Companies Act, insolvency appeals under the Code including appeals from the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India, appeals from the National Financial Reporting Authority, and competition appeals from the Competition Commission of India, with onward appeal to the Supreme Court on questions of law.

The question-of-law limit on the Supreme Court appeal is the most widely misunderstood feature of the whole design, and the brief for this article marks it as such. A dissatisfied litigant does not get a second appeal on the facts. The Supreme Court examines whether the Appellate Tribunal's order raises a question of law, and findings of fact, assessments of evidence and commercial evaluations stand unless they cross into legal error or perversity that itself raises a question of law. The design choice is deliberate and it shapes behaviour all the way down: because the factual record closes at the Appellate Tribunal, the quality of adjudication at the two tribunal tiers is effectively final for most disputes, which is precisely why the constitutional insistence on senior, independent members was not decoration. A litigant choosing advisors for tribunal litigation should treat the Appellate Tribunal as the last forum that will fully re-examine the record; this article offers general information on the design and is not a substitute for advice on a live matter.

## The Appellate Tribunal at work

The Appellate Tribunal is smaller, less visible and, in design terms, the most consequential tier of the system, because it is the last tier at which facts are examined. Its presiding officer is the Chairperson, whose statutory qualification is the highest in the tribunal system: a person who is or has been a judge of the Supreme Court or a Chief Justice of a High Court. Around the Chairperson sit judicial members drawn from the High Courts and from senior membership of the Tribunal, and technical members drawn from the same senior pools as the Tribunal's. It sits principally in New Delhi, and a bench of the Appellate Tribunal also sits at Chennai, a recognition that a single-seat appellate forum for a national first-instance system imposes travel and access costs on litigants from the south that sit awkwardly with a reform justified by efficiency. Its four streams arrive with different characters. Companies Act appeals carry full records and resemble classic appellate work. Insolvency appeals arrive against the Code's brutal clocks, frequently as applications for interim protection against an admission order whose moratorium has already changed the parties' positions on the ground. Competition appeals bring a regulatory record and questions of economic evidence. Appeals touching the financial reporting authority and the insolvency board are fewer and more doctrinal. The mixture means the Appellate Tribunal functions, in substance, as India's commercial appellate court for tribunalised subjects, and its accumulated case law, reported and followed across benches of the Tribunal, is the closest thing the system has to a unifying commercial jurisprudence at the appellate level.

The standard the Appellate Tribunal applies to itself deserves note, because it is the hinge between the design and the constitutional settlement. It is an appellate body, not a supervisory one: it may confirm, modify or set aside the order under appeal and may examine the evidence afresh, which is what makes it the true second instance and what gives the Supreme Court's question-of-law limit its practical meaning. At the same time, on the commercial decisions of a Committee of Creditors the Code withdraws merits review from both tiers, so the Appellate Tribunal's insolvency work, like the Tribunal's, is concentrated on legality: the existence of default, the compliance of a plan with the Code, the fairness parameters the Supreme Court has located inside the statute rather than outside it. The two tribunals were designed as a hierarchy and have matured into a division of labour, in which the first tier manages volume and the second tier manages doctrine. When commentators describe the system as top-heavy or bottom-heavy, it is worth locating their complaint in that division: the system's doctrinal output, produced by the smaller tier, is generally respected, while its managerial output, produced by the larger tier against the larger shortage, is where the failure concentrates.

## The bodies the National Company Law Tribunal replaced

The constitution of the Tribunal on 1 June 2016 dissolved the Company Law Board, the executive-era forum that had decided company petitions since 1991 on delegated authority, and Section 434 transferred the Board's pending proceedings to the Tribunal, which took them up from the stage they had reached. The Board being replaced had itself an instructive history. The Company Law Board was first constituted in 1964, working within the department that administered company law, and was reconstituted in 1991 as a quasi-judicial body under Section 10E of the 1956 Act, with benches that included a principal bench at New Delhi and regional benches, exercising powers Parliament progressively delegated to it, culminating in the oppression and mismanagement jurisdiction and the power to compound offences. For a quarter of a century it was Indian company law's workhorse forum, and its limits were the limits of its parentage: its members included serving and retired officials appointed without a judicial selection process, its powers were formally the Central Government's own powers exercised by delegation, and its orders travelled upward as administration rather than as adjudication. Litigants used it because it was there and because the alternative, the High Courts, was slow; nobody defended its design as a matter of principle, and when the tribunal project finally cleared the courts, the Board's absorption was the least mourned of the three inheritances.

The replacement changed the legal character of the forum as much as its address. The Board decided as a delegate of the Central Government's own powers under the old Act, its members appointed without the judicial selection discipline the courts had demanded for the Tribunal; the Tribunal decides as an independent adjudicatory body whose composition had been fought over for a decade precisely because it would no longer be a department by another name. Matters that had waited in the Board's queue did not restart. They changed forum and governing culture at once.

The High Courts' company jurisdiction followed in stages. Section 434(1)(c) took effect on 15 December 2016 and the Companies (Transfer of Pending Proceedings) Rules, 2016 moved the High Courts' pending company proceedings across in categories: proceedings other than winding up, such as arbitration references, compromises, arrangements and reconstructions, transferred at the stage reached unless an order had been reserved; winding-up petitions founded on inability to pay debts that had not been served on the respondent transferred to be dealt with under the Code, while the most advanced petitions, served and substantially heard, remained with the courts that had seized them. The staging was an admission written into the transfer rules themselves: a new forum with eleven benches could not absorb four dockets in a single day without breaking the matters in transit.

The third inheritance came from the sickness regime. The Sick Industrial Companies (Special Provisions) Repeal Act was brought into force on 1 December 2016, and the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction and its appellate authority, the Appellate Authority for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction, stood dissolved; the story of that repeal and of the regime it ended is told in the account of [the SICA repeal and the end of the BIFR](/2012/06/01/sica-1985-bifr-repeal/). Their pending references, inquiries and appeals abated rather than transferred, the statutory shield that had frozen creditor action under the sickness law fell away, sanctioned revival schemes continued to run, and companies that had been inside the sickness process were given a window of one hundred and eighty days from the commencement of the Code to approach the Tribunal under the insolvency provisions instead. Three inheritances, three different mechanics: transfer from the Board, staged transfer from the courts, and abatement from the sickness bodies, each depositing its own legacy of aged matters at the door of a forum that had existed for six months.

## The record: sanctioned strength against assigned work

The institution profile discipline this series follows is to state the design first and the record second, and the record of the Tribunal is a record of arithmetic. The sanctioned strength of the Tribunal has stood at sixty-three, the President and sixty-two members, while its jurisdiction has done nothing but grow: the Companies Act docket it was constituted for, the insolvency jurisdiction added within months, the personal guarantor stream added in 2019, and the Appellate Tribunal's additional streams from financial reporting and competition. Against that fixed sanctioned strength, the pattern of vacancies has been documented repeatedly and from the government's own side of the record. Answering a question in the Lok Sabha in July 2022, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs recorded sixteen benches, a Principal Bench at New Delhi and fifteen others, thirty vacancies among members and two hundred and seventy vacancies among officers and staff, and pendency of about twenty thousand nine hundred and sixty-three cases recorded on 31 May 2022. The Tribunal's own public accounting of its working has recorded forty-eight members in position against the sanctioned sixty-two besides the President, with a substantial share of its benches sitting for half days because members were not available to compose full courts, and the NCLT Bar Association has carried the same arithmetic into proceedings before the Supreme Court.

The consequences are located exactly where the design is most sensitive. A bench of the Supreme Court, taking suo motu notice of the condition of the Tribunal, described as grim the position at the Principal Bench, where several hundred applications seeking approval of resolution plans awaited decision, the oldest of them delayed by periods running into hundreds of days. Plan approval is the stage at which the Tribunal's own speed, rather than any party's conduct, determines whether the Code's promise of time-bound resolution survives; a plan that waits a year for approval is being renegotiated by inflation, attrition and decay while it waits. The full accounting of what that does to resolution outcomes belongs to [the implementation record of the Code](/2012/04/15/ibc-implementation-record/), and the design of the process whose admission and approval stages the Tribunal staffs is set out in [the guide to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code](/2012/04/01/insolvency-bankruptcy-code-2016-guide/). The institutional fact beneath both accounts is the same: the constraint on the system is not the substantive law, which the courts have settled with unusual completeness, nor practitioner willingness, nor the text of the timelines. It is the number of benches sitting on a given day.

The practitioner grievance, which any honest profile must record at full strength, is that this arithmetic is experienced as arbitrariness. Matters are listed before benches that do not sit, or sit for half a day, or are reconstituted mid-stream when a member retires or is transferred, and hearings restart before a bench that has not heard the earlier arguments. Vacancies are not announced with succession in place; a member's five-year term can run out with no appointment notified, so the institution loses experienced members to the calendar rather than to any decision. The complaint is not that the Tribunal is hostile or incompetent. It is that the institution is run as though its staffing were an administrative afterthought, when its staffing is the entire product: a tribunal sells decided cases, and every unfilled seat is inventory that can never be recovered, because hearing days lost do not accumulate as capacity later.

The queue itself has a composition worth recording, because not all of it is the institution's fault in the same way. Part of the pendency is inherited age: matters that arrived from the Company Law Board and the High Courts in 2016 arrived already old, and a forum can dispose at rates well above its filings for years while its oldest cases still grow older. Part is structural growth: filings under the Code rose as lenders learned the forum, and each policy decision since, the guarantor notification, the homebuyers' recognition as financial creditors, the threshold revision that filtered smaller defaults, moved volume up or down by amounts no bench plan anticipated. Part, the part for which the institution and its administering ministry bear direct responsibility, is the gap between sanctioned and sitting strength, which converts every other pressure directly into waiting time. Disposal effort is not the missing variable: benches that sit dispose at rates comparable to the forums they replaced, and the institution's aggregate disposals across its first years are substantial by any measure. The missing variable is sittings multiplied by benches, which is to say members in post, which is to say the appointment pipeline treated in this article's reform discussion. Analysts who quote a single pendency figure for the Tribunal without separating the three components are measuring the institution's inheritances and its assignees' decisions as though they were its own performance, and the record section of an honest profile should refuse that shortcut as firmly as it refuses the excuse that the whole queue is inherited.

The government's side of the record also deserves its statement. Appointments have been made in successive rounds, including a substantial round of twenty members in 2021, benches have been added beyond the original eleven, and the digitisation of filing and cause lists is genuine. The defence, in its strongest form, is that appointment to a judicial forum cannot be hurried past the selection discipline the courts themselves imposed, and that the same judgments which demand speed in disposal also demand a judiciary-led, deliberative selection that takes time. There is force in that, and it is the force of a real dilemma rather than an excuse. But the dilemma has a known institutional answer, which is to begin selection processes well before vacancies arise and to keep a pipeline of selected members ready, and the vacancy pattern documented across successive years is a pattern of processes begun late, not of processes that were inherently slow.

## The territorial map: benches and the federal spread

The Tribunal is a national institution that litigants meet locally, and its geography is part of its design. The eleven benches of 2016 followed the map of the old company courts, which followed the map of industrial India as it stood in the middle of the twentieth century, and the network has since widened: the government informed the Lok Sabha in July 2022 that five new benches, at Jaipur, Kochi, Indore, Amravati and Cuttack, had been taken up for establishment during 2018 and 2019, and spoke of sixteen benches in all, the Principal Bench at New Delhi and fifteen others. Each bench exercises the jurisdiction assigned to it over the states and union territories notified against it, and jurisdiction follows the registered office, so the map decides, for every company in the country, which city's bench will hear its oppression petition, sanction its merger and admit its insolvency. The territorial principle has consequences the framers of a national code do not always count. A bench serving a large commercial hinterland carries a heavier and more complex list than a bench of identical sanctioned size serving a smaller one, so national averages of pendency conceal the institution's real geography, in which the crisis is concentrated at the busiest registries, above all the Principal Bench and Mumbai, while quieter benches run closer to their statutory clocks. Reform addressed to the average will miss the institution; reform addressed to the map, bench strength weighted by filings rather than by symmetry between regions, meets it.

The federal character of the institution also shows in what it is not. It is not a state institution: no state government appoints its members, funds its registries or can alter its jurisdiction, and its benches' orders run across the country without territorial embarrassment. For company and insolvency law, which sit in the Concurrent and Union fields and govern enterprises that operate across state lines as a matter of course, the single national forum with territorial benches is the constitutionally natural shape, and it contrasts with the fields, land, tenancy, much of taxation's administration, where adjudication remains state-shaped. The contrast sharpens the institution's accountability problem rather than relieving it: precisely because no state shares responsibility, the whole of the Tribunal's performance, its benches, its staff, its technology and its vacancies, is attributable to one government, and the record examined in this profile has exactly one address for its praise and its blame.

## Filings, representation and the working day of a bench

An institution profile should also record how the body actually meets the public, because procedure is where jurisdiction becomes experience. Proceedings before the Tribunal are governed by the National Company Law Tribunal Rules, 2016 and, for the Appellate Tribunal, its own rules of the same year, which prescribe the forms of petitions and applications, their verification, the payment of fees, service of notice, and the conduct of hearings. Section 432 of the Companies Act preserves a feature inherited from the Company Law Board that distinguishes this forum from the courts in daily practice: a party may appear in person or through an authorised representative, and the category of authorised representatives includes chartered accountants, company secretaries, cost accountants and legal practitioners in practice, alongside officers of the company. Company secretaries and chartered accountants therefore plead before this Tribunal as a matter of professional routine, which has made the institution a genuine multi-professional forum and has shaped its culture towards the documentary and the accounting-based, a culture closer to an audit file than to oral advocacy.

Filing has been progressively digitised, and insolvency applications in particular run through electronic filing systems that generate the case numbers, scrutiny reports and defect lists on which the registry works. The working day of a bench is published through cause lists, and the texture of those lists is itself evidence in the capacity debate: lists of scores of matters against a sitting of a few hours, in which the matters reached receive minutes, contested hearings are squeezed between mentionings, and the balance rolls forward. Practitioners adapt rationally to that texture: they seek interim protection early because final hearings are distant, they forum-proof their drafting against benches changing composition mid-case, and they price tribunal timelines into transaction documents. Each adaptation is individually sensible and collectively corrosive, because the longer the queue, the more interlocutory the litigation becomes, and the more interlocutory the litigation, the longer the queue. Breaking that loop, rather than any doctrinal refinement, is the operational priority the institution's own record sets, and it returns the analysis to the same point from a fourth direction: members, sitting, deciding, in sufficient number, is the whole reform.

## The wider argument: tribunalisation and its critics

The Tribunal's staffing history is one chapter in a larger Indian argument about tribunals, and the wider question of what the [Tribunals Reforms Act of 2021](/2021/08/15/tribunals-reforms-act-2021-explained/) did to conditions of service across the tribunal system belongs to that Act's own article. The outline needed here is short. The Madras Bar Association litigation did not end with this Tribunal's constitution. In Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank, the Supreme Court struck down tribunal rules framed under the Finance Act of 2017 and warned that short tenures and executive-dominated selection invite the interference they claim to guard against. When rule-making and then an ordinance and an Act returned with four-year tenures, panels of two names and appointment timelines that weakened the selection committee's recommendations, the Court struck the offending provisions down again in successive Madras Bar Association judgments, and in 2025 it struck down core provisions of the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 itself, holding that Parliament cannot re-enact, without curing the defects, provisions already held unconstitutional, and directed the establishment of a National Tribunals Commission within four months so that appointments, administration and conditions of service for tribunals would be overseen by a body built for the purpose. The through-line from 2010 to 2025 is one sentence: the courts will permit Parliament to move jurisdiction out of the courts, and will not permit Parliament to move it into dependence.

The two sides of the tribunalisation argument, stated at full strength, are these. For the design: courts of general jurisdiction in India carry arrears that make specialist queues rational; a tribunal concentrates expertise, standardises procedure, develops a coherent appellate line through a single national appellate body, and can be created, expanded and located by notification rather than by the slow accretion of judicial strength; for technical regulatory fields, mixed benches of judicial and domain members decide better than either would alone. Against the design: every tribunal transfer withdraws work from an independent judiciary and re-houses it in a body whose members the executive selects, houses, pays and can decline to reappoint; the proliferation of tribunals fragments jurisprudence; and the Indian record shows governments of every composition treating tribunal vacancies as low-priority vacancies, so the promised speed is spent waiting for benches that do not sit. The Tribunal illustrates both cases at once, which is why it is the right institution through which to teach the argument. Its appellate output on company and insolvency law is by common professional consent expert and coherent. Its first-instance queues are the exhibit for the other side.

A reform proposal that follows from the institution's own record, and which surfaces in the practitioner discourse whenever capacity is discussed, is the separation of the two jurisdictions: distinct benches, or in the fuller version a distinct tribunal, for insolvency, so that the time-critical Code caseload does not compete with company petitions for the same sitting days, and neither queue can cannibalise the other. The case for separation is queue logic. Insolvency is perishable in a way that a compromise scheme or an oppression petition is not, and a single roster forces a daily, invisible triage between them. The case against is that the two jurisdictions share doctrine, counsel and often the same companies, that a split duplicates appellate structures, and that the binding constraint is total members rather than their allocation: a split tribunal at the same combined strength merely partitions the shortage into two smaller shortages, each less able to absorb an absence. The honest conclusion is that separation is a second-order reform. The first-order reform is embarrassingly plain: fill the sanctioned strength, select in advance of vacancies, and treat bench days as the scarce public resource they are. Anyone assembling a confidential briefing on the two queues faces the separate problem of keeping the material and its sources safe, and [VaultBook's account of how journalists protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) is a useful parallel for how that storage discipline is organised in practice.

## What the record suggests: the reform menu

If the diagnosis is capacity, the reform menu writes itself, and its items differ sharply in how much law they need. The first item needs no law at all: synchronise selection with tenure. Every member's term end is known on the day of appointment, five years in advance, so a selection process begun eighteen months before a vacancy is a matter of calendar discipline rather than prediction, and a panel of selected candidates held ready converts retirements from capacity shocks into routine successions. The second item is the sanctioned strength itself. Sixty-three was fixed when the insolvency jurisdiction was an expectation; a strength review that priced the three jurisdictions the Tribunal actually carries, and the Appellate Tribunal's four streams, against disposal rates would produce a number entitled to replace it, and expanding sanctioned strength by notification is administratively trivial compared to the litigation its absence generates. The third item is the secretariat: registry staff, courtrooms and technology funded and administered at arm's length from the litigant ministry, which is the function the National Tribunals Commission has been directed to perform and the test by which that Commission, when it is established, should be judged. The fourth item is roster design inside the institution: protected insolvency benches whose sitting days cannot be borrowed by the company list, protected company benches in return, and listing practices that group connected matters before benches that will survive to finish them. None of these requires a constitutional amendment, a new statute, or a fresh theory of tribunals. All of them are unglamorous. The institution's history suggests that the unglamorous items are precisely the ones that decide whether the glamorous ones, the codes and the consolidated forums, work.

Two items on the public menu deserve a cooler assessment. The first is the proposal, heard whenever a large insolvency fails, to return company jurisdiction to the High Courts and end the tribunal experiment. It answers the wrong question: the High Courts' arrears, which justified the transfer, have not been cured in the intervening years, the doctrine built since 2016 presupposes the tribunal structure, and a second migration of lakhs of matters between forums would repeat the transition costs of 2016 at a larger scale, this time without a destination demonstrably better staffed. The second is the proposal to solve delay by multiplying benches without asking why sanctioned benches sit unfilled: bench creation by notification is cheap, and governments have announced benches that waited long for members, so the binding constraint must be named honestly, which is appointments completed on time. An institution profile ends where this one began: the forum is its members, and every reform that does not reach the members reaches nothing.

## Verdict: the bottleneck, twice

The One Test for this institution is whether the fourteen-year wait and the subsequent record are one story or two. They are one story. The Tribunal was the bottleneck twice: first by absence, when company law reform, the class action, the consolidated forum and the sickness exit all waited on a forum that could not be constituted until the executive surrendered control of its selection; and then by capacity, when the insolvency jurisdiction, the most time-sensitive jurisdiction Parliament has ever assigned, was routed to benches whose sanctioned strength was fixed for a smaller docket and whose actual strength has repeatedly run far below even that. In both periods the substantive law was ready before the forum was. In both periods the delay was attributed, in the discourse of the day, to complexity, transition and the inherent difficulty of the subject, when the operative cause was simpler: a forum is its members, and members were either not appointable or not appointed.

The lesson this institution teaches the rest of the statute book is about sequence. Jurisdiction is created by a section and a notification, in a day. Adjudicatory capacity is created by selections, appointments, courtrooms and staff, over years. A legislature that moves jurisdiction faster than it builds capacity is not reforming the forum; it is relocating the queue and lending the queue a new address. Parliament got the substantive designs right twice, in the Companies Act and in the Code, and in both cases the forum question, treated as an administrative detail, became the substance. Whether the National Tribunals Commission, directed by the Court and awaited from the government, changes that sequence is the next institutional question, and it will be answered, as the earlier questions were, not in the text of any statute but in the arithmetic of benches sitting on ordinary days.

The measure for judging the institution, finally, should be its own. The statute that created it promised disposal in three months with a recorded ninety days of grace. The judgments that permitted it promised a forum as independent as the courts it replaced. The Code that enriched it promised resolution within a frame of a few hundred days. Each promise was made by a different author at a different moment, and the institution is answerable to all three at once, which is why single-figure verdicts on it, whether celebratory or dismissive, miss the structure of its situation. It has kept the second promise better than the first and the third, and the distance between the promises kept and the promises missed is the distance between design and staffing, between what the law of the institution says and what the administration of the institution supplies. Hold the design constant and vary only the staffing, and the institution described in the first half of this profile is the institution the country would have. That counterfactual, and not nostalgia for the courts or the Board, is the standard this profile applies, and by that standard the verdict stands: the law was ready, twice; the forum, twice, was not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What kinds of cases does the National Company Law Tribunal hear?

The Tribunal hears the company law matters the Companies Act, 2013 assigns to it and the insolvency matters the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code assigns to it. On the company side, these include oppression and mismanagement petitions, class actions, compromises, arrangements, mergers and amalgamations, reduction of share capital, conversion of public companies into private companies, restoration of struck-off companies, compounding of offences and winding up on grounds other than inability to pay debts. On the insolvency side, sitting as the Adjudicating Authority, it admits or rejects insolvency applications against corporate debtors, approves resolution plans, orders liquidation, and decides the insolvency of personal guarantors to corporate debtors in the linked situations the Code specifies. Each head of jurisdiction arrives by an express statutory grant.

### Q: Why did it take fourteen years to constitute the National Company Law Tribunal?

Parliament created the Tribunal in the Companies (Second Amendment) Act, 2002, but the provisions were challenged on the ground that the qualifications, tenure and selection arrangements for its members compromised judicial independence. In 2010 the Supreme Court upheld the Tribunal's creation but held the staffing model defective: three-year tenure was too short, selection required a committee led by the judiciary with a casting vote, and technical member qualifications had to be raised. When Parliament re-enacted the scheme in the Companies Act, 2013, a second challenge succeeded in part in 2015, with the qualification clauses in Sections 409 and 411 and the executive-majority selection committee in Section 412(2) held invalid. Only after those corrections did the government constitute the Tribunal, on 1 June 2016.

### Q: What is the appeal route from an NCLT order?

An appeal from an order of the Tribunal lies to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, under Section 421 of the Companies Act for company matters and under Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code for insolvency matters. From the Appellate Tribunal, a further appeal lies to the Supreme Court, under Section 423 of the Companies Act or Section 62 of the Code, and in each case only on a question of law arising out of the order. The High Courts' writ jurisdiction over tribunal decisions also survives under the Constitution, and civil courts are barred by Section 430 of the Companies Act from entertaining matters the Tribunal is empowered to determine, so the statutory route through the Appellate Tribunal is the ordinary path.

### Q: Can the tribunal review a Committee of Creditors decision?

Within narrow limits only. The Committee of Creditors is the commercial decision-maker under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, and its commercial wisdom, including the approval or rejection of a resolution plan by the required majority, is not open to review on the merits by the Tribunal or the Appellate Tribunal. What the Tribunal examines is legality: whether the process followed the Code, and whether an approved plan complies with the requirements of Section 30(2), including the treatment the Code mandates for operational creditors and dissenting financial creditors. The Supreme Court settled this boundary in the K. Sashidhar and Essar Steel litigation. A plan that is lawful stands even if the Tribunal considers it unwise; a plan that breaches the Code falls even if the committee supported it.

### Q: How are NCLT members appointed?

The President and the Judicial Members are appointed by the Central Government after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, and the remaining members are appointed on the recommendation of a Search-cum-Selection Committee whose composition follows the Supreme Court's directions: the Chief Justice of India or a nominee chairs it with a casting vote, joined by a senior judge and by the secretaries for corporate affairs and for law and justice. The structure was litigated twice, because the enacted text of Section 412 gave the executive three of five seats on the committee, and the Supreme Court held in 2015 that an executive majority in selecting the members of a judicial forum compromised its independence. Qualifications for each class of member are prescribed in Sections 409 and 411 of the Companies Act.

### Q: Why is the National Company Law Tribunal so backlogged?

The backlog is principally an arithmetic condition. Sanctioned strength has stood at sixty-three, the President and sixty-two members, while the jurisdiction has grown from the Companies Act docket alone to include the whole corporate insolvency jurisdiction under the Code and, from 2019, insolvency of personal guarantors to corporate debtors. Vacancies widen the gap: the government itself recorded thirty member vacancies in a statement to the Lok Sabha in July 2022, with pendency of about twenty thousand nine hundred and sixty-three cases recorded on 31 May 2022, and the Tribunal's working has repeatedly included benches sitting half days for want of members. Caseload routed to a forum is a decision; the benches available to hear it are another, and the two decisions have not been matched.

### Q: Which bodies did the National Company Law Tribunal replace?

The Tribunal absorbed the work of three sets of forums. The Company Law Board, which had decided company petitions on delegated executive authority, was dissolved on 1 June 2016 and its pending proceedings transferred under Section 434. The company jurisdiction of the High Courts, including pending company petitions, compromises and winding-up matters, moved across in stages from December 2016 under the transfer rules, the most advanced winding-up petitions remaining with the courts. The Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction and its appellate authority were dissolved when the sickness law was repealed with effect from 1 December 2016, their pending matters abating, with affected companies able to approach the Tribunal under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code.

### Q: Can an NCLAT order be appealed to the Supreme Court?

Yes, but only on a question of law. Section 423 of the Companies Act permits an appeal to the Supreme Court within sixty days of receipt of the Appellate Tribunal's order, with a possible condonable extension of a further period not exceeding sixty days, and confines the appeal to a question of law arising out of the order. Section 62 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code does the same for insolvency appeals within forty-five days, with condonation capped at fifteen days. Findings of fact and assessments of evidence made by the tribunals are not reopened as a matter of course; the Supreme Court's role is to correct legal error, which makes the Appellate Tribunal the last forum that examines the full record in most disputes.

### Q: What is the difference between the NCLT and the NCLAT?

The Tribunal is the forum of first instance and the Appellate Tribunal is the appellate forum above it. Cases begin before the Tribunal: company petitions under the Companies Act and insolvency applications under the Code are filed, heard and decided there, by benches sitting across the country. The Appellate Tribunal, seated in New Delhi and presided over by a Chairperson who is or has been a Supreme Court judge or a Chief Justice of a High Court, hears appeals against the Tribunal's orders and does not conduct trials of its own in this stream. It also receives appeals from other regulators, including the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India, the National Financial Reporting Authority and the Competition Commission of India, which the Tribunal itself never hears.

### Q: What is the tenure of the President and members of the NCLT?

Section 413 of the Companies Act gives the President and every member a term of five years, with eligibility for reappointment for one further term of five years. Service is also bounded by age: the President may hold office until sixty-seven and a member until sixty-five, while in the Appellate Tribunal the ceilings are seventy for the Chairperson and sixty-seven for members. A person below the age of fifty cannot be appointed a member. Tenure length was one of the central issues in the constitutional litigation over the Tribunal, the original three-year term in the 2002 provisions having been condemned by the Supreme Court as too short to attract members of standing or to protect their independence in office.

### Q: Can the National Company Law Tribunal punish for contempt?

Yes. Section 425 of the Companies Act gives the Tribunal and the Appellate Tribunal the power to punish for contempt of themselves, in the same manner and to the same extent as a High Court under the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, which applies to those proceedings with specified modifications, including reading references to a High Court as including the Tribunal or the Appellate Tribunal. The power protects the forum's process: disobedience of its orders and conduct that obstructs or scandalises its functioning can be proceeded against without the Tribunal having to borrow another court's authority. It sits alongside the tribunals' other enforcement equipment, including the power under Section 424 to have their orders executed in the manner of decrees of a court.

### Q: What happened to company cases pending in the High Courts when the NCLT began?

They transferred in stages rather than at once. Section 434(1)(c) of the Companies Act took effect on 15 December 2016, and the Companies (Transfer of Pending Proceedings) Rules, 2016 moved the pending proceedings across in categories. Proceedings other than winding up, including compromises, arrangements and reconstructions, transferred at the stage they had reached unless an order had been reserved. Winding-up petitions based on inability to pay debts that had not been served on the respondent company transferred to be dealt with as insolvency applications under the Code, and their petitioners had to supply the particulars the Code requires within a stipulated period, failing which the petition abated. Petitions that had been served and were substantially advanced, including those where a winding-up order was imminent, stayed with the High Courts.

### Q: Why does the NCLT hear insolvency cases of personal guarantors?

Because the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code links the guarantor's insolvency to the corporate debtor's process. Section 60(2) provides that where a corporate insolvency resolution process or liquidation proceeding of a corporate debtor is pending before a bench of the Tribunal, an application relating to the insolvency of a personal guarantor to that corporate debtor shall be filed before the same bench. The connection is substantive: the guarantor's liability is the mirror of the corporate debt, claims and recoveries in the two estates affect each other, and divided forums would risk inconsistent findings on the same default. The provisions were brought into force on 1 December 2019 and their routing to the Tribunal was upheld by the Supreme Court in Lalit Kumar Jain v. Union of India. Insolvency of other individuals remains with the Debt Recovery Tribunals.

### Q: How much delay can the NCLAT condone in an insolvency appeal?

At most fifteen days beyond the thirty days the Code allows. Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code requires an appeal against an order of the Adjudicating Authority to be filed within thirty days, and permits the Appellate Tribunal to condone a delay of not more than fifteen further days if it is satisfied there was sufficient cause. The Supreme Court held in National Spot Exchange Limited v. Anil Kohli that once forty-five days from the order have run, the Appellate Tribunal has no jurisdiction to entertain the appeal at all, however strong the explanation for the delay. The strictness is deliberate and contrasts with the Companies Act route, where Section 421 allows forty-five days for filing and condonation of delay by a further period of up to forty-five days.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court strike down in the NCLT's design in 2015?

In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India, decided in May 2015, the Court upheld Section 408 and the constitution of the Tribunal under the Companies Act, 2013, but invalidated two parts of the scheme as departures from its 2010 directions in the R. Gandhi case. The first was a set of qualification clauses for technical members in Section 409(3) of the Act, with the parallel provision for the Appellate Tribunal in Section 411(3), which admitted categories of officials at a seniority below what the Court had required for persons exercising High Court jurisdiction. The second was Section 412(2), which composed the selection committee with an executive majority. The government was directed to correct the provisions, and the Tribunal was constituted the following year under the corrected discipline.

### Q: Can a civil court decide a company matter within the NCLT's jurisdiction?

No. Section 430 of the Companies Act provides that no civil court shall have jurisdiction to entertain any suit or proceeding in respect of any matter which the Tribunal or the Appellate Tribunal is empowered by or under the Act to determine, and that no injunction shall be granted by any court or other authority in respect of action taken or to be taken under the Act. The bar makes the Tribunal's enumerated jurisdiction exclusive rather than parallel, and a party who files a civil suit on a matter assigned to the Tribunal faces dismissal on jurisdiction alone. The bar does not extend to constitutional remedies: the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court over tribunal decisions survives, as the jurisprudence on tribunals has consistently preserved it.

### Q: Which bench of the NCLT should a company case be filed before?

The bench with territorial jurisdiction over the company's registered office. The Tribunal sits through benches notified under Section 419 of the Companies Act, each exercising jurisdiction over the states and territories assigned to it, and a company matter is filed before the bench within whose territorial limits the registered office of the company is situated; the Principal Bench sits at New Delhi. The same principle governs insolvency, since Section 60 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code ties the Adjudicating Authority to the place of the corporate debtor's registered office, which also fixes where a linked personal guarantor application goes. Parties do not choose among benches by convenience: the registered office fixes the forum, and a case filed before the wrong bench is liable to be returned or transferred.

### Q: What did the Eradi Committee recommend on company adjudication?

The High Level Committee on Law Relating to Insolvency and Winding Up of Companies, chaired by Justice V. Balakrishna Eradi, a former judge of the Supreme Court, recommended at the end of the nineteen nineties that the fragmented company jurisdiction of the period, divided among the High Courts, the Company Law Board and the sickness machinery under the Board for Industrial and Financial Reconstruction, be gathered into a single specialist tribunal with an appellate tribunal above it. The reasoning was that dispersion produced delay, inconsistency and forum-dependent outcomes in matters that turned on the same body of law. Parliament implemented the recommendation through the Companies (Second Amendment) Act, 2002, and the Tribunal the committee proposed was eventually constituted, after two rounds of constitutional litigation, in 2016.

### Q: Should insolvency and company matters be heard by separate tribunals?

The proposal surfaces regularly in practitioner debate, and each side has a real argument. Separation would stop the time-critical insolvency queue, in which delay destroys the asset under resolution, from competing for sitting days with company petitions that can bear longer timelines, and would let each forum develop an undivided roster and expertise. Against separation, the two jurisdictions share doctrine, counsel and frequently the same companies, a split would duplicate appellate and administrative structures, and the binding constraint on either forum is the number of members appointed rather than their allocation, so a split at unchanged strength partitions the shortage instead of curing it. The stronger reading of the record is that capacity is the first-order problem and separation a second-order refinement.

### Q: Why were company cases transferred from the High Courts to a tribunal?

The transfer rested on the condition of the company jurisdiction in the High Courts and on the case for specialisation. Company petitions in the High Courts competed with the full range of civil, criminal and constitutional litigation for judicial time, company benches carried heavy arrears, and outcomes in winding up and sickness matters were widely documented as taking years that destroyed the value in dispute. A specialist tribunal promised members who heard only this class of work, procedures designed for corporate disputes rather than borrowed from general civil process, benches located in commercial centres, and a single national appellate forum to settle divergent regional approaches. The Supreme Court accepted the transfer as constitutionally permissible in 2010, while insisting that the receiving forum match the independence of the courts it replaced.
