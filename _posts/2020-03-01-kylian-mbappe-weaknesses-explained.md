Any honest account of Kylian Mbappe's weaknesses has to begin by admitting what the phrase means and what it does not. It does not mean that a forward of this level has holes an ordinary side can walk into. It means that his output is unusually conditional, that the conditions which reduce it are identifiable and repeatable, and that a team which knows exactly what those conditions are can decide, in advance, whether it is capable of holding them. That is the useful version of the question. The useless version is the one that circulates constantly, in which a limitation is discovered, announced as a character flaw, and then never priced.

Pricing is the whole argument of this article. Every state that suppresses a space-dependent forward costs the defending side something, and usually costs it in a currency that has nothing to do with him. Compress the pitch vertically and you concede the ball in front of your block for long stretches. Double the wide channel and you concede the switch. Sit a midfielder permanently in the inside lane and you lose him as a passing option when you win possession back. So the meaningful question is never whether Mbappe can be limited. He can, and sides have done it, and the mechanisms are not mysterious. The question is whether your team can afford the state that limits him for ninety minutes, against everything else the opposition is doing at the same time.

That framing matters more than usual with this subject because the public conversation about him has drifted a long way from mechanism. Two positions dominate. One holds that he does not defend. The other holds that he vanishes when a team sits deep. Both contain a real observation and both then convert it into something the observation does not support. This article takes each position seriously, finds the football underneath it, and reaches a verdict rather than a shrug. Where the limitation becomes a plan, the sequenced version of that plan lives in the counter blueprint on [how a defensive structure is actually built against him](/2020/02/15/how-to-stop-kylian-mbappe/), which owns the how-to-stop query; the category-level frame, meaning what makes any elite forward hard to contain, belongs to the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/).

## What Kylian Mbappe's weaknesses actually are, stated as conditions

The anchors first, because accuracy in the basics is what buys the right to argue about the difficult parts. He is a right-footed French forward developed at Monaco before Paris Saint-Germain and Real Madrid, a World Cup winner with France, who has operated both from the left touchline and through the middle. His defining quality is acceleration carrying the ball, expressed most dangerously when he receives facing forward with grass ahead of him. He finishes with either foot, though the right is dominant when he cuts inside from the left. Those are stable facts, and everything that follows is built on them rather than on anything that changes.

Now the limitations, each stated in the only form that survives scrutiny, which is a condition plus an observable consequence.

The first and largest is that his threat is coupled to available running distance. Reduce the distance between his reception point and the last line, and the component of his game that produces the most dangerous actions is reduced with it. This is not a flaw in the ordinary sense. It is a coupling, and the same coupling runs in the other direction: the acceleration that makes a forty-metre corridor lethal makes a six-yard corridor comparatively ordinary, because there is nothing in a six-yard corridor for acceleration to convert into an advantage.

The second is that receiving with his back to goal in a congested area is a smaller and less refined part of his game than receiving in stride. He can do it, and he has done it more as his career has progressed, but it is not where his edge lives, and a defender who forces the reception into that shape has changed the nature of the contest from one he loses often to one he loses less often.

The third is that aerial contests are not a primary weapon. He is not a target for a direct delivery into the box, he is not a set-piece threat of the kind that forces a marking scheme to be rewritten, and a cross that arrives above shoulder height is not a chance he converts at the rate he converts a ball played across the ground into his path.

The fourth is structural rather than individual: what a forward of this profile is asked to do out of possession, and what the team behind him can sustain as a result, has been a recurring topic of tactical discussion throughout his career. That is a real subject and it is treated here strictly as a question of role and structure. It is not a question about willingness, effort, or professionalism, and this article will not treat it as one.

The fifth, and the most frequently misdescribed, is that the version of him deployed as a central forward and the version deployed from the left present genuinely different profiles, with different strengths and different failure conditions, and that the central version is more exposed to the compressive states described below than the wide version is.

Every one of those five is conditional. Written without the condition, each becomes a false sentence.

## The coupling, not the flaw: why runway is the root variable

Start with the geometry, because the geometry explains everything else and almost nobody states it.

A forward whose primary weapon is acceleration converts a specific input into a specific output. The input is unopposed running distance between the point at which he gains control of the ball and the point at which a defender can make contact with him or the ball. The output is separation. Separation is what produces the shooting position, the cutback, the penalty-area entry at speed, and the foul in a dangerous area. If the input is large, the output is large and the defensive contest is close to unwinnable in isolation. If the input approaches zero, the output approaches the output of an ordinary technical forward in a crowd, which is to say the contest becomes normal.

The critical point, and the one that most analysis skips, is that this is a smooth function rather than a switch. There is no threshold at which the threat disappears. There is a continuous reduction, and the shape of that reduction is what a coach actually needs. A ten-metre corridor is not half as dangerous as a twenty-metre corridor; it is far less than half, because acceleration needs distance to express itself and because a defender's recovery angle improves faster than the attacker's speed advantage grows over short distances. That non-linearity is why compression works at all, and it is also why compression has to be near-total to work, which is exactly what makes it expensive.

There is a second input alongside distance, and it is the one defenders control more easily. Body orientation at the moment of reception decides whether the distance available is usable at all. A forward who receives already facing the goal converts the corridor immediately. A forward who receives with his shoulders square to the touchline, or facing his own goal, has to spend a touch and a turn before the corridor exists, and that touch is the window in which a second defender arrives. This is why the orientation battle is worth more coaching time than the pace battle, and why a slower defender who consistently wins the orientation battle outperforms a quicker one who does not.

The third input is the arrival time of a second defender. A one-on-one against this forward in an open corridor is a duel a defender loses more often than he wins, whatever his own speed. A two-on-one in the same corridor is a contest the defending side wins far more often, not because the second defender tackles anybody, but because the first defender can now show him in a direction rather than trying to stay in front of him, and showing is a solvable problem where staying in front is not.

Those three inputs, distance to the last line, orientation at reception, and second-defender arrival time, are the entire mechanism. Each of the limitations discussed in this article is a statement about what happens when one or more of them is taken away, and every counter-plan against him is a plan to manipulate them. The sequenced version of that manipulation, the line heights and the cover angles and the triggers that move a block between states, is the subject of the counter blueprint rather than this article.

### Why does compressing the pitch against a fast forward cost the defending team something?

Because the same compression that removes his runway also moves your whole block backward and inward, which surrenders the ball in front of you, shortens your own counter-attacking distance, and concentrates your defenders in the exact zone where cutbacks and second balls are decided. You suppress one threat by manufacturing a different one.

That trade is the hinge of the entire article, and it deserves to be stated without softening. There is no free method of reducing a space-dependent forward. Every method has a bill attached, the bill is paid in a different area of the pitch, and the bill compounds over ninety minutes because the states that suppress him are physically and mentally expensive to hold. A team that holds a compressed block flawlessly for sixty minutes and then holds it imperfectly for thirty has not limited him; it has simply chosen which thirty minutes to concede.

## The Mbappe condition ladder

The most useful thing an analyst can hand a coach is not a list of weaknesses but a map of match states, showing what the forward can still do in each state, what he cannot, what the state costs the defending side, and how long that side can realistically hold it. Everything below is stated in terms a coach can observe from the touchline without a data feed.

The ladder runs from the state most favorable to him, which is open transition, down to the state least favorable, which is a fully compressed block with the ball in front of it. The states are not stable. A match moves up and down this ladder constantly, and the single most common planning error is to prepare for one state and then fail to notice that the match has moved.

| Match state | What he can still do | What is substantially removed | What the defending side pays | Realistic hold time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Open transition, ball turned over in your half | Everything: the run in behind, the carry from halfway, the isolation duel, the shot from the angle | Nothing meaningful | Nothing yet; this is the state you are trying never to enter | Not a state you hold, a state you avoid |
| Settled possession against a high line, ball in midfield | Depth runs behind the last line, diagonal runs across the far centre-back, receiving in stride between the lines | Little; the corridor still exists | Nothing structurally, but the line height itself is the concession | Indefinite, and indefinitely dangerous |
| Mid-block, last line around twenty-five metres from goal | Wide isolation from a standing start, the inside cut onto the right foot, the run onto a through ball into the channel | The long carry from deep; the pure footrace behind | Territory and the initiative in midfield; a longer route back into attack | Most of a half, with concentration lapses at the ends |
| Low-mid block with a doubled wide channel | Combination play, the disguised inside run, the arriving shot from the edge, the decoy that drags two markers | The one-on-one he wants; the runway; the isolation | A permanent numerical loss on the far side and in central midfield | Around an hour before the far-side concession starts to hurt |
| Compressed low block, last line inside the penalty area | Short receptions in a crowd, the shot through bodies, movement that occupies two defenders and creates space for others | The carry, the acceleration, the run behind, the finishing angle he prefers | Complete surrender of territory, no attacking outlet, escalating set-piece exposure, physical and mental load on every defender | Rarely more than a phase before the block needs to expand to breathe |
| Compressed block plus a man advantage in his zone | Very little independently | The individual threat almost entirely | An outnumbered rest of the pitch, which competent opponents attack directly | Minutes, not a match |

Read the right-hand columns rather than the left-hand ones. The left column is the part everybody already knows. The two right columns are the article's actual contribution, because they convert a weakness into a decision. A coach who reads that table and concludes that the fifth row is the answer has misread it. The fifth row is the answer to a question nobody asked, which is how to stop him for eight minutes. The genuine question is which row your side can occupy for the whole match without the price becoming larger than the threat you removed.

## The compression cost principle

Here is the claim this article advances, named so it can be argued with and used.

The compression cost principle: every match state that reduces a space-dependent forward also imposes a cost on the defending side somewhere else on the pitch, so the honest question is never whether he can be limited but whether your team can afford the state that limits him for the full duration of a match.

The principle has three consequences, and each one changes how a reader should treat almost every claim made about this player online.

The first consequence is that any statement of the form "he was quiet against a deep block" is incomplete until it names what the deep block paid. A side that defends its own penalty area for an hour and concedes nothing to him has usually conceded the entire match elsewhere: no attacking phase of its own, an accumulation of set pieces against, defenders making repeated maximal decelerations, and a substitution plan that has to start early. Sometimes that is a good trade and a coach should make it deliberately. It stops being analysis the moment the cost is left out of the sentence.

The second consequence is that the cost is not linear either. Moving from a mid-block to a low block does not double the cost; it multiplies it, because the low block removes your outlet, which lengthens the phases you defend, which raises the physical load, which is what causes the compression to fail in the final quarter of a match. The states that suppress him most are the states hardest to sustain, and that inverse relationship is the reason he scores late goals against sides that had contained him comfortably for an hour. Those goals are usually described as a player raising his level. It is more accurate to describe them as a block losing the state it needed.

The third consequence is the one that matters most for a coach with an ordinary squad. Because the cost is real, the correct plan is almost never the maximum-suppression plan. It is the highest row on the ladder your side can genuinely hold, combined with a specific answer for the two or three moments per match when the state breaks. A team that holds a disciplined mid-block for ninety minutes and accepts three dangerous moments has usually defended him better than a team that holds a compressed block for sixty minutes and then falls apart, even though the second team's first hour looked more impressive.

### What does a defending side actually pay to hold a compressed state?

It pays territory, the ball, and legs. A compressed block has no outlet, so each defensive phase runs longer, defenders make more maximal accelerations and decelerations, corners and throw-ins accumulate against, and the substitutions that would normally freshen midfield are spent on holding the line instead. The cost arrives late and all at once.

That answer is worth sitting with, because it reframes the most common piece of evidence used against this forward. Highlight compilations of quiet matches almost always come from fixtures in which the opponent chose maximum suppression. What the compilation cannot show is the opponent's own attacking output in that match, which is usually close to nothing, or the state of the same block in the closing stages of similar fixtures.

## Limitation one: the congested reception and the back-to-goal question

This is the most technically specific of the limitations, and the one where the evidence is easiest for an ordinary viewer to check.

The situation is narrow and recurring. The ball is played into him in a central area with a defender tight behind him, no immediate runner ahead, and limited support within one pass. The task is to secure the ball, absorb the contact, and either turn or lay it off cleanly under pressure. It is the daily work of a centre-forward, and for a forward whose game is built around receiving in stride, it is the least natural situation on the pitch.

What actually happens mechanically is a body-shape question rather than a strength question. Receiving with your back to goal well requires the defender to be located and felt before the ball arrives, an arm across to establish a barrier, a first touch taken with the far foot away from the pressure, and a decision made before the touch rather than after it. Forwards who specialize in that reception do not out-muscle defenders; they occupy the space the defender wants to occupy, half a second earlier, and then use the defender's momentum against him. It is a learned craft, and the forwards who are best at it have spent whole careers in situations that demanded it.

His career has not primarily demanded it. A forward deployed from the left, in teams built to attack space, receives facing forward far more often than he receives with a defender on his back. That is not an accusation; it is a description of the situations his roles have produced. The observable consequence is that in the congested central reception his first touch is more likely to travel further from his body than a specialist's would, that he is more likely to seek an immediate lay-off than to turn into contact, and that a defender who arrives at the same moment as the ball wins the sequence more often than the reputation of the two players would suggest.

Three qualifications keep the claim honest, and all three are necessary.

The first is that this has improved across his career. A forward who has spent extended periods as a central striker in demanding teams accumulates thousands of these receptions, and the difference between his early and later handling of them is visible to anyone who watches both. Describing this as a fixed deficiency is wrong, and it is the kind of wrongness that dates an article badly.

The second is that the situation is rarer for him than the framing implies, because his teams do not generally play into it. A forward who receives facing forward twenty times and with his back to goal three times is being judged, in this argument, on the smaller number.

The third, and most important tactically, is that the defensive answer to this limitation is not what most sides attempt. The instinct is to press him hard from behind the moment the ball is travelling. That is the wrong answer against most forwards and it is a poor one here, because contact from behind on a player with this acceleration produces either a foul in a dangerous zone or a first-touch flick past the presser into precisely the running situation you were trying to prevent. The correct behavior is to stay close enough to deny the turn while remaining goal-side and balanced, force the lay-off, and then attack the receiver of the lay-off rather than the forward. That is the coachable version of this limitation, and it is a midfield instruction as much as a defensive one.

## Limitation two: the aerial dimension and what its absence changes

Aerial threat is not a primary weapon in his game. Stated plainly and without embellishment, that is the whole of the claim, and it is one of the few limitations in this article that requires no conditions attached to remain true.

What matters is the tactical consequence, which is larger than it sounds and almost always underestimated.

The first consequence is that a defending team gets to make a delivery decision without penalty. Against a forward who is a genuine aerial threat, a full-back who is beaten to the byline faces a real problem whichever way he shows the crosser, because the ball above head height in the six-yard area is dangerous. Against this forward, showing a crosser onto his weaker side and forcing a high, hanging delivery into a crowded area is a clean win, because the delivery arrives in the one currency he does not trade in. That single asymmetry gives a defensive coach a free structural preference, and free preferences are rare.

The second consequence is on set pieces. Attacking corners and deep free kicks do not require a marking scheme rewritten around him. A defending side can assign a standard marker, keep its best aerial defenders on the genuine threats, and treat him as a short-corner and second-ball concern instead. That is a meaningful saving of defensive resources across a match, and it compounds: the resources saved on dead balls are the same resources spent on holding the compressed block described earlier.

The third consequence runs in the opposite direction and is the reason this limitation is smaller than it first appears. A forward who is not an aerial target is generally not asked to be one, so his teams do not build attacking patterns that end in high deliveries to him. The threat is routed around the limitation by design. He attacks the ground ball across the face of goal, the cutback, the low delivery to the near post, and the second phase after a knockdown, and those routes are unaffected. So while the aerial absence is genuinely a hole, it is a hole in a part of the pitch his teams rarely ask him to occupy, which is why its practical cost to his output is modest and its practical benefit to a defending team is mainly one of resource allocation rather than direct chance prevention.

There is an honest addition to make here. A forward with limited aerial threat is also a limited outlet, and an outlet is the thing a compressed side most needs when it is under sustained pressure. A centre-forward who can win a long ball and hold it up gives his defenders forty seconds of rest. That is a real function, and its absence changes what his teams can do when they need to relieve pressure without the ball. This is a team-level cost of the profile rather than an individual criticism, and it belongs in a fair account.

### Which observable moments tell you which version of the threat you are facing?

Watch the first reception in each attacking phase. If he receives in stride facing your goal with grass ahead, you are near the top of the ladder and that phase is already lost. If his first receptions are static, side-on, and inside twenty metres, your block is holding.

## Limitation three: the crowded box and the finishing profile

The finishing conversation about this forward is usually run backwards. He is described as an outstanding finisher, which is defensible, and then the description is used to imply that finishing quality is a constant across situations. It is not, for anybody, and the situational split here is unusually wide.

His most reliable finishing situations share a structure. He arrives at the ball moving forward, with at least a stride of separation from the nearest defender, in a position where the goalkeeper has to commit to an angle rather than simply set. From that structure he finishes with either foot at a level almost nobody matches, and the goalkeeper's realistic options narrow to reducing the angle early or accepting a low percentage. Those situations are produced by the coupling described at the start of this article: they are downstream of runway.

His least reliable finishing situations are the ones that occur inside a crowd, from a standing start, with the ball arriving at an awkward height and a defender within contact distance. Those are the situations produced by the lower rows of the condition ladder. They are also, incidentally, the situations that dominate a compressed match, which is why a compressed match reduces both his chance volume and his conversion within the chances that remain. Both effects run in the same direction, and describing only the first understates the compression effect while describing it as a finishing flaw overstates it. It is a situation effect.

The observable evidence is available to any viewer willing to watch a full match rather than a compilation. Count the receptions inside the penalty area that arrive with a defender in contact and no forward momentum available. Then count what happens next. The pattern is not a player failing at something; it is a player operating outside the structure that generates his advantage, which is exactly what the defending team paid a great deal of territory to achieve.

There is a related and more specific point about angles. A forward cutting inside from the left onto his right foot generates a shooting geometry that is easy to describe and easy to defend if it is anticipated: the ball travels across the goalkeeper toward the far corner, and the two counters are to force the shot from a wider starting angle or to arrive with a second defender on the shooting side rather than the touchline side. Neither is a comment on his quality. Both are a comment on the fact that a repeated geometry can be prepared for, and prepared geometry is worth more than most defensive attributes. The full sequencing of that preparation belongs to the counter blueprint on [how a team is organized to shorten his runway and angle his receptions](/2020/02/15/how-to-stop-kylian-mbappe/).

## Limitation four: the out-of-possession question, treated as structure

This is the loudest topic attached to him and the one most frequently handled badly, so the ground rules come first and they are not negotiable in this series.

What follows is a discussion of role, structure, and team sustainability. It is not a discussion of effort, attitude, commitment, or professionalism, and no observation in it should be read as one. A forward's out-of-possession output is determined overwhelmingly by his instructions, by the shape around him, by what his team needs him to conserve for its attacking phases, and by the pressing scheme his coach has installed. Those are the variables under discussion. Nothing else is.

Begin with what a pressing scheme actually requires from a front player. A coordinated press is not running. It is a set of angles held in sequence: the first presser approaches from a curved path that eliminates one pass, the second presser holds the next lane rather than following the ball, and the players behind them step up at a fixed distance so the space the press creates behind it is not usable. If any one of those elements is missing, the press does not merely fail; it inverts, and the pressing side finds itself stretched with its full-backs advanced and its centre-backs isolated. That inversion is the worst state in football to occupy against fast forwards, which is why coaches are careful about who they ask to press and when.

Now the structural fact about this particular profile. A forward whose value is created through acceleration in open space is, in an accounting sense, an expensive presser. Every high-intensity effort spent closing a centre-back is an effort not available for the transition twenty seconds later, and the transition is where his output is produced. Coaches have historically resolved this trade in different ways, and the resolution is a coaching decision rather than a player decision. Some teams have asked him to trigger the first press and have accepted a slightly slower second wave behind him. Some have asked him to hold a central rest position and screen the pass into the opposition's pivot while others press around him. Some have asked him to conserve almost entirely and have built the defensive structure to function as a unit of nine plus a goalkeeper, on the reasoning that a compact nine is more effective than a stretched ten.

All three are legitimate designs with documented histories in the sport, and none of them tells you anything about the individual. What they do tell you is what an opponent should expect, which is the part a coach can use.

The genuine tactical consequences are these, and they are worth more than the argument they are usually buried under.

When a side plays with a front player who conserves, the players immediately behind him carry a larger workload. The pivot and the ball-side wide midfielder cover more lateral ground, the far-side full-back tucks further, and the team's ability to press in the opposition's half depends on winning the first ball rather than on sustained pressure. This produces a recognizable pattern: bursts of high pressure at specific triggers, usually a backward pass or a poor first touch, and a controlled drop into a mid-block otherwise. An opponent who recognizes that pattern gets a large advantage, because the trigger-based press is beatable by a goalkeeper and two centre-backs who are calm on the ball and by a pivot willing to receive on the half-turn.

The second consequence concerns the opposite full-back. Where the forward starts wide on the left and conserves, the space in front of the opposition's right-back is the least contested area of the pitch. That is a structural gift to a team that is prepared to use it, and using it is the single most reliable method of attacking sides built around this profile. The pattern is not subtle and it does not need to be: circulate to the far side, invite the wide midfielder to step, and play behind him into the channel the conserving forward would otherwise be covering.

The third consequence is about rest defense rather than pressing. A team that keeps a forward high and wide as a permanent counter-attacking outlet is accepting a numerical shortfall in its own rest-defense structure whenever it loses the ball in the opposition's half. This is a deliberate exchange: the team accepts a defensive vulnerability in return for an immediate outlet that turns turnovers into shots within seconds. Whether the exchange is good depends entirely on the quality of the outlet, and with an outlet of this quality the exchange has frequently been correct.

None of that is a weakness of the player. It is the cost side of a team-level decision, and a fair account of his limitations has to distinguish the two. What can honestly be said as an individual observation is narrower and it is this: the specific technical craft of pressing, meaning the curved approach run, the cover shadow held for four seconds, the second press immediately after the first, is not the most developed part of his skill set relative to forwards who have spent careers in systems that demanded it daily. That is a skills observation of the same kind as the aerial one, it is stated without moral content, and it is the only version of this argument that is defensible.

### How should a coach account for a conserving front player when planning against this profile?

Plan against the shape rather than the individual. Identify which zone the front player does not cover, which is usually in front of one full-back, and build your circulation to arrive there with a runner. Expect trigger-based pressure rather than sustained pressure, and prepare a calm first pass out of it.

## Limitation five: the central version and the wide version are different problems

The question of whether he is more effective from the left or through the middle is asked constantly and answered lazily. The honest answer is that the two deployments produce different threat profiles with different failure conditions, and that the correct comparison depends on what the opposition does.

From the left, the mechanism is isolation. He starts wide with the touchline behind him, receives facing the pitch, and attacks a full-back who has to defend both the outside and the inside cut without immediate help. The corridor he attacks is diagonal, running from the wide area toward the penalty spot, and it is the most productive corridor in his game because it combines running distance with a finishing angle he prefers. The failure condition for this version is a doubled channel: a full-back who shows him inside into a screening midfielder, or a wide midfielder who tucks to take the inside lane before it opens.

Through the middle, the mechanism is depth. He attacks the space behind the last line, runs the shoulder of a centre-back, and receives balls played into the channel rather than into feet. The corridor is vertical, the distances are shorter, and the finishing positions are more central. The failure condition for this version is different and it is simpler: a deep last line removes the space behind, and unlike the wide version there is no touchline duel to fall back on. A central forward denied depth has to receive in a crowd, which is the reception described in the first limitation.

That is the entire answer, and it explains why the same player looks like two different problems in consecutive matches. Against a high line, the central deployment is the more dangerous of the two, because it converts the smallest error of line height into the highest-value chance available in football. Against a deep block, the wide deployment is the more dangerous, because the wide isolation still exists when the depth does not, and because a wide start creates the cutback geometry that a deep block is specifically vulnerable to.

A defending side that gets this wrong pays for it twice. Teams that prepare a deep block and then find him deployed wide have removed the threat they were most afraid of and enabled the one they were least prepared for. Teams that prepare a doubled wide channel and then find him starting centrally have committed two players to an area he is not occupying and have left a centre-back defending depth without cover. The planning implication is that the block has to be able to move between states in-match rather than commit to one before kick-off, and that the trigger for moving is his starting position rather than the scoreline. The mechanics of that in-match shift belong to the counter blueprint; the diagnosis belongs here.

## The weaker-foot question, answered properly

The claim that his left foot is a weakness circulates widely and it is one of the least accurate things said about him.

He finishes with both feet. The right is dominant, particularly when cutting inside from the left, and the pattern of his most repeated finishing situations makes the right foot more visible. But visibility is not capability, and the practical test of a weaker foot is not how often it is used; it is whether an opponent can profitably show a player onto it.

That test is the one that matters and it is worth explaining, because it applies to every forward a reader will ever plan against. A genuinely one-footed attacker gives the defending side a free structural decision: force him onto the weak side and the duel becomes winnable, because the finish that follows is low probability and the cross that follows is worse. Against a forward who can strike a ball cleanly with either foot, that free decision does not exist. The defender still has to choose a side, but he is now choosing between two live outcomes rather than between a live one and a dead one, which is a categorically harder job.

By that test the left foot is not a weakness. Showing him onto his left does not neutralize the shot, does not remove the ability to strike a cutback first time, and does not make the wide duel comfortable. It does change the geometry, and the change is usable: from the left channel, forcing the ball onto the left foot pushes the shot toward the near post and a tighter angle, and it removes the inside cut that produces the far-corner finish. That is a marginal gain and a real one, and marginal gains are what defensive plans are made of. It is not a weakness, and describing it as one causes coaches to plan for a neutralization that will not arrive.

The honest version of the claim, and the one worth teaching, is that the two feet are not equal in the pattern of use, that the right is where the highest-value repeated finish lives, and that showing him left is a small edge to be combined with other constraints rather than a solution on its own.

## The first-touch-under-pressure question

A related claim holds that analysts identify a flaw in his first touch under pressure. This one needs to be separated into two different observations, because the loose version is false and the precise version is useful.

The false version is that his touch is unreliable. Any viewer of a full match can falsify it: taking a ball at speed, in stride, across the body, and retaining control against a recovering defender is one of the harder technical actions in football, and he performs it repeatedly.

The precise version concerns a specific reception type rather than touch quality in general. When a ball arrives at an awkward height, from behind or across him, with a defender in immediate contact and no forward direction available, his first touch travels further from his body than a specialist's would. That is the same observation made in the congested-reception section, and it is a consequence of the same thing: the touch is optimized for continuing a movement rather than for killing a ball dead in traffic. The two skills are genuinely different, and forwards do not usually possess both at the same level, because the environments that develop them are different environments.

The coaching consequence for a defending side is precise. The way to attack this is not to press harder but to arrange the pass into him so that the awkward reception is the only reception available. That means denying the vertical ball into feet with a screening midfielder so the ball has to arrive from a wide or backward angle, and it means the marker staying goal-side and tight rather than jumping. When the reception is forced into that shape, the second defender is attacking the loose touch rather than the player, and a loose touch is a far better target than a duel.

The coaching consequence for an attacking player reading this is different and more useful. If your game is built on receiving in motion, the congested static reception is the part of the craft you will not develop by accident, because your role will not produce it. It has to be trained deliberately, which is a point returned to in the development section below.

## What has genuinely changed: link-up play across the career

An article about limitations that describes them as permanent is an article that will be wrong within a few years. Several of the items discussed above have moved, and describing the direction of movement is part of an honest account.

The clearest movement is in link-up play. A forward who begins his career as a wide runner in a counter-attacking structure and later operates as a central forward in possession-dominant teams accumulates an enormous volume of a different kind of repetition: receiving between the lines, laying the ball off and spinning, combining in triangles at the edge of the area, and playing the pass that releases a third runner rather than taking the ball himself. Those are learnable actions and the learning is visible.

What has moved most is the decision-making before the touch rather than the touch itself. The recognizable early pattern of receiving and immediately looking to attack a defender individually has become a wider set of options, including the early lay-off, the delayed run made after releasing the ball, and the deliberate occupation of a centre-back to open a lane for somebody else. That last one is the most underrated development, and it is the reason the disappearance claim is partly a visibility problem rather than an output problem, which is the subject of the next section.

## The compensations: how the limitations get hidden

Every elite player's limitations are covered by something, and the covering mechanisms are as informative as the limitations themselves, because a defending side that removes the compensation exposes the limitation underneath it. Five compensations recur, and each has a corresponding removal.

The first is starting position. A forward who receives on the touchline, facing the pitch, has arranged the reception so that the congested static reception rarely occurs. The removal is a wide midfielder or full-back who takes away the facing-forward reception by arriving on the touchline side early, forcing the ball to be played to his feet with the defender behind rather than into the space in front.

The second is timing of movement rather than speed of movement. Depth runs are started before the passer looks up, which manufactures separation that has nothing to do with acceleration and everything to do with anticipation. The removal is a last line that steps as a unit on the passer's body shape rather than on the runner, which is the least glamorous and most effective piece of defending in the sport.

The third is teammate design. A team built around this profile creates the conditions for it: a full-back who overlaps to occupy the defender who would otherwise double, a midfielder who plays the vertical ball early rather than after two extra touches, and a second forward who occupies a centre-back to prevent the cover slide. The removal is not to defend him at all; it is to defend the supply, which is why the counter blueprint spends more time on the passers than on the runner.

The fourth is the penalty area itself. A forward who is not a target in the air is still a beneficiary of chaos in the area, and arriving late at a cutback is a threat that requires no runway and no aerial ability. The removal is a specific and unglamorous discipline: the far-side defender who does not ball-watch and the deepest midfielder who occupies the top of the area rather than dropping into the crowd.

The fifth is the decoy function, and it is the compensation the public conversation misses entirely. A forward who has to be respected as a depth threat drags a last line backward even when he never receives the ball. Every metre that line retreats is a metre of space created for the midfielders in front of him. The removal is genuinely difficult: a defensive line has to be willing to hold a height it is uncomfortable holding, which requires either exceptional recovery pace or an offside discipline the defending side has rehearsed until it is automatic. Most teams cannot do it, which is why the decoy function keeps working.

The sixth, which belongs with the fifth because it works the same way, is the manufactured foul. A forward who accelerates past a defender's shoulder inside twenty-five metres of goal converts a defensive error into a dead ball in a dangerous zone rather than into nothing, and that conversion happens whether or not the run produces a chance. This is a compensation because it means a defensive plan that concedes only fouls has not held; it has simply moved the concession into a category coaches undercount. The removal is a first defender who does not commit his weight, and a cover defender close enough that the first defender does not need to.

Those six compensations should be attacked in a specific order, and the order is the opposite of the one most sides choose. Teams usually begin with the individual, assigning their best defender and hoping the duel holds. The compensations that actually decide matches are the structural ones: the supply, the line height, and the timing of the vertical pass. A plan that removes the third compensation, meaning the passer who releases the ball early, has done more damage to the threat than a plan that removes the first, and it has done it without exposing a defender to a duel he was always likely to lose. The general principle, which applies well beyond this subject, is that the closer a defensive intervention sits to the ball's origin rather than its destination, the cheaper and more reliable it is.

That fifth point is the bridge into the disappearance argument, so it is worth pausing on. The reason a forward can be described as absent in a match in which he substantially shaped the opposition's defensive shape is that the shaping is invisible on a highlight reel. Nothing he did in ninety minutes will appear in a compilation. Everything he did will appear in the positioning of the opposition's back line, which is not something compilations record.

## Limitation six, or is it: the disappearance claim against a deep block

This is the second of the two dominant online positions and it needs a verdict rather than a diplomatic non-answer.

The claim has a true component. Against a fully compressed block his most dangerous actions are substantially removed. This article has spent several sections explaining precisely why: the runway is gone, the reception shape is unfavorable, the finishing situations that remain are the ones that suit him least, and the aerial route around the problem is not available to him. That is not a small effect and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. A reader who wants the direct comparison with the other most-discussed modern forward, whose response to compression is different in kind rather than in degree, will find it resolved in the head-to-head on [which of the two sets the harder defensive question](/2020/05/15/haaland-vs-mbappe-harder-to-defend/).

The claim also has a false component, and it has two parts.

The first part is the visibility bias just described. In the compressed state, his contribution shifts toward functions that do not generate highlights: occupying a defender so a teammate receives, running a channel that is never used but which prevents a centre-back from stepping, and pinning a full-back who would otherwise join an overload elsewhere. A viewer watching for events will record an absence. A viewer watching the opposition's back line will record a constant constraint. Both are watching the same match.

The second part is the accounting failure the compression cost principle exists to correct. A match in which he is contained by a fully compressed block is, in almost every case, a match in which the opposition abandoned its own attacking ambitions to achieve the containment. Reading that scoreboard as evidence about a forward, rather than as evidence about a trade, is a category error. The relevant question is what the opponent's expected output was in the same match, and it is usually close to nothing.

The verdict, stated plainly. The reduction is real, larger than his supporters concede, and it is the single most useful fact a coach can know about him. The word "disappears" is wrong, because it describes an absence of contribution when what is actually present is a change in the type of contribution. And the framing as a criticism of the player is the weakest part of the claim, because the reduction is a property of the match state rather than a property of the player, and the same match state reduces every forward whose game is built on running distance, which historically has been most of the fastest forwards the sport has produced.

That last point leads directly into the counter-position, which deserves the same scrutiny as the criticisms.

## The counter-position: is space dependence even a criticism?

The defensive argument goes like this. Every fast forward in the history of football has been more dangerous with space than without it, therefore space dependence is a property of the archetype rather than a flaw in the individual, therefore it should not appear in a list of weaknesses at all.

This argument is about seventy percent correct and the remaining thirty percent is the interesting part.

It is correct that the coupling is universal to the archetype and that describing it as a personal failing is wrong. It is correct that the same critique applied to the fastest forwards of any era would produce the same list. And it is correct that the archetype has produced an enormous share of the sport's most decisive players, which suggests the trade-off has generally been worth making.

Where it fails is in the leap from "universal to the archetype" to "not a weakness." A weakness in the sense this series uses the word is a condition under which output falls relative to elite peers, and the relevant peer group is not other fast forwards; it is other forwards of comparable overall standing. Some forwards of comparable standing are far less conditional. A complete centre-forward who can receive with his back to goal in traffic, win an aerial duel, finish from a standing start, and also run behind, has a flatter output curve across match states. That flatness is worth something real, and its absence is worth something real. The general, subject-free version of this argument, meaning which limitations recur across all great players and which are properties of archetypes rather than individuals, is the canonical territory of the synthesis article on [the universal weaknesses of great players](/2032/03/01/universal-weaknesses-great-players/), and this article defers to it there rather than restating it.

The verdict on the counter-position: space dependence belongs in an account of his limitations, because it is the condition that governs every other item in the account, but it belongs there as a coupling with a price attached rather than as an accusation. Calling it a flaw is sloppy. Leaving it out is worse, because it is the single most decision-relevant fact about planning against him.

## The claimed weaknesses that do not survive contact with the mechanism

Four claims circulate widely and do not hold up. Each is worth taking apart, because the errors that produce them are errors a reader will make again on the next subject if they are not named.

The first is that he is a flat-track forward, meaning a player whose output is generated against weak opposition and who is contained by good sides. The mechanism prediction is the opposite. His output is coupled to space, and space is a function of match state rather than opponent quality. Ambitious, well-coached sides that press high and defend a high line produce more space behind the last line than limited sides that defend deep, so the archetype has historically produced its most decisive performances in the matches this claim says it disappears from. The observation underneath the claim is usually a specific match against a specific compressed opponent, generalized into a rule that the mechanism does not support.

The second is that he is only dangerous in transition and therefore keeping the ball is a sufficient plan. Transition is the state in which he is most dangerous, which is true and unhelpful, because the settled attack against a high line produces the same runway by a different route, and because the wide isolation duel does not require a transition at all. A team that keeps the ball but defends a high line when it loses it has not implemented a plan; it has implemented a hope. Possession is a useful component of a plan against him and it is not a plan by itself, which is the reason the counter blueprint treats rest defense as a separate problem from ball retention.

The third is that a deep block neutralizes him. A deep block reduces him, at a price, and it also creates the exact wide reception and cutback geometry his teams are built to exploit. A block that drops to remove the depth threat has moved its defensive line into the zone where the cutback from the byline is most dangerous, and has usually done so with its full-backs isolated against wide attackers with the whole width of the pitch to work in. Sides have been beaten by this precise sequence often enough that it should not need restating: the depth was removed, the cutback was not, and the cutback is a route that requires no runway.

The fourth is that his aerial limitation is a significant defensive opportunity. It is a resource-allocation advantage, as described earlier, and it is not a route to stopping him, because his teams do not attack through the air. Planning a defensive scheme around the thing an opponent does not do is one of the more common ways to lose a match while feeling well prepared.

There is a fifth claim that sits in a different category because it is not a football claim at all: the assertion that he is overrated relative to his reputation. That is a claim about public opinion rather than about football, it cannot be settled by mechanism, and this series has no interest in adjudicating reputations. What can be said is narrow and it is enough. The archetype he represents is genuinely conditional; the conditions that reduce him are expensive; and a coach who prepares for the archetype rather than for the name will be better prepared than one who has decided in advance whether the reputation is deserved.

## What each limitation costs to exploit

This is the section that turns the analysis into a decision, and it is the part almost no article on this subject includes. Each limitation is listed with the specific price a defending side pays to attack it, because a weakness that costs more to exploit than it yields is not an opportunity.

Exploiting the runway coupling requires holding a compressed vertical structure. The price is territory, possession, and an escalating physical load on the back six, plus the loss of your own counter-attacking distance. This is the most effective single measure available and the most expensive one, and its cost is concentrated in the final third of a match, which is why the plan must include what happens when the state breaks rather than assuming it will not.

Exploiting the congested reception requires a screening midfielder who lives permanently in the vertical lane between the ball and the forward. The price is a midfielder removed from your own build-up and from your counter-attacking structure, effectively playing as a defender with the ball as well as without it. Against sides with a genuine second creator, this price is often unaffordable, because the screen cannot cover two threats.

Exploiting the aerial absence requires committing to a delivery preference: showing crossers onto their weaker side and accepting the high ball into your area. The price here is small, which is why this is the one limitation almost every side should attack. It is also the one that yields least on its own.

Exploiting the orientation vulnerability requires a first defender who consistently takes the touchline side and shows inside, which is uncomfortable defending and requires an inside screen to be genuinely present rather than nominally present. The price is a full-back who cannot press the ball aggressively and must instead delay, which cedes the initiative in the wide area and invites the opposition's full-back to overlap unopposed.

Exploiting the wide isolation requires a second defender committed to the channel. The price is a permanent numerical disadvantage on the opposite side of the pitch, and it is the price sides most consistently underestimate, because it is paid in a place nobody is watching. A doubled left channel means an unassisted right-back on the far side, and competent opponents will find that within twenty minutes.

Exploiting the structural pressing question requires attacking the zone the conserving front player does not cover. The price is close to zero, which makes this the highest-value item in the list for a coach preparing an attacking plan rather than a defensive one, and it is the reason this article treats the topic as a structural opportunity rather than a moral complaint.

Read together, the list produces a conclusion that runs against instinct. The two cheapest exploitations, the delivery preference and the attack on the uncovered zone, are the ones that yield least against him directly and most in the rest of the match. The two most expensive, compression and the doubled channel, are the ones that reduce him most and are hardest to sustain. A coach with a strong squad can afford the expensive items. A coach with an ordinary squad should buy the cheap ones and accept that he will be contained rather than removed.

## The debate register, with verdicts

Seven positions dominate the argument about this player. Each gets a mechanism and a verdict, because a series that refuses to commit is not worth reading.

He does not track back. Verdict: the underlying observation is about a role instruction and a structural trade, and the phrasing is a category error. What is defensible is that the technical craft of coordinated pressing is not the most developed part of his skill set, and that his teams have generally been built to conserve his output for attacking phases rather than to spend it on the first press. What is not defensible is any inference about the person. The tactical takeaway, which is worth more than the argument, is that the zone in front of one opposition full-back is systematically under-covered, and that is where an attacking plan should go.

He is a flat-track forward. Verdict: false, and the mechanism predicts the opposite. Ambitious opponents produce the space his game converts. The strongest performances of this archetype have historically come against sides that defended high, not against sides that defended deep.

He needs space and disappears without it. Verdict: half true. The reduction is real and substantial, and it is the most useful planning fact about him. The word "disappears" is wrong, because the contribution changes form rather than ceasing, and because the state that produces the reduction costs the opposition its own attacking match.

He is worse through the middle than from the left. Verdict: depends on the opposition, and this is a genuine dependency rather than a hedge. Against a high line the central deployment is more dangerous, because it converts a small line-height error into the highest-value chance in the sport. Against a compressed block the wide deployment is more dangerous, because the isolation duel survives compression and the depth run does not.

He is overrated relative to his reputation. Verdict: not a football question. The football question underneath it is whether his output is conditional, and the answer is yes, more conditional than a complete centre-forward's and less conditional than the claim implies, since the conditions that suppress him are ones few sides can hold for ninety minutes.

His aerial game is a hole. Verdict: true as stated and smaller than it sounds. It is a genuine absence, it saves a defending side real resources on dead balls and deliveries, and it does not open a route to stopping him, because his teams do not attack through the air.

Space dependence is true of every fast forward and is therefore not a criticism. Verdict: mostly right, and the exception matters. It is a property of the archetype and not a personal failing, and it still belongs in an account of limitations, because the relevant comparison is with forwards of similar standing whose output curve is flatter across match states.

## How to test a role-based criticism without sliding into character judgment

This is a method section, and it is the part of the article most transferable to other subjects, because the error it corrects is the most common error in football discussion.

The method has four steps and it is not complicated.

Step one is to state the observation as a countable event rather than as a disposition. "He does not press" is a disposition. "In this phase, the far-side forward held a central position while the ball-side midfielder pressed the full-back" is an event. Dispositions cannot be checked. Events can.

Step two is to check whether the event varies with the coach and the system rather than with the player. If the same player produces different out-of-possession outputs under different coaches, in different structures, and in different competitions, the variable is not the player. This step alone resolves the majority of role-based arguments, and it is almost never taken, because it requires watching matches from periods people are not currently arguing about.

Step three is to ask what the alternative would cost the team. A forward asked to press for ninety minutes is a forward with less available for the transitions his team is built around. If the trade is negative for the team, then the behavior under discussion is a coaching decision that is probably correct, and criticizing the player for executing his instructions is analysis pointed at the wrong person.

Step four is to convert the finding into a plan. If the structural gap is real, the output of the analysis is not a verdict on the individual; it is a zone on a pitch map and an instruction for your own attacking phase. Analysis that ends in a judgment has produced nothing. Analysis that ends in an instruction has produced everything.

That sequence is the difference between an argument and a preparation, and it applies identically to every named subject in this series. The full analyst workflow, meaning how many matches to watch, in what order, and how the observation becomes a meeting with players, belongs to the scouting articles rather than to this one.

### How many matches does it take to identify which version of this forward a team is facing?

Three, chosen deliberately rather than recently: one against a high line, one against a compressed block, and one in which his side chased the match. The first shows the depth threat, the second shows what he does when the runway is gone, and the third shows the deployment his coach reverts to under pressure.

## What a coach actually does with all of this

Convert the ladder into a decision before the match rather than during it.

Decide which row your side can hold for ninety minutes. This is a squad question rather than a tactical preference: it depends on the recovery pace of your centre-backs, the discipline of your wide midfielders, the concentration of your goalkeeper in a deep block, and the fitness level of a group that will make a very large number of decelerations. Most sides overestimate this and choose a row lower than they can sustain, which produces the pattern of a comfortable hour followed by a collapse.

Decide what you are buying with the price. If you choose compression, you have chosen to give up your own attacking match, and you should build a set-piece plan and a single long outlet pattern, because those will be your only routes to a goal. If you choose a mid-block, you have chosen to concede two or three genuinely dangerous moments, and you should decide in advance which two players are responsible for the emergency cover in those moments rather than leaving it to instinct.

Decide the trigger for changing rows. The most useful triggers are his starting position after a restart, the introduction of a second fast attacker, and the scoreline. A block that changes rows on a fixed clock rather than on a trigger will change at the wrong moment.

Then rehearse the state, because a defensive state that has not been rehearsed is not a state, it is a hope. The session design for that rehearsal, including the constraints that produce a compressed block under fatigue and the small-squad version for teams without a full complement, is the territory of the training article on [rehearsing a plan to contain an elite individual](/2020/10/15/training-to-contain-a-superstar/), and coaches building the week around it can save the dossier, the clips, and the session plan together with the [free opposition planning tools on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/football-match-planner.html).

## The observable evidence: how to check every claim in this article yourself

Nothing above should be taken on trust, and none of it requires a data subscription to verify. Each limitation has a viewing procedure attached, and the procedures are the most valuable part of this article for a student, an aspiring analyst, or a supporter who wants to stop arguing from compilations.

For the runway coupling, watch a full match with a notepad and record only one thing: the distance between his reception point and the deepest opposition defender at the moment the ball leaves the passer's foot. Estimate it in penalty-area widths rather than metres, since precision is unnecessary and false precision is worse than none. Then mark which receptions produced a shot, a shot for a teammate, or a foul in a dangerous area. The relationship will emerge within one match and it will be steeper than expected. This is also the single best exercise for training a coach's eye for line height, because it forces attention onto the defensive line rather than onto the ball.

For the congested reception, watch only the receptions in which a defender is within one metre and behind him. There will not be many, which is itself part of the finding, and that scarcity should be recorded rather than ignored. For each one, note the touch distance, whether he turned, whether he laid off, and whether possession survived three seconds. Compare the same count for a specialist back-to-goal centre-forward in another match. The difference will be visible and it will be smaller than the loudest version of the criticism suggests and larger than his defenders concede.

For the aerial claim, count the deliveries above shoulder height into the penalty area while he is inside it, and note how many he contests as the primary attacker rather than as a second-ball threat. This is the fastest of the checks and the least ambiguous.

For the structural pressing question, do not watch him at all. Watch the opposition's build-up and note which of their defenders is under pressure and which is not, then note which zone of the pitch his team consistently leaves uncontested. Recording the zone rather than the player is the entire methodological point, and a reader who does this once will never make the disposition error again.

For the deployment question, watch two matches from different phases of his career, one wide and one central, and record where his first touch in each attacking phase occurred. The map that emerges is the difference between the two problems, drawn by the player rather than argued by a writer.

For the decoy function, ignore him completely for a ten-minute passage and watch the opposition's deepest defender instead. Note how often that defender's starting position is set by the threat of a run rather than by the location of the ball. This is the hardest of the checks to perform, because it requires watching a player who is not near the ball, and it is the one that most changes how a viewer understands the game generally.

For the finishing situation split, separate his shots into two piles: those taken while moving forward with a stride of separation, and those taken from a standing start in traffic. Do not compare conversion rates across a small sample, because a small sample will mislead. Compare the situations themselves and the goalkeeper's set position in each. The mechanism will be obvious without any arithmetic.

That set of seven procedures is the practical core of this article, and a reader who runs three of them will know more about this subject than they would from a hundred arguments about it. The wider version of this method, meaning how a full opposition dossier is built from a small number of matches and turned into a plan a squad can execute, sits with the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/) at the category level.

### What is the most common analytical mistake made about this player?

Using highlight absence as evidence. A compilation records events, and the compressed match state converts his contribution into non-events: a defender occupied, a line held deep, a full-back pinned. Absence from a highlight package is evidence about the editing, not about the match.

## Where the limitations do not apply

An account of weaknesses that never states the boundary of its own argument is propaganda in the other direction, so the boundary is worth drawing explicitly.

None of the limitations discussed above applies in transition. In an open field, with a turnover and a broken opposition structure, there is no known individual defensive answer, no compensating limitation that becomes relevant, and no reason to expect anything other than a high-value chance. Every plan against him is a plan to avoid this state rather than to survive it, and a team that reaches this state has already lost the phase regardless of what its defenders do next.

None of them applies to a settled attack against a high line either. The depth run behind a line defending above the halfway line is not a duel; it is a race with a head start decided by the timing of the run rather than by top speed, and the timing is a strength rather than a limitation. A side that defends a high line and does not have a rehearsed offside mechanism has chosen the one state in which none of this article's findings will help it.

None of them applies to the wide isolation duel. Given a facing-forward reception on the touchline with a defender alone in front of him and space behind that defender, the duel is not close to even, and no limitation in this article changes the odds. Only the second defender changes them, which is why every serious plan is a plan about the second defender.

And none of them applies to the finishing action itself, considered as a skill. Striking a ball with either foot, at speed, into the corner the goalkeeper cannot reach, is not on the list of things this article has questioned, and any reader who leaves with the impression that it is has read the article backwards. The limitations are about which situations occur, not about what he does with the situations that suit him.

Those four boundaries are what makes the rest of the argument credible. A weakness anatomy that finds a limitation in every part of a great player's game has stopped analysing and started campaigning.

## Why the limitations are not additive

A list invites a reader to imagine that limitations stack, so that a plan removing three of them is three times as effective as a plan removing one. They do not stack, and understanding why is what separates a plan that works from a plan that reads well on a whiteboard.

The limitations are nested rather than parallel. The runway coupling is the parent condition, and the congested reception, the standing-start finish, and the reduced value of the isolation duel are its children. You do not attack them separately; you attack the parent and the children follow. A team that spends a screening midfielder on the congested reception without also managing line height has removed a limitation that was not going to occur anyway, because a forward receiving in a corridor never has to solve the congested reception in the first place. That is the most common wasted resource in plans against this profile: a defensive job created to solve a problem the plan has already allowed the attacker to bypass.

The aerial limitation sits outside the nest, which is why it behaves differently from the others. It is available in every match state, it costs almost nothing to exploit, and it yields a small constant advantage rather than a large conditional one. Nothing you do about the runway makes the aerial advantage larger or smaller. This is the only item on the list a coach should treat as free, and free items should always be taken.

The structural out-of-possession question sits outside the nest in the other direction, because it is not a defensive opportunity at all. It is an attacking one. It affects what your team can do with the ball rather than what it must do without it, and folding it into a defensive plan is a category error that produces confusion in a team meeting. It belongs in the section of your plan about your own build-up, and it should be delivered to your full-backs and midfielders rather than to your defenders.

The consequence for planning is worth stating in one sentence, because it is the practical version of the whole article. Attack the parent condition with everything you have, take the free aerial advantage because it costs nothing, put the structural opportunity in the attacking half of your plan where it belongs, and do not spend a single defensive resource on a child limitation until the parent has been dealt with.

There is a second interaction worth naming, and it runs the wrong way for the defending side. Compression suppresses the parent condition and simultaneously increases the value of the one route that survives compression, which is the wide isolation duel followed by a cutback. That is why plans against him so often fail in a specific and repeated way: the block drops, the depth threat vanishes, everyone relaxes, and the goal arrives from a byline cutback to a runner arriving at the penalty spot. The suppression worked exactly as designed and the concession came through the door the suppression opened. Any plan that does not include a specific answer for the cutback has not finished being written, and the answer is not a general instruction to defend crosses; it is a named player whose job is the top of the six-yard box and a second named player whose job is the penalty spot, decided before kick-off.

## The amateur and youth-level translation

Almost everything above was written with a full squad and a rehearsed structure in mind, so it is worth translating for the reader with fourteen available players, two training sessions a week, and a pitch that has not been cut since the last home fixture. The archetype is not confined to the elite game. Every league at every level contains one player who is significantly faster than everybody else, and the planning problem is identical in shape even though the execution is not.

The first translation is that compression is harder at amateur level than at elite level, not easier. A compressed block requires simultaneous decisions from eight players, rehearsed until they are automatic, and it collapses the moment one player steps when the others hold. A group that trains twice a week will not hold a genuinely compressed structure for ninety minutes, and a coach who plans for one is planning for a team he does not have. The realistic target is the mid-block row of the ladder, held honestly, with a specific answer for the moments it breaks.

The second is that the individual duel should never be the plan. The instinct is to assign the quickest defender available and consider the problem solved. Against a genuinely fast attacker with a facing-forward reception, an isolated defender loses often enough that the plan has no floor, and it is worse at amateur level because the recovery pace gap is usually larger. The plan has to be a second-defender plan, and the second defender at this level is almost always a midfielder who has been told, in plain language, that his job for the match is the inside lane behind his full-back and that he does not leave it.

The third is that the free items matter more, not less, when resources are short. Showing crossers onto the side that produces a hanging delivery, keeping a standard marking scheme on dead balls rather than redesigning it, and putting a body at the penalty spot on every cutback are all achievable by ordinary players who have been told once and reminded at half time. They will not stop a fast attacker. They will remove the cheap goals, and at amateur level the cheap goals are the majority.

The fourth is the honest one. Against a player who is substantially faster than anybody on your team, you are going to concede chances, and the plan's purpose is to decide which chances rather than to prevent all of them. A coach who tells his players the truth about that gets a calmer defense than one who promises a shutout, because a defense that believes the plan has failed after the first dangerous moment starts improvising, and improvisation against this archetype is where the second and third goals come from.

There is a fifth translation that applies to youth football specifically, and it is a selection point rather than a tactical one. The temptation when facing a much quicker opponent is to move your own fastest player into the defensive duel, which usually means taking your best attacker out of the position where he wins you matches. That trade is almost always bad, because the quick attacker on your side is your only route to a goal and the duel he has been moved into is one he will lose anyway without help. The better answer is to keep him where he is, accept a structural solution behind him, and give the covering job to a player whose absence from your attacking phase costs you nothing.

## What a fast young player should take from this

The most useful audience for a weakness anatomy is not the coach preparing against the subject; it is the player who shares the archetype and is currently building the same ceiling into his own game without noticing.

The mechanism explains why the ceiling forms. A quick young player is selected, at every level, for the thing that wins matches immediately. He is played wide, given the ball facing forward, and asked to run. Because that works, he receives more of the same. Over several years he accumulates enormous repetition in one reception type and almost none in the others, and the gap is not a matter of talent or attitude; it is a matter of what his environment produced. The forwards who arrive in senior football able to receive with a defender on their back are usually the ones who were not fast enough to be given the easy version, and who therefore had to learn the difficult version to survive.

That produces a clear and slightly counterintuitive development instruction. If your advantage is speed, the part of your game you must deliberately train is the part your speed lets you avoid. Specifically, three things.

The first is receiving with your back to goal under contact. Not as a drill performed occasionally but as a weekly habit: locating the defender before the ball arrives, establishing an arm barrier, taking the first touch with the far foot, and deciding before the touch rather than after it. Every hour spent on this is an hour that raises the lower half of your output curve, which is the half that decides matches against organized opposition.

The second is finishing from a standing start in traffic. The finish that arrives at the end of a run is the one you already own. The finish that arrives when a ball breaks to you at an awkward height, with a defender in contact and no space to set your feet, is the one that separates forwards at senior level, and it is trained by deliberately practicing in the crowded version rather than the clean version.

The third is the aerial contest, and the honest note here is that this one has a genuine ceiling set by height and jumping ability. What is trainable regardless is the timing of the run to attack a delivery, the ability to win the space rather than the ball, and the second-ball threat that follows a knockdown. A forward who cannot dominate in the air can still be the most dangerous player in the box in the second after a header, and that is coachable at any level.

For a youth coach the instruction inverts. The temptation with a quick young player is to build the team around the thing that already works, because it wins age-group matches and because it is what everybody enjoys watching. The cost is paid five years later, in a career that is heavily conditional in exactly the way this article has described. The practical answer is not to stop playing him in space; it is to make sure that a fixed proportion of his weekly repetitions occur in the crowded, back-to-goal, standing-start situations, and to protect that proportion when results are under pressure. Teaching a quick young player to receive facing his own goal is one of the least glamorous jobs in youth coaching and one of the highest-value ones.

There is also a broader lesson available for players who are not quick, and it is worth stating because most readers are in that group. The output curve of a complete forward is flatter than the output curve of a fast one. Flatness is a genuine competitive advantage in senior football, because most matches are played in the compressed middle states of the ladder rather than in open transition. A forward who is average in transition and reliable in traffic will play more matches, in more teams, than one who is exceptional in transition and helpless in traffic. That is not a consolation; it is a description of how squads are actually built.

## Closing verdict

The honest summary of Kylian Mbappe's weaknesses is short, and it is this.

His output is coupled to available running distance, and every other limitation in his game is a consequence of that coupling rather than an independent flaw. The congested reception, the standing-start finish, and the aerial absence all matter for the same reason, which is that they are the actions that remain when the coupling is broken. The out-of-possession discussion is a structural and role question that produces a usable attacking opportunity for opponents and produces nothing at all when it is converted into a claim about the person. The deployment question has a real answer, which is that the central version is more dangerous against a high line and the wide version is more dangerous against a deep one.

And the finding that matters more than any of them is the compression cost principle. He can be reduced. The states that reduce him most are the states that cost the defending side its own match, its territory, its legs, and its final twenty minutes, and a side that cannot hold the state it chose will be beaten by the version of him that arrives when the state breaks. The correct question for a coach is never which weakness to attack. It is which row of the ladder your squad can genuinely occupy for ninety minutes, and what you have decided to do in the two or three moments when it cannot.

One last thing is worth saying to the reader who came here hoping the article would settle an argument in his favour, whichever side of it he is on. The most common failure in football discussion is not being wrong about a player. It is being right about an observation and then attaching the observation to the wrong object. He is reduced by compression: correct observation, and it is a fact about match states rather than a fact about him. His pressing craft is not his strongest attribute: defensible observation, and it is a fact about roles and training environments rather than about character. His aerial threat is limited: correct observation, and it is a fact about a route his teams do not use rather than a route his opponents can attack. Attach each observation to the right object and you have a preparation. Attach it to the person and you have an argument that will still be running, unchanged and unresolved, long after the matches it was supposed to be about have been played.

Everything after that decision is sequencing, and the sequencing is a separate document: the line heights, the first defender's shoulder orientation, the moment the cover defender commits, the rest-defense shape when the ball is lost high, and the fallbacks when the first plan fails all belong to the counter blueprint on [how a defensive plan against him is actually built and executed](/2020/02/15/how-to-stop-kylian-mbappe/). The comparison a reader will want next, against the other forward who defines the modern defensive planning problem from the opposite direction, is settled in the head-to-head on [which of the two is harder to defend](/2020/05/15/haaland-vs-mbappe-harder-to-defend/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Kylian Mbappe's biggest weakness?

The coupling between his output and available running distance. Everything else people list is downstream of it. Shorten the corridor between his reception point and the last defender, force him to receive without forward momentum, and the actions that produce his highest-value chances stop occurring. That is a real and measurable reduction, and it is also the only limitation worth building a plan around. The qualification that keeps the answer honest is that the states which shorten that corridor are expensive to hold, because they cost the defending side territory, possession, and the legs it will need in the closing stages. He is not difficult to reduce for twenty minutes. He is difficult to reduce for ninety.

### Q: Does Mbappe struggle against a deep block?

His most dangerous actions are substantially removed by a genuinely compressed block, which is a real effect and larger than his supporters usually concede. The depth run has nowhere to go, receptions become static and crowded, and the finishing situations that remain are the ones that suit him least. Two honest qualifications belong with that. First, a deep block does not remove the wide isolation duel or the cutback geometry, both of which his teams attack directly. Second, a side defending that deep has usually surrendered its own attacking match to do it, and the block tends to lose its shape late, which is when the reduction stops holding.

### Q: How good is Mbappe with his back to goal?

Competent and improving, and not the strongest part of his game. Receiving with a defender tight behind him in a congested central area is the situation furthest from the one his roles have produced, and specialists at that reception have spent whole careers in situations demanding it. The observable difference is in touch distance and in the preference for an immediate lay-off over turning into contact. It has clearly moved as his career has progressed and as he has spent longer periods as a central forward, so treating it as a fixed deficiency is wrong. The correct defensive response is not to press him from behind but to deny the turn while staying goal-side, force the lay-off, and attack the receiver.

### Q: Is Mbappe's left foot a weakness?

No, and the claim survives mainly because his most repeated finishing pattern happens to use the right. The practical test of a weaker foot is whether an opponent can profitably show a player onto it, and against him that free decision does not exist: the left foot strikes a ball cleanly, finishes a cutback first time, and does not turn a live threat into a dead one. What is true is smaller and usable. Forcing the ball onto his left from the left channel pushes the shot toward a tighter near-post angle and removes the inside cut. That is a marginal edge to be combined with other constraints, not a neutralization.

### Q: Does Mbappe do enough defensive work?

The question as phrased cannot be answered, because it asks about a disposition rather than an instruction, and a forward's out-of-possession output is determined overwhelmingly by his role, his shape, and what his coach wants conserved for attacking phases. The defensible version is narrower. The technical craft of coordinated pressing, meaning the curved approach and the cover shadow held through a sequence, is not the most developed part of his skill set relative to forwards trained daily in systems that demanded it. The useful version is different again. His teams systematically leave one zone in front of an opposition full-back under-covered, and that zone is where an attacking plan should be aimed.

### Q: Is Mbappe as effective through the middle as from the left?

They are different problems rather than better and worse ones, and the answer depends on the opponent. Centrally his mechanism is depth: running the shoulder of a centre-back and attacking balls into the channel, which makes him more dangerous against a high line because it converts the smallest line-height error into the best chance available in football. From the left his mechanism is isolation: a facing-forward reception on the touchline and a diagonal corridor toward the penalty spot, which survives compression because it does not need space behind. Against a deep block the wide version is the more dangerous of the two, which is the reverse of what most planning assumes.

### Q: Does Mbappe rely on open space to be dangerous?

He is more dependent on it than a complete centre-forward and less dependent than the claim usually implies. Space is the input his acceleration converts, so removing it removes his highest-value outputs, and no honest account denies that. What the claim leaves out is that reduction is not elimination: the wide duel, the arriving shot, the combination at the edge of the area, and the movement that occupies two defenders all survive without a corridor. The claim also leaves out the price. Every state that removes the space costs the defending team territory and legs, and the sides that pay it most heavily are the ones most likely to concede in the final quarter.

### Q: How does Mbappe perform in the air?

Aerial contests are not a primary weapon, and this is one of the few limitations that needs no conditions attached. He is not a target for a direct delivery, not a dead-ball threat that forces a marking scheme to be rewritten, and a hanging cross into a crowd is not a chance he converts at his usual rate. The tactical value of that to an opponent is mostly in resource allocation: standard marking on set pieces, the best aerial defenders assigned elsewhere, and a free preference for showing crossers onto the delivery he cannot attack. It is not a route to stopping him, since his teams attack along the ground.

### Q: Why do critics say Mbappe disappears in tight contests?

Because a compressed match converts his contribution into actions that do not appear in a highlight package. In that state he occupies a centre-back so a teammate can receive, runs channels that are never used but which prevent a defender from stepping, and pins a full-back who would otherwise join an overload. A viewer watching for events records an absence. A viewer watching the opposition's back line records a permanent constraint. The reduction underneath the claim is real, but the word chosen for it describes an absence of contribution when what actually happens is a change in the type of contribution.

### Q: Has Mbappe improved his link-up play?

Yes, and the direction of that movement is one of the reasons an article about his limitations has to be written carefully. A forward who begins as a wide runner in counter-attacking structures and later operates centrally in possession-dominant teams accumulates a different kind of repetition: receiving between the lines, laying off and spinning, combining in tight triangles, and releasing a third runner rather than taking the ball himself. What has moved most is the decision made before the touch. The early pattern of receiving and immediately attacking a defender individually has widened into a set of options that includes the deliberate occupation of a defender to open a lane for somebody else.

### Q: What limits Mbappe inside a possession system?

A possession-dominant team produces the match states that suit him least, and this is the underappreciated tension in his profile. Sustained possession invites the opposition to defend deep and compressed, which removes the corridor, and it reduces the number of transitions, which is the state where he is close to unstoppable. It also asks a forward to hold positional discipline, receive in crowded areas, and accept long spells without the ball arriving in a usable shape. The resolution most coaches reach is to keep him wide and high as a permanent outlet, accepting a numerical shortfall in rest defense in exchange for turning any turnover into a chance within seconds.

### Q: Do analysts see a flaw in Mbappe's first touch under pressure?

Not in his touch generally, which is falsified by any full match: controlling a ball at speed, across the body, against a recovering defender is among the harder technical actions in the sport and he performs it repeatedly. The precise observation is about one reception type. When a ball arrives at an awkward height, from behind or across him, with a defender in contact and no forward direction available, the touch travels further from his body than a specialist's would, because it is optimized for continuing a movement rather than for killing a ball dead in traffic. Those are different skills, developed in different environments.

### Q: What can young wingers learn from Mbappe's limitations?

That the part of the game your speed lets you avoid is the part you must deliberately train, because your environment will never produce it for you. A quick young player is given the ball facing forward, in space, at every level, because it wins matches immediately, and several years of that produces enormous repetition in one reception type and almost none in the others. The three worth protecting in a weekly plan are receiving with a defender on your back, finishing from a standing start in traffic, and the timing of the run to attack a delivery. All three raise the lower half of an output curve, which is the half that decides senior matches.

### Q: Is Mbappe overrated by some supporters?

That is a question about public opinion rather than about football, and this series has no way to settle it and no interest in trying. The football question underneath it can be answered. His output is more conditional than that of a complete centre-forward whose curve stays flat across match states, and it is far less conditional than the critical version claims, because the conditions that suppress him are ones very few sides can hold for a full match. A coach who prepares for the archetype rather than for the reputation will be better prepared than one who has already decided which side of the argument he is on.

### Q: Does Mbappe's game depend on a particular system around him?

It depends on conditions rather than on a specific formation, and the distinction matters. What the structure has to supply is a passer willing to release the ball early rather than after two extra touches, a teammate who occupies the defender who would otherwise slide across to cover, and a shape that keeps him high enough to attack a corridor rather than dropping him into build-up. Several different systems supply those conditions, which is why he has produced in structurally dissimilar teams. What no system can supply is the corridor itself when the opponent has chosen to remove it and can afford the removal.

### Q: Is Mbappe harder to defend at speed or in a crowd?

At speed, and it is not close. In an open corridor with a facing-forward reception, no individual defensive answer exists, and the contest is decided before it starts by whether a second defender can arrive. In a crowd the contest becomes normal: a defender in contact, no forward direction, an awkward reception height, and a finishing situation that suits him least. The honest addition is that the crowd is not free. Manufacturing it requires a compressed block, and holding that block for ninety minutes costs a defending side its territory, its outlet, and usually its final twenty minutes of concentration.
