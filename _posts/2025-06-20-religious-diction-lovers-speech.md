## Religious Diction in the Lovers' Speech: The Verdict First

The religious diction of the lovers' speech in Romeo and Juliet is not ornament laid over desire. It is the load-bearing structure of the love itself. When Romeo and Juliet meet at the Capulet feast, they do not merely reach for pretty comparisons drawn from church language. They build, word by word and correction by correction, a small working liturgy of their own: a shrine, a saint, a pilgrim, a rite of touch, a doctrine of sin, a form of prayer, and eventually a marriage demanded from the real Church because the private rite requires a public sacrament to complete it. The verdict defended across this article is that the sacred vocabulary consecrates rather than decorates, and that it consecrates because the lovers submit it to discipline. Romeo opens nearly every sacred figure. Juliet corrects, restricts, and completes nearly every one. The diction that survives her editing is the diction the play endorses, and the words that fail her editing, the loose oaths, the moon-swearing, the idol-talk she herself flags and fences, are the words the tragedy punishes.

![The lovers exchanging the religious diction of the pilgrim sonnet at the feast - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The brief for this article names five of the words in its summary sentence, saint, pilgrim, shrine, sin, and the vocabulary of faith itself, and the five make a fair map of the territory in miniature. Saint and shrine name the beloved, the object of the whole system. Pilgrim names the lover, the subject who approaches. Sin names the law, the moral physics that governs the approach and prices its offences. Faith names the stake, the thing risked and kept by both parties across the play. Object, subject, law, and stake: a complete religion could be organised under those four heads, and the lovers' diction, sixteen words in all, organises itself under them without any speaker ever announcing the scheme. The sections that follow work outward from the map, counting the words first, then following them through the scenes that spend them, then weighing the charge that the spending was theft.

That division of labour is the single most important fact about this vocabulary, and it is visible in counts as much as in impressions. Romeo speaks the greater number of sacred words; Juliet speaks the decisive ones. His register arrives ready-made, inherited from the Petrarchan love poetry he had been reciting at Rosaline, and its first form in the play is pagan machinery, Cupid's arrow, Dian's wit, a siege conducted in mythological counters (1.1). The Christian lexicon appears only when Juliet appears, and it appears because the encounter demands a vocabulary of reverence that the Rosaline stock cannot supply. From that first exchange onward, the lovers' sacred diction behaves like a currency under joint management. Terms are minted by one speaker, audited by the other, and either passed into circulation or withdrawn. Saint circulates. Pilgrim is corrected into palmer. Prayer is rationed. Sin is transferred, returned, and finally laid on a killer's head in the tomb. The glossary below counts the currency; the sections after it trace the management.

A word on method, because the distinction governs everything that follows. Imagery and diction are not the same evidence. An image can drift through a play on currents no character notices; the light imagery of this tragedy falls on speakers who never chose it and scenes that never discuss it. Diction in dialogue is different. A spoken word is chosen in the hearing of another character, who may accept it, return it, correct it, or refuse it, and the next speech records the decision. The lovers' religious vocabulary is therefore evidence of a kind imagery can never supply: it is a transcript of negotiations. When Juliet answers pilgrim with palmer, the correction is not a pattern a critic counts after the fact. It is an event inside the fiction, a ruling delivered by one character on another character's word, with consequences for every later use of the term. This is why the present article works from a glossary of words in mouths rather than from a catalogue of pictures in the air. The pictures belong to the playwright's atmosphere. The words belong to the lovers, and the play is careful, scene by scene, about which lover holds which word at which moment, and about what the holding costs.

The stakes of the vocabulary are set by the world around it. Verona's official religion is present in the play chiefly as furniture: a friar's cell, a confession errand used as an alibi, a wedding advanced and then converted into a funeral, a churchyard where a suitor strews flowers and a husband breaks in with a crowbar. Against that institutional backdrop the lovers' diction does the work the institution fails to do. It names the beloved as sacred, it builds a rite for approaching her, it defines the moral law of the approach (sin, trespass, purging), and it supplies the words in which the pair marry, part, and die. Restoring this diction to view changes what the love is understood to be. Heard without it, the play sounds like romance decorated with church colouring. Heard with it, the play is the story of two people who made a faith out of the only elevated language their world allowed them, and whose faith was exact enough to be judged, by the play itself, as both devotion and danger.

### Which word carries the heaviest count in the lovers' speech, and why is it sin rather than saint?

Sin carries it, five occurrences against saint's four. The lead belongs to sin because the sonnet's sacred machinery exists to manage a debt: Romeo's touch is framed as a profanation, the kiss purges the sin, Juliet returns it, and Romeo begs it back.

The word is still on his lips in the tomb when he begs Paris not to lay another sin upon his head (5.3). Saint names the beloved; sin names the moral physics of approaching her, and the play spends more of its sacred currency on the physics than on the name.

Read as a whole, the glossary shows a vocabulary with a centre of gravity in a single scene and long echoes afterward. Nine of its sixteen terms are coined or concentrated at the feast in Act 1 Scene 5. The orchard adds angel, blessed, idolatry, and gracious. The wedding contributes holy in its sacramental sense. After the marriage the mint closes: no new sacred term is coined for the love in Acts 3, 4, or 5, and the old terms return only under pressure, faith divided in the parting speech, holy borrowed as a cover story for a disobedient daughter, righteous spent once over a corpse. The distribution is the argument in miniature. The lovers build their lexicon while building the love, in the two nights of courtship and wedding. What the later acts test is not the vocabulary's invention but its endurance, and the testing is severe: banishment, a forced second marriage, a potion, a tomb. That so few of the words survive the testing, and that the survivors return altered, is a fact about diction that no paraphrase of the plot can capture.

Among the sixteen, the adjective holy does the quietest and perhaps the deepest work, because it is the vocabulary's instrument of transfer. Holy is the one term whose grammatical job is to move sacredness from one noun to another, and its itinerary across the play maps the faith's expansion. At the feast it consecrates a place, the shrine that is Juliet's hand, and an act, the palmers' kiss that is palm to palm. In the cell it consecrates speech itself, the holy words that close the lovers' hands in marriage. In Act 4 it consecrates a person by office, holy Laurence, in the mouth of a girl using the consecration as a passport. Place, act, words, officer: the adjective visits each category of the sacred in turn, as if the vocabulary were checking its own completeness, and it never once attaches to the lovers themselves. Neither is ever called holy by the other. The system's restraint survives even in its most mobile word: the pair will consecrate everything around the beloved except the beloved in so many words, leaving that final transfer to the fathers' statues and the audience's memory.

A note on the counting itself, since a glossary stands or falls by its honesty about method. The counts above tally occurrences of each term and its close inflections in the lovers' own speeches in the text Weis prints from the 1599 quarto tradition, and they count the word wherever the lovers speak it, in soliloquy as in dialogue, since a private faith is practised in both. Three consequences follow. First, the totals are small, and smallness is information: a vocabulary can be the load-bearing structure of a play while numbering sixteen terms and some forty occurrences, because the terms are placed at the hinges, the meeting, the vow, the wedding, the parting, the tomb. Second, the totals are edition-sensitive at the margins. The 1597 quarto's variant at the sonnet's fourth line would add a further occurrence of sin to the feast's tally, and its compressed text drops lines the fuller version keeps, so any glossary built on the earlier quarto would show a slightly poorer, slightly more sin-heavy vocabulary. Third, the counts deliberately exclude the penumbra: words like church, friar, and ghostly, which belong to the institution's furniture, and words like soul and heart, which belong to every register in the play at once. A glossary that admitted the penumbra could double its rows without doubling its knowledge. The discipline of the count is the exclusion, and the exclusion is what lets the distribution argue.

Two features of the counts deserve emphasis before the scenes are examined. The first is asymmetry of coinage. Romeo introduces shrine, profane, pilgrim, angel, blessed, and righteous. Juliet introduces palmer, devotion, idolatry, and gracious, and she is the one who converts pilgrim, saint, and prayer from his opening offers into governed forms. Coinage is his; legislation is hers. The second feature is the near-absence of the largest words. Neither lover calls the other god outright; the single approach to it, Juliet's god of my idolatry, names the thing as a fault in the act of permitting the oath. Neither lover uses the word worship of the other. The diction is ardent and it is fenced, and the fencing is internal, performed by the speakers themselves rather than imposed by any chorus, friar, or parent. When the blasphemy charge is weighed later in this article, that internal fencing is the evidence that matters most.

## Before Juliet: The Pagan Stock the New Diction Replaces

The Christian lexicon has a predecessor in the play, and the predecessor's failure is part of its meaning. Romeo enters the tragedy already speaking a sacred language of sorts, but its gods are pagan and its terms are counters from a book. For Rosaline he deploys the machinery of Cupid and Diana: "She'll not be hit / With Cupid's arrow; she hath Dian's wit" (1.1). The beloved is a votary of a goddess of chastity, the lover a besieger repelled by artillery of myth, and the whole system runs on personifications no one in Verona worships. His set speech of oxymorons, "O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1), is the same stock at the level of the phrase: bright smoke, cold fire, sick health, a liturgy of paradox recited to an audience of friends who recognise every item as a quotation from the culture's anthology of unrequited love. The diction is fluent and it is dead, in the precise sense that none of its terms can be negotiated. Benvolio cannot correct Cupid. Rosaline, absent and silent, cannot ration Diana. The pagan stock has no second speaker, and a sacred vocabulary with one speaker and no respondent is not a language but a recitation.

Mercutio's Queen Mab speech is the pagan register's set piece, a fairy mythology delivered as a tour de force by the play's great sceptic (1.4). Its diction is miniature and material, hazelnut chariots, spiderweb spokes, moonshine collars, and its theology is openly derisory: dreams are the children of an idle brain. Set beside the feast sonnet, the contrast is the play's own comment on its two sacred vocabularies. The pagan system is brilliant, encyclopaedic, and believed by nobody, least of all its speaker. The Christian system that replaces it is narrow, sixteen governed words, and treated by its speakers as binding law. The play marks the transition between the systems with unusual clarity. Romeo's last pagan utterance in the old style comes on the way to the feast, where he surrenders his course to a higher pilot, "he that hath the steerage of my course" (1.4), a providence still phrased in the language of fortune and navigation. Inside the hall, within the hour, he is speaking of shrines. The conversion of diction is the conversion the character himself undergoes, registered at the level of the word before it is registered anywhere else.

This prehistory disciplines the reading of the feast sonnet in two ways. First, it explains Romeo's fluency. The sonnet's sacred terms arrive quickly because the speaker arrives practised: he has been performing devotional address for months, in the other system, and the feast exchanges one devotional lexicon for another rather than teaching him devotion from nothing. Second, it isolates Juliet's contribution with new sharpness. Against Rosaline, the pagan diction ran uninterrupted because its object never answered. Against Juliet, the Christian diction is interrupted, amended, and legislated from its fourth line onward, by a respondent who speaks the system as if she had been waiting to correct it. The play's claim about the two loves is made in the diction before it is made anywhere else: the first love was a monologue in a dead sacred language, and the second is a dialogue in a living one. Everything the glossary counts follows from that difference.

## The Shared Sonnet: Who Supplies the Sacred Words, Who Corrects Them

The lovers' first exchange is a sonnet they compose together, and its diction is negotiated line by line. The form itself has been anatomised elsewhere in this series, in the study of [the shared sonnet the lovers speak at their first meeting](/2023/07/07/shared-sonnet-first-meeting-analysis/), and the kiss it engineers has its own scene study in [the first kiss and its pilgrims and palmers' hands](/2023/07/14/first-kiss-pilgrim-imagery-analysis/). What belongs here is narrower and, for the diction question, decisive: the distribution of the sacred words between the two voices, and the pattern of offer and amendment by which the vocabulary is built. Treated as a scene, the exchange is a courtship. Treated as a text, it is a drafting session in which a private religious language is written, reviewed, and passed into law.

Romeo opens with a legal fiction disguised as an apology. "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle fine is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5.93-96). Four sacred or quasi-sacred terms are spent in four lines: profane, holy, shrine, pilgrims. The logic is a small syllogism of devotion. A hand that touches a shrine unworthily commits a profanation; profanation incurs a fine; the fine appointed is a kiss. Every element is borrowed from the practice of pilgrimage, where the shrine is approached, touched, and paid its offering, and every element is transferred to a girl's hand at a dance. The daring of the lines is the transfer itself, and the diction knows it: profane is the confession that the transfer might be a misuse, spoken in advance so that the rite can proceed under its own absolution.

Juliet's reply is the founding act of the lovers' joint diction, because she accepts the vocabulary and amends it in the same breath. "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much, / Which mannerly devotion shows in this; / For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch, / And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss" (1.5.97-100). The corrections are precise. Romeo had called his hand unworthy; she rules that the hand has shown mannerly devotion, devotion being her first sacred word in the play and a term of disciplined worship rather than of transport. Romeo had cast himself as a pilgrim still travelling; she promotes him to palmer, the pilgrim whose journey is accomplished and who carries the palm as its sign, because a traveller may only approach a shrine while a palmer may touch it. Romeo had made her hand a shrine; she answers with saints, distributing the sacredness from the place to a class of persons with rules of conduct, saints have hands, saints may be touched in a prescribed way. Each amendment keeps the figure and governs it. The blasphemy risk of the exchange, a kiss solicited in church language, is managed not by abandoning the language but by tightening its definitions until the kiss becomes the permitted form of contact.

### Who speaks more of the sonnet's sacred words, Romeo or Juliet, and by what count?

Romeo speaks more of them, roughly ten sacred tokens against her seven by the glossary's way of counting, and the imbalance is the point. His courtship proceeds by expenditure: profane, shrine, pilgrims, saints, palmers, saint, pray, faith, prayer again, and despair.

Hers proceeds by legislation: devotion, palmer, prayer, saints, each of her terms either correcting one of his or fixing the condition under which his may stand. Quantity is his strategy. Jurisdiction is hers.

The second half of the sonnet shows the legislation at work on the most dangerous word in the exchange. Romeo presses the logic of the shrine toward lips: "Have not saints lips, and holy palmers too?" (1.5.101). Juliet concedes the fact and rations its use: "Ay, pilgrim, lips that they must use in prayer" (1.5.102). The demotion is easy to miss and fatal to miss. He has just been promoted to palmer; in her answer he is pilgrim again, because the promotion held only for hands. Lips are restored to their liturgical function, prayer, and the concession is framed so that any other use must now be argued for as a kind of prayer. Romeo accepts the frame and argues inside it with the sonnet's most compressed line of sacred diction: "O then, dear saint, let lips do what hands do. / They pray; grant thou lest faith turn to despair" (1.5.103-104). The word faith enters the lovers' vocabulary here, as the stake of the petition: if the saint refuses to grant, faith itself curdles into despair. The couplet then performs the granting under rule. "Saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake" (1.5.105) fixes the saint's stillness as a condition of the rite, and "Then move not while my prayer's effect I take" (1.5.106) converts the stillness into the kiss. Prayer, in this diction, is not a metaphor for the kiss. The kiss is defined as prayer's effect, its answered form, and the definition has been negotiated into existence by two speakers in fourteen lines.

### What is the one sacred act the sonnet's diction keeps postponing?

The kiss itself. Every sacred term in the sonnet is a deferral device: the hand must be apologised for, the fine defined, the pilgrim promoted, the saint petitioned, the prayer granted, before lips may do what hands do at last.

The diction stretches fourteen lines between desire and its object, and the stretching is the courtesy the vocabulary exists to supply. When the kiss finally arrives it arrives as a conclusion, an effect taken, not as a grab.

What follows the sonnet, the brief quatrain in which sin changes hands, is the diction's first stress test, and it passes by turning sacramental. "Thus from my lips, by thine, my sin is purged" (1.5.108) treats the kiss as an absolution that has transferred the penitent's sin to the absolver. Juliet accepts the transfer with a logician's calm: "Then have my lips the sin that they have took" (1.5.109). Romeo demands the debt back, "Give me my sin again" (1.5.110), and Juliet closes the exchange with the verdict that has governed the whole scene: "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.111). The book is at once the prayer book, the book of courtly rule, and the literal book of the sonnet form they have just completed. In diction terms the line certifies that the sacred vocabulary has been used according to its own regulations. The lovers have not raided the church's words for decoration. They have observed the words' grammar, and the grammar has licensed the act.

One word in the post-sonnet quatrain carries a charge out of proportion to its single occurrence, and the glossary folds it into sin's count only because its family is the same. Trespass, in Juliet's "Sin from my lips? O trespass sweetly urged!", is the period's most familiar sacred word in its most familiar setting, since every churchgoer in the first audience said it weekly in the Lord's Prayer's petition about debts and trespasses forgiven as they are forgiven. The word imports the prayer's whole economy into the kiss: offences as debts, debts as transferable, forgiveness as the mechanism that keeps the accounts moving. Juliet's oxymoron, sweetly urged, performs the exchange rate. A trespass in the prayer is a burden to be released; a trespass in her mouth is a compliment to the urging, the offence revalued by the manner of its commission. The single word thus demonstrates the diction's central operation at its smallest scale. A term is lifted from the liturgy with its structure intact, debt, transfer, release, and its valuation inverted by the transfer, burden become prize. Romeo's demand to have his sin again completes the inversion: in the prayer's economy nobody petitions for the return of a forgiven debt, and in the lovers' economy the returned sin is the second kiss, the debt reissued as a gift. Four lines perform what the whole play will perform at scale, the sacred economy running correctly and backwards at once.

Three further features of the sonnet's diction repay attention, because each one sets a term of the vocabulary's later history. The first is an absence. The word love does not occur in the fourteen lines. The most famous love scene in the language conducts its business entirely in the vocabulary of devotion, hands, palms, pilgrims, saints, prayer, sin, faith, and never once names the emotion it is transacting. The omission is a diction fact with an argument inside it: the sacred terms are not describing the love from outside, as commentary might, but carrying it, as the only currency the speakers will accept for the transaction. When the word love finally floods the orchard scene an act later, it floods a channel the sonnet dug in other words.

The second feature is the verb grant, the sonnet's operative word, spoken twice in the couplet's machinery, once by Juliet as the condition of the rite, "though grant for prayers' sake", and once by Romeo as the petition's hinge, "grant thou lest faith turn to despair" (1.5.104-105). Grant is a word with two homes, the law court and the liturgy: a grant conveys property, and a prayer asks a grant of grace. The sonnet holds both homes in one syllable. What passes between the lovers is figured simultaneously as a legal conveyance and as an act of divine generosity, and the doubleness is the whole conceit of the scene stated at the level of a single verb. Later acts keep returning to the word's legal home, in contracts, bargains, and seals, while its liturgical home quietly empties, until the tomb's dateless bargain restores both at once.

The third feature is the pronoun. The sonnet's speakers address each other as thou from the first exchange, the form the period reserved for intimates, inferiors, and God. In the mouths of two strangers at a dance the form is a small scandal, and its liturgical resonance is part of the scandal's design: the thou of the sonnet is the thou of prayer, the pronoun in which the period addressed heaven, here used between a masked guest and a girl whose name he does not know. The play notices the audacity and lets it stand, because the diction's whole wager is that the forms of prayer are the correct forms for this address. The pronoun is the smallest word in the glossary's story and in some ways the boldest, since it cannot be corrected into safety the way palmer corrected pilgrim. It simply commits both speakers, from the first minute, to speaking to each other in the grammar of speaking to God.

The manner of Juliet's corrections deserves a closer look, because it identifies the intellectual tradition the diction is drawing on besides the liturgy. Her moves in the sonnet are the moves of scholastic disputation, the schoolroom art of the distinction. Romeo proposes a term in its loose sense; Juliet concedes the term and distinguishes its senses, granting the one that helps her case and refusing the one that helps his. Saints have hands: conceded. Therefore lips may do what hands do: refused, by distinction, since lips have an appointed use that hands do not. Pilgrim: distinguished into pilgrim and palmer, the second sense granted because it serves the rite. The diction of the scene is thus double-sourced. Its nouns come from the chapel, but its grammar comes from the disputation, and the combination explains the scene's peculiar flavour of ardent logic, a courtship conducted as a viva voce examination in which the candidate keeps passing by being corrected. Romeo, notably, welcomes the method. His answers accept her distinctions and argue from inside them, which is why the exchange builds a shared system rather than staging a contest. Two speakers disputing in good faith about the senses of sacred words are, by the period's own understanding of the activity, doing a kind of theology, and the play lets its teenagers do it better than its friar does.

The textual history of the sonnet underlines the sin economy's centrality. The earliest quarto of 1597 reads, at the fine's definition, a phrase in which the penalty itself is named as the gentle sin, where the fuller 1599 text, followed by Weis, prints the gentle fine. Editors have debated the variant for centuries, and the debate's existence is the diction point: in the play's first printed form, the word sin stood inside the sonnet's fourth line, governing the exchange from even earlier in the scene than the familiar text allows. The vocabulary's heaviest word was heavier still in the version the first audiences read. A glossary is only as good as its edition, and on this word the editions agree in tendency whatever they print in the line: the sonnet's economics are an economics of sin, priced, purged, transferred, and demanded back.

The sonnet's world ends, fittingly, on an interruption in another register. The Nurse's summons, madam, your mother craves a word with you (1.5), is household diction, kinship and errand, and it cuts the liturgy off mid-rite, returning Juliet to the family whose marriage market the rite has just bypassed. The play will repeat the pattern at every stage: the sacred diction builds its enclosure, and the world's registers, a mother's summons, a cousin's rage in the same hall, a prince's edict, a father's new wedding day, break in upon it. Tybalt's fury runs underneath the feast scene itself, so that the sonnet is spoken within earshot of the feud's vocabulary at its most concentrated, hate being sworn in one corner while prayer is negotiated in another. The diction's achievement at the feast is therefore not escape but enclosure under pressure, a rite completed in a room where another rite, the feud's, is simultaneously in session. The glossary's first nine terms were all coined inside that pressure, which may be why they hold: the vocabulary was never a hothouse growth, and its later survivals in cells and churchyards continue a habit formed in its first fourteen lines.

## After the Feast: Angel, Saint and Idol in the Orchard

The orchard scene moves the sacred register from touch to sight, and the diction changes its sensory allegiance accordingly. Romeo, hidden below the balcony, watches Juliet appear at the window and reaches for the vocabulary of vision and altitude: "O speak again, bright angel, for thou art / As glorious to this night, being o'er my head, / As is a winged messenger of heaven" (2.2.26-28). Angel is coined here and never used again by either lover. The word belongs to distance. At the feast she was a saint, approachable under rule, with hands that could be touched in a prescribed manner. In the orchard she is overhead, glorious, a messenger whose element is air, and the diction registers the geometry of the scene before it registers anything else: the sacred word for a beloved who can be seen but not yet reached.

### Why does the orchard scene move the sacred words from touch to sight?

Because the scene's obstacle is vertical. The feast vocabulary was tactile, hands, palms, lips, since the lovers stood within reach of each other and the problem was permission. The orchard's problem is access across a wall and a height, so the diction ascends.

Angel, messenger of heaven, eyes like stars, a face that shames the lamps of heaven: the orchard's sacred terms all belong to sight and altitude. Touch returns only when the words have done their climbing, in the exchange of vows the scene builds toward.

The orchard also produces the diction's most self-aware moment, and it is Juliet who speaks it. Romeo, overheard swearing by the moon, is instructed to abandon celestial oath-objects altogether: "Do not swear at all, / Or if thou wilt, swear by thy gracious self, / Which is the god of my idolatry, / And I'll believe thee" (2.2.112-114). The line is usually quoted as surrender, and its diction says something more exact. Juliet names the theological fault inside the lovers' vocabulary, idolatry, the worship of a creature in the place of the creator, and she names it as her own: my idolatry. The word gracious, her coinage here, does quiet work beside it, borrowing the language of grace for Romeo's self and making that self the only admissible object of an oath. A vocabulary that can diagnose its own excess in its own terms is not naive borrowing. It is a working theology with a doctrine of its limits, and the doctrine is spoken by the same speaker who rationed prayer at the feast. The pattern holds across both scenes: Romeo spends, Juliet fences, and the fencing is what keeps the spending from becoming mere blasphemy.

Saint makes its last appearance in the orchard, in a context that shows how far the word has travelled from the feast. Romeo, asked who he is, answers: "By a name / I know not how to tell thee who I am. / My name, dear saint, is hateful to myself / Because it is an enemy to thee" (2.2.55-57). At the feast, saint was a title within a rite, the still figure who grants for prayers' sake. Here it has become a form of address inside a confession: the saint is now the confessor to whom a hateful name is admitted. The shrinkage is characteristic of the diction after the first night. The feast coined terms for a public rite performed in a crowded hall under the cover of a dance. The orchard reuses the terms in private, between two speakers, and the reuse narrows them from liturgy to intimacy. Nothing new is minted for the love in this scene except the diagnosis, idolatry, and the oath-object, gracious self. The vocabulary has passed from invention to application, which is the biography of most working languages, sacred ones included.

The orchard also changes the diction's mode, and the change is easy to miss because the vocabulary stays recognisably itself. The feast ran on petition: a petitioner, a saint, a prayer, a grant. Petition is asymmetric and temporary, ended by the granting. The orchard builds toward covenant: vows exchanged, an oath-object chosen, a contract named and then refined, a marriage proposed as the institution that will make the exchange permanent. The sacred diction follows the mode change exactly. Prayer words recede, since the petition has been granted at the feast and need not be re-pleaded. Oath words advance to take their place, and Juliet's legislation turns from rationing prayer to policing oaths: not by the moon, not at all, or by the gracious self alone. The sequence of her corrections across the two scenes is a curriculum in how a private faith matures. First she governs the approach. Then she governs the promise. A vocabulary that can change its governing mode without changing its speakers' allegiance to it is behaving like a language and not like a costume, which is one more reason the ornamental reading of the diction fails.

The orchard's central transaction is a confession, and its diction deserves the name. Romeo's identity is extracted from him in the form of an admission against interest: asked who he is, he answers that he knows not how to tell it, since his name, spoken to the saint, is hateful to himself for being her enemy (2.2). The structure is the confessional's. A penitent kneels in darkness, screened from the confessor, and speaks the thing that shames him; the confessor, elevated and unseen, hears, judges, and directs what follows. Juliet occupies the confessor's position for the whole first movement of the scene, dispensing rulings on oaths and names from her window as from a grille, and the diction keeps the arrangement visible: his speeches confess, hers absolve, direct, and finally covenant. That the confessor is the beloved herself is the scene's quiet audacity, a further transfer of office from the institution to the pair. In the cell scenes the real confessor will marry them, plot for them, and fail to console them. In the orchard the office has already been exercised, more effectively, by a girl at a window, and the play's later failures of the official are measured against the unofficial success.

One more orchard coinage deserves its line in the record, because it shows the diction working at the border of the visible. Romeo's first orchard speech makes Juliet's eyes an embassy from the sacred world: two of the fairest stars in all the heaven, having some business, entreat her eyes to twinkle in their spheres till their return (2.2). Heaven here is not a doctrine but an optics, a machinery of light and deputised shining, and the word does the same work angel does a moment later, lifting the beloved into a register where sight substitutes for touch. The play returns this heaven of optics to its sender in the tomb, where Romeo's last speech inventories the same visible signs, breath, blood, colour in the lips and cheeks, and reads them through the diction of a liturgy rather than of astronomy. The orchard's heaven taught him what to look for. The tomb's diction decides what the looking means.

## Holy Words: The Wedding and the Diction That Follows It

The wedding scene is the diction's moment of maximum institutional contact, and its key phrase belongs to Romeo at the door of the Friar's cell: "Do thou but close our hands with holy words" (2.6). Holy, the adjective of the shrine and the palmers' kiss, is transferred to words themselves, to the marriage service the Friar is about to speak. The transfer completes a circuit the feast began. There, a hand became a shrine and a kiss became a prayer. Here, speech becomes the sacramental instrument: hands are closed, joined, sealed by words, and the words are holy because of what they effect rather than how they sound. The Friar's closing couplet answers in the institution's own diction, promising that the pair shall not stay alone "Till holy church incorporate two in one" (2.6). Incorporate is a theologian's word, one flesh, one body, and for once the lovers' private lexicon and the Church's public one say the same thing in nearly the same terms. The wedding is the only point in the play where the two vocabularies fully coincide, and the coincidence lasts exactly one scene.

### What changes in the diction inside the very scene that marries them?

The direction of borrowing reverses. At the feast the lovers borrowed the Church's words for a rite of their own. In the cell the Church's words do the work and the lovers mostly receive them: holy words spoken over joined hands, incorporation pronounced by an officiant.

Romeo's single coinage of the scene is a request, not a rite. The diction's centre of gravity shifts, briefly, from the pair's invention to the institution's authority, and the tragedy begins when that authority has to operate in hiding.

The scene's other speeches keep the two vocabularies in their assigned places with almost diagrammatic neatness. Romeo's contributions, once the request for holy words is made, shrink to a single defiance and a single act of naming: come what sorrow can, it cannot countervail the exchange of joy, and it is enough, he says, that he may but call her mine (2.6). The possessive is the one false note in the lovers' diction across the whole play, the market's word mine doing duty inside the liturgy, and the Friar's answering couplets correct it in the institution's own manner, by redefining the possession as incorporation: not mine but one, not held but incorporated, two in one flesh under holy church. The correction passes without remark, as Juliet's corrections pass at the feast, by being absorbed into the rite itself. Even here the pattern of the whole vocabulary holds. A term is offered in a flawed sense, a ruling voice amends the sense, and the amended sense is the one the play keeps.

After the marriage, the sacred diction goes underground with the marriage itself, and its later history is a record of strain. In the Friar's cell, banished and frantic, Romeo turns the orchard's heaven language against itself: "Heaven is here / Where Juliet lives, and every cat and dog / And little mouse, every unworthy thing, / Live here in heaven and may look on her, / But Romeo may not" (3.3). Heaven has become a taunt, a place defined by exclusion from the beloved rather than by approach to her. The word keeps its altitude and loses its consolation. This is the diction under its first real pressure, and the pressure shows: the term survives, but inverted, spoken as an accusation against the geography of the world. The Friar answers not in the lovers' lexicon but in his own doctrinal register, cataloguing blessings and preaching philosophy, and the scene's failure to console is partly a failure of translation between the two vocabularies. The Friar's religious diction is a system of doctrine. The lovers' is a system of address. Doctrine can describe Romeo's condition; only address could reach it, and the one speaker fluent in address is across the city, learning of the banishment in the same hour.

Juliet's parting speech divides the glossary's word faith along the seam of the separation: her husband is on earth, her faith in heaven (3.5). The sentence is a piece of diction analysis performed by the character herself. Faith, which entered the vocabulary as the stake of a granted prayer, is now assigned an address, and the address is not where the beloved is. The lovers' faith had been located in each other; the parting relocates half of it upward, into keeping, into storage, into a heaven that has so far in this play been a source of messengers rather than of help. Her single Amen in the same scene, flung at the Nurse's counsel to marry Paris, shows the diction doing cruder work: a liturgical assent used as a weapon, agreement pronounced in the form that ends prayers and ends arguments (3.5).

The dawn parting that opens the scene is, at the level of diction, the joint language's last exercise and its first failure. The lovers' final shared conversation is an argument about naming: whether the bird is the lark or the nightingale, whether the light is the sun's or the meteor's, whether the grey in the east is night's cloak parting or day's ensign (3.5). Every earlier exercise of the vocabulary had settled a name by negotiation, palm fixed as palmer, lips fixed as prayer's instrument, and the settlements held because both speakers wanted the rite the names served. At the window the same machinery runs and produces opposite settlements in alternate speeches, Juliet naming the sound a nightingale while she wishes the night to last and a lark the moment she must let the morning take him. The diction has not lost its skill. It has lost its jurisdiction, since the thing now being named is time itself, the one subject on which the private faith never held authority. A vocabulary that could govern shrines, sins, and sacraments meets the hour hand and breaks, and the breaking is audible in the scene's shrinking speeches, the long negotiations of the feast and orchard compressed into traded half-lines, as if the language itself were being rationed by the light. And in Act 4 the vocabulary is borrowed back as camouflage. Juliet returns from the Friar's cell and performs obedience for her father in the Church's own terms, enjoined by holy Laurence, pardon begged, a daughter ruled henceforward (4.2). The words are accurate and the use is false, a confession errand converted into a plot. Her genuine sacred diction of the same act is spoken to the Friar himself, and it is the diction of a contract already sealed by heaven: "God joined my heart and Romeo's, thou our hands" (4.1). God appears in her speech as the senior officiant of a marriage the Friar merely instrumented. The private faith, pressed by a forced second wedding, now cites divine authorship as its legal defence. The vocabulary has become jurisprudence.

One scene earlier, the wedding night itself had exposed a limit in the diction that the glossary's distribution quietly records. Juliet's great speech of anticipation, waiting for the Nurse and then for night, reaches not for the Christian lexicon the lovers built but for the pagan machinery Romeo discarded: "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2). Phoebus, Phaethon, the chariot of the sun: the bride summons the old gods at the very moment the new faith should be most entirely hers. The borrowing is instructive rather than embarrassing. The lovers' sacred diction was engineered for approach, petition, granting, and rite, the liturgy of two people negotiating reverence across a distance. The wedding night's speech is about desire in the interval before possession, a condition the private liturgy has no office for, since its rites all end in a granting that has now already occurred. Faced with a feeling her own vocabulary cannot celebrate, Juliet borrows the discarded system, whose whole stock was built for longing at a distance. The exception proves the glossary's rule about what the Christian diction is for. It is a language of governed approach. Where governance is finished and only appetite remains, even its chief legislator speaks another tongue.

Act 4 gives the diction its strangest assignment: it must be performed by a girl alone, to an audience of enemies, as the cover for a plot hatched in the Church's own cell. Juliet's apology scene with her father is a masterclass in split register. The warrant she cites is sacred and real, enjoined by holy Laurence, since the Friar has indeed set her a penance of obedience as the plot's first move. The performance she gives is secular and feudal: pardon begged on her knees, a promise to be ruled henceforward, a daughter's diction of submission drawn from the vocabulary of household government (4.2). Neither register is exactly lying. The sacred warrant is genuine and the submission is acted, and the scene's discomfort comes from hearing the two run in parallel, the true word holy doing service as the passport for a feigned obedience. The private faith has reached the stage where its vocabulary must be spent as camouflage, and the glossary records the cost: holy, coined for a shrine and a wedding, is here spent on a stratagem, the first of the lovers' sacred words to be used as an instrument rather than as an address.

Her soliloquy before the potion shows the complementary condition, the diction withheld. Alone with the vial, Juliet's speech fills with invocations, yet the invocations address no saint and no heaven: they address the vial, her fears, the imagined dagger, the ghost of Tybalt, the vault's horrors itemised one by one (4.3). The sacred register that might have steadied the speech is precisely what the speech cannot use, because the rite she is performing has no celebrant, no respondent, and no place in any liturgy the Church would own. She ends the speech by converting its last scrap of ceremony into a toast, drinking to Romeo in the diction of the table rather than of the altar. The pair of Act 4 scenes thus brackets the vocabulary's late condition. In company, the sacred words survive as camouflage. In solitude, they lapse into silence. Both conditions follow from the same fact the glossary has tracked since the feast: this diction is a duet, and it cannot be sung by one voice without changing into something else, a cover story in the hall, a silence in the chamber.

## The Tomb: The Diction Returns as Rite

The churchyard at night is where every strand of the diction is gathered and spent. Paris arrives first, strewing flowers on a bridal bed that has become a grave, and his diction borrows the lovers' register without its grammar: flowers, sweetness, nightly rite, a devotion performed for a girl who never heard a word of it (5.3). The borrowing fails because the vocabulary was never a set of ornaments to be applied to an occasion. It was a negotiated system between two speakers, and Paris, who speaks it alone, produces only its surface. Romeo's entrance converts the scene into the diction's last examination. To Paris himself he speaks the sonnet's key word in its darkest inflection: "put not another sin upon my head / By urging me to fury" (5.3). Sin, which at the feast was a flirtatious debt passed between lips and cheerfully returned, is now a burden tallied on a killer's head, the same word carrying five acts of accumulated weight. The glossary's count gives sin the lead among the lovers' sacred terms; the tomb scene explains why. Sin is the one term of the private liturgy that scales from a kiss to a killing without changing its meaning, only its price.

The tomb itself receives the diction's darkest act of naming. Romeo, forcing the monument open, addresses it as a detestable maw and a womb of death, gorged with the dearest morsel of the earth (5.3). The figure is the feast's shrine inverted with terrible exactness. A shrine is a container made holy by what it holds, approached on the knees, opened only under rite. The maw is a container made ravenous by what it holds, approached with a crowbar, forced to rot its doors and disgorge its tenant. The diction remembers its own first principles and applies them in negative: same structure of vessel and treasure, same grammar of approach, every value reversed. That Romeo reaches for the inverted figure rather than abandoning the figure altogether shows how completely the sacred frame has become his native way of seeing. Even desecration, in his mouth, is described in the vocabulary of shrines.

The exchange with Paris at the tomb door belongs in the same record, because Paris comes closer than any other speaker to naming the diction's offence, and still misses it. He charges Romeo with conjurations, with villainy and felony, with coming to do some villainous shame to the dead bodies (5.3): the accusations of a man who senses a rite being performed and can only file it under sorcery and crime. The play's long-deferred blasphemy charge thus arrives at last, in the wrong mouth, at the wrong object, minutes before the rite completes itself in earnest. Romeo answers in the diction of the sonnet one final time, courteous, petitioning, begging the gentle youth to leave and be gone, the pilgrim's manners intact at the edge of a killing. The manners fail, as the article's earlier section records, and sin is tallied on his head. But the failure is not the diction's exposure. It is the diction's last proof: the forms held even when the world they were built for had shrunk to a churchyard, a crowbar, and a corpse.

Over Juliet's body the diction achieves its final form, and the form is a rite performed correctly at last. "Eyes, look your last! / Arms, take your last embrace! and lips, O you / The doors of breath, seal with a righteous kiss / A dateless bargain to engrossing death" (5.3). Righteous, the glossary's last coinage, is a word from the vocabulary of law and liturgy at once: the kiss is pronounced lawful, sealed as a bargain is sealed, dated outside time. Lips, which at the feast were pilgrims under instruction, are now doors performing their office without instruction, because the rite's rules were learned in the sonnet and are being executed from memory. The scene reads, at the level of diction, like the feast exchange with the corrections complete and the congregation gone. Romeo no longer petitions. The saint no longer answers, corrects, or rations. The vocabulary works perfectly and there is no one left to govern it, which is the tragedy's bleakest statement about language: a liturgy perfected at the moment its community falls to one, then none.

Juliet's waking speech closes the diction's history by abandoning it. Her last lines reach not for saints or shrines but for instruments and vessels: a cup closed in true apothecary hands, a friendly drop unleft, a dagger addressed as a sheath's tenant (5.3). "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3). The one figure in the speech is domestic and mechanical, a blade and its case, and the sacred register that governed her speech from mannerly devotion onward is simply gone. The abandonment is legible in glossary terms: she dies outside the vocabulary because the vocabulary was a shared language, and a shared language with one speaker left is not a language but a memory. Romeo could still perform the rite because he believed the other celebrant dead and the rite therefore funerary. Juliet, waking to the celebrant's corpse, has no rite left to perform, and her diction says so by falling silent in the sacred register altogether.

## The Settlement: Statues, Gold and the Diction of the Ending

The last scene's diction about the lovers, spoken by everyone except them, completes the glossary's story from outside the wall. The fathers' reconciliation is conducted in the vocabulary of sacrifice and price. Montague promises a statue in pure gold; Capulet answers with a counter-gift, the rate of the other's figure matched joint by joint (5.3). The words are the market's, and the play does not hide the fact: the city's peace is a transaction, costed in metal. Yet the object the transaction buys is the lovers' diction made solid. A statue of the beloved, raised at public expense, honoured by a city, is a shrine image by another name, and the fathers speak of raising such images in the same scene where the Prince pronounces the story's official title, a tale of woe. The official religion of Verona, which never found a word for the lovers while they lived, ends by adopting the private faith's central practice, the enshrining of the beloved, and conducting it in the settlement's dialect because the settlement has no other. Diction judges diction here. The lovers' sixteen words built a rite the city could not speak. The city's gold builds the rite's monument without learning the words.

The Prince's closing couplets keep the two vocabularies apart to the very end, and the separation is the tragedy's last comment on its languages. His register is judicial throughout: pardons, punishments, discourse, a glooming peace. No sacred term crosses his lips over the bodies, although the scene before him, a girl on a bier in a tomb furnished like a chapel of death, invites the whole lexicon. The invitation is declined, and the declining is legible. The state can settle the feud, price the statues, and file the story. It cannot consecrate anything, because consecration in this play is a speech act reserved to the two speakers who built its vocabulary, and both are dead on the bier. What survives of the private faith is therefore exactly what survives of any liturgy whose celebrants perish: the forms, preserved in the memory of the audience, and the monument, preserved at the fathers' expense. The glossary is the forms' archive. The statues are the monument. Between the two of them, the diction outlives its speakers by precisely the margin the play's fame has since supplied.

## Ennobling or Blaspheming: The Charge the Diction Must Answer

The complication the diction has carried from its first line is whether it ennobles the love or blasphemes with borrowed holiness, and the charge deserves its strongest form before any verdict. The case for blasphemy runs as follows. The lovers take the vocabulary of a faith neither of them is shown practising, for ends the faith would not license: a kiss at a dance, a secret marriage, a suicide pact executed in a tomb. They make a creature into a saint, a hand into a shrine, a kiss into an absolution, and they do so in a Catholic city whose actual shrines stood within walking distance of the Capulet hall. Juliet herself supplies the prosecution's best word, idolatry, and Romeo himself supplies the prosecution's best image, a man sealing a bargain with death in the diction of a lawful rite. A fuller mapping of how the sacred register collides with the profane across the whole play, scene by scene, belongs to the companion study of [the sacred and the profane in the lovers' language](/2024/10/18/sacred-and-profane-lovers-language/); the diction evidence alone, however, already supports a verdict, and the verdict acquits.

### Which speaker in the play ever accuses the lovers of misusing sacred words?

No speaker does. The charge of blasphemy is never spoken on stage: the Friar rebukes haste and despair, the parents rebuke disobedience, the Prince rebukes the feud, and no voice in Verona names the diction itself as an offence at all.

The accusation comes from outside the play, from readers and from the theological logic the lovers themselves articulate, which is why Juliet's self-diagnosis in the orchard carries such weight in the weighing.

The sharpest form of the charge, sharper than idolatry, concerns penance. The lovers use the sacrament's machinery, confession, absolution, purging, while omitting the sacrament's substance, which is contrition. Romeo confesses a sin at the feast and is absolved by a kiss, yet the sin was the touching itself, committed in the act of confessing it, and no amendment of life follows or is intended. Juliet returns the sin as a courtesy. The whole transaction treats the penitential system as a currency exchange in which debts are pleasantly circulated and nobody repents of anything. A theologian of the period would have a word for absolution pronounced over an unrepented sin by an unordained girl at a dance, and the word would not be devotion. This is the prosecution's best evidence, and it is drawn entirely from the diction, which is why the diction must also supply the defence.

The defence is that the play knows the objection and builds the answer into the vocabulary's later history. The feast's easy absolution is the diction in its first hour, before it has been tested, and the testing consists precisely in sin acquiring the weight the feast denied it. By the tomb, sin is no longer a circulating courtesy. It is a tally on a head, a burden a courteous killer begs not to have increased, a thing priced in corpses. The word's journey from the sonnet to the churchyard is the vocabulary's own doctrine of penance learned the hard way: debts that circulate pleasantly in Act 1 are collected in Act 5, in the same currency, at ruinous rates. A diction that begins by spending sin lightly and ends by counting it accurately has not mocked the penitential system. It has recapitulated, in miniature and at speed, the education the system exists to give. The blasphemy charge assumes the lovers' usage is static, a fixed raid on sacred terms. The glossary shows a usage that matures under punishment, which is the shape not of mockery but of belief acquired at cost.

The acquittal rests on three findings from the diction itself. First, the vocabulary is governed, not looted. A looted vocabulary shows wear in the direction of inflation: terms spent ever more cheaply on ever larger claims. This vocabulary shows the opposite wear. Its terms are defined at first use, corrected at second use, and rationed thereafter, and the two largest available claims, that the beloved is a god and an object of worship, are either fenced by the speaker who approaches them or never made at all. Second, the diction submits to the institution at the decisive point. A private religion content to remain private would have married the pair in the orchard with exchanged vows, and the play shows the lovers refusing that sufficiency: they require holy words from a friar, incorporation by holy church, a sacrament from the real order of things, however compromised its local administration. The private faith seeks public form. Blasphemy, in the period's understanding, was a contempt of the sacred; the lovers' diction is a hunger for the sacred's forms, pressed into service because the forms' custodians could not be approached openly. Third, the diction is answered by the ending's own imagery. The fathers close the play by commissioning golden statues, images of the lovers raised at public expense in the city that forbade them (5.3). Verona ends by doing to the lovers what the lovers' diction had done to Juliet: setting a beloved figure in a shrine and paying it honour. The play's final gesture ratifies the vocabulary's central act as an act the whole culture recognises, once the cost of recognising it has been paid in corpses.

The historical setting of the words sharpens the verdict rather than softening it. For the play's first London audiences, the lovers' vocabulary was already an archaeology. Pilgrimage had been abolished in England two generations earlier, the shrines stripped and their images burned, the palmer's palm reduced to a figure in old romances. To speak of shrines and saints' intercession on a London stage in the 1590s was to speak a language whose institutions had been dismantled within living memory, preserved intact only abroad, in the Catholic Europe to which Verona belonged. The diction thus carried a double charge for its first hearers: it was the language of a forbidden practice and the language of a neighbouring world, exotic and suspect in exactly the measure that it was beautiful. Shakespeare spends that charge deliberately. The lovers' faith is set in the one kind of city where its vocabulary is still legal tender, and the English audience is made to feel both the legality and the distance. When the same audience watched the fathers raise golden images at the close, in a country whose churches had been emptied of images by law, the diction's final vindication arrived with an edge no modern revival can reproduce: the play ends by restoring, in fiction, the very cult of honoured images that the audience's own world had abolished in fact.

One witness complicates the charge from inside the institution, and his testimony should be entered before the verdict. The Friar is the only churchman who hears the lovers' diction at length, and he never hears it as blasphemy. What he hears in Romeo's request for holy words is a marriage to be performed, and his own stated reason for performing it is phrased in his doctrinal diction of opposites reconciled, the alliance that may turn two households' rancour to pure love (2.3). The institution's local representative thus treats the private faith as a client of the public one, irregular in its secrecy but legible in its forms, and his criticisms, when they come, are aimed at haste, despair, and inconstancy, never at the vocabulary. If the diction were the offence the prosecution describes, the play had the perfect officer to name it, standing in a cell with the offenders, and it gave him other charges to bring. His silence on the words is not proof of their innocence, since the Friar is fallible in much else. It is evidence that within the fiction the diction reads, even to a professional, as devotion misdirected by circumstance rather than as mockery of the sacred.

The verdict, then, is that the diction ennobles, and that its blasphemy risk is the price of its precision rather than evidence against it. Sacred words are the only words in Verona calibrated finely enough to register what the lovers take themselves to be doing: approaching another person as an end, under rule, with awe, at cost. The secular vocabularies on offer in the play, the feud's language of honour and injury, the market language of the marriage negotiation, the bawdy language of the street and the nursery, are all calibrated for lesser transactions. The lovers borrow the church's register because it is the sole register in their world whose terms assume that the thing approached is holy and that the approach must be governed. Their heresy, if the word must be used, is jurisdictional rather than doctrinal: they administer the rite themselves, because no one else would administer it for them.

## A Walled Vocabulary: The Sacred Register Against the Bawdy

The diction's distinctness is sharpest at its borders, and the play maintains the border patrol in both directions. Mercutio's conjuration in the orchard scene, summoning Romeo by Rosaline's bright eye, her fine foot, straight leg, and quivering thigh (2.1), is the anti-liturgy in person form: a catalogue of body parts recited as a spell, in the same scene where Romeo's sacred diction is about to address a girl as an angel and a saint. The conjuration fails, and its failure is lexical. Romeo has changed vocabularies; the words that once summoned his performed melancholy cannot reach the man who now speaks of shrines and messengers of heaven. The Nurse presents the subtler border case. She carries the lovers' messages with perfect fidelity and can repeat their arrangements to the syllable, yet her own diction never rises into their register: her sacred words are oaths and asseverations, her register for the marriage itself a brisk compound of errand, rope ladder, and supper. She is the vocabulary's courier and never its citizen, and the play marks the boundary by giving her the wedding night's arrangements and none of the wedding's words.

The apothecary scene in Mantua shows the wall from its far side, in a speech where the sacred vocabulary is wholly absent and its absence is the horror. Romeo, buying the poison, addresses the transaction in the diction of the market inverted: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1). Gold, compounds, selling, law, famine: every term belongs to the economy the feast's liturgy was built to escape, and the scene's one gesture toward moral vocabulary, the judgement that gold out-poisons poison, is spoken as an aside to the audience rather than as an address to any person present. In Verona the lovers' diction had made a market word, fine, serve a rite. In Mantua the market has reclaimed the speaker, and he conducts the most solemn purchase of his life in the dialect of price. The scene is the glossary's negative plate. It shows what Romeo's speech sounds like with the sacred register switched off, and the sound is the treaty's proof that the register had been doing protective work all along: while it lasted, even his sins were courtesies. Without it, his courtesies are felonies.

The wall matters because it converts the diction from a stylistic preference into a social fact. Within the wall, two speakers share a lexicon of sixteen governed terms and a set of rules for spending them. Outside it, Verona speaks in feud, in market, in bawdy, in doctrine, in lament. The lovers' register is set apart from the bawdy around them not by prudery, the sonnet's destination is a kiss and the wedding night's language is frankly desiring, but by governance: the bawdy spends the body's vocabulary without rules of approach, and the lovers' diction is nothing but rules of approach. That is why the register can absorb desire without dissolving into the register of Mercutio's dial and noon. Desire inside the wall is a petitioner. Desire outside it is a noise.

Two more borders mark the wall from outside. The Chorus frames the play in a diction that belongs to neither lover: star-crossed, fate, misadventured pities, a vocabulary of astrology and accident that describes the lovers' lives as weather describes a voyage, from outside and above (Prologue). The frame's words are the ones the cliché about the play has inherited, fate and stars, and the lovers' diction spends five acts quietly refusing them, building a counter-account in which approaches are governed, grants are given, and debts are counted by the persons involved. Even Capulet's great consent speech in the marriage negotiation, for all its courtesy, speaks the wall's outside dialect: will, consent, scope of choice, the terms of a property settlement in which a daughter is the estate under discussion (1.2). Juliet's orchard and cell speeches answer that dialect from inside the wall, where consent is not a clause in a settlement but the saint's granting of a prayer. The same word, consent, prices differently on the two sides of the wall, and the play lets an audience hear both prices.

## What the Critics Make of It: Spurgeon, Lewis and the Editor's Text

The criticism of this diction divides over a single question: whether the vocabulary is a convention the lovers inherit or an instrument they build. Caroline Spurgeon's counting method, set out in Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us, established the factual ground: the sacred images of saints, shrines, pilgrims, and prayer form one of the play's recurrent clusters, gathering at the lovers' meetings and thickening the atmosphere through which the audience perceives them. For Spurgeon, a cluster is evidence of the imagination's habits, a pattern laid down by sensibility rather than by design, and the sacred cluster testifies to a mind steeping this love story in the colouring of devotion. C. S. Lewis, writing on the courtly tradition in The Allegory of Love, supplies the opposing frame. The religion of love, the lover as worshipper, the lady as saint, the kiss as grace, was a codified medieval inheritance, a game with known rules that poets had played for centuries before Shakespeare. On Lewis's account the diction is not built at the feast; it is dealt, from a pack every educated listener recognised, and its blasphemy is formal rather than felt, since no one mistook the game's theology for doctrine.

Lewis's frame deserves its full strength before the adjudication, because it explains features of the diction that no other account touches. The religion of love in the courtly tradition he describes was a system with offices, doctrines, and a calendar: the lady enthroned as the lover's saint and sovereign, the lover enrolled as her pilgrim and servant, his sufferings reckoned as penances, her favour dispensed as grace, her cruelty lamented as a saint's deafness to prayer. A poet inheriting that system inherited its vocabulary as a set, which is why the sonnet's terms arrive in a cluster, mutually implicating, each one summoning the others. Romeo's fluency at the feast, the speed with which shrine summons pilgrim and pilgrim summons prayer, is the fluency of a man reciting a creed he learned from books. On Lewis's reading the blasphemy question dissolves into a question about genre: a convention cannot blaspheme, any more than a chess opening can cheat, since the terms are counters whose sacred value is suspended by agreement for the duration of the game. The reading is elegant and it accounts for the words' arrival. What it cannot account for is their behaviour after arrival, their amendment, rationing, inversion, and eventual collection as debts, since counters in a game do not mature and are not collected. Games end. This vocabulary is still being spent, at compound interest, in a tomb.

The disagreement is genuine and the diction evidence adjudicates it, in parts. Lewis wins on origin. The vocabulary demonstrably pre-exists the lovers: Romeo arrives at the feast already fluent in a neighbouring dialect of it, the Petrarchan stock he had spent on Rosaline, and the sonnet's figures are the tradition's standard counters, pilgrim, saint, shrine, prayer. No reader should claim the pair invent the lexicon. Spurgeon wins on use, and use is where the play's distinction lives. Her cluster evidence, distribution rather than mere presence, shows the sacred terms gathering precisely where the two speakers are together and thinning where they are apart, a pattern the convention alone cannot explain, since a dealt pack would distribute itself by genre rather than by companionship. The glossary's finer count sharpens her point into a rule: the terms are not merely dealt but amended in the dealing, palmer correcting pilgrim, prayer rationed to lips, sin audited as it changes hands. Convention supplies the words. The lovers supply the edits. The play's sacred diction is an inherited liturgy revised by its two celebrants into a rite for two, and criticism that stops at the inheritance misses the revision, which is the artwork.

A third critical instrument refines the adjudication at the level of the syllable. M. M. Mahood's Shakespeare's Wordplay taught readers to treat the period's puns not as decoration but as a mode of thought, two meanings held in one sound so that each glosses the other. The sonnet is her kind of evidence. Palm and palmer chime through Juliet's quatrain until the hand and the pilgrim become one word's two senses, and the kiss the pun finally names; sin and shrine, fine and grant, carry their double homes in law and liturgy through every exchange. On Mahood's account the diction's seriousness is proved by its wordplay rather than despite it: the puns are the mechanism by which the sacred terms are made to transfer their meanings onto the lovers' acts, palm into palmer, prayer into kiss, fine into sin. Her instrument also explains the diction's durability across the play. A metaphor can be dropped when its scene ends. A pun lodged in a common word, palm, sin, grant, travels with the word into every later scene, which is why the tomb can still spend the feast's currency without re-minting it.

Spurgeon's method, finally, needs one correction that the glossary exists to supply, and the correction reconciles her with Lewis more completely than either position manages alone. Her counting was deliberately blind to speakers: an image counted the same in any mouth, since the quarry was the playwright's imagination rather than the characters' intentions. In most plays the blindness costs little. In this one it costs the main event, because the sacred cluster's meaning is its distribution between two mouths and its amendment in transit between them. A count that cannot tell Romeo's pilgrim from Juliet's palmer cannot see the legislation that turns a convention into a rite. The glossary therefore keeps Spurgeon's discipline, the counting, the suspicion of impressionistic claims, and adds the column her method omitted: the speaker. With that column restored, her cluster and Lewis's convention stop competing. The cluster is the convention, counted; the convention is the cluster, inherited. What neither frame alone predicts is the editing, and the editing is the lovers'.

The editor's evidence supports the adjudication. René Weis, editing the play for the Arden Third Series, prints a text in which the sonnet's corrected forms stand stable in the authoritative 1599 quarto tradition: the palmer, the rationed prayer, the granted effect are what the text transmits, not later tidying. His notes trace the same discipline at the level of the single word, glossing profane in its period sense of irreverent, unholy use, which restores Romeo's opening confession to its full legal weight: the first word of the lovers' sacred diction is an admission that the diction might be a crime. A vocabulary whose founding term is its own risk assessment is a vocabulary under government from the start. Weis's text also preserves the boundary the lovers themselves drew, printing Juliet's idolatry line without softening, so that the fence around the diction remains part of the transmitted work. Weis's glosses repay a final word, because an editor's notes are where a diction goes to be either preserved or embalmed, and his preserve. His note on palmer restores the word's technical sense, the pilgrim returned from the Holy Land with the palm as credential, without which Juliet's correction reads as a synonym swap instead of a promotion with legal consequences for the rite. His note on the sonnet's book, in "You kiss by th' book", keeps the triple reference open, prayer book, rule book, and the book of the sonnet itself, where a tidier editor might have closed it to one. Editing of this kind is criticism conducted at the level of the word, and it converges with the glossary's method from the opposite direction: the glossary counts the words in the mouths, the notes restore the words' period weight, and between count and weight the diction stands as the lovers spoke it. The critics' disagreement adjudicated above is, at bottom, a disagreement about whether weight or count tells the truth. The article's position is that each is the other's check.

Between Spurgeon's cluster, Lewis's convention, and the editor's page, the diction emerges as neither spontaneous overflow nor empty inheritance. It is a received form taken seriously enough to be rewritten by the people who needed it.

## The Verdict Restated: Love Made a Faith

The series thesis holds that the cliché surrounding this play hears only romance, and that restoring the religious diction shows love made a faith. The glossary and the scenes support the thesis with a precision the cliché cannot survive. A faith, minimally, is a shared account of what is sacred, a rite for approaching it, a moral law governing the approach, and a community that keeps the account. The lovers' diction supplies the first three in sixteen words: Juliet sacred under the names saint, shrine, and angel; the rite of pilgrim, palmer, prayer, and holy words; the law of sin, trespass, profane, and righteous. The fourth element, community, is the one the world withholds, and the play's tragedy can be stated in diction terms as the story of a complete faith with a congregation of two, administered in secret because the city's congregation would have torn the celebrants apart. The ending grants the community at the price of the celebrants: statues in gold, a city converted into custodians of images it would not harbour alive.

For a reader coming to the play with the glossary in hand, the practical gain is a changed ear. Passages that once sounded like undifferentiated richness resolve into transactions: a word offered, a word corrected, a word spent for the last time. The feast becomes a drafting session, the orchard a constitutional debate, the cell a change of government, the tomb a final audit in which every surviving term is presented and discharged. Teachers reach for this play more than for any other in the canon, and the diction offers the surest way into it, since sixteen words can be tracked by any reader willing to count, and the counting leads, by the play's own design, to every large question the tragedy asks about love, law, worship, and waste.

The play's final line offers the official alternative, and the contrast is the cleanest measure of what the diction achieved. The Prince closes the story by naming it: never was a story of more woe than this of Juliet and her Romeo (5.3). Woe is the state's word for the lovers, the lament register's word, accurate and entirely external, the title under which the case will be filed. Against it stands the lovers' own vocabulary, which never once called what they had woe, or fate, or even love inside the sonnet that founded it, but called it pilgrimage, prayer, granting, sin, and grace. Two accounts of the same events thus leave the theatre with the audience: the filed account, in which two young people were destroyed by a feud and a sequence of mishaps, and the spoken account, in which two people built a faith exact enough to marry in, part in, and die in, using sixteen borrowed words they governed better than the city governed its own. The play's five centuries of afterlife suggest which account audiences have kept. The statues were the fathers' idea. The words were the lovers', and the words are what the world remembers.

That is what the religious vocabulary does for the love. It gives the love a grammar of reverence in which every approach is governed, every debt is counted, every granting is an act of grace rather than a collapse of resistance. It sets the pair apart from every other speaker in Verona, whose vocabularies price, threaten, mock, or mourn, but never consecrate. And it leaves the play's final irony in the mouths of the fathers, who end the story building shrines. The lovers' diction was right about one thing from its first corrected word: what stood between them at the feast was holy ground, and it deserved better custodians than a feud.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch devotion glossary, and what distribution does it chart?

The InsightCrunch devotion glossary is the table in this article listing the sixteen recurring religious terms in the lovers' own speeches, with their speakers, scenes, counts, and functions. The distribution it charts is a concentration and a thinning: nine of the terms are coined or concentrated in the shared sonnet at the feast, the orchard adds four more, the wedding adds the sacramental sense of holy, and after the marriage no new sacred term is coined for the love at all. The old words return only under pressure, altered by what they have survived. The glossary also records who holds each word: Romeo coins the terms of approach, Juliet coins the terms of government, and the shared terms are the ones that passed between them under correction. Read whole, the table is less a word list than a constitution, the founding document of the lovers' private faith, with its articles dated by scene.

### Q: Which religious word opens the lovers' first exchange, and who speaks it?

Romeo speaks it, and the word is profane. His opening line runs "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine" (1.5), so the first sacred term in the lovers' vocabulary is a confession of possible misuse, spoken before shrine, saint, or pilgrim has been named. The order matters. The diction begins by assessing its own risk, admitting that a touch can be a sacrilege, and only then builds the rite that will absolve the touch. A vocabulary whose founding word is a warning about itself is governed from its first syllable.

### Q: Why does Romeo cast his own lips as pilgrims but Juliet as the saint?

Because the conceit assigns each lover the role the ritual requires. Pilgrims travel, petition, and pay; saints remain still, receive devotion, and grant or refuse. Romeo's lips are "two blushing pilgrims" (1.5), travellers arrived at their destination and asking for the appointed offering. Juliet is placed in the saint's position, the still centre of the shrine whose rules govern the approach. The casting also distributes power as the scene understands it: the pilgrim has the motion, but the saint has the jurisdiction, and the sonnet's negotiations all follow from that division.

### Q: Why does Romeo call Juliet "dear saint" a second time in the orchard, and what work is the word doing there that it did not do at the feast?

In the orchard the word has shrunk from a title inside a rite to a form of address inside a confession. At the feast, saint named Juliet's office in a public ceremony of approach, complete with rules about hands and granting. Below the balcony, Romeo says "My name, dear saint, is hateful to myself" (2.2), and the saint is now the confessor receiving an admission about his name. The rite has become intimacy. The word's survival in private, stripped of its ceremonial machinery, shows the diction passing from invention to ordinary use between the pair.

### Q: Why does the lovers' religious vocabulary nearly vanish from their exchanges in Act 3, and what vocabulary replaces it?

Because Act 3 separates the speakers, and the sacred diction is a shared language that needs two voices in one room. The act's dominant vocabularies are haste, time, and fortune: wedding days advanced, hours counted, banishment measured in distances. What sacred diction remains in the act is spoken apart and under inversion. Romeo turns heaven into a taunt in the Friar's cell, and Juliet assigns her faith an address in heaven while her husband stands on earth. The words survive the separation, but they no longer build anything together, because building was always their joint activity. The act makes the point structurally as well: the lovers share no scene between the wedding and the tomb, and a diction whose essence was exchange has no occasion to practise itself. What each speaker does alone with the vocabulary, taunt, store, or spend it as camouflage, measures the distance between a language and a relic.

### Q: Which sacred terms belong to Romeo alone, which to Juliet alone, and which do the two of them share between them?

Romeo alone coins shrine, profane, angel, blessed, and righteous. Juliet alone coins palmer, devotion, idolatry, and gracious. The shared terms are saint, pilgrim, holy, sin, prayer, faith, and heaven, and the sharing is the point: each shared word was offered by one speaker and amended, rationed, or returned by the other before it entered common use. The glossary's distribution shows coinage divided between the pair and legislation exercised jointly, which is why the vocabulary reads as a language with two native speakers rather than as one lover's style imposed on a silent beloved.

### Q: Does either lover ever pray in earnest anywhere in the play, and what does the play do with the word pray instead?

Neither lover is ever shown at prayer in earnest. The play spends the word instead on the lovers' negotiation with each other: lips that they must use in prayer, a petition granted for prayers' sake, an effect taken from a prayer's success (1.5). Prayer in their diction is the form of address between petitioner and saint, transferred from the chapel to the beloved. The single institutional prayer-space they enter, the Friar's cell, is used for marriage, counsel, and conspiracy rather than for devotion, which confirms the transfer: in this play, prayer is something the lovers do to each other, in words, under rules.

### Q: Why is devotion the first religious word Juliet speaks, and what does she use it to correct?

Devotion is her instrument for correcting Romeo's apology. He has called his hand unworthy and his touch a profanation; she answers that the hand "mannerly devotion shows in this" (1.5), ruling that the touch was an act of disciplined worship rather than an offence requiring pardon. The choice of word is characteristic. Devotion names worship as a practice with manners, a governed activity, and Juliet reaches for it before any term of transport or ecstasy. Her first sacred word establishes the principle her whole management of the vocabulary follows: the rite is legitimate when it is disciplined.

### Q: What does the word shrine claim about Juliet that the word saint does not?

Shrine claims place; saint claims personhood. A shrine is a location where holiness resides and to which journeys are made, passive, fixed, and approached under rules set by custom rather than by the place itself. A saint is a person, with conduct, intercession, and the power to grant or refuse. Romeo's first figure makes Juliet a destination, "this holy shrine" (1.5); Juliet's answering figure makes her class a company of agents, saints who have hands, who do not move, who grant for prayers' sake. The exchange of the terms is the scene in miniature: his geography, her jurisprudence. It also sets the pattern for the whole vocabulary's career, in which Romeo's figures consistently locate the sacred somewhere, a shrine, a heaven, a tomb, while Juliet's consistently staff it with persons who have rules, rights, and the power of refusal.

### Q: How does the Friar's religious diction differ from the lovers', and why does the play keep the two vocabularies apart?

The Friar's diction is doctrinal where the lovers' is devotional. He speaks in systems: grace and rude will encamped in man as in herbs (2.3), philosophy as an armour against adversity, blessings catalogued like stores. His words describe the world from above it. The lovers' words address a person within reach of them. The play keeps the vocabularies apart because their functions differ and because the tragedy turns on the difference: doctrine can marry the pair and can plot for them, but it cannot console them, since consolation in this play is an act of address, and address is the lovers' register, not the Friar's.

### Q: Why does Romeo's heaven language turn to mockery in the Friar's cell in Act 3 Scene 3, and what does the Friar answer it with?

Banishment has inverted the word's meaning for him. In the orchard, heaven was the home of the messenger whose likeness Juliet bore; in the cell, "Heaven is here / Where Juliet lives" (3.3), and every unworthy thing may look on her while Romeo may not, so heaven names his exclusion rather than his approach. The Friar answers with doctrine, a catalogue of blessings and an exhortation to philosophy. The answer fails in the scene because it speaks the wrong vocabulary: Romeo's grief is phrased in the diction of address, and only an answering address, which the Friar cannot supply, could have met it on its own ground.

### Q: Why does Juliet reach for fortune's vocabulary, not faith's, at the dawn parting in Act 3 Scene 5?

Because the parting has moved the lovers from the world their diction governs into the world it cannot. At the window she calls her soul ill-divining and pictures Romeo as one dead in the bottom of a tomb (3.5), the language of omen and fortune rather than of rite and granting. Her one faith word in the scene assigns faith an address in heaven, away from the earth where her husband stands, which is a storing of the word rather than a spending of it. Fortune is the vocabulary of events no rite can negotiate with, and the parting is the first event of that kind.

### Q: Why does the word angel live only in the orchard scene in Romeo's mouth, never at the feast or in the tomb?

Because angel is the diction's word for a beloved who can be seen but not touched. At the feast Juliet stood within reach, and the vocabulary was tactile: hands, palms, lips, shrines approached and saints petitioned. Below the balcony she is overhead and out of reach, "a winged messenger of heaven" (2.2), and the register ascends to match. In the tomb she is within reach again, terribly, and the diction returns to the tactile and the legal, lips as doors, a kiss as a sealed bargain. Angel belongs to the interval between the two reachings, the scene of pure sight. Its single occurrence is also a measure of the diction's economy: a lesser vocabulary would have repeated the compliment until it dulled, whereas this one spends the word once, at the exact geometry that requires it, and retires it when the geometry changes.

### Q: What work does the word despair do inside the sonnet's prayer, and why do the lovers never spend it again?

Despair is the stake that gives the sonnet's petition its urgency. Romeo's line runs "grant thou lest faith turn to despair" (1.5): if the saint refuses the prayer, faith itself does not merely cool but converts into its opposite. The word names the failure condition of the whole sacred vocabulary, the state in which the private faith collapses. The lovers never spend it again because the play reserves its reality for action rather than for their diction. When despair actually arrives, in cells and churchyards, it is enacted in poison, daggers, and haste, while the word itself remains locked inside the sonnet where it was first priced.

### Q: Why do modern productions and translations struggle with the lovers' religious words, and what tends to be cut or softened first?

The struggle is that the diction depends on a lived practice its audience may no longer share. Pilgrims at shrines, palmers with palms, sins purged and trespasses urged, were working institutions for the first listeners and are footnotes for later ones, so the sonnet's logic can sound like decoration instead of negotiation. Productions tend to soften the theological precision first: palmer becomes a generic pilgrim, purged loses its sacramental sense, idolatry is played as a compliment rather than a diagnosis. Each softening costs the scene its governing mechanism, since the kiss persuades only because the words around it keep their exact institutional weight. A production that restores the weight, by staging the sonnet as the rite its diction describes, discovers that the scene needs no embellishment: the negotiation is the romance, and the audience watches two people build a church government in fourteen lines because the words are allowed to mean what they say.

### Q: Why does Juliet's final speech trade the language of saints and shrines for the language of instruments and vessels?

Because the shared language has lost its second speaker. Waking in the tomb, Juliet addresses a cup, a drop, a dagger: "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3). The sacred diction was built between two voices, offered and amended across exchanges, and with Romeo dead there is no answering voice to govern or receive it. Her diction falls back on the vocabulary of things that can be handled alone, containers and blades, because instruments require no congregation. The fall in register is the tragedy's final measurement of what the shared vocabulary had been worth.

### Q: Which single line best shows Juliet correcting Romeo's sacred diction instead of echoing it?

"Ay, pilgrim, lips that they must use in prayer" (1.5). Romeo has asked whether saints and palmers have lips, pressing the shrine logic toward a kiss. Juliet concedes the anatomy and rations the function: lips exist, and their appointed use is prayer. She also demotes him in passing, palmer back to pilgrim, withdrawing the promotion her previous speech had granted. In eleven words she accepts his vocabulary, reverses his conclusion, and resets his status inside the rite. Every later correction she makes, of oaths, of swearing by the moon, of despair itself, follows the pattern this line establishes.

### Q: How can an essay use the devotion glossary to argue that the lovers' faith is built from words before it is built from deeds?

By following the counts in sequence. The glossary shows the vocabulary complete in outline by the end of the first meeting: shrine, saint, pilgrim, prayer, sin, and faith are all in circulation before the lovers have exchanged names, let alone vows. Every later deed, the marriage, the parting, the deaths, is then conducted in terms the words had already defined, holy words closing hands, sin tallied on a head, a kiss sealed as righteous. An essay can therefore argue that the faith's doctrines precede its acts, that the lovers do in the tomb only what their diction had legislated at the feast.

### Q: What does the lovers' diction owe to the language of the prayer book and the marriage service, and where does it break from both?

The debt is structural. Petition and granting, confession and purging, the joining of hands under holy words, the incorporation of two in one: each element follows the shape of the period's liturgy, in which worship proceeds by prescribed exchange and marriage is effected by spoken form. The break is jurisdictional. The prayer book's exchanges are administered by an authorised minister before witnesses; the lovers administer their rite themselves, in a crowded hall and an orchard at night, borrowing the forms while bypassing the office. Their wedding in the cell repairs the jurisdiction for one scene, which is why that scene's diction sits closest to the service's own. The debt runs the other way as well: the service lends the lovers its forms, and the lovers return the forms charged with a dramatic life the bare rite cannot supply, which is one reason the play's wedding, two speeches and a warning, outweighs many a fully staged ceremony in the period's drama.

### Q: How does the lovers' religious diction finally judge Verona's official religion?

It judges the institution by outperforming it at its own work. The official church of Verona marries the lovers in secret, lends its cell to a conspiracy, converts a wedding into a funeral on a day's notice, and arrives at the tomb with explanations. The lovers' sixteen governed words, by contrast, name the sacred accurately, approach it under rule, count its debts, and keep its faith to the death. The diction's verdict is delivered in the play's last image, when the city raises golden statues to the pair: Verona's religion ends by enshrining the two celebrants of the private rite it never managed to serve.
