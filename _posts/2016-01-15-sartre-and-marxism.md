Jean-Paul Sartre is the most famous philosopher in this series, and for many readers the most surprising name in it. Sartre built his reputation on a philosophy of radical freedom, on the claim that a human being is condemned to invent himself without excuse or blueprint, and that philosophy, existentialism, is usually filed in the mind as the opposite of any doctrine that explains people by their class position. Yet from the end of the Second World War until his death in 1980, Sartre moved steadily toward Marxism, described it as the unsurpassable philosophy of the age, wrote a vast methodological treatise to repair it, and broke with friends, parties, and countries in its name. He never joined the French Communist Party, whose orthodoxy he spent decades attacking from the left. His position was stranger and more interesting than membership: he held that Marxism was true and that the Marxists of his day had stopped doing the work that made it true.

That double position is the subject of this profile. Sartre's complaint against the Marxism around him was precise, and this article names it the mediation charge: his objection was not that the categories of Marxism were wrong but that its practitioners had stopped explaining how a general structure produces a particular person, skipping the chain of mediations between a class position and a finished human life. Around that charge he built the most ambitious attempt of the century to fuse a philosophy of freedom with a theory of history, the two volumes, one finished and one abandoned, of the Critique of Dialectical Reason. Readers who want the tradition he worked inside mapped as a whole can start with the series guide to [Western Marxism](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/); this profile concentrates on the man who tried to give that tradition back its individuals.

## Sartre's road from existentialism to Marxism

The young Sartre was not a Marxist, and said so. The philosophy of Being and Nothingness, published in 1943, worked with an almost empty stage: a consciousness, a world that resists it, other consciousnesses whose look turns the self into an object. History barely appears in that book, and society appears mainly as a threat to authenticity. Sartre had read Marx as a student and admired the historian while rejecting the determinism he found in the doctrine's official versions. What changed him was not a book but an occupation. The war, the Resistance, the prison camp, and then the Liberation taught a generation of French intellectuals that private freedom was a luxury arranged by public events, and Sartre's postwar turn began as a moral discovery before it became a theory: a writer is situated, his words are acts, and neutrality between oppressor and oppressed is itself a choice with consequences.

The journal he founded with Simone de Beauvoir and Maurice Merleau-Ponty in 1945, Les Temps modernes, became the vehicle of that discovery, and its pages carried his slow approach to Marxism through the late 1940s: essays on literature as commitment, on the Jewish question, on colonialism in Indochina and Algeria, each one pushing the philosophy of freedom toward material conditions it had not been built to carry. The approach was not smooth. He quarrelled with the Communist Party over its cultural policing and its treatment of writers, and the party's intellectuals returned the hostility with interest, denouncing existentialism as a bourgeois philosophy of despair. For several years the two camps circled each other. The reconciliation, when it came in 1952, came through politics rather than philosophy: the arrest of a communist leader, police raids, American power in Europe, and the Korean War convinced Sartre that in a world divided into camps, the refusal to choose was a choice for the stronger camp. He became the party's most famous fellow traveller without ever taking its card.

### Was Sartre a Marxist?

From the mid-1950s he described Marxism as the unsurpassable philosophy of the age while arguing it had become sclerotic. He never joined a communist party, broke with the party over Hungary, and proposed existentialism as a temporary supplement to restore Marxism's grasp of individuals.

The answer depends on what the question is measuring, and the profile keeps the three measures separate. By allegiance, he was a Marxist of a specific and public kind: he held that the philosophy of Marx expressed the historical world that had produced it so completely that any attempt to go beyond it, before that world itself was transformed, would fall back into pre-Marxist ways of thinking. By membership, he was nothing of the sort, and the distinction mattered to him: the card would have obliged him to defend positions he had not examined, and he regarded unexamined defence as the exact disease he was diagnosing. By method, he was a dissenter inside the house, arguing that the doctrine's official custodians had converted a method of inquiry into a filing system. The three measures explain the odd shape of his career: a man perpetually described as Marxism's most famous recruit and its most embarrassing ally at the same time.

### What did Sartre mean by calling Marxism unsurpassable?

He meant that a philosophy stays unsurpassable while the world that produced it endures, because it expresses that world's self-understanding at the deepest level available. Attempts to leap past Marxism while capitalism persisted would relapse into older idealisms. Only a transformed society could father a successor philosophy equal to its own conditions.

The claim is often quoted as a slogan of loyalty, but in its setting it is a piece of historical theory, and a demanding one. Sartre took from Marx the idea that philosophies are not refuted the way propositions are refuted; they are surpassed when the praxis that sustained them is surpassed, when the material world that made them plausible has been remade. Kantianism did not fall to a better argument alone; it faded as the bourgeois world that needed its categories gave way to another. On that logic, the long line of thinkers announcing that Marxism had been refuted were making a category error, mistaking the refutation of particular theses for the death of the philosophy that framed the questions. The claim also cut against the communists who quoted it with pleasure, since it implied that Marxism would one day be surpassed too, and that treating it as a finished catechism was a way of killing it in advance. A philosophy that cannot die cannot inquire; Sartre's compliment contained his whole accusation.

### Why did Sartre describe existentialism as an enclave?

Because he had demoted his own philosophy to the rank of an ideology: a partial, parasitical system living on the margin of a knowledge it could not replace. Existentialism survived inside Marxism as a corrective, guarding the individual project until Marxism recovered the means to explain singular lives and absorbed the enclave back into itself.

The demotion was deliberate and cost him standing among his own followers, many of whom had built careers on existentialism as a complete worldview. Sartre insisted it was nothing of the kind. A worldview tells you what the world contains; existentialism, on his later account, contained almost nothing: a method for following a life, a refusal to let categories eat persons, a set of questions about how structures are lived from inside. Everything with content, the economy, the class struggle, the period, the mode of production, came from Marxism. The enclave image also fixed the terms of the surrender: the supplement was temporary, and its success would be measured by its disappearance. When Marxist anthropology learned again to pass from the epoch to the childhood, from the class position to the singular style of a life, existentialism would have no separate work left. That this absorption never occurred, and that the enclave is still inhabited long after the empire around it changed shape, is one of the ironies his readers are left to manage.

His route into the tradition also ran through its humanist wing, since the Marx he championed was the philosopher of praxis and self-creation rather than the economist of iron laws; the series account of [Marxist humanism](/2011/07/15/marxist-humanism-explained/) sets out the current he joined and the uses he made of it. What he added to that current was a demand it had not faced so squarely: humanism had celebrated man in general, while Sartre wanted the machinery by which this man, in this family, in this decade, came to be the one who wrote those books and signed those manifestos.

## The unpolitical years: Berlin, phenomenology, and the education of an eye

The war educated a man who had spent the previous decade declining the education. Sartre's thirties were the decade of the pure philosopher: the year in Berlin in 1933 and 1934, reading Husserl at the French Institute while the republic across the street was dismantled, a season he later recalled with self-lacerating clarity, since the philosophy of consciousness had given him, he said, no instruments for seeing what was in front of his face and he had not looked for others. The apricot cocktail of legend, the evening when Raymond Aron, returned from Berlin before him, pointed at his glass and remarked that one could make a philosophy of it, belongs to the same years and to the same innocence: phenomenology arrived in France as a liberation from the Sorbonne's idealism, a licence to describe the world in its concreteness, and its first French master spent the decade describing, in the essays on the emotions, on the imagination, and in the melancholy masterpiece Nausea, a consciousness alone with a world that kept slipping its meanings. The political record of those years is a blank he never forgave himself: no party, no campaign, a single signature on a petition almost forgotten, and a posture toward the approaching catastrophe that he would later classify under his own most damning category, bad faith at the scale of a generation. The confession matters to this profile because it governs the ferocity of everything after: the fellow travelling, the manifestos, the refusal of every honour, can be read as the instalments of a debt contracted in the thirties by a man who had been given the finest philosophical education in Europe and had used it to contemplate apricot cocktails while the continent armed. Marxism, when it came, came among other things as the discipline that would make such innocence impossible for him again, and the mediation charge's impatience with intellectuals who file and forget has its autobiographical root in a Berlin winter.

## A prisoner, a resister, a witness: the war that made the Marxist

The conversion began in a prison camp. Mobilised in 1939 as a meteorological soldier, a posting arranged by a myopia so severe that the army's doctors doubted he could aim a rifle, Sartre was captured in June 1940 and spent nine months in a stalag near Trier, an experience he later described, to the discomfort of purists, as among the most formative of his life: for the first time the philosopher of solitary consciousness lived inside a collectivity, acted in its amateur theatricals, wrote and staged a play for his fellow prisoners at Christmas, and discovered the warmth of the fused group a decade before he had the concept for it. Released in 1941, in circumstances his biographers still debate, he returned to occupied Paris, taught in lycées, co-founded a resistance group whose name, Socialism and Freedom, announced the two poles of his future politics, watched it dissolve for want of exactly the organisation it lacked, wrote for the clandestine press, and produced, in The Flies in 1943, a play about Orestes that Parisian audiences under the occupation understood perfectly as a call to assume one's acts without remorse. The sequence matters because it is the biography of his concepts in embryo: seriality in the failed resistance circle, fusion in the camp's theatre, the practico-inert in a France administered by others, and, over it all, the discovery that a man's situation is not the scenery of his freedom but its very substance.

The Liberation completed the education. Paris in 1944 and 1945 was a city in which intellectuals were judged as combatants, in which the purge trials asked writers to answer for their sentences as for deeds, and in which a philosopher who had spent the war years becoming famous discovered that fame itself was a political fact with political obligations. Sartre's essay of those months on the republic of silence, his insistence that never had the French been freer than under the occupation since every word then carried the weight of an act, is sometimes quoted as paradox-mongering; read against the biography it is a thesis about the practico-inert of language under terror, and the founding document of the committed literature he was about to proclaim. The war had taught him the lesson the Critique would systematise: that there is no position outside history from which a consciousness might contemplate it, that the contemplator is himself a deposit of the history he contemplates, and that freedom, to be anything at all, must be the name for what a situated being does with a situation it did not choose. A Marxism that could not say that would never have held him; the one he built was designed, from the first page, to say nothing else.

## The philosophy he brought with him: freedom, facticity, and bad faith

To see what Sartre thought he was adding to Marxism, a reader needs the shape of what he already had. The existentialism of Being and Nothingness rests on a division of being into two kinds: the in-itself, dense, self-identical, simply what it is, and the for-itself, consciousness, which is what it is not and is not what it is, a nothingness at the heart of being that makes negation, time, and freedom possible. A man, for the early Sartre, is the being through whom nothingness comes into the world: he can always say no to his situation, imagine it otherwise, and project himself toward a future that his past does not dictate. Freedom in that book is not a possession but a condemnation, since the projects by which a man escapes his facticity are themselves chosen and must be owned without excuse. Bad faith is the name for the excuses: the waiter who plays at being a waiter as though the role exhausted him, the woman who leaves her hand in a suitor's hand while pretending not to notice, each fleeing the anguish of freedom by impersonating a thing. Facticity is the counterweight, the given that freedom did not choose and cannot abolish: a body, a birthplace, a class, a past, the existence of others whose look fixes the self from outside as an object in their world.

Two features of that philosophy matter for everything that followed. The first is its treatment of the Other. In Being and Nothingness the encounter with another consciousness is a fall: the look steals my world, makes me an object, and conflict is the meaning of the relation before any history has occurred. Readers who later met the Critique's warm pages on the fused group were reading a philosophy that had reversed its own foundations, since the group in fusion is precisely an experience of the other as the same, and Sartre knew the distance he had travelled; the later work does not retract the look but historicises it, arguing that conflict between consciousnesses is mediated, and usually manufactured, by scarcity and the practico-inert rather than inscribed in consciousness as such. The second feature is the project, the original choice by which a life organises itself toward its ends. The project survived the Marxist turn intact and became the hinge of the whole synthesis: in the Critique and the biographies, the project is what a person does with what conditions have done to him, the forward movement that the regressive analysis must recover if it is not to dissolve him. Sartre's famous summary of the position in his later years, that men are what they make of what they are made, is the early philosophy and the later Marxism in a single sentence, and it is the formula under which the two halves of his career can be read as one inquiry.

## A doctrine goes public: the humanism lecture and committed literature

Existentialism became a public doctrine, and Sartre a public man, through a lecture delivered in a packed Paris hall in October 1945 and published as Existentialism Is a Humanism. The lecture was a defence against the doctrine's first critics, communist and Catholic alike, who had charged it with quietism, despair, and bourgeois individualism, and it answered by presenting existentialism as a philosophy of responsibility: if man is what he makes of himself, then in choosing himself he chooses an image of man for all, and the anguish of that responsibility is the opposite of despair. The text is a simplification Sartre later regretted in places, and its slogan that existence precedes essence became the movement's passport and its caricature. But the lecture matters to this profile because it shows the direction of his thought before the Marxist turn was complete: the philosophy of freedom was already straining toward a doctrine of man in society, and its author was already accepting the role, which the party intellectuals found so suspect, of the philosopher as public combatant.

The companion piece is What Is Literature?, serialised in his journal in 1947, where prose is defined as action and the writer as a man who has chosen to reveal the world and to implicate his reader in its transformation. Committed literature, the doctrine the essay founded, held that the writer's freedom is addressed to the freedom of others and is therefore political in its essence: to write is to take a position in the city, and silence is also a position. The essay's blind spots were noted early, its exemption of poetry from commitment, its confidence that prose could be a transparent instrument, and its author's own later work complicated both. Its place here is as the bridge across which the existentialist walked toward Marxism: a writer who believes that words are acts, that his situation is his material, and that his reader's freedom is the stakes of his work has already accepted the premises from which the fellow travelling and the manifestos would follow. What the bridge did not yet contain was the theory of the structures his acts would meet, and supplying that theory is the Critique's whole labour.

## The portrait of the anti-Semite: the method before Marxism

His first great exercise in the analysis of a social type was not about class at all. Anti-Semite and Jew, written in 1944 and published in 1946, argues that the anti-Semite is not a man with false opinions about Jews but a man who has chosen a whole mode of being: he flees the anguish of freedom into the solidity of passion, preferring a hatred that explains everything to a reason that obliges him to think, and he constitutes the Jew as the object his choice requires. The book's most famous turn reverses the expected order of explanation: it is the anti-Semite who makes the Jew, since the Jew, in the analysis Sartre develops, is a being defined by the regard of others, a man whom other men hold to be a Jew and who must live that imposed definition as his situation, whether by flight, by defiance, or by the inauthentic effort to be universal by erasing himself. Whatever its limits, the essay is the mediation method in action before the method was Marxist: a social category is neither a nature nor an economic reflex but a situation totalised by freedoms on both sides of it, and the analysis moves without embarrassment between the psychology of the hater and the structures, property, nationalism, the fear of dispossession, that supply his hatred its materials.

The criticisms arrived early and have never been withdrawn, and accuracy requires them at strength. Jewish readers and scholars objected, first, that a Jew defined entirely by the anti-Semite's regard is a Jew without a history, a culture, a religion, or an interior of his own: the book's Jew has no Torah, no languages, no jokes that are not reactions, and the analysis that set out to destroy the anti-Semite's fiction ends by agreeing with it that the Jew is a function of other people's eyes. Second, that the typology flatters the analyst's own camp: the democrat and the authentic Jew are drawn thinner than the anti-Semite, who gets all the psychology. Sartre, to his credit, absorbed part of the correction in life if not in a rewritten text: his later record, support for the existence of Israel alongside insistence on the rights of the Palestinians, visits to both peoples in 1967, and a refusal to the end to let either cause confiscate his judgement, is the conduct of a man who had learned that the defined also define themselves. The essay remains in print as the clearest short specimen of his instrument at work, and as a warning its author would have endorsed: a method that follows the look of others can forget to ask what the looked-at were doing while they were being looked at, and the forgetting is a mediation skipped.

## The collective he actually ran: Les Temps modernes as an institution

Sartre theorised institutions from the experience of running one. Les Temps modernes, founded in 1945 with a manifesto that remains the charter of French engaged literature, was for thirty-five years the nearest thing his century produced to a one-man counter-university: the journal that published the first extracts of Beauvoir's Second Sex and of Fanon's work in France, that broke with the party's cultural line and then with the party, that carried the campaigns on Algeria and Vietnam, and whose editorial committee, Merleau-Ponty and Camus lost along the way, Beauvoir constant, a rotating cast of younger editors successively adopted and outgrown, enacted in miniature every stage of the group's life cycle its director was describing. It had its fusion, the heroic founding under the Liberation's afterglow; its pledge, exacted in the currency of signatures and shared positions; its organisation, the monthly discipline of the issue; and its institutional old age, in which the journal's line was awaited as an exigency, a practico-inert authority in the Parisian field, by readers who experienced its judgements as Sartre himself said readers experience prices. He was not blind to the irony, and the late interviews treat the journal with the same mixture of loyalty and disenchantment he applied to the Critique: an instrument that had made certain sentences possible and certain others unprintable, a group that survived its founder's intentions the way institutions do. For the researcher, the run of the journal is the single richest archive of the whole enterprise, since it preserves the positions in their first, dated, polemical form, before the treatises cooled them into doctrine.

## The mediation charge: what Marxism had stopped doing

The charge is stated most sharply in the methodological essay Sartre published in 1957 and then placed at the head of the Critique, the text known in English as Search for a Method. Marxism, he argued there, had once been a living inquiry and had become a lazy one. Its concepts, which in Marx's hands were open instruments for following a process, had hardened into a priori labels applied from outside. The lazy Marxist knows in advance what he will find: the writer's class origin is established, the work is filed as the expression of that origin, and the inquiry closes before it has opened. Sartre's name for the operation was liquidation: the particular is dissolved into the general, the event into the structure, the man into his class, and nothing remains to be explained because everything has been classified. Against this he set a single demand, that explanation must pass through the mediations, the long chain of intermediate terms, family, childhood, schooling, profession, the available instruments of an epoch, through which a structure actually reaches a person and a person answers back.

His illustration of the failure has become the most quoted sentence in the essay. Valéry, he wrote, is a petit-bourgeois intellectual, but not every petit-bourgeois intellectual is Valéry. The first half of the sentence is the classification, true and nearly empty; the second half is the whole of biography, everything that separates the writer of the Cahiers from the thousands who shared his class position and wrote nothing. A Marxism that cannot travel from the first half to the second has, on Sartre's charge, abandoned explanation at the exact point where explanation begins. It can say what a man represents; it cannot say why he is the one who represents it, why the representation took this style, this timing, this strange personal shape. The mediation charge is the name this profile gives to that objection, and it is the standard by which Sartre asked to be judged himself, since his own later work, above all the enormous study of Flaubert, was an attempt to pay the debt he had charged to others.

The charge had a second edge, directed at the theory of knowledge inside official Marxism. The doctrine of reflection, in which consciousness copies a reality already complete without it, made the knower vanish from the known; Sartre countered that human beings know the world by working on it, and that any Marxism without a theory of praxis, of action that transforms its materials and is transformed by them, would keep producing the same scholastic results. He was equally severe on the dialectics of nature, the attempt to read dialectical laws directly off the physical world, which he regarded as a metaphysical decoration the doctrine could shed without loss. These positions placed him at odds with the party philosophers on nearly every front at once, which is why the essay reads less like an application for admission than like a bill of indictment served on the house he was entering.

It is worth pausing on the fairness of the charge, since a profile that repeats it must also weigh it. Sartre was attacking the Marxism of the French party schools and the Soviet manuals, and against those targets the accusation of laziness lands: the manuals did classify before they inquired, and their literary criticism did move from origin to verdict in a paragraph. But Marxism as a whole contained other practices, historians who followed mediations patiently, sociologists who built the intermediate terms he demanded, and Sartre's polemic, aimed at the custodians, sometimes reads as though the filing system were the entire tradition. His defenders reply that he knew this, that the essay praises Marx's own concrete analyses precisely for their patience with mediations, and that the laziness he named was a historical degeneration of a method rather than the method itself. That reply is consistent with everything else he wrote on the subject. The mediation charge is a charge against a church in the name of its founder, which is why it could be pressed by a man who never doubted the founder.

## The biographies before the method: Baudelaire, Genet, and the named child

The method of 1957 was a theory built on a practice, and the practice was biography. Sartre published his study of Baudelaire in 1947, a short fierce book that read the poet's life as a single project: the child who lost his father and then his mother's exclusive love to a remarriage chose, on Sartre's telling, to become the man who would judge himself before an internal tribunal for the rest of his life, converting an inflicted wound into an elected destiny. The book is unfair to Baudelaire in ways scholars have catalogued, and its psychology runs ahead of its documents, but its form is the point: an entire existence interpreted as a free totalisation of given conditions, neither deduced from them nor detachable from them. Five years later came Saint Genet, the vast study of Jean Genet, thief, convict, and playwright, which Sartre placed at the head of Genet's collected works and which remains the most dazzling performance of the method before the method existed. Genet, on Sartre's account, was made a thief before he chose theft: caught as a child with his hand in a drawer, named by the word of respectable people in a moment that fixed his being from outside, he took the imposed identity and willed it, deciding to be what crime and the look of others had made of him, and then, in a second conversion, to make of that willed evil a work of art.

The Genet book is where a reader can watch the mediation charge being born. Between the foster child's social position, an abandoned infant raised by peasants under the eye of public assistance, and the finished writer of The Thief's Journal, there yawns everything the label cannot cross: the naming scene, the prison eroticism, the slow transmutation of abjection into style. Sartre crosses it by following the project through its metamorphoses, and the six hundred pages are his proof, offered a decade before the Critique, that a Marxist-flavoured explanation of a man must be a biography or be nothing. That the proof's subject was a saint of transgression rather than a worker or a statesman is characteristic: Sartre's Marxism was worked out on writers, on Flaubert and Genet and Baudelaire and Mallarmé, and its concepts bear the marks of that laboratory. A theory of mediation built from the lives of authors will be strong exactly where the tradition was weak, on style, vocation, and the singular shape of a life, and correspondingly thin where the tradition was strong, on the anonymous processes of production that no biography can narrate. The profile returns to that asymmetry when the strongest objection is weighed.

## Beauvoir and the method's other practitioner

Any account of Sartre's method that confines itself to Sartre understates the evidence, because the method's most consequential application in its own decade was written by Simone de Beauvoir. The Second Sex, published in 1949, two years after the Baudelaire study and a decade before the Critique, performs on the condition of women exactly the operation the mediation charge demands: it refuses both the biological filing of woman as a nature and the economic filing of her as a reflex of property, and instead follows the mediations, the girl's formation in the family, the myths that await her, the economic and marital structures she enters, the literature that teaches her to read herself, until a singular situation stands explained as the totalisation of all of them by a freedom that did not choose them. Her formula for the result, that one is not born but rather becomes a woman, is the progressive regressive method in a single clause, and Sartre, who encouraged the book and learned from its composition, acknowledged the companionship of the enterprise in terms his readers sometimes forget when they treat the method as a solo instrument. The partnership matters methodologically as well as biographically. The two philosophers read each other's drafts for fifty years, and the existential Marxism that emerged from that household was from the start a two-handed instrument, its theory of the situation sharpened on the case where situation presses hardest and is most easily mistaken for nature. Feminist scholarship has since divided over the inheritance, some claiming Beauvoir as the method's true founder in practice, others resisting the subordination of her originality to his system; the division itself testifies to the fact this section exists to record, that the mediation charge had two practitioners, and that the one's most famous book is unintelligible without the other's most famous problem.

## Existential psychoanalysis: the unconscious refused

The biographical method carries a declared enemy inside psychology itself. Sartre rejected the Freudian unconscious from his earliest work, on a ground that never shifted: a censor that knows what it represses, in order to repress it, smuggles consciousness back into the machinery that was supposed to replace it, and the patient's divisions are better explained by bad faith, a project of self-concealment conducted by a consciousness that knows what it is doing in the mode of not knowing it. In place of the unconscious he proposed existential psychoanalysis, an interpretation of a life as the progressive decipherment of its fundamental project, the original choice of being that each act expresses and that the analyst, like the biographer, recovers by regressing from the acts to their common style. The Flaubert study is the method's cathedral: the novelist's passivity, his epilepsy-like crisis, his cult of art, his hatred of the bourgeoisie he fed on, are read as metamorphoses of a childhood choice made under the pressure of a father's contempt and a brother's precedence, a choice the subject lived without ever formulating.

The refusal of the unconscious is the point where his Marxist readers and his psychoanalytic ones divide most sharply, and the profile should mark the cost honestly. What the refusal buys is responsibility: no depth excuses the surface, and the mediation between a childhood and a work remains a human mediation, owned by its subject. What it costs is a theory of everything in mental life that genuinely operates without the subject's complicity, symptom, compulsion, the return of what was never chosen in any sense a child could own; and the Flaubert book, precisely where it is most brilliant, sometimes pays the cost visibly, bending recalcitrant material toward the project it has posited. Orthodox Marxists added a second charge: that existential psychoanalysis, whatever its disclaimers, reinstates the individual as the unit of history at the moment the doctrine requires classes. Sartre's rejoinder is the singular universal once more: the project is never private property, since its materials are an epoch's, and to follow one life to the bottom is to find the epoch waiting there, totalised. Whether the rejoinder answers the charge or restates the wager is left, in this profile as in the literature, to the reader's judgement of the Flaubert volumes themselves.

## Search for a Method and the two movements of understanding

The remedy Sartre proposed was a method, and he gave it a double name: the progressive regressive method. Its two movements answer the two halves of the mediation charge. The regressive movement works backward and downward from the finished object, a book, a career, a political act, toward the conditions that made it possible, and it keeps descending: from the work to the man's situation, from the situation to his class as it stood in that decade, from the class to his family, from the family to the childhood in which the fundamental project was laid down under pressures the child did not choose. Regression alone, however, produces only an archaeology, a person explained by his strata. The progressive movement then reverses direction and follows the life forward, watching the project take up those conditions, bend them, fail against them, and produce the singular object that no inventory of conditions predicted. Explanation, for Sartre, is the controlled oscillation between the two movements, each correcting the other's characteristic lie: regression without progression dissolves the person into his causes, progression without regression dissolves the causes into a myth of pure freedom.

### How does the progressive regressive method actually proceed?

It begins from the finished thing, a text or a life as completed, and regresses through its conditions: epoch, class, family, childhood, each layer dated and documented. Then it turns and progresses forward, following the person's project as it reworks those conditions into a singular outcome that the conditions alone never entailed.

The method was not invented from nothing. Sartre borrowed its skeleton from the sociologist Henri Lefebvre, who had sketched a regressive progressive procedure for rural history, and from his own earlier biographical practice in studies of Baudelaire and Genet, where he had tried to read a life as a single sustained answer to an original situation. What the essay of 1957 added was the claim that this biographical procedure was not a literary indulgence but the missing organ of Marxism itself: the doctrine possessed a theory of epochs and a theory of classes, and lacked a theory of the passage between them and the person, which is where all its explanations silently failed. He called the object of the completed inquiry a singular universal, a life that is entirely of its epoch and entirely itself, universal because the epoch is totalised in it, singular because the totalisation bears one man's signature. The phrase is the method's promise in miniature.

Critics of the method, including sympathetic ones, have noted how rarely the full oscillation can be performed. The regressive movement is hungry for documents: letters, school records, family finances, the reading lists of a childhood. For most historical actors the archive runs out long before the childhood is reached, and the method then tempts its user to invent the missing mediations in the style of a novel. Sartre's own applications were built on figures, Valéry, Genet, Flaubert, whose archives were unusually deep, and even there the joins show. The objection matters for the grading of the mediation charge itself: a standard that only the best-documented lives can be judged by is a standard most explanation will fail through poverty of evidence rather than laziness of mind. Sartre would presumably have accepted the correction as a friendly one. His target was never the historian defeated by a burnt archive; it was the theorist who never set out for the archive because the label had already told him what the life contained.

## Analytical and dialectical reason: two logics for two objects

The Critique's full title promises a theory of reason, and the promise is kept in a distinction that organises everything else in the book. Analytical reason is the logic of the practico-inert and of the sciences that study it: it divides, classifies, counts, and treats its object as exterior to itself, part outside part, each element indifferent to the others. Within its proper domain, matter already worked and cooled, analytical reason is not an error but the appropriate instrument, and the economist's tables and the demographer's series are its legitimate products. Dialectical reason is the logic of praxis in motion: it is the intelligibility of a totalisation grasped from inside the totalising act, where parts are moments of a whole that exists only in their movement, and where the knower is himself a part of what he knows. Sartre's claim is not that analysis is false but that it is regional: applied to praxis itself, to men making history, it produces the characteristic falsifications of the age, human beings rendered as the sums of external determinations, which is the lazy Marxism of the essay of method translated into a theory of knowledge. His provocation to the Marxism of the manuals was to argue that their official materialism was analytical through and through, a positivism with a revolutionary vocabulary, and that Marx's own best pages were dialectical in the sense the Critique defines, following processes from inside their movement.

The distinction carries a consequence for the status of his own book that Sartre states with unusual care. If dialectical reason is the logic of totalisation, it cannot itself be totalised from outside by a superior reason, and the Critique cannot claim to stand above history delivering its laws; it can only be one totalisation among others, dated, situated, open to the same regressive inquiry it practises on its objects. This self-limitation is the source of the book's odd humility beneath its systematic surface, and of its author's willingness, in the interviews of his last decade, to treat its constructions as revisable. It is also the point of maximum friction with the doctrine he claimed to serve, since a Marxism whose reason is situated has surrendered the claim that made the manuals comfortable, the claim to be science in the sense in which physics is science, exempt from the history it explains. Sartre accepted the surrender and called it honesty; the custodians called it idealism; the argument between them is the argument of the whole tradition in miniature, and it is not finished.

## Totalisation: the word that holds the system together

One term does more work in the Critique than any other, and a reader who masters it can follow the rest. Totalisation names the act by which a praxis gathers a multiplicity into a moving whole: the worker totalises his materials and tools into a task, the historian totalises documents into a period, the group totalises its members into a common act. Sartre sets it against totality, which is the same whole considered as finished, inert, a thing. A totality is a totalisation that has stopped, praxis cooled into the practico-inert; history, on his account, is not a totality, since no one performs it whole and it is never complete, but an endless plurality of totalisations that overlap, collide, and partially unify one another without ever summing into a single story told by a single subject. The distinction is his answer to a dilemma that had dogged Marxist philosophy of history: either history has a meaning guaranteed by a subject, the proletariat as collective hero, providence in secular dress, or it has no intelligibility at all and dissolves into chronicle. Totalisation without a totaliser, intelligibility without a guarantor, is Sartre's third position, and his claim that dialectical reason is the logic of such processes, the reason appropriate to wholes that are made rather than found, is the Critique's philosophical core.

The term also disciplines the biographical method, since a life, for Sartre, is a totalisation in progress: the child totalises his family situation into a project, the adult totalises the project against new conditions, and the biographer's regressive progressive movement is an attempt to re-totalise, in knowledge, what the subject totalised in action. Even the Flaubert study's notorious length follows from the term's honesty: a totalisation can only be followed at the speed at which it was lived, through the documents in which it deposited itself, and a summary that skips the deposits is the lazy method wearing the clothes of economy. Whether the concept earns its keep or merely renames the historian's ordinary practice in a private dialect is a question the objection section will press; what cannot be denied is its generative power inside the system, where seriality, the group, and the institution are all defined as modes in which totalisation succeeds, fails, or congeals.

## The practico-inert: worked matter that answers back

If the method supplies the Critique's procedure, the practico-inert supplies its central object. The term names worked matter: matter that human praxis has shaped, and that thereafter confronts human beings as an alien power with demands of its own. A cleared hillside, a built factory, a price system, a language, a city's street plan: each began as action, often as somebody's free project, and each persists after the project has been forgotten, imposing its exigencies on everyone who follows. The machine demands a worker paced to its rhythm. The deforested slope answers centuries of reasonable individual decisions with floods, Sartre's favourite example, drawn from the Chinese peasants whose clearing of the hills was rational plot by plot and catastrophic in sum. Nobody intended the flood. Everybody produced it. The practico-inert is the name for the level of reality at which intentions are stored, combined, and returned to their authors in a form no author recognises.

### How does the practico-inert turn intentions against their authors?

By storing praxis in matter, where it outlives the project that made it and joins with the stored praxis of strangers. Once embodied, inherited solutions harden into constraints: tools dictate gestures, prices dictate choices, cleared land dictates floods, and each agent meets the sum of everyone's action as an alien necessity ruling him.

This is the point at which Sartre's Marxism touches the older doctrine most closely, since the practico-inert is his reconstruction, in a philosophy of action, of what Marx had analysed as the domination of dead labour over living labour and what an earlier generation of the tradition had called alienation; the series treatment of [alienation at work](/2011/06/15/alienation-at-work-today/) follows the kindred concept into the workplace. Sartre's version has a distinctive coldness. Alienation in the humanist register suggests a fall from an essence, a self that could be recovered whole. The practico-inert promises no recovery: worked matter is not a spell to be broken but the permanent medium of human action, the price of having a history at all. Men make their history, but the materials they make it with are the congealed results of other men's making, and the results combine according to the logic of matter, scarcity, and number rather than the logic of any plan. Sartre called the outcomes counter-finalities, ends achieved by no one and suffered by all, and the concept does the work in his system that unintended consequences do in plainer vocabularies, with one difference: he insists the counter-finality is not an accident befalling praxis from outside but praxis itself, returning in its material form.

The concept also carries his quarrel with the idea of structure as the structuralists were developing it. A structure, in the usage he opposed, is a system that functions without subjects, intelligible from outside as a set of relations. The practico-inert is Sartre's counter-proposal: what looks like a subjectless structure is sedimented subjectivity, praxis gone rigid, and its intelligibility is therefore historical through and through, recoverable by tracing the actions that sedimented it. Whether the counter-proposal succeeds is the substance of the strongest objection, taken up below. What it achieves even on a cautious reading is a vocabulary in which institutions, markets, and built environments can be described as frozen action without being described as anyone's design, a description that fits a great deal of social experience better than either conspiracy or mechanism.

## Counter-finality: two worked examples

The practico-inert is easiest to grasp where Sartre works it hardest, in the production of outcomes that nobody chose. His celebrated case is agrarian: over centuries, Chinese peasants cleared the forests from the hillsides, each household acting rationally to gain fields and fuel, each generation extending the work of the last, until the denuded slopes could no longer hold the rains and the valleys below inherited floods that drowned the descendants of the clearers. Every act in the sequence was intelligible; the result belongs to no one's project; and the result is not bad luck but the systematic product of praxis mediated by matter, since the hillside stored each generation's labour and returned the sum in a currency no labourer had minted. Sartre's second stock case is monetary: the gold and silver of the Americas, pouring into sixteenth-century Spain and then into Europe, cheapened the metals it enriched, so that the treasure of empire quietly taxed the empire's own creditors and wage-earners through rising prices, an outcome pursued by no minister and suffered by millions. The conquered continent took its revenge in the metal of its conquerors.

The pair of examples shows what the concept is for, and also where its difficulties start. What it is for is the vast middle region of social life between conspiracy and accident, where outcomes are systematic, explicable, and unintended; any social theory that lacks a category for that region will keep mistaking structures for plots or for weather. Where the difficulties start is in the aggregation: the hillside stored labour because soil erodes by laws that physics supplies, and the gold cheapened by relations that economics must specify, so that at each step the practico-inert borrows its causal machinery from a science Sartre does not provide. His defenders answer that the borrowing is the point, that the concept is a form into which the findings of the particular sciences are to be poured rather than a rival to them, a way of holding the place of human action inside explanations that would otherwise be written without agents. The answer is consistent with the method's modesty elsewhere, and it locates the practico-inert correctly: not a mechanism but a discipline, a standing instruction to ask of every apparently natural outcome whose stored labour produced it, and of every free project what stored labour it is about to become.

## Seriality: the crowd that is not yet a group

Most human collectivities, most of the time, are not groups in Sartre's sense at all. They are series. A series is a plurality of people united only from outside, by their common relation to a practico-inert object, each interchangeable with the others, each separated by the very thing that joins them. His examples are deliberately unglamorous: the queue at a bus stop, the audience of a radio broadcast, the buyers and sellers dispersed through a market. The people in the queue are together because the bus is scarce; each one's interest is that the others not be there; their unity is real but it is a unity of separation, manufactured by the object and dissolving the moment the object is removed. The radio listener is lonelier still, united with millions precisely by receiving, alone, a voice none of them can answer. Sartre called this mode of being seriality, and he meant it as a description of ordinary social life under scarcity: not false consciousness, not a failure of solidarity that better propaganda could repair, but a structure of separation built into the way worked matter organises people who have never met.

### What holds a series together when its members never meet?

Nothing inside the members at all: the bond sits outside them, in the practico-inert object they each confront alone. A queue, a broadcast audience, a market's scattered buyers are unified by the bus, the programme, the price, and the unity is one of separation, since each member's interest runs against the rest.

Seriality is the concept in the Critique that has travelled furthest into ordinary social analysis, and it earns its keep by refusing two sentimentalities at once. Against the sentimentality of community, it observes that most collectivities are held together by an external object and by mutual indifference shading into mutual threat, and that appeals to their latent unity misunderstand what they are. Against the sentimentality of atomism, it observes that the separated individuals are not natural units either: their separation is produced, manufactured by the practico-inert arrangements through which they meet the world, so that the isolated consumer and the solitary listener are social products as surely as any crowd. The series also sets the problem to which the group is the answer. A series cannot act; it can only wait, disperse, panic, or be acted upon. For history to be made, seriality must be overcome, and the Critique's most famous pages describe the overcoming.

Seriality has a second theatre besides the queue and the audience, and Sartre's analysis of it reaches toward the heart of the older Marxism. A market, considered as a collectivity, is a series with a peculiar perfection: buyers and sellers who never meet are unified by prices none of them set, each man's rational pursuit of his interest altering the conditions of all the others, so that the outcome, a rise that ruins the prudent and enriches the lucky, belongs to the practico-inert logic of the whole and to no participant's design. The economists of the liberal tradition had celebrated this order as a harmony; the Marxist tradition had condemned it as anarchy of production; Sartre's contribution is to give its social form a name and a phenomenology, the experience of being governed by the sum of one's own kind's actions while experiencing that government as a foreign power, which he calls the serial impotence of men who together produce what each undergoes alone. On this analysis, the celebrated invisibility of the market's hand is not a mystery but a description of seriality itself: the hand is invisible because it is everyone's and no one's, praxis dispersed through an object that returns it as fate. The analysis also sharpens his difference from the doctrine he served. Where a cruder Marxism saw in the market a chaos to be replaced by a plan, Sartre saw a mode of human relation, alterity organised by worked matter, which a plan would have to overcome as a relation and not merely administer as a mechanism; the bureaucracies that administered without overcoming are, in the later chapters of his theory, seriality restored under the management of an institution, which is as concise a judgement on the planned economies of his century as any dissident delivered.

## The group in fusion: seriality overcome

The transformation Sartre calls the group in fusion occurs when a series, under the pressure of a common danger or a common task that cannot wait, discovers itself as a group. His worked example is the storming of the Bastille in July 1789: a Parisian crowd that had existed serially, as queues for bread and audiences for rumour, finds the royal troops' advance making its separation lethal, and in the act of arming itself and moving on the fortress, each person begins to act as the same as every other. The group's unity is not produced by a leader, a programme, or an organisation, none of which yet exist. It is produced by the situation and by a new relation Sartre calls the third: each member regulates the action of the others and is regulated by them, so that my act is mine and everyone's, and the distinction between the individual project and the common project temporarily vanishes. In fusion, praxis is transparent to itself; the group is pure freedom organised by urgency.

### What does the storming of the Bastille show about a group in fusion?

It shows a series becoming a group without leader, programme, or prior organisation, fused by danger alone. In the fused moment each participant acts as a regulating third for all the others, his own act returning to him as the group's act, so that individual and common praxis briefly coincide.

Sartre is emphatic, against a whole tradition of romantic readings, that fusion is not the truth of the group but its birth, and a birth under emergency conditions that cannot last. The danger passes; the fortress falls; the crowd must eat, sleep, disperse to its trades and parishes, and at that moment the group faces the problem that generates everything else in his political theory: how to persist when the fusing pressure relaxes. A fused group that simply dissolves returns its members to seriality, which may be acceptable for a crowd that wanted bread and took a fortress on the way. A revolutionary group, whose project extends beyond the day, cannot accept dissolution, and so it invents the instruments of its own survival. Those instruments, followed honestly, are Sartre's theory of how liberation builds its own cage, and they make the Critique the least consoling book in the Marxist tradition: the degeneration of revolutionary collectivities is not, for him, a story of bad leaders or betrayed ideals but a structural sequence inscribed in the group's attempt to outlive its founding moment.

Sartre has a word for the fused moment in its experiential intensity, and the word surprises readers who meet it first in summaries: he calls the group in fusion an apocalypse, in the word's old sense of an unveiling. In fusion the social world is suddenly legible: each man sees the common praxis in every other man's act, sees himself as the group's deed and the group as his own, and the opacity that seriality imposes on collective life is burned away for as long as the emergency lasts. The term explains both the glory veterans attach to such moments and the political danger Sartre insists on in the same breath, since a collectivity that has known transparency will spend its subsequent existence trying to recover it, and the instruments of recovery, the pledge, the purge, the ritual renewal of unity, are the group's life cycle driven by nostalgia for its first day. The revolutionary calendar is full of manufactured apocalypses, festivals and anniversaries and emergency sessions staged to refound the fusion that organisation has dissipated, and Sartre's analysis predicts their diminishing returns: fusion cannot be scheduled, because its condition is a danger that is real, and a sovereignty that must stage its founding emergency has already confessed itself an institution. This is among the places where the Critique reads less like a treatise of 1960 than like a commentary on every movement that has tried to govern in the name of its founding crowd, and it is why the book's bleakest pages are quoted with recognition by readers whose politics its author never shared.

## The group's life cycle: pledge, organisation, institution

The survival begins with the pledge. The group, fearing its own dispersal, swears itself into permanence: each member binds himself to the others for the future, and because a free promise can be freely broken, the group arms the pledge with terror, the standing threat that the one who deserts or betrays will be treated as an enemy. Sartre calls the resulting bond fraternity-terror, and insists on both halves of the compound: the fraternity is genuine, the warmth of the fused group prolonged by an act of will, and the terror is genuine too, since the pledge's guarantee is violence held in reserve against the group's own members. From the pledge follows organisation: the group differentiates itself, assigns functions, divides the common task into specialised posts, and accepts that members are no longer interchangeable thirds but occupants of roles. From organisation follows the institution, in which the differentiation hardens, authority concentrates in a sovereign who incarnates the group's unity, and the members' relation to their own collectivity becomes serial again: they confront the institution as a practico-inert power, obeying its exigencies as they obeyed the queue and the price. The cycle closes where it began, at a higher level of organisation and a lower level of freedom.

The table below sets out the group's life cycle stage by stage, since the sequence, rather than any single stage, is the argument.

| Stage | What holds the collectivity together | What threatens it | What it loses in passing to the next stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Series | A shared practico-inert object confronted by each member alone | Nothing internal; the series cannot act and barely coheres | The passivity and separation of its members, surrendered under pressure |
| Group in fusion | A common danger or task, and each member acting as a regulating third for the rest | The passing of the emergency that fused it | Its transparency and spontaneity, traded for permanence |
| Pledged group | The oath of permanence, guaranteed by fraternity-terror against desertion | Defection, and the relaxation that makes the oath feel unnecessary | The freedom to leave, and innocence about the violence held in reserve |
| Organisation | Assigned functions, division of labour, and differentiated posts | Rigidity, and the divergence of specialised interests | The interchangeability of members, each now bound to a role |
| Institution | Sovereignty that incarnates the group's unity, and ritual that renews allegiance | The seriality of its own members, who face it as an alien power | Common praxis itself, which hardens into the practico-inert it was built to escape |

### What does the pledge add that shared enthusiasm cannot?

Duration, and a guarantee. Enthusiasm lasts as long as the emergency that produced it; the pledge binds the group's future, obliging members when feeling has cooled. Because a free promise can be freely revoked, the group secures it with terror against the defector, purchasing permanence at the price of internal violence.

### How does an organisation differ from the group that founded it?

By differentiation. The fused group knows no fixed roles: each member is a third for all. An organisation assigns functions, divides the common task into posts, and makes members occupants of places in a structure. Efficiency rises; interchangeability dies; the group begins to be mediated by its own apparatus.

### What is sovereignty inside an institution?

It is the concentration of the group's unity in one place: a leader or office that incarnates the whole, issues the unity the members no longer produce among themselves, and is obeyed as the group made visible. Sovereignty solves the institution's coordination problem by restoring seriality under a single regulating centre.

The pledge deserves a slower look, because it is the hinge on which the whole cycle turns and the point where Sartre's analysis bites deepest into revolutionary experience. His model scene is an oath: men who have acted together in fusion, sensing the dispersal that tomorrow threatens, swear to remain what the day has made them. The oath of the deputies in the tennis court in June 1789, refusing to separate until they had given France a constitution, stands behind his analysis as the pledge in its innocent form; the revolutionary tribunals and the terror exercised by governments of public safety stand behind it as the pledge in its armed form, fraternity enforcing itself against the brother who falters. Sartre's term for the enforcer's stance is exact: the terrorist, in his special sense, is not the enemy of the group but its most faithful organ, the member who treats the pledge's guarantee as his personal commission and experiences the liquidation of the defector as fraternity's own act. By building terror into the pledge's logic rather than attributing it to the cruelty of particular revolutionaries, Sartre removes the two standard consolations at once, the liberal consolation that the terror was an accident befalling a noble movement and the apologetic consolation that it was the work of enemies within. It is, on his account, what a sworn group does when its survival is at stake and its members are free to defect: it makes defection mortal, because nothing else in its armoury can make the future obey. Readers who know the fate of revolutionary organisations in his century will judge for themselves whether the analysis explains too much or exactly enough; its bleakness is at least evenly distributed, since it convicts no faction and acquits none, and it converts the history of revolutions from a gallery of betrayals into a study of a structure that any reader's own movement, party, or campaign would reproduce under the same pressures.

Read as political theory, the cycle is Sartre's answer to the question that haunted his century: how do movements of emancipation produce bureaucracies that rule in emancipation's name? His answer declines the consolations. It is not that the wrong people seized the apparatus, though wrong people may; it is not that the ideal was betrayed, though ideals are; it is that a group which refuses to dissolve must pledge, organise, and institutionalise, and each step deposits freedom into structures that then govern the depositors. The Soviet bureaucracy, on this account, is not an aberration requiring a special explanation but the cycle's most documented run, a fused revolutionary group hardened through siege, pledge, and terror into an institution whose members related to it serially. Sartre never worked the Soviet case through the whole machinery in print, and the second volume of the Critique, which was to carry the analysis to the scale of a society's history, remained unfinished; but the application is unmistakable, and it is why the theory of the group, buried in a difficult book, keeps being rediscovered by readers interested in organisations of every kind. The group became his model of history in miniature because the cycle shows, inside a single episode, the whole dialectic the Critique exists to describe: praxis creating the practico-inert that will govern praxis.

The cycle also explains his ambivalence about parties, which the political sections below take up. A party is an institution by design, and Sartre, who needed parties as instruments and distrusted them as ends, spent his political life oscillating between the fused group he celebrated, the crowd, the movement, the commune of a moment, and the organised force he knew the moment required to survive. His critics called the oscillation opportunism; his theory calls it the group's life cycle lived from inside, by a man who could describe the trap completely and still had to act within it.

## A strike through Sartre's categories: the theory applied end to end

The machinery earns its keep when it is run, in sequence, over a single familiar event, and the strike is the event Sartre's readers most naturally test it on. Before the strike, the workforce is a series: men and women unified from outside by the factory, the shift pattern, the wage scale, each experiencing the others as competitors for overtime, for promotion, for the foreman's favour, their common condition lived as private grievance. A provocation, a sacking, a death at a machine, a wage cut, alters the practico-inert field; the grievance is suddenly legible to each as everyone's, and the meeting in the yard performs the fusion, each speaker's anger returning to him multiplied by the crowd's recognition, the third in every member regulating all. The strike committee is born as the group itself, rotating, transparent, mandated afresh each morning. Within days the pledge appears, in the moral form first, solidarity as fraternity, and then in its armed form, the picket's judgement on the man who goes in, fraternity-terror at the factory gate, experienced by those inside not as violence but as the group's self-defence against dissolution. Organisation follows as the strike lengthens: funds, rotas, negotiations, spokesmen, a division between those who speak and those who are spoken for. And if the strike becomes a union and the union a fixture, the institution completes the cycle: officials whose function is the organisation's survival, members who meet their own collectivity as dues, ballots, and directives, seriality restored under a banner that still shows the fused crowd of the founding week.

No participant needs Sartre's vocabulary to live that sequence, and the theory's claim is not that it teaches strikers their experience but that it explains the sequence's dreary reliability across centuries and causes, the way emancipations in particular keep arriving at the same terminus. The explanation's strength, against rival accounts, is that it requires no villains and no illusions: the officials who end by policing the members are not traitors to the founding moment but its product, the pledge's grandchildren. Its weakness, pressed by historians, is the obverse of its strength: a cycle that explains every outcome explains any outcome, and the strike that dissolves in victory, the union that renews itself from below, the institution that dies rather than hardens, all get absorbed as eddies in a current whose direction was fixed in advance. Sartre's likely reply stands in the text: the cycle is not a law but a field of pressures, and its whole point is that men who know the pressures can act against them with open eyes, which is why the theory of the group's decay was written by a man who kept joining groups. The reader can test the exchange against any movement whose history he knows at first hand, which is the use to which its author, who tested it against his own life in public for forty years, always intended it to be put.

## Ideology in Sartre: thought that has gone serial

A Marxist profile owes its reader the subject's theory of ideology, and Sartre's is among his most original constructions, precisely because it is built from his own machinery rather than inherited. In the essay of method and again in the Critique, ideology appears not as a set of false propositions floated by a class but as a lived totalisation that has stiffened: an ideology, in his formulation, is a creative thought, the work of a singular thinker responding to his epoch's questions, which a later age inherits as a ready-made system and repeats without recreating. While its author lives, the thought is a praxis, risking itself against its materials; once he is gone, the thought passes into the practico-inert, is taught, excerpted, and applied by men who receive it as they receive a price or a street plan, as an exigency from outside. Ideology is thus serial thought, the same doctrine in two states of being, living as totalisation and dead as totality, and the difference lies not in the propositions but in the relation of the thinkers to them. The reader will notice that this is the mediation charge restated as a theory of intellectual history: lazy Marxism is Marxism become ideology in Sartre's exact sense, Marx's totalisation inherited as a filing system by men who totalise nothing.

The construction pays its way in two directions. Toward the past, it explains how doctrines decay without any villainy: no censor is required to freeze a living inquiry into a catechism, since inheritance itself, the practico-inert character of all transmitted thought, does the freezing unless each generation performs the dangerous act of re-totalisation for itself. Toward his own work, it supplies the standard by which Sartre asked, with increasing explicitness, to be judged: existentialism itself, he conceded, had become an ideology in his sense wherever it was repeated as a vocabulary rather than practised as a method, and the concession is built into the enclave doctrine, which promises the supplement's dissolution as the sign of its success. The contrast with the structuralist theory of ideology, in which ideology is the imaginary relation of individuals to their conditions and interpellation does the work, belongs to the confrontation mapped elsewhere in this series; what is distinctive in Sartre's version is its temporality, ideology as thought's old age rather than its falsehood, and its corollary that the cure for ideology is never a truer doctrine but always a resumed act of thinking. Whether a theory that defines its rivals' decay so elegantly has thereby excused its own is a question he would have recognised as fair, and it hangs over every page of the reception traced below.

## Scarcity, need, and the roots of conflict

Underneath the theory of the group lies a grimmer foundation: scarcity. Sartre's term, rareté, names the condition in which there is not enough, of food, of goods, of places, of time, for everyone who needs it, so that each person's satisfaction appears, in material fact, as another's deprivation. He is careful about the status of the claim. Scarcity is not an eternal law of human nature and not a feature of every imaginable world; it is a contingent condition that happens to have governed the whole of recorded history so far, the milieu in which every known society has formed its relations. Within that milieu, scarcity does quiet and terrible work. It makes the other man appear as a threat before he has acted, since his consumption is, arithmetically, the possible subtraction of my survival. It turns the practico-inert field into a field of passive hostility, fills the series with its latent antagonism, and gives violence its standing invitation: under scarcity, men do not need wickedness to become enemies, because the material situation has already defined each as the other's possible death.

This is the load-bearing wall of the Critique's anthropology, and it carries more weight than some readers think it can bear. Sartre needs scarcity to explain why praxis, which is free, so reliably issues in conflict and domination rather than in the transparent cooperation of the fused group; if scarcity were merely one circumstance among others, the group's degeneration would be one possibility among others, and the theory's pessimism would soften into contingency. By making scarcity the near-universal milieu of history to date, he gets necessity without metaphysics: violence and seriality are not written into man, but they are written into every situation man has yet inhabited, which for a theory of history is near enough. The Marxist objection, pressed by economists in the tradition, is that scarcity in Sartre's sense is too undifferentiated to explain anything specific: it names the general fact of finitude, which no theory denies, while the actual mechanisms of conflict in a given society run through its property relations, its mode of production, its class structure, the very mediations Sartre accused others of skipping. The objection has force, and Sartre's rejoinder, that scarcity is the matrix within which class structures themselves form and acquire their sting, relocates the dispute rather than settling it: whether one begins from scarcity or from the mode of production decides what the theory treats as given and what it treats as explained. The choice is visible in his pages as a preference for beginning where the individual stands, in need, facing a world that does not contain enough, and it marks the Critique as an anthropology before it is an economics.

Scarcity also disciplines his treatment of need itself. Need is the first relation between the organism and its material field, the lack that launches praxis; labour is need organised against the practico-inert; and the whole human world of tools, institutions, and meanings is sedimented around the repeated attempt to close a gap that scarcity keeps reopening. A Marxism that begins from production, Sartre implies, begins one step too late, after the producer has already been constituted by need and by the hostile field in which his need must be met. Whether that step back is an enrichment or a detour remains one of the live questions in the literature the Critique generated.

One further turn of the scarcity argument should be stated, since it governs Sartre's distance from a certain optimism inside Marxism itself. A famous strain of the tradition looks beyond scarcity to a realm of freedom founded on abundance, where the shortening of necessary labour dissolves antagonism and administration of things replaces the government of men. Sartre's response, scattered through the Critique and the interviews, is a refusal to let the eschatology do the theory's work. Matter worked by men remains practico-inert under any mode of production: machines will still dictate rhythms, cities will still impose their exigencies, and the sediment of past praxis will still return intentions inverted, even where no one starves. Scarcity in the strict sense might be abolished, and with it the arithmetical form of human rivalry; what cannot be abolished is the exteriority of the worked world, the fact that men must live inside their own products as inside a second nature. A socialism that promised the end of that condition would be promising men an escape from history, and Sartre, who had read his Marx closely enough to find the promise's texts, treated it the way he treated all guarantees, as a temptation to stop inquiring. The consequence for his politics is a permanent austerity of expectation: emancipation, on his account, is not a state in which the practico-inert has been transcended but a practice that knows it cannot be, and organises its groups with open eyes about the cycle that awaits them. His critics have called this the pessimism of a bourgeois philosopher who could not believe in the future; his defenders call it the only optimism that has survived its century's evidence. The profile records both judgements and notes that the theory's fruitfulness, in the study of organisations that betray no one and still harden, does not depend on choosing between them.

## Need, labour, and the organism: the ground floor of the Critique

The Critique's own order of exposition begins lower than scarcity, with the individual organism and its need, and the choice is a manifesto in itself. Sartre starts from the living body as a material being among material beings, maintaining itself against entropy by work: need is the organism's lack made active, the first negation of a negation, and labour is need's organised answer, the project by which a man inscribes his purposes into matter and is inscribed by it in return. From that ground floor the whole edifice rises: because the organism works on a field already worked by others, praxis meets the practico-inert; because the field is scarce, praxis meets the other as rival; because rivals can fuse, groups form; because groups must last, institutions harden. The architecture is deliberate, a materialism rebuilt from the body outward, and it explains the book's strange double character, at once an anthropology of the isolated agent and a theory of the collectivities that swallow him.

The point of the ground floor is to deny two rival starting points their first move. Against the economist's starting point, production already social and divided, Sartre insists that production is unintelligible except as the act of needy organisms, and that a Marxism which begins with the factory has skipped the mediation between nature and history that its own founder, in the manuscripts on labour as man's metabolism with the earth, had kept in view. Against the philosopher's starting point, consciousness contemplating its freedom, he insists that the for-itself of the early work must be re-founded as the organism in danger, its freedom the practical field opened by need rather than a metaphysical nothingness. Critics have asked whether the detour earns its length, since the isolated organism of the opening chapters is an abstraction no history contains, and Sartre's regress from it to actual societies retraces, at great cost, the distance his critics preferred to assume. His answer is the mediation charge once more, applied to Marxism's own foundations: the distance between the needy body and the class struggle is exactly the distance lazy theory jumps, and a foundation that cannot be reached by steps is a foundation that will be filled, in practice, by slogans.

## The Family Idiot: the mediation charge put to work

Late in his life Sartre attempted to pay, in full and in public, the debt the mediation charge had recorded. The Family Idiot, his study of Gustave Flaubert, published in two large volumes in 1971 and a third in 1972, with a fourth left unfinished, is the progressive regressive method applied to a single life at a scale no one had attempted: several thousand pages to answer the question Sartre set at its head, what can we know about a man, in the case of the man who wrote Madame Bovary. Flaubert is chosen with malice aforethought. He is exactly the kind of figure lazy Marxism files in one line: a bourgeois writer, son of a chief surgeon, rentier, celebrant of art for art's sake, therefore explained. Sartre's study exists to show how much that line leaves out: the younger son constituted passively in a family that prized the elder brother, the childhood experienced as a failure to master language at the family's speed, the famous crisis at Pont-l'Évêque in 1844 that closed the legal career prepared for him and installed him in the vocation of the idiot of the family turned artist, the class position of the provincial bourgeoisie after 1848 lived through one man's nerves, digestion, friendships, and style. The epoch is all there, but it is there as Flaubert totalised it, and the totalisation is the explanation.

### Why did Sartre spend a decade writing about Flaubert?

Because Flaubert was the test case his method had promised: a bourgeois writer whom lazy classification files in one line. By regressing through family, childhood, and epoch, then progressing forward through the man's project, Sartre meant to prove that a single life could be explained completely without being dissolved.

The scale of the attempt invites the objection it received, that the demonstration devours its object, that three thousand pages on one novelist is a disproportion no method can justify, and that the Flaubert who emerges is suspiciously Sartrean, a hero of bad faith and the imaginary constructed from the categories the study was meant to vindicate. Sartre's answer is built into the wager: if the method is sound, its proof can only be a case carried through without shortcuts, and the length is the honesty. Readers remain divided, and the division is instructive. The Family Idiot is admired as the century's most ambitious biography and shelved as its most magnificent excess, sometimes by the same reader in the same week. For this profile its standing matters more than its verdict: it is the mediation charge cashed, the demonstration that its author held himself to the standard he had used to indict a tradition, and it fixes the meaning of his Marxism as a discipline of explanation before it was a politics.

Two technical terms from the Flaubert study deserve a sentence each in the record, because they show the method at its most exposed. Sartre calls Flaubert's constitution an objective neurosis: a way of living a class situation, the declining rentier bourgeoisie of the July Monarchy and the Second Empire, in the register of the nerves, so that the man's famous ailments, his hatred of his class voiced in the accents of that class's own drawing rooms, become legible as history conducted by other means. And he describes the novelist's art itself as the imaginary resolution of a real defeat: the boy who could not win his father's esteem in the world of action wins it posthumously in the world of the book, which is why style, for Flaubert, had to bear the weight of a salvation. Historians of literature have objected that the reading steamrolls the scholarship, that Sartre's Flaubert is ignorant of findings a specialist would start from, and that the objective neurosis is a concept designed to be unanswerable, since every document can be filed as its expression and none as its refutation. The objection is the falsifiability charge that dogs the whole method, and it is not strengthened by being true of this case in particular: the study's defence has never been that it is cautious but that it is complete, and readers continue to divide between those who find in its completeness a new kind of knowledge and those who find a novel about a novelist, written by the century's most gifted practitioner of both forms.

## The unfinished novel: The Roads to Freedom and the turn from fiction

Sartre's Marxist decades were also the decades in which his fiction stopped, and the stoppage belongs to the record because he theorised it himself. The Roads to Freedom, the novel cycle begun in the war years, followed a philosophy teacher, Mathieu, and a widening circle through the collapse of the 1930s toward an engagement the third volume, Iron in the Soul, brought to the threshold of the Resistance; the fourth volume, which was to carry its hero into communism and disillusion, appeared only in fragments and was abandoned. The abandonment has been read as a failure of talent and as a scruple: the novel of the fellow travelling years would have required its author to render from inside a conversion he was performing in public on weaker evidence than his fiction demanded, and the book's hero kept refusing the conclusion his author's articles had already reached. What is certain is the redirection of the energy: the thousands of pages that the novel would have consumed went into the Critique, the biographies, and the theatre, forms in which a thesis could be carried by an argument or a stage rather than by the slow interiority of a character whose freedom the author had to respect. Sartre's literary criticism of the same years, above all his essays on the nouveau roman and on the death of the bourgeois novel, supplied the theory of the desertion: the novel, he argued, was the form of a class that still believed in the individual destiny, and a literature adequate to seriality and the practico-inert would have to be written otherwise, a judgement his own unfinished cycle illustrates more eloquently than his essays. For the series' purposes the point is structural: the thinker who accused Marxism of dissolving individuals spent his creative life migrating from the one form built to honour individuals toward forms built to explain them, and the tension between the migration and the accusation is the tension of the whole enterprise, visible in the bibliography before it is visible in the arguments.

## Words: the method turned on its author

In 1964, the year of the refused Nobel, Sartre published the first and only completed volume of his autobiography, and it is the method's most elegant application because its subject is the author himself. Words tells of a childhood inside the bourgeois republic of letters: the father dead in the boy's infancy, the mother returned to her parents' house, the grandfather a Alsatian schoolmaster and author of language textbooks who staged the child as a prodigy before an audience of family friends. Sartre reads that childhood with the instruments of the Critique before the fact, showing how a class's ideology, the cult of literature as salvation, was lived by one boy as a personal vocation, how the imposture he felt at the family's theatre became the engine of a life's work, and how the writer he became was the totalisation of a situation he had spent decades mistaking for a nature. The book closes by dissolving its own premise: the author presents himself as a man made by words who has ceased to believe that words save, and the autobiography ends in a disenchantment that is also a self-explanation, the bourgeois writer accounting for his vocation as a class product he chose.

For this profile the volume's importance is diagnostic. Sartre never wrote the equivalent volume about a worker, a peasant, or a party militant, and his critics have not failed to notice that the mediation method's masterpieces are all, like its author's masterpiece about himself, studies of writers from the educated bourgeoisie, the one milieu whose archives, letters, juvenilia, and published confessions make the regressive movement feasible at full stretch. The method's apparent universality may thus be an artefact of its evidence: where documents abound, mediations can be followed; where they are silent, as for most of the humanity Marxism exists to explain, the method has least to say. Sartre was aware of the difficulty and answered it in part with the Flaubert study's wager, that one case followed completely teaches more than a thousand cases filed. The answer persuades as literature and troubles as methodology, and it is the gentlest form of the structuralist objection this profile states later at full strength: a theory of mediation whose proofs are all biographies of authors may have mistaken the best-documented stratum of human life for human life itself.

## Fellow traveller: Sartre and the French Communist Party

The political record begins from a refusal that never changed. Sartre would not join the French Communist Party, and the party, for most of their long entanglement, did not want his membership so much as his name. What it got, between 1952 and 1956, was the most valuable fellow traveller in Europe: a philosopher of world reputation who defended the party against its critics, attacked its attackers, and argued that the working class's organised expression, however flawed its leadership, was the only vehicle through which history could be moved in France. His articles of those years, gathered under the title The Communists and Peace, reasoned from the camp logic of the Cold War: in a world armed for a war between two blocs, the intellectual who weakened the party that organised the French workers objectively served the bloc preparing that war, whatever his subjective intentions. The prose was brilliant and the conclusion was a trap he had built for himself, since it obliged him to silence about the Soviet camps and trials at exactly the moment when the evidence was becoming undeniable, and he later said plainly that he had known and had chosen not to know, on grounds his own philosophy of bad faith described better than any critic could.

### Did Sartre ever hold a party card?

No. Through four decades of alliance, defence, rupture, and reconciliation with French communism, he never took membership in the party or in any other. The refusal was principled in his own account: the card would have required defending positions unexamined, and unexamined defence was the laziness he had charged against the doctrine's custodians.

The entanglement cost him the two great friendships of his intellectual life before it cost him his illusions. Albert Camus was lost in 1952, when Les Temps modernes carried a savage review of The Rebel and Sartre answered Camus's protest with a public letter of contempt; the quarrel, conducted before all Paris, turned on whether revolt could set limits to violence or whether history's tribunal dissolved such scruples, and it has stood ever since as the emblematic rupture of the engaged intellectuals. Maurice Merleau-Ponty, his co-editor and the subtler political thinker of the two, broke over the same period from the other direction: where Camus accused Sartre of surrendering to history, Merleau-Ponty accused him of something colder, of making the party's pronouncements the criterion of political truth while reserving a private freedom to philosophise, a combination the Adventures of the Dialectic named ultrabolshevism and anatomised with a friend's precision.

### What broke the friendship between Sartre and Merleau-Ponty?

Politics, at the point where their judgements of communism diverged. Merleau-Ponty concluded that a wait-and-see liberalism was the honest position once the Soviet camps were undeniable; Sartre chose the party as the workers' only instrument. The quarrel, sealed by a bitter exchange and a devastating critical portrait, ended their editorial partnership and their friendship.

What survives of that rupture, beyond its biographical pathos, is the clarity of Merleau-Ponty's charge, which any honest profile must let stand at full strength because it identifies the exact weakness of Sartre's fellow travelling years. If the party line is to be defended because the party is the proletariat's organ, and the proletariat is history's subject, then the philosopher's own judgement has been delegated to an institution whose errors he is forbidden, by his own argument, to correct in public; the philosophy of freedom becomes a private hobby carried on beside a public obedience. Sartre never answered the charge in its strongest form during those years. His answer, when it came, was an act rather than an argument: the break of 1956, which the next section follows, conceded by deed much of what Merleau-Ponty had said, without ever conceding it in words to the friend, by then dead, who had said it.

## The year of the choice: Korea, the arrested deputy, and the great turn

The turn itself deserves its dates, since Sartre's later reputation was built by compressing them. In the spring of 1952 the American general Ridgway, architect of the war in Korea and newly appointed to command the Atlantic alliance's forces in Europe, was met in Paris by communist-led demonstrations that the police dispersed with deaths; within days the government arrested the acting head of the French party, Jacques Duclos, on evidence drawn from racing pigeons supposedly trained for espionage, a charge so flimsy that its collapse became a byword. Sartre, who had spent the spring quarrelling with the party's cultural commissars, read the arrests as the opening of a different kind of war, the domestic front of the Korean one, and concluded in print that an anti-communist was a dog and that he would not retreat from the word, that the intellectual's first duty in a France drifting toward an anti-communist purge was to stand where the purge would fall. The articles that followed, The Communists and Peace, built the fellow travelling position into a system: the party, whatever its leadership's errors, was the form in which the French working class existed politically; to weaken it in the name of its errors was to deliver the class, undefended, to the men with the pigeons and the prisons. Friends who had marched with him against one empire now watched him decline to march against the other; the essay on the Soviet camps could not be written by the author of those articles, and was not.

Posterity has been kinder to the diagnosis than to the prescription. The France of 1952 was indeed arming itself against its own left with a servility toward Washington that Sartre's polemic exposed in real time, and the camp logic he described, in which every domestic question was settled by the global confrontation, governed more minds than his. But the prescription, silence about the allied camp's crimes as the price of solidarity, was the precise arrangement by which a generation of honest intellectuals became the accomplices of what they privately knew, and Sartre's later shame about those years was more philosophically interesting than his defences, since it amounted to his own theory turned against himself: he had treated the party's line as a practico-inert deposit of truth, exempt from the regressive inquiry he demanded for Valéry. The turn of 1952 is thus the hinge of the whole political record, the moment at which the theorist of mediation accepted a mediation, the party, on trust; everything after, including the break of 1956 and the refusals of his old age, can be read as the slow recovery of the trust's price.

## The Camus quarrel: revolt, limits, and the tribunal of history

The public rupture with Albert Camus in the summer of 1952 belongs to the same year and the same choice, and it has outlived both men's politics as the emblematic quarrel of the engaged century. Camus's The Rebel, published the year before, had argued that revolt discovers a limit in itself, a value that forbids murder in the cause of history, and that the revolutionary who kills by historical necessity has ceased to rebel and begun to administer. The review in Sartre's journal, written by a young protégé at Sartre's direction, mocked the book as the philosophy of a beautiful soul; Camus answered not the reviewer but the editor, addressing him as Monsieur le Directeur in a letter of wounded dignity; and Sartre's reply, printed in full, is among the most savage documents of French intellectual life, a demolition of the friendship in the form of a verdict on the friend's talent, courage, and good faith. Beneath the cruelty, the philosophical stakes were exact and remain so. For Camus, a value that does not bind the revolutionary in the act of revolution is decoration, and history's tribunal is a fiction tyrants cite. For Sartre in 1952, the refusal to choose within history was itself a choice, subsidised by the comforts of the camp that could afford scruples, and limits announced in advance would be defined by those who held power while the powerless died of them.

Neither man won, and the quarrel's afterlife has distributed the honours with an evenness neither would have granted the other. The decades since have vindicated Camus's limit wherever revolutions ate their children in the name of the tribunal, and Sartre's rejoinder wherever respectable opinion used the vocabulary of limits to excuse its comforts during colonial wars, in some of which Camus himself, on Algeria, fell silent in the way Sartre's argument predicted. What the quarrel establishes for this profile is narrower and more useful: the fellow travelling was not a lapse of intelligence but one horn of a genuine dilemma, chosen by a man who could state the other horn as well as its owner could, and who chose against it on grounds, the priority of the organised class over the solitary conscience, that his philosophy of the group was even then assembling. When the grounds gave way in 1956, the friendship did not return; Camus died in a car crash in January 1960, and Sartre's published tribute to him, generous about the work and silent about the quarrel, is the record of a reconciliation history did not permit in life.

## Neither Washington nor Moscow: the third camp episode

The fellow travelling of 1952 was not Sartre's first political position, and remembering the earlier attempt clarifies what he thought he was choosing. In 1948 he had lent his name and energy to the Rassemblement démocratique révolutionnaire, a movement founded with the journalist David Rousset and others to gather a revolutionary left that refused both the Atlantic and the Soviet camps, democratic and socialist and independent of the two armed orthodoxies. The venture drew meetings, manifestos, and the support of writers across Europe and America, and it collapsed within two years under the pressure of the polarisation it opposed: money, organisation, and an audience all belonged to the camps, and a third force of intellectuals without workers or weapons discovered that neutrality between armies is a position only armies can afford. Sartre drew the lesson that governed his next decade: the independent intellectual left, however correct its analysis, was a series in his later vocabulary, an aggregate of separated consciences unified only by a shared object of refusal, incapable of the fused and organised action through which history is actually moved. The turn toward the party in 1952 was thus not a conversion but a conclusion, drawn from the third camp's failure, that the price of efficacy was an alliance with the institution that organised the class in whose name history could be made. The Critique's theory of seriality was written by a man who had lived inside a series and watched it disperse; the fellow travelling was the pledge he took instead, and its price, the public silences, was the fraternity-terror of the alliance paid in the coin of his own judgement. That he later judged the price too high is the burden of the next section; that he paid it knowingly, on a theory of the alternatives, is part of the accuracy the record requires.

## The theatre of the turn: devils, gods, and the condemned of Altona

Sartre's Marxist turn was rehearsed in his theatre before it was argued in his treatises, and two plays mark its stations. The Devil and the Good Lord, first performed in 1951, follows a medieval freebooter, Goetz, who wagers first on evil and then on goodness as absolute gestures, and discovers in the wreckage of both that men do not need his absolutes: the play closes with his acceptance of a finite human task, command in a peasant war whose justice is partial and whose outcome is unsure. Critics at the time and since have read Goetz's itinerary as its author's: from the metaphysical absolutes of the early philosophy, through a conversion to the good that merely inverted them, to the committed finitude of the Marxist years, in which action is justified not by heaven or by history's guarantee but by the concrete liberation it can effect here. That the hero ends as a leader of men, having learned that the age of solitary gestures is over, made the play the aesthetic manifesto of the fellow travelling period, all the more effective for being set four centuries away from the camps of its own decade.

The second station is darker. The Condemned of Altona, performed in 1959 as the Algerian war entered its fifth year, imprisons its action in a German industrialist's house where a son who tortured prisoners in the East hides from a world that has agreed to forget, while his father's fortune, built under one regime and laundered under the next, seals the family's pact of silence. No spectator in Paris in 1959 needed the parallel named: a nation conducting torture in Algeria while prospering in amnesia was watching its own arrangement on the stage, and the play's German setting was the thinnest of veils, adopted partly for prudence and partly because Sartre's target was never one nation's guilt but the practico-inert of respectable fortunes, which stores the violence of one decade as the comfort of the next. The two plays together trace the arc of his political theatre: the first announces that the age of absolutes has ended and men must act in history without guarantees; the second asks what it costs a society when it takes that advice in the form of forgetting. Both remain in repertory when the treatises are in the library, which would not have surprised their author, who held that a play reaches freedoms a treatise can only address.

## Dirty Hands: the play the party could not forgive

One play of the turn years belongs not with the theatre of consolation but with the political record itself, since it was treated by everyone concerned as an act. Dirty Hands, first performed in 1948, sets its action in a fictional eastern European party during the war: a young bourgeois intellectual, Hugo, burning to prove himself by a real act, is ordered to assassinate the party's leader Hoederer, whose crime is tactical, a willingness to compromise with other factions and to govern by stages rather than by purity. In the long central scene Hoederer dismantles the boy's scruples with the century's most quoted defence of political realism: purity, he tells him, is an idea for fakirs and monks, and a man who intends to govern, to change the world rather than to keep his soul, must accept that his hands will be dirty to the elbows in blood and mire. Hugo shoots him for reasons the play deliberately muddies, love and pride and the party's counter-order arriving too late, and returns from prison to discover that the party has adopted the dead man's line and now requires the assassination to have been a mistake, the assassin a liability to be liquidated by the truth's new custodians. The play's last movement, in which Hugo refuses the revision and claims his act as his own, is the mediation charge staged as tragedy: a man insisting that his deed be explained through his project and not filed under the line's newest version.

The party's response told its own story. Communist critics denounced the play as an anti-communist provocation, performances were fought over in several countries, and Sartre, who refused for years to authorise productions where he thought the play would be used as a weapon against the left in the Cold War's terms, found himself in the position his cycle describes, the author as a series of one, governed by the practico-inert of his own text. His defence of the play was constant and narrow: it was not an indictment of the party but of purity, and its Hoederer, the man of dirty hands, is its hero precisely because he accepts the mediations, the stages, the compromises, that the boy's beautiful soul refuses. Read against the biography, the play's date is its sharpest irony: written in 1948, before the fellow travelling, it contains the whole critique of the arrangement its author was about to enter, the party as the institution that rewrites its members' acts to fit its line, and it was available to him, in his own hand, throughout the years when he declined to apply it. The critics who call that decline bad faith have no stronger exhibit; the defenders who call it the price of the camp logic at least cannot call it ignorance. He had written the warning himself, in dialogue better than the apologia, and the warning is in repertory still.

## The Soviet Union, Hungary, and the limits of loyalty

Sartre's position on the Soviet Union passed through three phases, and the profile states them in order, since conflating them is the commonest error in accounts of his politics. In the first phase, the fellow travelling years, he treated the Soviet Union as the camp of the exploited in a divided world and disciplined his public judgements accordingly; after a visit in 1954 he gave interviews describing a country in which criticism was free and progress rapid, statements he later acknowledged had been the politeness of a guest converted by his hosts into propaganda, and which his critics, Raymond Aron foremost among them, preserved as the standing evidence of his credulity. In the second phase, opened by the Soviet intervention in Hungary in November 1956, he broke: the long essay The Ghost of Stalin, serialised in his journal across that winter, condemned the intervention as a crime, held that socialism could not be built by tanks against a people's movement, and severed his alliance with the French party, which had endorsed the crushing of Budapest. In the third phase, which lasted the rest of his life, he remained a man of the left who condemned each subsequent Soviet use of force, Czechoslovakia in 1968 included, while refusing to convert the condemnations into the anti-communism he regarded as the other camp's recruiting instrument; socialism, he said after Prague, would have to be reinvented by men who had learned what the frozen version had become, a socialism that came in from the cold.

### What did Sartre write in The Ghost of Stalin?

Written in the weeks after Soviet armour crushed the Hungarian rising, the essay condemned the intervention as a crime, rejected the claim that socialism could be imposed on a people by a foreign army, and broke his alliance with the French party that had approved the action. It demanded a socialism answerable to the workers it governed.

The criticisms of this record deserve their full weight, because the defence is too often allowed to consist of the 1956 essay alone. The charges, as his opponents framed them and as the documents support them, are these. First, lateness: the facts about the Soviet camps were available, through defectors, trials, and published testimony, years before Budapest, and Sartre's public silence was not ignorance but policy, adopted for camp reasons his own philosophy could not honourably state. Second, asymmetry: the violence of the Western camp, in Korea, in Indochina, in Algeria, received his immediate and total condemnation, while the violence of the Eastern camp received, until 1956, the benefit of every doubt and the shelter of every context; a moral standard applied by camp is not a standard. Third, the 1954 interviews stand unretracted in their original form in the record; his later explanation, that a guest does not insult his hosts and that the reports were edited, may be true and is certainly convenient, and it asks the reader to weigh the guest's embarrassment against the use made of his words by a police state. Fourth, and most damaging to the philosophy rather than the man: the fellow travelling years contradict the Critique before the Critique was written, since the thinker who would soon analyse how institutions serialise their members had lent his name to the serialisation of judgement itself, treating the party's line as the practico-inert deposit of a wisdom individuals need not re-examine. Sartre's own retrospective judgements were severe, he spoke of those years with a mixture of defiance and shame and never attempted a systematic acquittal, and the honest summary is the double one this profile maintains: the break, when it came, was real, principled, and permanent, and it came later than the evidence required, for reasons his theory explains and his ethics condemn.

The Prague Spring tested the settlement of 1956 and held it. When the Warsaw Pact armies entered Czechoslovakia in August 1968, Sartre condemned the invasion without the hesitations of an earlier decade, travelled to Prague in the months after to meet the writers whose union had been the spring's engine room, and drew from the episode the phrase that summarised his final position on the Soviet state: the socialism that had arrived on foreign tanks had frozen in the cold of its own bureaucracy, and a socialism worthy of the name would have to be reinvented by peoples who had thawed themselves. The phrase's itinerary is worth noting, since it was quoted back at him by both camps, by the party's loyalists as evidence that he had joined the enemy and by the anti-communists as evidence that he had joined reason, while its author meant something more precise and more uncomfortable for both: that the judgement applied to a system built in his own tradition's name, by the standards of that tradition's best self-understanding, and that it therefore obliged the left and not only its opponents. His meetings in Prague also completed a personal symmetry the newspapers missed: the students of Paris had that spring accused him of being a monument, and the writers of Prague received him as a witness, and he preferred, on the record of his own remarks, the second employment.

It should also be recorded what he did not do after 1956. He did not become a Cold War liberal, did not join the congresses and committees of the anti-communist intelligentsia, and did not recant his conviction that capitalism was a system of organised violence whose victims were entitled to his solidarity. The independence cost him both camps' platforms and much of his audience in the years when the camps were the only megaphones. His position, a revolutionary left opposed to both empires, was the position of the tradition this series calls Western Marxism in its political form, and his journal became one of its European houses.

## Colonial violence and the preface to Fanon

The second checkpoint of this profile concerns the text for which Sartre is most often prosecuted in summary accounts: his preface to Frantz Fanon's The Wretched of the Earth, published in 1961, at the height of the Algerian war. The preface is not a summary of Fanon's book, and Sartre, characteristically, used the commission to write his own. Its argument, stated accurately, runs as follows. Colonialism is not a policy but a system of violence that created the colonised as a subhuman category and maintained him there by force; the colonised who takes up arms is therefore not introducing violence into a peaceful order but returning the order's own violence to its source, and in that act he remakes himself as a man, since the humanity the coloniser denied him cannot be received as a gift from the coloniser's conscience but only seized. Sartre addressed the preface over Fanon's shoulder to European readers, telling them that the book was not written for them, that their humanism was the ideology of the theft, and that the violence now shocking them was Europe's own product coming home. The essay remains the most uncompromising statement by a major European philosopher of the case for anti-colonial violence, and the series treatment of [Fanon and Marxism](/2017/02/15/frantz-fanon-and-marxism/) gives the argument its full context in Fanon's own theory.

### What did Sartre argue in his preface to Fanon's book?

That colonial rule was violence in institutional form, that the colonised man's counter-violence was the means by which he re-created himself as human, and that European readers should recognise in the Algerian struggle the return of a violence Europe had exported for a century. The preface radicalised Fanon's argument rather than summarising it.

The criticisms, which the brief requires at full strength and which are substantial, fall into four groups. The first is moral: the preface celebrates violence without supplying a limiting principle, and its logic covers not only the soldier of an occupying army but the settler as such, the civilian, in principle the child; a philosophy that had built its ethics on the other's freedom here argues, in passages of unmistakable rhetorical exaltation, that killing the oppressor is the oppressed man's self-creation, and critics from liberal humanists to veterans of the anti-colonial struggle itself asked what act the argument could still condemn. The second criticism is exegetical: Fanon's book is a clinical and political analysis in which violence is one instrument among others, shadowed by a psychiatrist's knowledge of what violence does to the violent, while Sartre's preface strips the qualifications and keeps the fire; Fanon scholars have spent decades separating the patient's doctor from his Parisian amplifier. The third is political and biographical, and it is the one Merleau-Ponty's shade might press: the preface's author risked nothing in Paris that the Algerians he exhorted did not risk daily in Algiers, and the asymmetry between the philosopher's safety and the fighter's exposure does not refute the argument but does discount its exaltation, as Sartre's sterner critics, including some who shared his anti-colonialism, observed at the time. The fourth comes from inside the tradition: Marxists who held that emancipation is the work of organised masses building a new order found in the preface a voluntarism of the deed, violence as sacrament rather than instrument, and noted that the theory of the group in the Critique, published the year before, had already shown where unmediated fusion ends.

Sartre's defenders answer, and the answers belong in the record beside the charges. The preface was written to break a European audience's complacency during an actual war in which the French state was torturing and killing on a large scale, and its violence of language was calibrated against the violence of the facts it opposed; to demand of it the tone of a seminar is to demand that it fail at its purpose. Its argument, they add, is descriptive before it is celebratory: it claims that colonised people in fact recover agency through resistance, a claim Fanon's clinical chapters support in their own register, and that Europeans who deplore the recovery while enjoying the colony are deploring the symptom of their own conduct. And they note that Sartre's commitment to the Algerian cause was not rhetorical only: he signed the manifesto of the 121 asserting the right of insubordination, supported the networks that aided the FLN, and was twice the target of plastic explosive attacks on his apartment by the settler ultras, which somewhat narrows the distance the third criticism alleges. This profile's obligation is the one the brief sets: the position stated accurately, the criticisms at full strength, and no verdict smuggled into the arrangement of the evidence. The reader who wants the argument's afterlife in the tradition can follow it through the Fanon article linked above; the reader who wants Sartre's late politics will find the Algerian war's aftershocks running through them.

## After the break: the long estrangement from the party

The rupture of 1956 settled the alliance without settling the relationship, and the quarter century that followed has its own pattern, which a complete profile should trace because it contradicts the simpler story of a clean divorce. Through the Algerian war Sartre and the party found themselves on the same side of the barricade more often than either admitted, voting the same condemnations, signing against the same tortures, and the party's press, which continued to abuse his philosophy, learned to quote his politics when the politics were useful. The student May found them on opposite sides again, the party husbanding its organisations against the fused groups he celebrated, and its leader's contempt for the student left answered by his public preference for the students' disorder over the party's order. In the 1970s the estrangement took its final form, which was not enmity but irrelevance in both directions: the party, entering its long decline, no longer needed the shield of his name, and he, aligned with forces that defined themselves against it, no longer needed the discipline of its alliance. What replaced the relationship, in his last decade, was a sympathy his younger self would have classified without hesitation: he lent his name to the defence of dissidents inside the socialist states, to the Czechs of the Charter when their turn came, and to the proposition, which he had resisted at the height of the camps' exposure, that the intellectual's first solidarity is owed to the persecuted of his own camp before the persecuted of the other. The proposition's arrival, thirty years late, is either the record's most hopeful page or its most damning, and this profile, faithful to its method, leaves the valuation to the reader while noting that Sartre himself, in the dialogues of those years, did not flinch from either.

## The traveller: Cuba, China, and the verdicts of the road

Between the break with Moscow's French friends and the explosions of 1968, Sartre's politics were conducted substantially on the road, and the journeys belong in the profile because they show the camp logic dissolving under the evidence of his own eyes, at different speeds in different capitals. He had visited the Soviet Union first in 1954, on the journey whose interviews he later disowned, and returned in the early 1960s as an honoured guest of the writers' unions, received, fêted, and privately more critical than his hosts' communiqués recorded. China he visited in 1955, in the first flush of the People's Republic, and wrote of it with the disciplined admiration of a man determined not to repeat the error of judging a revolution by its enemies' newspapers or its hosts' itineraries, a discipline his critics thought indistinguishable from the error itself. Yugoslavia he praised as an experiment in self-management worth the West's attention. Cuba, which he visited in the spring of 1960, received the most famous of these travel writings, the series of reports on the revolution's first year published in France-Soir under the figure of a hurricane over the sugar crop, in which the island's fused groups, its bearded guerrillas become a government improvising a society, appeared to him as the Critique's hopeful chapters enacted: a people in fusion refusing, so far, to let the institution close over them.

The sequel is the part the enthusiasts omit. Through the following decade Sartre watched the Cuban revolution institutionalise, and when in 1971 the poet Heberto Padilla was arrested and made to confess his counter-revolutionary errors in the manner of an older place and time, Sartre signed the letter of protest that broke his public allegiance to Havana, declaring with his co-signatories that the spectacle recalled the most sordid moments of the Stalinist era whose return they had believed impossible. The letter matters to this profile less for Cuba than for the pattern it completes: Budapest in 1956, Prague in 1968, Havana in 1971, each time the same sequence of hope invested in a revolution's fusion, loyalty maintained through its organisation, and rupture at the moment the institution demanded the ritual of the pledge enforced by terror, the confession, the self-criticism, the defector named enemy. A thinker who had theorised that sequence as the group's life cycle can hardly be accused of failing to understand what he was watching; he can be accused, and was, of needing to watch it three times, in three hemispheres, before the understanding governed the hope. His defenders reply that the hope was the method: a philosophy that begins by granting revolutions their self-understanding and judges them by their own mediations will always arrive at its verdicts later than the philosophy that files them in advance, and the lateness is the mediation charge's shadow, the price of the road he had insisted, against the lazy, that explanation must travel.

## The tribunal and the manifesto: the anti-colonial record in acts

The preface to Fanon was the most famous of Sartre's anti-colonial acts, but it was not the first, the riskiest, or the most sustained, and a profile that rested on it alone would repeat the error of treating his politics as a series of texts. The record in acts runs from the late 1940s, when his journal attacked the French war in Indochina while much of respectable Paris still called it a pacification, through the Algerian years, in which he signed the Declaration on the Right of Insubordination in the Algerian War, the manifesto of the 121 in September 1960, asserting that young Frenchmen called to serve in Algeria had a right to refuse and that those who aided the Algerian fighters were not traitors; the signature exposed him to prosecution, from which the government of the day was widely reported to have recoiled on the ground that one does not imprison Voltaire, and to the plastic bombs of the settler ultras, which twice wrecked his apartment while he worked elsewhere. He wrote in defence of Henri Alleg, the communist journalist tortured by French paratroopers, whose account The Question he helped to publicise when its publisher was seized. After Algeria came Vietnam: with Bertrand Russell, whose name headed the enterprise and whose foundation organised it, Sartre served as executive president of the international tribunal that sat in Stockholm and Copenhagen in 1966 and 1967 to judge American war crimes, a body without powers whose verdict of genocide against the United States government he signed and defended as the judgement of peoples against the silence of institutions.

One episode of the Algerian years deserves separate record because it fixed the meaning of his commitment for the French public more sharply than any manifesto. In September 1960 the trial opened in Paris of Francis Jeanson's network, the French men and women who had carried money and suitcases for the FLN inside France itself, and Sartre, abroad when the proceedings began, sent the court a letter that the defence read into the record: if Jeanson had been able to reach him, and if he had been asked to shelter Algerian militants or to carry their suitcases, he would have done it, and he considered that every Frenchman who refused such aid while his country waged the war was making himself an accomplice of the war. The letter's logic was the philosophy of the situation applied as a legal provocation: the citizen of a state at war does not stand outside the war's acts, and the only neutrality available is the refusal of neutrality. The government that prosecuted the network declined to prosecute its most famous self-accused accomplice, and the asymmetry, applauded by his supporters as proof that the republic dared not jail its conscience, was noted by his critics as proof of something else, that the philosopher's solidarity stopped at the exact frontier where its price would have been collected, since the letter cost its author nothing that Jeanson's defendants were not paying in his place. Both readings have survived because both are supported by the record, and the episode is the tribunal of his reputation in miniature: the act was real, the risk was asymmetrical, and the judgement divides, as so much in this profile divides, on the weight one gives to words that their author was never made to pay for.

The tribunal is worth a paragraph of weighing, since it concentrates the virtues and defects of his political method in a single institution. Its defenders note that it gathered evidence under rules, heard witnesses, published its proceedings, and put into the record testimony that the press of the belligerent nations had underreported; its standard of judgement-by-manifesto, they argue, is the only one available when the actual courts are the property of the accused. Its critics, who included jurists sympathetic to its cause, objected that a tribunal whose verdict precedes its sessions is a theatre of judgement rather than a judgement, that the accused's absence was a choice the organisers made for effect, and that the word genocide, stretched to the Vietnamese case, was spent as rhetoric at the cost of the precision law exists to protect. Sartre's own rationale was characteristic and candid: where institutions refuse their office, intellectuals must improvise counter-institutions and accept the charge of partiality as the price of refusing the greater partiality of silence. The reader will recognise the structure of his whole political life in that rationale, and also its standing vulnerability, which is that the improvised institution inherits the vices of the group in fusion, certainty first among them, without acquiring the disciplines by which real institutions correct themselves. The tribunal outlived its sessions as a model for citizens' tribunals on later wars, a legacy its critics and its heirs continue to divide between them.

## The student revolt, the Maoist years, and the late politics

The events of May 1968 found Sartre, at sixty-three, in the position his theory had prepared for him: the students in the occupied Sorbonne were a group in fusion if France had ever produced one, and he went to them, spoke in the grand amphitheatre, and defended the movement against both the government and the Communist Party, whose suspicion of the students confirmed everything his cycle predicted about institutions facing fusion. The alliance of his last active decade was with the Maoist groups that came out of May, young militants whose papers, factories agitation, and contempt for the established left matched his own late temper. When the editors of the Maoist paper La Cause du peuple were arrested in 1970, Sartre took the post of its director himself, a legal shield that made prosecution awkward, and sold the paper on the street with Beauvoir beside him, the most famous philosopher in France hawking a banned newspaper on the Boulevard Saint-Germain. He co-founded the daily Libération in 1973 as a paper answerable to no party or proprietor. He lent his name, his presence, and his remaining eyesight, which failed him through the same years, to causes that the organised left found embarrassing: prisoners, immigrants, striking workers occupying their factories, the defence committees of the Maoist trials.

His instruments in those years were refusals as often as signatures. In 1965 he cancelled a series of lectures at Cornell rather than speak, honoured and well paid, in the country whose aircraft were then escalating the bombing of North Vietnam, explaining that an intellectual who accepted the platform while deploring the policy would be performing the exact bad faith his philosophy existed to expose; the cancellation was reported on two continents and did what his articles could not, attaching his anti-war position to a sacrifice his audience could price. The gesture's critics observed that the sacrifice was small and the publicity large, which was true and beside the point he would have pressed: a refusal is a serial act by design, addressed to other consciences one by one, and its measure is not the refuser's loss but the copies it finds. He made the same instrument serve against both empires across the decade, declining honours, platforms, and invitations whose acceptance would have enrolled his name in a legitimacy he disputed, until the refusals themselves became a kind of curriculum vitae, the negative image of the honours list he had spent a lifetime not collecting.

The May events themselves deserve the precision the theory supplies. When the students occupied the Sorbonne and the police retook it, when the factories followed and ten million workers struck, Sartre did not hedge in the manner of an institution protecting its assets: he went into the occupied amphitheatre and spoke, he recorded an interview with the student leader Daniel Cohn-Bendit in which the twenty-three-year-old instructed the philosopher, to the philosopher's visible delight, that the movement wanted no fathers, and he used his journal and his name against the settlement that ended the strike as a restoration of seriality by wage agreement. The Communist Party's apparatus, which had negotiated that settlement and denounced the students as adventurers, drew from him the contempt his cycle prescribed for an institution facing a fusion it could not control: here, in the party of the working class acting to disperse the working class's own movement, was the institution's sovereignty photographed mid-gesture. He was sixty-three, half blind, and happier, on the testimony of everyone around him, than he had been in a decade, because the streets of his own city were performing his book. The performance did not last the summer, as his book had also predicted, and the decade that followed, the Maoist alliance, the factory gates, the tribunal at Lens, can be read as the long attempt to refuse the prediction's second half with the energies of its first.

### Why did Sartre lend his name to a banned Maoist newspaper?

Because its editors had been jailed for what it printed, and his name was the one asset the state would hesitate to prosecute. By becoming the paper's legal director he turned his celebrity into a shield for speech he did not control, a use of fame consistent with his refusal of every other institutional honour.

The late politics invites two opposite misreadings, and the profile refuses both. The first treats the Maoist years as a dotage, the old lion patronising the young; the record shows instead a coherent position continuous with the Critique, a preference for fused groups over institutions, for movements over parties, for the moment of collective freedom over its apparatus, held by a man who had theorised exactly why such preferences must disappoint and held them anyway. The second misreading finds in the last decade a sage above politics; the record shows a partisan to the end, signing, marching, funding, and lending himself to specific and contestable causes, including a disastrous softness, in the dialogues of his final months with his last collaborator Benny Lévy, toward formulations his oldest readers found unrecognisable and whose authenticity Beauvoir herself questioned in print. Between the dotage and the sage stands the man the documents show: diminished in sight, undiminished in appetite for the fray, and consistent in the one commitment that organised his whole political life, which was to be found wherever seriality was being overcome. His death in April 1980 brought crowds estimated at fifty thousand into the streets of Paris behind the coffin, a series become, for an afternoon, something very like a group in fusion, in honour of the philosopher who had explained why it could not last. The tradition he left behind is assessed, with his place in it, in the series article on [the New Left and its Marxism](/2016/03/01/new-left-marxism-explained/), where his reception by the generation of May is traced in detail.

## The philosopher at the factory gates: Sartre and the workers

A standing embarrassment of the record, which his enemies used and his friends regretted, is the thinness of Sartre's direct acquaintance with the class in whose name his politics were conducted. The philosopher of the proletariat spent his working life in cafés, editorial offices, and lecture halls; his knowledge of factory life came through militants, journalists, and the inquiries of his journal's reporters rather than through any formation of his own, and the occasions when he addressed workers directly, at the gates of the great Renault works at Billancourt during the disputes of the early 1970s, megaphone in hand before a crowd that included as many photographers as fitters, were received by the party's workers with a politeness that the memoirs of the period record as distinctly cool. The episode is usually told against him, and the standard moral, that his Marxism was a literature in search of a class, contains enough truth to keep. The mediation charge, applied to its author, returns an awkward verdict: the man who demanded that explanation pass through every intermediate term between structure and person organised his own politics across a mediation, the militant intermediary, that he rarely inspected, and the workers of his theory remain, with honourable exceptions in the inquiries he sponsored, closer to a category than to the singular lives his method existed to recover.

The countervailing record should also be stated, since the profile's rule is the whole of it. He put his journal's pages, his money, and eventually his failing body at the service of specific workers' struggles whose names the history books have kept: the miners of the north in their tribunal and their strikes, the watchmakers of Lip who occupied their factory in 1973 and ran it under their own management, an experiment that drew him as the fused group's economics and that he supported in print and in person, the immigrant workers whose hostels and hunger strikes his last decade repeatedly championed. Lip in particular deserves the record's emphasis, because it was the closest his century came to staging his theory's hope: a workforce that refused serial dispersal, governed itself in fusion longer than fusion is supposed to last, and was defeated in the end less by its own cycle than by the practico-inert of the market and the state arrayed outside it, a defeat his categories describe with an accuracy that amounts, in retrospect, to a kind of fidelity. He knew the distance between the café and the gate better than his caricaturists did; it is inscribed in the theory itself, in the intellectual defined as a man whose competence ends where his trespass begins, and in the late interviews' recurring, unappeased question of what a writer's solidarity is worth when it cannot be cashed in a shared life. The question is left open by the record, and this profile leaves it open too, as the honest remainder of the political account.

## What he took from the Maoists: base democracy and the people's tribunal

Sartre's Maoism is often named and rarely specified, and the specification matters because what he took from the young militants of the Gauche prolétarienne was almost the opposite of what the label suggests to a reader who thinks of Beijing. He took, first, an anti-electoralism he had already announced in his own name: elections, he wrote in 1973, in an article whose title called them a trap for fools, are the serial ritual by which a people is dissolved into isolated counters and its sovereignty confiscated for five years at a time. He took, second, the practice of direct intervention at the point of grievance: the inquiry conducted with workers rather than about them, the newspaper written to be sold at factory gates, the refusal of the division between those who think and those who are thought about, which matched the last article of his intellectual's creed. He took, third and most controversially, the form of the people's tribunal, presiding in December 1970 at Lens, in the Pas-de-Calais, over a popular judgement on the mining company whose safety failures had killed sixteen miners at Fouquières earlier that year, lending the proceedings a prosecutor's rhetoric and a philosopher's authority. The tribunal could sentence no one; it could only find, before an audience of miners' families, that the deaths were not accidents but the foreseeable yield of a system that priced coal in men, and its defenders called that finding the beginning of justice while its critics, liberals and communists together, called it the rehearsal of a lynching jurisprudence by other means.

What he did not take is equally diagnostic. From Mao himself, from the Chinese state and its doctrines, he took almost nothing that can be documented: no defence of the Cultural Revolution's campaigns survives in his name comparable to his defences of Havana or his silences on Moscow, and his Maoism was a French affair, a marriage between an elderly theory of the fused group and a young practice that seemed, for a few years, to enact it. The militants, for their part, took from him the shield of his name, the pages of his journal, and a legitimation in the republic of letters that their own resources could never have bought; the exchange was candid on both sides and has been judged accordingly, as the last of his fellow travellings, with the difference that this time the institution he shielded was too young and too weak to serialise him in return. When the Gauche prolétarienne dissolved itself in 1973, judging its own cycle with a lucidity its patron might have dictated, he followed its veterans into the founding of Libération and into the looser campaigns of the decade, on prisons, on immigration, on the factories occupied by their workers at Lip and elsewhere, the causes of a left that had given up parties and had not yet given up.

## The dialogues of the seventies: revolt, power, and the last interviews

The political Sartre of the 1970s survives most vividly in conversation, and the conversations deserve a section because they show the group's life cycle being applied, by its author, to the movements he had joined. In the dialogues published as On a raison de se révolter, conducted with the Maoist militants Philippe Gavi and Pierre Victor, the pen name of Benny Lévy, Sartre subjected the post-1968 left's own experience to his categories without sparing his hosts: the fused groups of May had pledged and organised on schedule, the Maoist formations had reproduced the party form they existed to refuse, and the intellectual's role within them, his own included, was dissected as a problem rather than celebrated as a solidarity. The exchanges are remarkable for their reciprocity: the young militants interrogate the philosopher about the fellow travelling with the same directness he had once reserved for others, and his answers, conceding the silences and defending the choices by the alternatives he had faced, amount to the nearest thing he produced to a political autobiography. Running through them is a late theme the treatises had not developed, the distinction between power and the collective capacity he called le pouvoir as domination versus the fused group's puissance, and a corresponding hope, never systematised, that new forms of organisation might hold fusion's gains without the institution's price, a hope he stated as a question because his own theory gave him no right to state it as a programme.

His final interviews extended the pattern to the edge of scandal. The conversations with Benny Lévy published in the weeks before his death in 1980 presented an elderly Sartre speaking of hope, of fraternity as a value to be founded rather than a terror to be administered, and of a messianic strain in the Jewish tradition in terms that startled readers who had filed him as an atheist materialist; Beauvoir's published doubts about the old man's freedom in the exchange, pressed by a young interlocutor with an agenda of his own, opened a controversy about the ownership of a philosopher's last words that remains unsettled and, on the evidence available, unsettleable. The profile's duty is the record: the dialogues exist, their authenticity as Sartre's considered positions is contested by the person who knew him best, and the fraternity they invoke reads, against the Critique, as either the theory's final correction by its author's conscience or its final temptation, the fused group's warmth remembered by a blind man who could no longer read the pages where he had priced it. Later in the same year as the funeral crowds, the argument about those last words was still running in the journals, which is perhaps the fitting end for a philosopher who held that a life is a totalisation other people finish.

## The intellectual's office: a plea and its critics

Among the roles Sartre played, the one he theorised most carefully was his own. In lectures delivered in Japan in 1965 and published as A Plea for Intellectuals, he defined the intellectual by a contradiction: a man formed by the bourgeoisie as a technician of knowledge, trained to serve its universal pretensions, who discovers in his own formation the gap between the universal he was taught and the particular interests his knowledge serves, and who resolves the contradiction by placing his competence at the service of the classes his class excludes. The intellectual is thus not a sage and not a spokesman but a traitor to his origins with a professional skill: his office is to contest, from inside the apparatus of knowledge, the uses to which that apparatus is put, and his authority extends exactly as far as his competence and no further. The definition was self-descriptive and was meant to be. It licensed the manifestos, the tribunals, the directorship of a banned paper, and it answered the charge of dilettantism in advance: the intellectual intervenes outside his speciality not as an expert on everything but as a citizen whose speciality has taught him how power disguises itself as truth.

The definition drew two criticisms that have outlived it. From the right, it was observed that the theory conveniently canonised its author's own career, converting a series of political choices, some of which this profile has recorded at the price they deserved, into the necessary structure of a vocation, so that to criticise Sartre's positions became, by definitional courtesy, to misunderstand the intellectual's office. From the generation that followed him, Michel Foucault most influentially, came the objection that the universal intellectual, the man who speaks in the name of justice and truth as such, was himself a historical figure in dissolution, being replaced by the specific intellectual who works within a local apparatus, the laboratory, the hospital, the prison, and contests power at the point where his knowledge touches it. The contrast is usually drawn at Sartre's expense, and in part fairly: the man who signed for the universe did sometimes mistake the range of his fame for the range of his knowledge. But the obituary was premature in one respect: the specific intellectuals of the following decades, Foucault among them, performed Sartre's office under new management, intervening on prisons, asylums, and wars with exactly the combination of competence and trespass his plea had described, and the difference between the generations is narrower in practice than in polemic. What the plea retains, beyond its period, is the first half of its definition, the intellectual as a product of a contradiction inside knowledge itself, which remains the sharpest short account of why universities produce their own most dangerous critics.

## The verdict's half-life: structuralism's own old age

The objection's subsequent career belongs to the record, because it illustrates the theory it attacked. Structuralism, the victorious school of the decade after the Critique, underwent within twenty years the full cycle of the group's life cycle as Sartre had described it for movements: a fusion around a few founding texts, a pledge exacted in the currency of vocabulary, an organisation of schools and journals, and an institutional old age in which the method was applied as a filing system by men who totalised nothing, until a younger generation experienced it precisely as Sartre had experienced the Marxism of the manuals, as a laziness with a prestigious accent. Lévi-Strauss ended in the Academy, the institution of institutions; the Althusserian school dissolved into its members' separate paths; and the philosophers who succeeded both, several of them formed in Sartre's own seminars of influence, rebuilt their work around the event, the act, and the subject whose obituary their teachers had signed. None of this refutes the objection, which stands or falls on the arguments this profile has stated. What it establishes is the objection's historical status: not a verdict delivered from outside history but a totalisation from inside it, subject to the same sedimentation as its target, and the spectacle of the two schools ageing in parallel, each becoming the other's caricature in the eyes of the young, is as close as intellectual history comes to a controlled experiment in the theory of the practico-inert. Sartre, who had predicted the pattern in general and exempted no one, least of all himself, had also described his own work's fate in advance, in the enclave doctrine and the theory of ideology as thought's old age; a thinker whose system contains the explanation of its own decay has not thereby escaped the decay, but he has deprived his gravediggers of the satisfaction of surprising him.

## The other Marxists: the objection from the revolutionary left

The liberal and structuralist objections have a third companion that a complete profile must not omit, the objection pressed on Sartre from inside the revolutionary left by men who shared his ends and indicted his means. Its sharpest early form came from the circle of Socialisme ou Barbarie, the group around Cornelius Castoriadis and Claude Lefort that had broken with Trotskyism over the nature of the Soviet bureaucracy, and it was Lefort, who had published in Sartre's own journal, who pressed the methodological point home in the mid-1950s: Sartre's history, for all its dialectical machinery, had no place for the creativity of the working class as a class, since in his pages the proletariat appears either as a series, dispersed and impotent, or as a party's material, organised from above, and the experience by which workers themselves produce new forms of struggle and meaning falls between the two categories unregistered. The charge generalises the mediation complaint against its author with uncomfortable precision. Between the individual project, which his method follows beautifully, and the institution, which his cycle anatomises beautifully, lies the collective subject that Marxism exists to theorise, and the Trotskyist critics of several schools added their codicil: that a philosophy which derives bureaucracy from the group's attempt to survive, rather than from a stratum's material interest in its own rule, has explained the bureaucracy's form while dissolving its sociology, mistaking a universal tragedy for what was also, and decisively, a particular group's usurpation with particular beneficiaries who could be named.

Sartre's defenders answer that the cycle was never offered as a substitute for the sociology of strata but as its precondition, since a stratum can only rule through forms, and the forms' logic, pledge, organisation, sovereignty, is what his theory supplies; and that the creativity Lefort demanded is present in the fused group, the theory's one moment of collective invention, whose fate the analysis then follows. The exchange was never resolved, and it identifies the genuine lacuna a reader should carry away: in the Critique's world, collectivities are born in fusion and die into institutions, and the long middle life of a class, its culture, its memory, its slow invention of itself across generations, the very matter of the best Marxist historiography of his century, has no native category and must be imported. The importation was performed by others, by the historians who read him alongside Gramsci and the English Marxists, and the fact that the system needed their help is the left objection's permanent residue. It is also, the profile notes in fairness, the residue his own definition of an enclave would predict: a supplement that mistook itself, in its author's grander moments, for the whole.

## Aron: the petit camarade as prosecution

No account of the criticisms is complete without the critic who shared Sartre's classroom. Raymond Aron entered the École Normale Supérieure in the same cohort of 1924, sat in the same lectures, and spent fifty years as the anti-Sartre: the liberal sociologist against the revolutionary philosopher, the analyst of ideologies against their most gifted French producer. Their divergence began over the same German philosophy both had imported, Aron having studied in Berlin while the catastrophe assembled and returned a liberal by evidence, and it hardened into the century's neatest intellectual antithesis, two normaliens dividing postwar France between them, the one read wherever ministries were staffed, the other wherever they were stormed. Aron's indictment of Sartre, refined across The Opium of the Intellectuals and the late book-length answer to the Critique, History and the Dialectic of Violence, holds that Sartre's political errors were not accidents of temperament but products of his philosophy: that a dialectic which treats history as totalisation will always excuse the totaliser, that the refusal of analytical reason in politics leaves the intellectual with nothing but allegiance to test the parties by, and that the fellow travelling, the tribunal, and the Maoist old age form a single series whose logic the philosophy supplied and the man merely executed.

The indictment deserves the same full-strength treatment as the structuralist one, because it is the strongest form of the liberal case and because parts of it are true. Aron was right that Sartre's judgements of regimes were worse than his own, right that the Critique's vocabulary can be used to launder any allegiance, and right that the positions of 1952 were a choice of the philosophy's author against evidence available to his schoolfellow in the same newspapers. Where the indictment overreaches is in its symmetry: it cannot explain, on its own premises, why the same philosophy produced the breaks of 1956 and 1971 as reliably as the allegiances of 1952, why the author of the laundering vocabulary kept applying it to his own camp's institutions, or why Aron's own camp, armed with analytical reason and the correct judgements, arrived so often at the defence of colonial wars its philosopher had to spend his honour resisting. The two men made their peace of a kind in 1979, appearing together to appeal for the boat people of Vietnam, the petit camarade and the fellow traveller on one platform in the cause of refugees from a communist state, an image each camp's faithful found clarifying in opposite directions. For this series, their antithesis is the cleanest map of the French century's options: the Marxism of engagement and the liberalism of limits, formed in one classroom, divided by one question, whether history is a tribunal before which scruples must answer or a process whose crimes scruples exist to name.

## The structuralist objection, stated at full strength

The brief requires this profile to state the strongest objection to Sartre's whole enterprise at full strength, and the objection is the structuralist one, since it denies not a conclusion but the possibility of the project. Put at its strongest, in the form Lévi-Strauss and Althusser gave it from opposite directions, it runs thus. A philosophy that begins from the individual project, from consciousness experiencing its freedom, has already decided its answer before the inquiry begins: whatever structures it discovers will be dissolved back into the praxis that supposedly made them, and the dissolution is not a finding but a grammatical consequence of the starting point. History becomes the story of men making themselves, with structures as scenery; the sciences of man, which had learned to explain kinship, language, myth, and economy as systems whose logic operates behind the backs and beyond the intentions of their bearers, are asked to surrender their object to a philosophy of the subject dressed in Marxist vocabulary. The practico-inert, on this objection, is the device that performs the trick: it lets Sartre concede everything the structural analysis asserts, inertia, constraint, the reversal of intentions, while reserving the metaphysical point that all of it is praxis at bottom, praxis stored, praxis alienated, praxis nonetheless. The concession costs him nothing and buys him everything, which is the mark of a device rather than a discovery. Lévi-Strauss closed The Savage Mind with the charge in its most elegant form: Sartre's dialectic is the mythology of a society that takes history as its myth, the cogito enlarged into a philosophy of the epoch, and the Critique's man, totalising his world, is the primitive of a tribe of historians. Althusser's version was colder: the philosophy of consciousness and the project is ideology in the strict sense, a pre-Marxist humanism, and the true theoretical revolution of Marx begins exactly where Sartre's anthropology ends, with processes without a subject. The two versions converge in the confrontation the series maps in [humanist versus structuralist Marxism](/2016/02/15/humanist-vs-structuralist-marxism/), where Sartre stands as the humanist pole's most considerable champion.

### What did Lévi-Strauss object to in the Critique's account of history?

That it enthroned history as the one intelligible totalisation and made the historian's own society the measure of all others. In the closing chapter of The Savage Mind he argued that Sartre's dialectical reason is analytical reason in costume, a philosophy of the cogito projected onto the past, which dissolves structures into the praxis that supposedly authored them.

### Can a philosophy of freedom ground a theory of structures?

That is the objection on which the whole enterprise stands or falls, and the honest answer is divided. Sartre shows that sedimented praxis can mimic structure convincingly, but the structuralist replies that mimicry is not identity: a language's rules constrain speakers who never made them, and deriving that constraint from stored action explains its origin, not its operation.

A younger voice gave the objection its most quoted epitaph. Michel Foucault, who had been formed in the same Parisian field and spent his career building the alternative, described the Critique in an interview as the magnificent and pathetic attempt of a man of the nineteenth century to think the twentieth: magnificent in its ambition to totalise, pathetic in its conviction that the totalisation must pass through a subject's experience. The sentence has done the work of a review in a thousand seminars, and its fairness divides as the rest of the objection divides. As a verdict on the book's moment it is exact: the Critique arrived in 1960 as the supreme statement of a philosophy of consciousness at the hour when the sciences of man were learning to do without consciousness, and its author knew the hour, which is why the book reads in places like a fortress built during the siege it describes. As a verdict on the book's contents it is less exact, since the practico-inert, seriality, and the institution are precisely an attempt to think the decentring of the subject from inside the subject's philosophy, to show how a world of structures is generated by praxis without being governed by any praxis, and Foucault's own later work on discipline and the carceral, whatever its distance from Sartre's vocabulary, inhabits a neighbouring problem: how men are made by arrangements no man designed. The epitaph should therefore be read as the objection's rhetoric rather than its proof, the younger school burying the elder with the honours of war.

The answer Sartre's system gives, stated as strongly as it can be stated, is that the objection confuses genesis with excuse. To say the practico-inert is stored praxis is not to say it is transparent, optional, or obedient; it is to say that its intelligibility is historical, that the flood from the cleared hillside can be understood, step by step, as action returned as fate, and that a structure whose genesis cannot in principle be narrated is a fetish, an idol of the social sciences. The mediation charge returns here with interest: structuralism, for Sartre, is the new laziness, a classification of systems that liquidates the men inside them as thoroughly as the manuals liquidated Valéry. And the concession the brief requires must be made without varnish: the reconciliation of freedom and structure is asserted in the Critique more successfully than it is demonstrated. The practico-inert names the junction with unmatched vividness, but a name is not a mechanism, and at the decisive points, where the theory should show how a totalisation binds men who do not experience it, Sartre's prose ascends into a dialectical lyricism that persuades the persuaded. The second volume, which was to demonstrate the intelligibility of a whole history in the Soviet case, was abandoned unfinished, and its non-existence is the objection's best evidence: the demonstration the system needed was the one its author could not complete. A fair profile leaves the matter exactly there, the objection standing, the answer standing, the unfinished volume standing between them.

## His reading of Marx: the philosopher against the economist's custodians

Sartre's Marx was a selected Marx, and a profile should say plainly which texts did the selecting. The Marx of the 1844 manuscripts, the philosopher of alienated labour and of man as a being who produces himself through his work, stands behind the whole enterprise: the Critique's praxis is that manuscript's labour raised to a system, and the humanism Sartre defended against the structuralists was, at its root, a loyalty to the young philosopher of the species-being against the economist his custodians had embalmed. The Marx of the great historical writings, the Eighteenth Brumaire above all, stands behind the method: Sartre returned repeatedly to that analysis of a revolution devoured by its own borrowed costumes as the model of what concrete Marxist explanation looks like when it is not lazy, an event totalised through its mediations, its classes fractioned, its phrases weighed, its outcome traced to the collision of projects with an inherited practico-inert of institutions and memories. The Marx of Capital he praised and, by the testimony of the work itself, used least: the Critique contains no sustained engagement with the theory of value, the falling rate of profit, or the reproduction schemas that the tradition's economists regarded as the doctrine's scientific core, and his Marxism is accordingly a philosophy of history and action built beside the critique of political economy rather than through it.

The selection was itself a historical event, and Sartre was conscious of standing inside it. The young Marx he championed had been available in full only since 1932, when the manuscripts of 1844 were first published, and the Marxism of his generation was formed in the aftershock of that publication: a doctrine whose founder turned out to have begun as a philosopher of alienation could be reread entire, and the humanist Marxist current to which Sartre belonged, with its counterparts in Yugoslavia, Poland, and the Italy of the operaisti, was the international form of that rereading. His loyalty to the manuscripts was therefore not an eccentricity but a position in the century's central exegetical war, against the custodians for whom the young Marx was a juvenilium the mature science had burned away. What he took from the manuscripts, beyond their vocabulary, was a warrant for his deepest conviction about the doctrine: that its centre is not a mechanism but a drama, man's self-production through labour and the theft of that production by the forms it creates, a drama his practico-inert would restage with the theology removed and the scarcity added. Readers who find the Critique's Marx unrecognisable beside the Capital commentaries are measuring the distance between two legitimate inheritances from the same founder, and the exegetical war between them is the humanist and structuralist confrontation once more, fought this time over the founder's corpse, with Sartre, who despised exegetical wars, conscripted as the humanist army's heaviest gun.

The gap was noticed, and the noticing shaped the reception. Economists of the tradition asked what a Marxism without the labour theory of value was a Marxism of, and the question is not rhetorical: strip the doctrine of its account of exploitation's mechanism and what remains, a philosophy of praxis and scarcity, may be profound without being Marxist in any sense the word's other users would recognise. Sartre's answer, given in interviews and implied throughout, was that the theory of value belonged to the analytical reason of a specific science and stood or fell with that science's work, while the philosophy of Marx, the unsurpassable part, was the dialectical conception of history as totalisation without a totaliser, which no result in economics could touch. The answer relocates Marxism's centre of gravity from the economy to the philosophy of history, and every reader must decide whether the relocation is a rescue or an eviction. His quarrel with Friedrich Engels's dialectics of nature belongs to the same settlement: the attempt to find dialectical laws in physics and biology he dismissed as a metaphysical hypothesis that materialism did not need and could not prove, and in dismissing it he surrendered, knowingly, the doctrine's claim to be a general science of nature and history alike. What he kept was a philosophy, with a philosophy's responsibilities and a philosophy's limits; what the custodians kept was a science, with a science's authority and a science's exposure; and the confrontation between the two, staged in the structuralist section below, is the deepest division inside the tradition this series records.

## Progress without a guarantor: Sartre and the meaning of history

One question was left standing by the theory of totalisation, and Sartre's handling of it distinguishes him from almost every Marxist of his generation: whether history, as a whole, has a direction and a meaning that a philosophy may certify. The orthodoxy he opposed answered yes by construction, its stages ascending under a law the party's science administered. Sartre's answer, worked out in the Critique's second volume and therefore in its unfinished part, is a disciplined refusal. History is intelligible, he argued, piecemeal and in retrospect, wherever a totalisation can be followed; but the sum of totalisations is not itself a totalisation, there is no standpoint outside the process from which its direction could be read, and the appearance of a single meaning is an artefact produced when one totalisation, a church, a party, an empire, temporarily dominates the field and imposes its own story as the story. Progress, where it occurs, is real but local and reversible: a technique, a right, a form of solidarity is won inside a practico-inert field that preserves the conquest as sediment and threatens it in the same act, since every sediment is also a new exigency under which men can be governed. The famous optimism of the tradition, its confidence that the arc of the whole bends toward emancipation, thus survives in Sartre only as a task, the obligation to totalise one's own epoch lucidly, stripped of the guarantee that made the obligation easy to bear.

The position explains the peculiar tone of his political writing, its combination of maximal commitment and minimal consolation, and it locates the exact point of his quarrel with the two philosophies of history on offer in his Paris. Against the Marxism of the manuals, he denied the destination. Against the liberalism of his old schoolfellow Aron, who had built a distinguished scepticism on the denial, he insisted that the denial did not dissolve the obligation to act inside the intelligible processes one could follow, and that a scepticism which ends in administration is a totalisation too, the practico-inert of a class that has made its peace with the sediment. Between a guarantee he would not forge and a resignation he would not sign, the space that remains is the space his whole work occupies, and the Critique's unfinished second volume is its monument: a philosophy of history that could neither complete its demonstration nor abandon it, because abandoning it would have surrendered history to the two custodians, the party and the academy, between whom its author had spent his life refusing to choose.

## Sartre among the Western Marxists: Lukács, Gramsci, and the neighbours

Sartre's relation to the tradition this series surveys was oblique, since he arrived at Marxism from phenomenology rather than from the movement, and read its classics with a philosopher's appetite and a latecomer's gaps. Georg Lukács he treated with a respect sharpened by disagreement: the Hungarian's great book of 1923 had made reification and totality the tradition's central categories, and Sartre's practico-inert and totalisation are unthinkable without that inheritance, yet the two men divided on the novel, on realism, and on the party, and Lukács, from his side, numbered Sartre among the irrationalist temptations of the age, a judgement the series profile of Lukács weighs in its own place. Antonio Gramsci he came to late and incompletely: the Prison Notebooks were appearing in Italian and French through the years of his own major work, and the parallels between hegemony and Sartre's account of ideology as serial thought have been drawn more thoroughly by later scholars than by Sartre himself, who acknowledged the Italian's importance without rebuilding his system around him. The Frankfurt School he knew at a distance and partly through its American exile literature; the affinity between the Critique's analysis of a administered world and the culture industry thesis is real, but it is an affinity of diagnosis rather than of influence, two solitudes arriving at neighbouring conclusions from Hegel by different roads.

The obliqueness matters for placing him in the series' map. The Western Marxists of the earlier profiles were formed inside parties and defeated revolutions; their Marxism was a inheritance administered under conditions of loss. Sartre's was an adoption, undertaken in middle life by the most famous intellectual in Europe, and it shows the marks of its route: stronger on the philosophy of action than on political economy, indifferent to the exegetical wars over Marx's texts that consumed his neighbours, and organised around the question a party formation never poses so nakedly, why a free man should bind himself to a doctrine at all. His nearest kin in the tradition is perhaps the young Lukács of the reification essay, who had also tried to rebuild Marxism as a philosophy of praxis against its custodians, and the kinship throws the difference into relief: Lukács submitted his philosophy to the party and spent decades recanting and revising under its discipline, while Sartre kept his philosophy outside every discipline and paid for the exemption with the isolation this profile has recorded. Between the commissar and the fellow traveller, the tradition exhibits its two permanent temptations, obedience and independence, and exhibits also the price of each, which is why the two profiles belong in one series and why neither man's solution can be recommended to the reader as the other's correction.

## Five misreadings of Sartre's Marxism, corrected

The brief lists the misreadings that follow this thinker, and the profile corrects them in turn. The first is that Sartre abandoned existentialism for Marxism, exchanging one doctrine for another at mid-life. The record shows a subordination, not an abandonment: existentialism was demoted to an enclave and a method, but its central instruments, the project, bad faith, the look, the situation, are all at work inside the Critique, doing the jobs the larger doctrine could not do for itself. The second misreading is that he was a party member or a party philosopher; he held no card, was denounced by the party's intellectuals as often as he defended the party's politics, and built his major work around concepts, the practico-inert and the institution, that the party's doctrine had no place for. The third is that the Critique is unreadable throughout, a thousand pages of neologism; the methodological essay prefixed to it is among the clearest things he wrote, and the unreadability, where it is real, is concentrated in stretches of the analytic chapters rather than spread evenly through the book. The fourth is that the group in fusion celebrates spontaneity, the crowd as redemption; the analysis is principally about the fused group's inevitable decay, and no writer in the tradition is less consoling about crowds. The fifth, and the one this profile has had to resist at every stage, is that his political errors are the interesting thing about him philosophically: the fellow travelling and the preface are part of the record and are stated here at length, but the mediation charge, the theory of the group, and the analysis of seriality would retain their force had their author never signed a manifesto, and they are the reasons the work is still argued with.

## The Anglophone Sartre: translation lag and the uses of a delayed arrival

The English-speaking world received Sartre's Marxism late and in pieces, and the delay shaped a distinct Anglophone Sartre who differs instructively from the Parisian original. Search for a Method reached English readers in 1963, in Hazel Barnes's translation, within a few years of its appearance, and it became the Sartre of the American campus: a method for humanising the social sciences, read by students of literature and history who had no party to be fellow travellers of and who took the mediation charge as a charter for biography and microhistory. The Critique itself waited until 1976 for an English translation, and arrived into a landscape already occupied by the structuralism it opposed, so that its first Anglophone readers met it as a monument of a defeated tendency rather than as a living option, a framing the French debate had not imposed in the same way. Two readings from those years did most to keep the book's claims in circulation. Fredric Jameson's chapter in Marxism and Form treated the Critique as the most ambitious attempt of its generation to think history as a totality without lapsing into mechanism, and carried its categories into literary theory, where totalisation became a working term. Raymond Williams, in Marxism and Literature, absorbed the analysis of seriality and the practico-inert into a cultural materialism that quietly corrected British Marxism's own lazy habits, the reduction of works to their class labels, with instruments borrowed from the French philosopher his tradition rarely acknowledged.

The traffic was not one-way flattery. Anglophone critics pressed the objections the Parisian debate had underplayed: historians asked for the evidence that the mediation method could survive archives thinner than a famous writer's, sociologists such as Anthony Giddens, who used the theory of the group while discarding the dialectical scaffolding, asked whether the machinery explained organisations or merely renamed their familiar hardness in an expensive vocabulary, and Pierre Bourdieu, writing from within French sociology but read everywhere, placed Sartre at the subjectivist pole of a false alternative, the philosopher of the project against the science of the habitus, in a judgement whose influence on Sartre's academic standing in the social sciences is difficult to overstate. The fair summary of the Anglophone career is that the Critique failed as a system and succeeded as a quarry: its concepts are at work in disciplines whose practitioners would not call themselves Sartreans, its method is the unnamed standard of every serious literary biography, and its author is read, in the English-speaking world, the way he read Marx, selectively, combatively, and with the conviction that the unsurpassable parts are the ones that survive their custodians. A delayed arrival, on the evidence, may be the kindest form of reception for a philosophy that distrusted institutions: it reached its English readers without a church to defend it, and has been used ever since by readers who owe it nothing.

## The posthumous Sartre: notebooks, drafts, and the estate of a method

Death did not close the bibliography. The decades after 1980 brought a second Sartre into print from the manuscripts: the Notebooks for an Ethics, drafted in the late 1940s and abandoned, where the philosophy of freedom first wrestled with the morality of oppression and conversion; the second volume of the Critique, edited from the drafts of the abandoned demonstration; war diaries, letters, the unfinished life of Mallarmé, and a late treatise on truth and existence that its author had withheld. Each publication adjusted the portrait. The Notebooks showed that the ethical theory his critics demanded had been attempted at the height of his fame and left in a drawer when history, in the form of the Cold War, made its abstractions feel obscene to him. The Critique's second volume showed the system's test being failed in slow motion, its author circling the problem of how a single history, Stalin's Russia, could be totalised without a providence or a devil to hold it together. And the interviews of the final decade, in which he reassessed the major work with a candour his enemies rarely matched, showed the method applied to its own author: the Critique, he said in the New Left Review conversation of 1969 that remains the best short guide to his intentions, contained a flaw he could name, a tendency to let the dialectic harden into the very scholasticism the mediation charge opposed.

The estate also posed a question the tradition has not settled: what is an interpreter to do with a system whose central demonstration is missing? One school reads the first volume as complete in itself, a prolegomenon whose concepts stand without the application they were built for, and points to their migration into other disciplines as the proof. Another reads the whole project as a ruin, magnificent and instructive, whose unfinished state is its verdict, and points to the same migration as the dispersal of a failure into spare parts. The dispute reproduces, at the level of scholarship, the divided answer this profile gave to the structuralist objection, and it is likely to reproduce it indefinitely, since the evidence genuinely supports both readings and the choice between them depends on what one thinks a philosophy owes its readers: instruments, or a completed world. Sartre's own late judgements lean toward the instruments. He spoke of the Critique as a work whose questions he still believed in, and of its author's younger self with the mixture of loyalty and irony that the progressive regressive method prescribes for any life examined whole: the conditions recovered, the project honoured, the failures left standing where the documents put them.

## The prose of the Critique: difficulty as method and as symptom

No profile of this thinker is honest that spares the reader a warning about the sentences. The Critique is written in a French of legendary resistance: paragraphs that run for pages, subordinate clauses nested like the mediations they describe, a private vocabulary minted at speed and spent without a glossary. Sartre composed at his lifelong pace, thousands of words a day, with the aid of the stimulants his secretary itemised and his physicians deplored, and he revised the big books less than any major writer of the century, so that the Critique reached print with the momentum and the sprawl of a first draft by a genius. Two defences of the difficulty are conventional and both contain something. The first is mimetic: a dialectical subject, in which every term is mediated by every other, cannot be rendered in the analytical sentence, subject verb object, without falsifying it, and the spiralling syntax is the price of writing about totalisation in a prose that totalises. The second is biographical: the difficulty is the sound of a man thinking faster than his secretaries, and the same speed produced, in the essays and the theatre, some of the clearest French of the age, so that the obscurity is a local weather rather than a climate. Against both stands the reader's plain experience, and the verdict of two generations of students who were assigned the book and mined the essay of method instead: whatever its justification, the prose functions in the world as a practico-inert barrier, rationing the theory's audience to the initiated, and a philosophy of mediation that cannot mediate itself to its readers has failed at a task its own standard sets. Sartre, who prided himself on being read by the workers he wrote about and was not, would have recognised the failure as his own categories' revenge, worked matter in the form of six hundred thousand words, governing the intentions of its author long after the author's hand had stopped.

## Reading the Critique: difficulty, survival, and the uses of an unfinished book

The Critique of Dialectical Reason appeared in 1960 as a single huge volume, with a second announced to follow; the second, devoted to the intelligibility of history as a whole and worked at through the Soviet experience, was set aside, and its drafts were published only after Sartre's death. The book's reception divided along the fault this profile has followed: hailed by its first reviewers as the major philosophical event of the postwar decades, it was overtaken within five years by the structuralist ascendancy, which made its vocabulary unfashionable in Paris with a speed that looked, to its author, like the seriality of intellectual life refuting and confirming him at once. The English translation of the first volume, when it finally arrived in 1976, was of a text already assigned to history in its homeland. Yet the concepts have had a second life detached from the book's reputation: the practico-inert circulates in sociology and organisation studies, the group in fusion and the pledge inform accounts of movements and institutions whose authors have never read the surrounding dialectic, and the mediation charge, under other names, is the standing reproach of every biography that files its subject under a class and calls the filing an explanation. Sartre himself, in later interviews, treated the Critique as a work whose programme he still endorsed and whose execution he knew to be flawed, a combination of loyalty and candour that his readers have mostly inherited.

### Why does the second volume of the Critique remain unfinished?

Because its task was the system's own test: to show one history, the Soviet one, as a single intelligible totalisation, neither providence nor chaos. Sartre worked at the demonstration for years and set it aside; the drafts, published posthumously, show the argument circling a problem its author could state better than he could solve.

### Why did Sartre refuse the Nobel Prize?

He declined the literature prize on principle, writing that a writer must refuse to be transformed into an institution, and that the honour would align his name in the cultural contest between East and West. The refusal repeated earlier refusals, of the Legion of Honour after the war and of academic chairs throughout his career.

### Where should a new reader begin with the Critique?

With the methodological essay prefixed to it, Search for a Method, which states the mediation charge, the progressive regressive procedure, and the Valéry illustration in a hundred lucid pages. From there the chapters on seriality and the group in fusion carry the theory's centre; the denser analytics of scarcity can be taken on a second pass.

For the student of the tradition, the order of reading matters more than completeness, since the book defeats more readers by sequence than by difficulty. Begin with the essay of method, where the prose is at its plainest and the polemic gives the concepts their charge; proceed to the Bastille chapters, where the theory of the group is carried by a narrative any reader can test against the history he knows; only then attempt the long analytic movement from need and scarcity, reading it as the foundation the later chapters presuppose rather than as a gate that must be passed before the interesting parts begin. A reader who reaches the pledge and the institution with the queue and the radio audience still in mind holds the whole system in miniature, and holds it in the form its author most valued, as an instrument for reading the organisations of his own world rather than as a monument to be admired from outside.

## What survives: the profile's balance sheet

A profile that has stated the criticisms at full strength owes its reader a closing account of what, after them, is left standing, and the account can be brief because the evidence has already been laid out in both columns. Three assets survive every audit this article has run. The mediation charge survives: no subsequent theory of society has made it respectable again to explain a person, a book, or an event by filing it under a structure, and the demand to pass through the intermediate terms is now so thoroughly absorbed into serious practice that its author's name is often missing from his own victory. The group's life cycle survives: the sequence from fusion through pledge to institution remains the single most powerful account of how emancipatory collectivities harden, confirmed by a century's evidence and unmatched by any rival that explains as much with as little recourse to villains. And the practico-inert survives, under its own name in some disciplines and under aliases in others, as the standing category for the region of social life where intentions go to become necessities. Against those assets the liabilities stand where the objections put them: a philosophy of history whose central demonstration was never completed, a scarcity asked to bear more explanatory weight than a single condition can carry, a politics whose allegiances arrived late at their own corrections, and a prose that rationed the theory's readership to the already converted. The net position is neither the monument his funeral crowds celebrated nor the ruin his obituarists in the structuralist decade announced. It is what his own vocabulary would predict for a body of work: a totalisation, unfinished, whose deposits have passed into the practico-inert of the tradition and now constrain and enable thinkers who never open his books, which is the fate he described for every serious thought, and the only immortality his philosophy of history permits anyone to claim.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The single most effective classroom instrument in this material is the lazy Marxism formulation, because it converts an abstract methodological dispute into a failure every student has committed. Set the sentence on the board, a writer is a petit-bourgeois intellectual, and ask the seminar what has been explained; then ask what remains unexplained, and let the gap between the two answers define the word mediation for the rest of the course. The group's life cycle teaches equally well as a simulation: have the seminar track a movement, a party, or a student society they know through the five stages, and mark the moment where each group traded freedom for duration. The political record should be taught whole, the fellow travelling beside the break, the preface beside its critics, since Sartre's classroom value lies precisely in the impossibility of recruiting him as a mascot. Students who leave able to state Merleau-Ponty's charge and Sartre's strongest reply have learned more political judgement than any verdict on the man could teach them.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research problems on this topic are problems of translation, of drafts, and of a reputation managed by its subject's own polemics. Cite the Critique from the French text where an argument turns on a term, since the English version, admirable as it is, compresses the vocabulary of seriality and the practico-inert in ways that have misled a generation of commentary. The posthumous second volume and the Flaubert drafts are evidence about the system's limits as much as about its claims, and should be weighted as drafts, not as doctrine. For the tradition that formed him and the humanist current he redirected, the series account of [Marxist humanism](/2011/07/15/marxist-humanism-explained/) supplies the background, and the [Western Marxism reading guide](/2016/03/15/western-marxism-reading-guide/) surveys the editions, translations, and archival state of the major works, including the correspondence and the journal files on which any revision of the political record will have to rest. Journalists reaching for Sartre as a symbol of the engaged intellectual should note that the documents complicate the symbol in both directions, and that the complication is the story.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Was Sartre a Marxist?

By his own declaration, yes, with a qualification that defines him. He held Marxism to be the philosophy that expressed his epoch so completely that no successor could arise before the epoch itself was transformed. Around that allegiance he built a lifelong dissent: he never enrolled in a party, treated the official doctrine as a method gone rigid, and offered his own philosophy as a temporary instrument for restoring what the doctrine had lost, its power to explain individual men and women rather than files of class labels.

### Q: How can existentialism be compatible with Marxism?

On Sartre's settlement, the two do different jobs. Marxism supplies the content: the epoch, the mode of production, the class struggle, the large structures within which any life is lived. Existentialism supplies a discipline of attention: the insistence that structures reach persons only through projects, families, childhoods, and choices, and that an explanation skipping those passages has explained a type rather than a person. He expected the smaller doctrine to be absorbed once the larger recovered that discipline, and described his own system in the meantime as an enclave tolerated inside the Marxist city.

### Q: What is the lazy Marxism argument?

It is Sartre's name for a habit he found in the Marxism of his day: settling an explanation before beginning one. The lazy procedure assigns a writer or an event to a class category and treats the assignment as the analysis, dissolving the particular into the general without travelling the road between them. His counter-example was a novelist-poet of bourgeois origin: the label fits him perfectly and distinguishes him from none of the thousands who shared the label and produced nothing, so everything that makes him himself lies in the territory the lazy method refuses to enter.

### Q: What is the Critique of Dialectical Reason about?

The Critique attempts to give Marxism the foundation Sartre thought it lacked: an account of how history can be made by human action and yet confront its makers as an alien force. Its machinery runs from need and scarcity through worked matter, serial dispersion, and the formation of groups, to the hardening of groups into institutions. The first volume builds the machinery piece by piece; the second, meant to prove that one whole history could be understood through it, was abandoned and published from drafts after his death.

### Q: What is the practico-inert?

The practico-inert is matter that human work has already shaped and that then turns back on human beings with requirements of its own. Roads dictate journeys, machines dictate rhythms, prices dictate decisions, and landscapes cleared by generations of sensible farmers dictate floods to their grandchildren. Nothing in the field is anyone's intention, yet everything in it is congealed intention, which is why Sartre treats it as the level where freedom is stored, combined with the freedom of strangers, and handed back as necessity.

### Q: What is a group in fusion?

A group in fusion is what a passive crowd becomes when a shared danger or an urgent task abolishes its separateness. In the fused state there is no leader and no programme yet: each member acts, and finds his own act multiplied and returned by all the others acting likewise, so that for a brief interval the distinction between my project and our project disappears. Sartre's model case is a city quarter arming itself as royal troops approach. His interest lies less in the blaze than in what the group must invent to survive its own cooling.

### Q: How does Sartre explain bureaucratic degeneration?

Not by betrayal and not by the wickedness of secretaries. A group that has tasted fusion and refuses to dissolve must guarantee its own future, so it swears permanence and backs the oath with sanctions against deserters. Permanence requires coordination, coordination requires assigned posts, assigned posts harden into hierarchy, and hierarchy finally concentrates in an office that embodies the group for members who no longer meet as equals. Bureaucracy is thus the price a collectivity pays for outliving its founding moment, charged in instalments of freedom.

### Q: Why did Sartre break with the Communist Party?

Precision matters here: he broke not as a member, which he never was, but as the party's most prominent ally. The rupture came when Soviet forces crushed the Hungarian rising of 1956 and the French party approved the crushing. In a long essay written that winter he condemned the intervention as a crime, argued that socialism imposed by foreign armour was a contradiction, and ended the alliance he had maintained through the earlier Cold War years, when he had held that criticism of the party served only the opposing camp.

### Q: What did structuralists say against Sartre?

They said that his foundation was sand. A philosophy beginning from individual consciousness and its projects, they argued, will always dissolve the structures it claims to respect, because it treats them as congealed action awaiting reactivation by a subject. Anthropology and linguistics had learned to study systems whose logic no subject intends or surveys; Sartre's dialectic, in the most famous formulation, was a mythology of history fit for a society that worships its own past. The parallel Marxist version dismissed his humanism as ideology and his subject as a survival from before Marx's theoretical revolution.

### Q: Is Sartre's Marxist work still read?

In parts, and the parts are not the ones his fame would predict. The methodological essay is assigned wherever Marxist theory is taught, because its central complaint has never been answered or outlived. The immense treatise is more often mined than read entire: its vocabulary of worked matter, serial dispersion, and fused groups circulates in sociology, organisation studies, and political theory, detached from the dialectical scaffolding that supported it. The Flaubert study is revered as a monument and finished by few. The reputation breathes in cycles, but the instruments keep working in other hands.

### Q: How does the mediation charge grade a biography that skips its links?

Harshly, and by a simple test: does the book travel from the general to the singular through documented intermediate terms, family formation, schooling, profession, the instruments an epoch put within reach, or does it leap from a class label to a finished character and call the leap an explanation? A biography that skips its links has classified its subject, not explained him, and on Sartre's standard it fails regardless of its elegance. The charge grades leniently only where the archive itself is destroyed, since the standard condemns laziness, not poverty of evidence.

### Q: What should a student separate between existentialism as method and Marxism as framework?

Separate the instruments from the architecture. The framework is the set of claims about how societies are built and move: production, class, epoch, the weight of inherited conditions. The method is a set of procedures for keeping those claims honest at the scale of a single life: regress from the finished work to its conditions, then advance again through the person's project, refusing to let either movement swallow the other. A student who confuses them will either defend the procedures as if they were a politics, or swallow the politics without ever testing it on a person.

### Q: Why did the group become his model of history in miniature?

Because the group's career compresses the whole tragedy his philosophy describes into a span one episode can hold. Within a single arc, free action creates unity, unity invents instruments to preserve itself, and the instruments end by governing their creators as an alien power. That is, for Sartre, the plot of history itself: praxis depositing itself in worked matter and returning as fate. A stormed fortress and a sworn brotherhood therefore teach what volumes of abstraction cannot, the mechanism by which emancipation manufactures its own cage while meaning to do the opposite.

### Q: How does scarcity function in his account of conflict?

Scarcity works as the silent premise beneath the drama. Where the means of life fall short of need, each person encounters every other first as a rival for what both require, and this arithmetical hostility precedes any ideology, hatred, or decision. Conflict on this account does not need villains; it needs a shortfall and a crowd. Sartre insists the condition is historical rather than metaphysical, a feature of every society so far rather than of human nature as such, but within history it is near enough to universal to explain why cooperation must be achieved against the grain of the material situation.

### Q: What did the Flaubert study attempt that the earlier works had not?

The earlier books sketched the method and applied it at essay length; the Flaubert study staked everything on one exhaustive case. It asked what can be known about a single man if the inquirer refuses every shortcut: the surgeon's household and the favoured elder brother, the slow child constituted as the family idiot, the collapse that ended a legal career, the class anxieties of a provincial bourgeoisie after a failed revolution, all regressed, then all advanced again through the project that converted a wounded child into the author of Madame Bovary. Nothing on that scale had been attempted in the tradition.
