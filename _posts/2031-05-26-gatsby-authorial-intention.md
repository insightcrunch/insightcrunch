The authorial intention debate in Gatsby asks one of the sharpest questions a reader can put to a novel. When the author's own letters describe what he meant, and his drafts show him changing his mind, do those records govern what the book means, or do they sit outside the meaning entirely? Fitzgerald left an unusually rich paper trail around The Great Gatsby. His correspondence with his editor discusses the title, the structure, and the revision of the proofs; his manuscripts and galleys record passages added, cut, and rewritten before publication in 1925. That documented record sharpens a disagreement that literary theory has carried for a century: whether authorial intention settles meaning, informs it, or has no claim on it at all.

This article maps that disagreement evenhandedly, position by position, so that the reader can see what each side claims and how each side handles the same documented evidence. The letters and the final page revisions are the evidence at issue here, not the subject of fresh coverage; they are catalogued in the companion articles, and this article treats them as the shared record on which the competing positions operate. What follows is a debate map, not a verdict. Each position is presented at its strongest, the handling of the Gatsby evidence is stated plainly, and the article declines to declare a winner, because the debate remains genuinely live.

## The Authorial Intention Debate in Gatsby: The Question That Divides the Field

Interpretation begins, for most readers, with an apparently simple assumption: the meaning of a book is what its author meant. The assumption feels natural because conversation works that way. When a friend says something ambiguous, the natural response is to ask what the friend meant, and the friend's answer settles the matter. The intentionalist case in literary theory extends that conversational habit to literature. It holds that a text means what its author intended it to mean, that the author's purpose is the proper standard of interpretation, and that documented statements by the author about the work therefore carry a special authority no other evidence can match. Against that case stands a second position, equally confident, which argues that the analogy with conversation breaks down the moment a work is published. A novel is not a remark in a dialogue. It is a public object made of language, addressed to readers the author will never meet, operating through conventions the author did not invent. The anti-intention case holds that meaning lives in what the words accomplish in that public space, and that the author's private designs, however well documented, cannot govern the public life of the work.

The authorial intention debate in Gatsby is the local form of that general dispute. It takes the familiar shape of a set of linked questions. Do Fitzgerald's letters to his editor about the novel's making tell readers what the novel means? Do the documented revisions, the added and cut passages, the deliberated titles, and the reshaped ending reveal the author's settled design, and does that design bind the interpretation? Or do the letters and drafts belong to the history of the book's production, interesting and illuminating in their own right, without authority over the meaning of the published text? The debate sharpens around Gatsby because the evidence is unusually good. Many novels leave behind almost nothing of their making; Gatsby leaves behind correspondence, manuscripts, and proofs that show deliberation in progress. That richness of evidence is precisely what makes the theoretical question unavoidable. When the record is thin, readers can ignore it. When the record is rich, readers must decide what it is for.

Three common mistakes distort the debate before it begins, and clearing them is the first work of any honest map. The first mistake is to treat the question as settled by fiat, assuming either that intention obviously governs meaning or that intention is obviously irrelevant. Both assumptions close the inquiry the map is meant to open. The second mistake is to confuse the positions with caricatures, attributing to one side a claim it does not make: the anti-intention case does not hold that authors are ignorant of their own books, and the pro-evidence case does not hold that a letter proves an interpretation by itself. The third mistake is to suppose that more evidence resolves the disagreement. The dispute is not about whether the letters exist or what they say; it is about what kind of authority they carry. Both sides agree on the documentary facts. They disagree on the theory that organizes those facts, which is why the debate survives every new archival discovery.

The conversational analogy deserves a closer look, because the intentionalist case stands or falls with it. In ordinary speech, the speaker is present to be asked, the context is shared, and the utterance vanishes as it is made. A novel reverses all three conditions: the author is absent, the readers are unknown and dispersed across time, and the utterance persists as a durable object that outlives every context of its making. The anti-intention case argues that these reversals break the analogy at the point that matters. Asking what Fitzgerald meant is not like asking a friend what she meant, because the friend can answer and the novel cannot, and because the novel's persistence in language gives it a life no conversational remark possesses. The intentionalist reply is that the differences are of degree rather than kind: readers of letters, diaries, and historical documents routinely recover communicative intentions across absence and time, and there is no principled reason novels should be exempt. The analogy is thus itself contested ground, and the map notes it as one of the places where the debate's deepest disagreement lives.

Why does any of this matter beyond the seminar room? Because the question of intention governs what counts as evidence in every interpretive dispute about the book. When two readers disagree about a passage, the first move each makes is to reach for support, and what each reaches for depends on the position held. The intentionalist reaches for the letters; the anti-intention reader reaches for the text's patterns; the hypothetical intentionalist reaches for both, ordered by the hypothesis. The debate is therefore not an abstract ornament on interpretation but its operating system, the set of rules that determines which moves are legal. A reader who has never heard of Wimsatt and Beardsley is still playing by some version of these rules, and the map's purpose is to make the rules visible so the play can be deliberate.

### Why does Gatsby sharpen the authorial intention debate?

Gatsby sharpens the debate because it pairs a rich documentary record with a contested text. Fitzgerald's letters record his deliberations over the title, the structure, and the proofs, tempting readers to treat his statements as the tiebreaker. Yet both sides read that same record consistently and reach opposite conclusions, so the evidence intensifies a theoretical dispute it cannot settle.

Neither reading can be refuted by pointing at the documents, since the dispute concerns what the documents mean for interpretation, and that question is theoretical rather than archival. The debate therefore stays live precisely where the evidence is best.

That point deserves emphasis because it runs against the instinct that better archives make better answers. A fuller record of Fitzgerald's deliberations does not narrow the theoretical gap; it widens the space in which the theory operates. Consider the title deliberation, which the documented correspondence records. The intentionalist case treats the considered alternatives as evidence of the author's settled design, a window into what he wanted the book to do. The anti-intention case treats the same deliberation as evidence of how fluid the design remained, and how little the final choice depended on any single controlling purpose. Both readings are consistent with the documentary facts. The facts do not select between them because the selection is done by a theory of meaning, not by an inventory of letters. This is the structural reason the debate persists. Evidence answers the question "what happened"; the debate asks the question "what follows from what happened", and no archive can answer the second question by itself.

The same structure repeats across the whole documentary record. The manuscripts show passages cut and passages added; the proofs show late changes; the correspondence shows doubts and second thoughts. Each side of the debate has a standing interpretation of each kind of evidence, and those interpretations were formed before any particular document was opened. The intentionalist case approaches a revision as a decision that reveals purpose; the anti-intention case approaches the same revision as an event in the production history that the text has absorbed and superseded. The dispute is therefore not empirical but principled. It concerns the standing of authorial evidence in interpretation, and principled disputes are not settled by exhibits. They are settled, if they are settled at all, by argument about what interpretation is for, and that argument has no archive.

## The Terms of the Dispute, Defined

Debates go astray when their key terms shift meaning mid-argument, and the intention debate has five terms that are routinely used in more than one sense. Defining them at the outset does not favor any side; it gives each side a stable vocabulary in which its claims can be stated and tested. What follows is a working glossary for the map, with each term anchored to the Gatsby case.

Intention, in the debate's technical sense, is not a private mental snapshot, a moment of wishing captured before writing. It is the author's communicative purpose as it can be inferred from public evidence: the words, the revisions, the correspondence, the context of publication. This definition matters because the crudest version of the dispute treats intention as something locked inside Fitzgerald's head in 1924, either recoverable or not. The serious positions on both sides work with the inferred sense. The intentionalist case holds that the inferred purpose is the meaning; the anti-intention case holds that the inferred purpose, however well supported, is not the standard of meaning. When the debate is conducted with the snapshot sense of intention, both sides end up arguing about psychology. When it is conducted with the inferred-purposes sense, the argument stays where it belongs, on the standing of evidence.

Meaning is the most contested term of all, and the positions divide over it more deeply than over any other. For the intentionalist case, meaning is what the author meant the words to convey, a property the text has in virtue of its maker's purpose. For the anti-intention case, meaning is what the text accomplishes in public language, a property the work has in virtue of its words and their effects on readers. These are not two names for one thing. They are two different candidates for the object of interpretation, and much of the debate's intractability comes from the fact that each side's arguments presuppose its own candidate. A later section takes up Hirsch's attempt to discipline the term by splitting it in two; for now, the map notes that whenever a participant says "the meaning of the novel," the first question to ask is which of the two candidates is meant.

Evidence, in this debate, is any document or fact that bears on an interpretive claim, and the crucial point is that evidence does not interpret itself. The letters, the drafts, the proofs, and the publication facts are all evidence in this sense. What they are evidence of depends on the theory: the intentionalist treats a revised sentence as evidence of purpose, while the anti-intention reader treats it as evidence of process. The documents are shared; their evidentiary role is not. This is why appeals to "the evidence" in the debate so often miss their target. There is no neutral pile of evidence that both sides read the same way. There is a shared archive read through incompatible theories of what archives are for, and the theories do the work that the word "evidence" seems to promise the documents will do alone.

Authority is the standing a piece of evidence has to settle a dispute. A letter has authority, in the relevant sense, when citing it ends the argument about what a passage means. The intentionalist case grants the documented statements a high degree of authority, though not an unlimited one: a single casual remark does not outweigh the text, but the convergent testimony of letters, drafts, and proofs constrains the acceptable readings. The anti-intention case grants the same documents zero authority in this sense while granting them full authority as historical evidence about the making. The distinction between being informative and being authoritative is the most useful tool in the whole map. Nearly every confusion in the debate comes from sliding between the two: from "the letters are illuminating" to "the letters decide," or from "the letters do not decide" to "the letters are worthless." The positions, stated carefully, never make those slides.

Interpretation is the activity the whole debate is about: the production of warranted readings of the text. The positions disagree about what warrants a reading. For the intentionalist, a reading is warranted when it recovers the author's communicative purpose, with the documentary record as the check. For the anti-intention case, a reading is warranted when it is supported by the public features of the work, with the author's statements as optional illumination. For hypothetical intentionalism, a reading is warranted when it matches the best hypothesis an ideal reader would form. These are different success conditions, and they explain why the debate's participants can each claim to be doing interpretation while doing such different things. The glossary does not resolve which success condition is correct. It makes the disagreement legible, which is the precondition of arguing about it honestly.

## The Anti-Intention Case

The position against authorial intention entered literary theory in its canonical form in 1946, when W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe C. Beardsley published the essay "The Intentional Fallacy" in the Sewanee Review. Their argument, which this article paraphrases rather than quotes, begins from a distinction between the work as a public object and the author's private design. A poem, they argued, is a linguistic artifact that belongs to the public once it is published. It detaches from its maker and takes on a life in the language, judged by what it achieves rather than by what its author hoped it would achieve. To judge the work by the author's intention, on this view, is to confuse two different things: the design of the making and the success of the thing made. The fallacy, in their framing, is not the ordinary act of wondering what an author meant. It is the critical move of treating intention as the standard of judgment, so that a work succeeds when it matches its design and fails when it departs from it.

The argument carries several strands that later writers developed in different directions. One strand concerns availability: the author's intention, as a private mental state, is not directly available to readers, and the documents that survive are themselves texts requiring interpretation. A letter about the novel is not the intention laid bare; it is another piece of writing, written for another audience, in another context, and it must be interpreted with the same care as any text. A second strand concerns relevance: even a perfectly known intention would not settle what the work means, because works accomplish things their makers did not plan, and readers legitimately respond to what the work does. A third strand concerns the public character of language: words carry meanings established by convention, not by private decree, so an author cannot simply intend a new meaning into a text by wishing it. The anti-intention case does not deny that authors have intentions, or that knowing them can be interesting. It denies that intentions govern meaning.

Applied to Gatsby, the position has a characteristic shape. The documented letters, on this view, are biographical and historical evidence about the making of the book. They can illuminate the composition, correct factual errors about the process, and enrich the reader's sense of the author at work. What they cannot do is fix the meaning of the published novel. The final text is the public object, and its meaning is a matter of what its language accomplishes for readers, including meanings Fitzgerald may not have foreseen. When the correspondence discusses the title or the structure, the anti-intention reader treats those remarks as one context among others, interesting for the history of the book but without authority over interpretation. The position is often misread as hostility to the author or to historical knowledge. It is neither. It is a claim about the proper standard of judgment: the work is judged by what it does, not by what its maker designed it to do.

A frequent objection to the position is that it seems to make the author's words about the work inadmissible, as though readers were forbidden from caring about the letters. The position does not go that far, and its defenders say so plainly. Interest in the author's statements is permitted; what is forbidden is treating them as the court of last resort. The distinction matters for how the position is stated fairly. The anti-intention case allows a reader to learn from the correspondence, to find it illuminating, even to change a reading in light of it. It only refuses the step of saying that the letter settles the reading. That refusal is the whole of the position, and it is worth stating with care, because caricatures of the position as indifference to history are the most common way the debate is distorted.

### Why do critics who reject intention still cite Fitzgerald's statements?

They cite the statements because the position forbids judging by intention, not learning from it. A letter can correct a factual error, illuminate the conditions of composition, or suggest a line of attention the reader then tests against the text. What the anti-intention case refuses is only the further step of letting the letter decide the interpretation.

This distinction answers the charge of inconsistency that is sometimes leveled at the position. There is no contradiction between consulting the archive and refusing it authority. Historians consult archives constantly without treating any single document as decisive, and the anti-intention case asks literary readers to adopt the same discipline. The letter is a datum, not a verdict. When a critic notes that the documented correspondence discusses the novel's title, the note can sharpen the reader's attention to how the title works in the text. The sharpening is legitimate; the authority is not. Keeping the two apart is the practical skill the position demands, and it is also the skill most often neglected when the debate is conducted casually, where a striking letter is waved as though it ended discussion.

## The Pro-Evidence Case

The position in favor of authorial evidence begins from the opposite intuition and builds it into a theory. On this view, meaning is a matter of communication, and communication is a matter of what a speaker or writer intends a hearer or reader to take from the utterance. The intentionalist case, associated in its canonical form with E. D. Hirsch's Validity in Interpretation of 1967, holds that the meaning of a text is the meaning its author intended, and that interpretations are valid when they recover that intended meaning. The argument rests on a claim about what distinguishes meaning from other properties of a text. A text can have many effects, many resonances, many uses. What makes one of those the meaning, on this account, is that the author meant it. Without the anchor of intention, the position holds, interpretation loses its object and becomes a record of readers' responses rather than an account of what the work says.

The position draws strength from the analogy with ordinary communication that the anti-intention case rejects. When a reader asks what a passage means, the intentionalist holds, the reader is asking what the author meant by it, and any answer that ignores the author has changed the subject. The documented record then takes on a central role. Letters, drafts, and revisions are not production history external to the meaning; they are the best available access to the meaning itself. Fitzgerald's correspondence with his editor, on this view, is not background color. It is evidence about what the author intended the book to be, and it therefore constrains which interpretations count as valid. A reading that the documented record contradicts is not merely unpersuasive; it is wrong about what the text means.

Applied to Gatsby, the intentionalist reading of the archive has a characteristic confidence. The letters discussing the title are taken as direct evidence of the author's design, showing what Fitzgerald wanted the book to foreground. The revisions recorded in the manuscripts and proofs are taken as decisions that reveal purpose, each change a small declaration of what the author meant the passage to do. The final shape of the text, on this view, is the shape the author's intentions gave it, and the critic's task is to recover those intentions as fully as the record allows. Where the anti-intention case treats the published text as having superseded its making, the intentionalist case treats the making as the explanation of the text, so that the fullest understanding of the novel runs through the fullest understanding of what Fitzgerald was trying to do.

The position is often misread as claiming that a letter proves an interpretation all by itself, as though quoting the author's statement ended the argument. Its defenders reject that caricature. The intentionalist case holds that authorial evidence constrains interpretation, not that any single document delivers a verdict. The letters must themselves be interpreted, weighed against one another, and tested against the text; a statement made in passing to a friend carries less weight than a deliberated decision recorded in proofs. The position also distinguishes between the author's intention and the author's wishes about reception. What matters is the meaning the author intended the words to carry, not whether the author hoped readers would admire or condemn the characters. Stated at its strongest, the case is not a crude appeal to authority but a disciplined program: recover the author's communicative intention from all the evidence, and let that recovery bound the interpretations the text can support.

A standard objection to the position is that it makes meanings private and inaccessible, since intentions are mental states that readers cannot inspect directly. The intentionalist answer is that intentions are inferred from public evidence, the same way any communicative intention is inferred in ordinary life. Readers infer what a writer meant from the words, the context, and the documentary record, and the inference is defeasible but genuine. Another objection is that authors' intentions are often vague, shifting, or contradictory, so that no single intended meaning exists to be recovered. The intentionalist answer is that interpretation is an evidence-weighing activity, and that ambiguity in the record produces ambiguity in the warranted conclusions rather than refuting the standard. The position does not promise certainty. It promises a standard: the interpretation is better when it is closer to what the author meant.

### How do intentionalists use the revision record?

Intentionalists read each cut, addition, and rearrangement as a decision revealing purpose. A passage added in proof shows an intention clarified; a passage cut shows one refined; a relocated passage shows one redirected. The record is not a neutral chronicle but layered testimony, and the intentionalist program reads through it to the communicative purpose it documents.

The use is disciplined rather than mechanical. The intentionalist does not treat every alteration as equally revealing. A late change made under the pressure of a deadline may reflect circumstance rather than purpose, while a change returned to across several drafts looks like a deliberate clarification. The case also distinguishes between revisions that change meaning and revisions that change expression. Both are evidence, but they are evidence of different things: the first of what the author wanted the passage to say, the second of how the author wanted it said. This discrimination is where the position shows its strength as a method. It does not wave the archive at the reader. It reads the archive as a record of choices, and it builds the interpretation from the choices the author made.

## Meaning Against Significance

The most disciplined attempt to stabilize the debate's central term came from the intentionalist side itself. In Validity in Interpretation, published in 1967, E. D. Hirsch drew a distinction that every participant in the dispute has had to reckon with since: the distinction between meaning and significance. Meaning, for Hirsch, is the author's intended verbal meaning, the stable content the author meant the words to convey. Significance is the relationship between that meaning and a reader, a situation, or a later context: what the meaning implies for someone, how it bears on a new question, what it comes to in changed circumstances. The distinction is designed to give each side part of what it wants. The intentionalist keeps meaning anchored to the author; the anti-intention reader keeps significance open to the public life of the work.

### Why does Hirsch separate meaning from significance?

Hirsch separates them to rescue interpretive stability without denying that works take on new life. Meaning, the author's intended verbal meaning, does not change when readers change; significance, the bearing of that meaning on new readers and new questions, does. Later generations can thus discover genuine novelty in Gatsby without claiming its meaning has shifted.

The novelty belongs to significance, the new relationships between the old meaning and new circumstances. The separation lets the intentionalist case acknowledge the full public history of the book's reception while holding the line on what interpretation must recover. Meaning is the author's; significance is everyone's.

The distinction does real work on the Gatsby evidence. Consider the documented deliberation over the book's title. On Hirsch's terms, the meaning of the chosen title is what Fitzgerald meant the words to convey, and the correspondence is evidence of that meaning. The significance of the title is another matter: what the title has come to evoke for readers who know the novel's canonical status, its film adaptations, and its place in the culture. Those significances are real and worth studying, but they are not the meaning, and confusing them is the characteristic error Hirsch wanted to prevent. The same analysis applies to the famous closing. Its meaning is the author's intended verbal meaning, recoverable in principle from the text and its context; its significance includes everything the passage has gathered in a century of quotation, teaching, and allusion. The distinction organizates the archive as well: letters and drafts are evidence of meaning, while reception history is the record of significance. A worked example makes the machinery visible. Take the green light at the end of Daisy's dock. Its meaning, on Hirsch's terms, is what Fitzgerald meant the image to convey in its textual place: a distant, yearned-for object tied to Gatsby's longing, established by the sentences that surround it. Its significance includes everything the image has gathered since: its career as the novel's most quoted symbol, the uses critics have made of it, the resonance it carries for readers who encounter it first as a cultural emblem and only later in the book. A dispute about the green light can therefore be diagnosed with the distinction. If the disputants disagree about what Fitzgerald meant the image to convey, they are arguing about meaning, and the letters and drafts are relevant evidence. If they disagree about what the image has come to signify for later readers, they are arguing about significance, and the reception record is the relevant evidence. The distinction does not tell them who is right, but it tells them what kind of argument they are having, which is often the more valuable service.

The anti-intention reply to the distinction is that it concedes the interesting half of interpretation while guarding an empty fortress. If significance is where the public life of the work happens, where readers actually live with the book, then making meaning the author's private property leaves interpretation's real work unaccounted for. The 1946 essay's defenders argue that what readers care about, argue about, and teach is overwhelmingly significance in Hirsch's sense, and that a theory which places that outside meaning has defined its central term so narrowly that it no longer describes the activity of reading. On this view, the distinction is a terminological maneuver: it lets the intentionalist claim victory by definition, assigning everything the other side cares about to a category called significance and then declaring meaning settled. The dispute then relocates rather than resolves, becoming an argument about whether the distinction itself is legitimate.

The hypothetical intentionalist has a characteristic use for the distinction as well. The ideal reader's hypothesis concerns meaning in Hirsch's sense: what the author is most reasonably taken to have meant. But the hypothesis is formed from public evidence and is answerable to the text, so the resulting meaning is never a private possession. Significance then names the further step, the ways the hypothesized meaning bears on new contexts. This use shows how the distinction can serve a middle position rather than the strong intentionalism Hirsch intended. It also shows the distinction's flexibility, which is both its strength and its vulnerability. A conceptual tool that every side can wield is a tool that settles nothing, and the meaning-significance distinction has become part of the debate's shared vocabulary precisely because it is useful to all parties and decisive for none.

There is a further subtlety worth mapping, because it is where the distinction meets the Gatsby record most sharply. Hirsch allowed that an author's intended meaning can be richer than the author's conscious formulation: a writer can mean more than he knows he means, where the "more" is still anchored in the linguistic and contextual resources the author commanded. This allowance lets intentionalism accommodate discoveries in the text that the letters do not anticipate, without surrendering the standard. The anti-intention case reads the same allowance as a collapse of the position: if the author's meaning can exceed the author's awareness, then the anchor to intention no longer constrains anything, and the theory has smuggled in the very public meanings it claimed to exclude. Each side, once again, has a coherent story. The distinction between meaning and significance does not adjudicate between them; it gives them a sharper vocabulary in which to disagree.

## The Gatsby Evidence Each Side Must Handle

The documented record around Gatsby is the shared ground of the debate, and a fair map states what each side does with each part of it. The correspondence with the editor discusses the title, the structure, and the handling of the proofs; the manuscripts and galleys record the deliberated revision of passages including the closing pages; the alternate titles considered for the book show the author's attention to how the work would be framed. The detailed inventory of that record belongs to the companion articles: the [letters article](/2031/05/19/fitzgerald-letters-gatsby/) maps the documented correspondence, and the [composition article](/2031/03/31/gatsby-final-page-composition/) traces the final page's assembly. This section takes the record as given and asks what each position makes of it, because the debate is about the standing of the evidence rather than its contents.

The table below pairs each major position on intention with its core argument and its characteristic handling of the Gatsby evidence. It is the article's findable artifact: a one-glance reference for what each side claims and what it does with the letters, drafts, and revisions.

| Position on intention | Core argument | Handling of the Gatsby evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The intentional-fallacy position | Intention is neither available nor a proper standard; the work is judged by what it achieves as a public object | The letters and revisions are production history, illuminating the making but without authority over the meaning of the published novel |
| Intentionalism | Meaning is the author's intended meaning; interpretation is valid when it recovers that intention | The correspondence, drafts, and revisions are the primary evidence of design and constrain which interpretations count as valid |
| Hypothetical intentionalism | Meaning is what an ideal reader would hypothesize the author intended, given the work and its context | The letters and revisions inform the hypothesis but do not settle it; the public record of authorship matters more than private statements |
| The anti-authorial position | The author is a function of discourse, not the source of meaning; the text is a site of multiple writings | The Gatsby documents are one discourse among others, interesting for the history of the book's production and circulation, without interpretive privilege |
| The moderate middle position | Authorial evidence informs interpretation without governing it; intentions are relevant data, not a verdict | The letters and revisions sharpen attention to choices in the text, and the text remains the final arbiter of what those choices accomplish |

Each row of the table represents a live option in the field, and each deserves a fuller statement than a cell allows. The intentional-fallacy position, following Wimsatt and Beardsley, treats the 1946 distinction between the work's achievement and the author's design as the organizing principle. Its handling of the Gatsby evidence is consistent: everything the archive shows is admitted as fact and denied authority. The letters tell readers what Fitzgerald was thinking; they do not tell readers what the novel means. The position gains its force from the observation that readers regularly find meanings in the novel that the letters do not anticipate, and that those findings are not errors to be corrected by the archive.

Intentionalism, following Hirsch, treats the same evidence as the path to the standard itself. Its handling of the record is equally consistent: the more complete the evidence of the author's communicative intention, the better constrained the interpretation. The letters are not one context among many but the privileged context, because they are the author's own account of what he meant. The position gains its force from the observation that readers do, in practice, appeal to authorial evidence when disputes arise, and that this appeal seems to be an appeal to what settles the dispute. When two readings of a passage conflict, the intentionalist notes, the reading the author's statements support is the one critics tend to accept, which suggests that intention is the operative standard even among readers who deny it.

Hypothetical intentionalism, associated with Jerrold Levinson's work of the early 1990s, occupies a distinctive middle ground. It agrees with the anti-intention case that the author's actual private intention is not directly available and not the proper object of interpretation. It agrees with the intentionalist case that meaning is tied to authorship. Its resolution is to make the standard hypothetical: the meaning of the work is what an ideal reader, knowing the work and its relevant context, would most reasonably hypothesize that the author intended. Applied to Gatsby, the position admits the letters and revisions as part of the context the ideal reader would know, but it denies them the last word. The hypothesis must fit the public work, and where a private statement conflicts with what the text best supports, the text wins. The position is often presented as the most sophisticated version of the middle ground, because it preserves the connection to authorship while blocking the appeal to private authority.

The anti-authorial position, associated with Roland Barthes's "The Death of the Author" of 1967 and Michel Foucault's "What Is an Author?" of 1969, goes further than the intentional-fallacy position. Where Wimsatt and Beardsley argue that intention is the wrong standard, Barthes argues that the author as source of meaning is a modern figure whose authority can be set aside entirely, and Foucault treats the author as a function that organizes discourse rather than an origin that explains it. Applied to Gatsby, the position treats the documentary record as one more set of writings about the book, to be read alongside criticism, reception, and the novel itself, with no privileged station. The letters are interesting because they show the book being produced and positioned, not because they explain it. This position is the least often held in its strong form among Gatsby scholars, but its weaker form, a thorough skepticism about authorial authority, runs through much of the field.

The moderate middle position is the one most working critics actually practice, whatever theory they profess. It holds that authorial evidence is relevant without being decisive: the letters and revisions inform the reading, suggest lines of attention, correct errors, and enrich the account, while the text remains the arbiter of what the reading claims. The position is sometimes dismissed as an evasion, a refusal to choose between the real alternatives. Its defenders reply that the practice of criticism has always worked this way, and that the demand for a single governing standard comes from theory rather than from the practice of reading. On this view, the Gatsby documents are handled pragmatically: consulted where they illuminate, set aside where they do not, and never allowed to close a question the text leaves open.

## One Document, Five Readings

Abstraction is the debate's occupational hazard, so this section works a single documented fact through all five positions from the table. The fact is the title deliberation: the documented correspondence shows Fitzgerald weighing alternative titles with his editor before settling on the published one. The fact is stated without quotation and without taking sides about what it proves; the companion letters article carries the documentary detail. What matters here is what each position does with the same fact, because the exercise makes the theoretical differences concrete. Five positions, one document, five readings.

### How do the five positions read the title deliberation?

They read it as five different kinds of fact: design made visible, production history, context for a hypothesis, a positioning act, and a sharpening of attention. Each position takes the deliberation seriously and gives a coherent account of what it is, and none needs to distort the fact to make its case.

The intentional-fallacy position notices that the author deliberated and concludes that the deliberation belongs to the making, not the meaning. What matters is what the chosen title accomplishes in the published book: how it frames the narrative, what expectations it sets, how it interacts with the text it names. The alternatives considered are interesting for the biography of the book, but they cannot tell a reader what the title means. The position's characteristic move is the redirect: from the author's choosing to the title's functioning.

Intentionalism reads the same deliberation as the author's design made visible, and therefore as evidence that constrains interpretation. The alternatives Fitzgerald weighed show what he wanted the book to foreground and what he wanted it to avoid; the final choice is a declaration of purpose. A reading of the novel that the title deliberation contradicts is not merely unpersuasive but wrong about what the book was meant to be. The position's characteristic move is the opposite redirect: from the title's functioning back to the author's choosing, on the ground that the functioning is explained by the choosing. Where the anti-intention reader sees the title working in the text, the intentionalist sees the author's purpose working through the title. The deliberation is not background. It is the record of the decision that the interpretation must honor.

Hypothetical intentionalism takes a third path. The ideal reader knows about the deliberation, since it is part of the public record of authorship, and folds it into the hypothesis about what the author is most reasonably taken to have meant. But the hypothesis is governed by the achieved work. If the chosen title accomplishes something in the published novel that the deliberation does not anticipate, the accomplishment governs, because the hypothesis must fit the public text. The position's characteristic move is the conditional admission: the document enters the context, but the text retains the last word on what the hypothesis can claim. The deliberation informs without deciding. For a reader sympathetic to this middle ground, the title case is exemplary, because it shows how authorial evidence can be fully honored and still not be authoritative.

The anti-authorial position reads the deliberation as a positioning act in the literary marketplace, one discourse among others. The weighing of alternatives shows an author and an editor negotiating how the book would be framed, sold, and received; it is evidence about the production and circulation of the work rather than about its meaning. The position's characteristic move is the reframing: the document is not about the novel's interior but about its exterior, the social and commercial conditions of its appearance. This does not make the deliberation uninteresting. For the anti-authorial reader, the exterior is where much of the real action is: how books are packaged, how authors are constructed, how the literary field operates. The title deliberation is valuable evidence for that inquiry, and the position values it highly, just not as interpretation of the novel.

The moderate middle position reads the deliberation pragmatically, as a sharpening of attention. Knowing that the title was weighed among alternatives alerts the reader to the title's work in the text: its resonances, its ironies, the expectations it sets and subverts. The moderate then tests those observations against the novel itself, keeping what the text supports and discarding what it does not. The position's characteristic move is the provisional use: the document suggests, the text decides. This is the reading most working critics actually perform, whatever theory they profess, and its prevalence is itself a datum for the debate. The intentionalist will say the prevalence shows that intention is the operative standard even where denied; the anti-intention reader will say it shows that the text does the real work even where the archive is consulted. ## Positions Between the Poles

The table's five rows simplify a field that is richer in its middle than at its edges. The strongest versions of the anti-intention and pro-evidence positions each admit qualifications that move them toward the center, and the qualifications are where most actual critical practice lives. Mapping those qualifications matters because the debate is often presented as a stark choice between two caricatures, the naive biographer who treats every letter as law and the dogmatic formalist who refuses all history. Few working readers of Gatsby occupy either extreme. The interesting theoretical work happens in the space between, where the question is not whether authorial evidence matters but how, when, and how much.

One qualification concerns the kind of intention at issue. The debate is often conducted as though intention were a single thing, a private mental state either accessible or not, either authoritative or not. But the Gatsby record shows several different kinds of authorial design, and the positions handle them differently. There is the intention behind a local choice, such as a revised sentence, which is relatively tractable and relatively modest. There is the intention behind the whole design, such as the shape of the book, which is more ambitious and more contested. And there is the author's intention about how the book should be read, which is a different matter again. The anti-intention case is strongest against the third kind, the author's wishes about reception, and weakest against the first, where a documented revision plainly explains a local effect. The intentionalist case is strongest at the local level and must work hardest to show that local decisions add up to a governing global design. Much of the apparent disagreement between the sides dissolves into agreement once the kind of intention is specified, which is itself an argument for mapping the debate carefully rather than choosing a side.

A second qualification concerns the kind of evidence. The letters are not all of one type, and the positions weight them differently. A letter to the editor about the proofs, written in the course of the work, is evidence of a different order from a later reminiscence, a casual remark to a friend, or a public statement made for an audience. The intentionalist case does not treat all these equally; it ranks them by proximity to the act of composition. The anti-intention case does not dismiss all these equally either; it finds some more historically informative than others, even while denying all of them interpretive authority. The debate, at its best, is a debate about weighting, not about admission. Both sides admit the documents. They disagree on what the documents are evidence of, and the disagreement is sharpest where the evidence is richest, which is why Gatsby is the exemplary case.

A third qualification concerns the reader. Hypothetical intentionalism makes the reader explicit in the standard, but every position has an implied reader. The intentional-fallacy position implies a reader who judges the public work by its achievement; intentionalism implies a reader who seeks the author's communicative purpose; the anti-authorial position implies a reader who treats the text as a field of writings. The Gatsby debate is therefore also a debate about what readers are for. This is one reason the dispute resists settlement. To decide between the positions, a reader would need a standard for what reading is, and each position supplies its own standard. The choice of a position on intention is entangled with a choice of a picture of reading, and the pictures do not share enough to permit a neutral adjudication.

A fourth qualification concerns time. The meaning of a work changes as it is read in new contexts, and the positions handle that change differently. The anti-intention case welcomes it: the public life of the work is precisely what exceeds the author's design, and later readings that the author could not have foreseen are legitimate discoveries rather than errors. The intentionalist case constrains it: later readings are legitimate only insofar as they recover what was meant, though defenders allow that intended meanings can be richer than the author's conscious formulation. The Gatsby record illustrates the point because the novel's reception history, traced in the critical-history article, shows meanings emerging that the documented statements do not anticipate. Each side has a story about those emergent meanings. The anti-intention story treats them as the work's public achievement; the intentionalist story treats them as unfoldings of a design richer than its documentation. Both stories are coherent. The choice between them is theoretical, not archival.

A fifth qualification concerns the temporal weighting of the author's statements. Not all authorial evidence is contemporary with the act of composition, and the positions weight the difference between contemporary and retrospective statements differently. A letter written during the revision of the proofs is evidence of a different order from a reminiscence offered years later, a public statement made for an audience, or a casual remark reported at second hand. The intentionalist case ranks these by proximity to the compositional act: the nearer the statement to the making, the stronger its claim on interpretation. A deliberated decision recorded in the proofs outweighs a passing comment to a friend, and both outweigh a later reconstruction colored by hindsight and by the book's reception. The anti-intention case accepts the ranking as history while denying it as interpretation: the contemporary letter is better evidence about the making, but no statement, however proximate, acquires authority over meaning. The moderate position uses the ranking pragmatically, consulting contemporary statements where they illuminate a choice and discounting retrospective ones where memory has likely reshaped the account. The qualification matters for Gatsby because the documentary record spans the compositional period and beyond, and careless citation treats all of it as one undifferentiated voice of the author. A disciplined map keeps the kinds of statement distinct, and the positions, stated at their best, all do.

The weighting can be shown in a small worked example. Suppose the record contains two items: a change made in the proofs, tightening a passage in the closing pages, and a remark made years later in which the author characterizes the book's theme in broad terms. The intentionalist case gives the proof change the greater weight, because it is a decision embodied in the text, an intention enacted rather than merely stated. The later remark is admitted but discounted, as the kind of retrospective summary that hindsight and the pressure of reputation can distort. The anti-intention case gives the proof change the greater historical interest, because it documents the making with precision, but denies both items interpretive authority: the tightened passage is judged by what it accomplishes, and the later remark is read as a piece of the author's self-presentation. The hypothetical intentionalist admits both into the context but lets the ideal reader's hypothesis be governed by the public work, so the proof change matters more simply because it is closer to the text the hypothesis must fit. The moderate consults both where they illuminate and sets both aside where they do not. Four handlings, no invented documents, and the same moral as the whole map: the weighting is where the theory lives.

### What changes when a reader sets the letters aside?

What changes is the vocabulary of explanation, not the availability of meaning. The novel still offers its sentences, structure, and images. What the reader loses is the appeal to the author's account: the title deliberation cannot be cited, the revision decisions cannot be invoked. What is gained is a discipline of attention to what the text accomplishes alone.

The change is often described as a loss, as though the letters were part of the meaning being set aside. The anti-intention case insists it is not a loss of meaning at all, only a loss of one kind of evidence about the making. The intentionalist case insists it is a genuine loss, a deliberate blinding to the best evidence of what the words were meant to do. The disagreement about what changes is itself an instance of the debate, which is why the exercise cannot settle it. Each side predicts a different outcome: the anti-intention side predicts that the reading survives the setting-aside, perhaps sharpened; the intentionalist side predicts that the reading is impoverished, perhaps distorted. Both predictions can be tested only by performing the exercise, and the results will be read through the theories that predicted them. The exercise is valuable not as a test but as a discipline, a way of seeing how much work each kind of evidence does.

## Why the Evidence Cannot Settle the Dispute

The structural reason the authorial intention debate survives is that it is a dispute about principles of interpretation, not about facts of composition. The facts are largely shared. The letters exist; the revisions are documented; the title was deliberated; the ending was assembled. The positions agree on these facts and disagree on what follows from them. A dispute of this kind cannot be settled by more facts, because the facts are not what is disputed. It can be addressed only by argument about the principles, and the principles are what the positions are. This is why each new documentary discovery is absorbed by both sides without changing either side's commitments. The discovery is fitted into the position's standing account of what evidence is for, and the position emerges unchanged.

It helps to state the point as a general feature of theoretical debates rather than a peculiarity of this one. Whenever two positions agree on the evidence and disagree on the theory that organizes it, the evidence cannot adjudicate between them. Adjudication requires a shared standard, and the standard is what is contested. The intention debate is the limiting case of this structure because the evidence is unusually complete. With a thin record, one side might hope that a new letter would decide the matter. With the Gatsby record, that hope is exhausted. The archive is rich enough that every position has already developed its account of every kind of document, and no new document can surprise a position that has already decided what documents are. The debate's persistence is therefore not a sign of insufficient research. It is a sign that the question is theoretical.

This point also explains why the debate is often conducted at cross-purposes. Each side tends to argue as though the other were disputing the facts, and each side's arguments are therefore unresponsive to the other's actual commitments. The intentionalist produces a telling letter, and the anti-intention reader replies that the letter is production history, which the intentionalist hears as evasion. The anti-intention reader produces a meaning the author did not foresee, and the intentionalist replies that the meaning was intended at a level the documentation does not capture, which the anti-intention reader hears as evasion. Each reply is consistent with the position that makes it. The appearance of evasion comes from the absence of a shared standard for what would count as an answer. Recognizing the structure does not resolve the debate, but it clarifies what would be required to resolve it: not more evidence, but agreement on what interpretation is for, which is a deeper and harder agreement to reach.

The wider field confirms the pattern. The [survey of Gatsby scholarship](/2030/12/02/gatsby-scholarship-survey/) shows the intention question recurring across decades of criticism, attached to different evidence and different theoretical vocabularies, without converging on a settlement. The critical history shows the debate predating the current documentary record and surviving its enrichment. These are not signs of a field failing to do its work. They are signs of a field working on a question whose form resists the kind of closure that evidence provides. The map's verdict, which follows, states this directly: the debate remains live because it is the kind of debate that evidence cannot close.

Consider a thought experiment that makes the structure visible. Two readers, equally informed, equally careful, and equally honest, open the same newly discovered letter. The first is an intentionalist. She reads the letter as further testimony to the author's design, integrates it with the correspondence already known, and revises her account of what Fitzgerald meant the disputed passage to do. The second is an anti-intention reader. He reads the same letter as further production history, integrates it with the compositional record already known, and revises his account of the conditions under which the passage was made. Both readers have learned something genuine. Neither has changed sides. The letter has done exactly what each position's theory says letters do, and the experiment shows why no accumulation of letters can do more.

The experiment can be run in the other direction as well. Give the two readers a reading of the novel that the documented statements do not anticipate, a meaning found in the text that the archive never mentions. The anti-intention reader treats it as a discovery, a legitimate finding about what the public work accomplishes. The intentionalist treats it as a hypothesis about the author's intention at a level the documentation does not capture, to be tested against the convergent evidence of the record. Again both readers are consistent, and again neither changes sides. The thought experiment does not favor either position. It favors the map's structural claim: the positions are closed under the evidence because each contains a standing account of what evidence is.

This is why the familiar call for more research as the solution to the debate misunderstands the debate's kind. More research is always welcome; the archive can always be enriched, particular interpretations can always be corrected, and the history of the book's making can always be told more fully. What more research cannot supply is the shared standard that would let the evidence adjudicate between the positions. The standard is the thing in dispute, and it is not an empirical finding. Recognizing this is not pessimism about scholarship. It is clarity about which questions scholarship can settle and which it can only illuminate, and the intention debate belongs, structurally, to the second class.

What would settle the debate, then, is not a document but an agreement: agreement on what interpretation is for, on what makes a reading warranted, on whether the object of interpretation is the author's communicative purpose or the public achievement of the text. That agreement is a philosophical achievement, not an archival one, and it would require the contending pictures of reading to find common ground they do not share. Until such ground is found, the honest posture is the map's: state each position at its strongest, show how each handles the evidence, and decline to adjudicate. The debate's persistence is not a failure to be repaired but a structure to be understood, and understanding the structure is the closest thing to progress the dispute allows.

### How can teachers map the debate without adjudicating it?

Teachers can present each position at its strongest and ask students to apply all of them to the same passage. Begin with a documented fact stated neutrally, then have students write the intentionalist reading, the anti-intention reading, and the hypothetical intentionalist version. The differences become visible in practice, and fairness is built into the procedure rather than the conclusion.

The method works because it separates the positions from the teacher's own commitments. A teacher who privately favors one side can still run the exercise fairly, since the fairness is in the procedure rather than the conclusion. The students learn that the dispute is principled rather than personal, that it survives because the positions are coherent rather than because one side is confused. The method also avoids the most common pedagogical failure, which is to present the debate as a prelude to the teacher's own verdict. A verdict, even a well-argued one, teaches students that the question is closed. The mapping exercise teaches them that it is open, and that openness is the more accurate lesson. The debate is genuinely contested, and a classroom that shows it as contested is teaching the field as it is.

## Misreadings That Distort the Debate

The debate is widely discussed and frequently misdescribed, and the misdescriptions have a pattern. The brief for this article names the recurring ones: assuming that intention either settles meaning or is irrelevant to it, attributing to the positions claims they do not make, and ignoring the evidence in favor of slogans. Each misreading is worth stating plainly, because each is a way of closing the question that the map is meant to keep open, and a reader who can spot them is equipped to read the field's arguments at their actual strength.

The first misreading is the assumption the complication names: treating the answer as obvious in either direction. In its pro-intention form, the assumption says that of course the author's meaning is the meaning, so the letters settle the matter and the debate is a contrived puzzle. In its anti-intention form, the assumption says that of course the author's designs are irrelevant, so the letters are curiosities and the debate is a waste of time. Both forms share a structure: they take for granted the very standard the debate disputes. The pro-intention form assumes that meaning is the author's intended meaning; the anti-intention form assumes that meaning is the public achievement of the text. But those assumptions are the positions, not arguments for them. A reader who begins with either has not engaged the debate but declined it, and the map's first service is to show that the decline is a choice with alternatives, not a deliverance from confusion.

The second misreading is caricature, the attribution of claims the positions do not make. The anti-intention case is routinely rendered as the claim that authors are ignorant of their own books, or that historical knowledge is forbidden, or that readers may make a text mean anything. None of these is the position. The 1946 essay permits interest in intentions, admits the letters as historical evidence, and insists that readings be warranted by the public features of the work; it denies only that intention governs judgment. The intentionalist case is routinely rendered as the claim that any letter proves any interpretation, or that the author's casual remarks are law, or that the text need not be read closely once the archive is open. None of these is the position either. The serious intentionalist ranks evidence, interprets the documents, and tests them against the text; the letters constrain interpretation rather than replacing it. Caricature is the debate's cheapest move and its most common, and it persists because a weakened opponent is easier to dismiss than a strong one is to answer.

The third misreading is the belief that the archive settles the dispute, the idea that one more letter, one more draft, or one more proof correction will finally decide what the positions cannot. This misreading ignores the theory-ladenness of evidence that the map has stated several times, and it is worth stating once more in its practical form. A new document enters a field in which each position already has a standing account of what documents are for, and the document is absorbed into those accounts without disturbing them. The misreading is tempting because it promises an empirical resolution to a theoretical dispute, and empirical resolutions feel more solid than theoretical ones. But the solidity is illusory where the standard is contested. The archive can correct particular interpretations, enrich the history of the book's making, and open new lines of attention. What it cannot do is supply the agreement on what interpretation is for, and that agreement is the only thing that would settle the debate.

The fourth misreading runs in the opposite direction: the dismissal of the middle positions as evasions. On this misreading, the moderate view that evidence informs without governing, and the hypothetical intentionalism that makes the standard a public hypothesis, are refusals to face the hard choice between the real alternatives. The misreading mistakes a principled position for a failure of nerve. The moderate's claim is substantive: that critical practice has always worked by consulting the archive where it illuminates and judging by the text where it matters, and that insisting on one governing standard reflects the theorist's preference rather than the reader's need. Hypothetical intentionalism's claim is equally substantive: that the standard can be tied to authorship without being tied to private mental states. These claims can be disputed, and the map disputes nothing, but they cannot be dismissed as evasions without begging the question against them. A map that presented only the two poles would be simpler and less accurate; the middle is where most of the field actually lives.

Avoiding these misreadings is largely a matter of discipline: state each position at its strongest before criticizing it, distinguish informing from governing, and remember that the documents are shared while their standing is not. The reader who practices that discipline will find the debate less frustrating than its reputation suggests. It is not a muddle but a structure, and the structure repays attention.

## What Each Position Gives the Reader

A map that only states positions leaves the reader asking what they are for. Each position in this debate is also a discipline, a way of reading with its own characteristic payoff. Stating the payoffs does not adjudicate between the positions; it shows why each has survived, because each answers a genuine need of readers. The intentionalist discipline gives the reader a check against overreading. When an interpretation runs far ahead of anything the author could have meant, the documentary record pulls it back. The letters and revisions function as a reality principle: the reading must remain compatible with what the evidence shows about the author's communicative purpose. For Gatsby, this means that flights of symbolic fancy must reckon with what Fitzgerald was actually doing in the passages at issue. The discipline's virtue is humility before the evidence, and its characteristic vice, as its critics see it, is timidity before the text's larger possibilities.

The anti-intention discipline gives the reader the opposite virtue: full attention to what the work achieves, unshadowed by the author's account of the attempt. Freed from the obligation to square every effect with a documented purpose, the reader can follow the language where it leads, including to meanings the letters never mention. For Gatsby, this means the famous images can be read for everything they do in the novel's economy, whether or not Fitzgerald deliberated over each resonance. The discipline's virtue is responsiveness to the text as it stands, and the characteristic objection is license: without the anchor of intention, readings can drift into the reader's preoccupations rather than the work's properties. The defenders reply that the text itself is the anchor, and that a public work needs no private supplement.

Hypothetical intentionalism gives the reader a procedure for using the archive without surrendering to it. The ideal reader's hypothesis lets the letters and revisions do their full illuminating work while keeping the public text as the final check. For Gatsby, this means the correspondence can be read closely and taken seriously, and still be overruled by the novel where the two conflict. The discipline's virtue is balance, and its characteristic vice, in the eyes of its critics, is artificiality: the ideal reader is a construct, and real readers may wonder whether the hypothesis they form is genuinely about the author or about their own best judgment dressed in authorial clothing. The defenders reply that all interpretation involves such constructs, and that making the construct explicit is more honest than hiding it.

The moderate middle position gives the reader permission to practice what most critics already do: consult the archive where it helps, judge by the text where it matters, and refuse to turn either move into a dogma. Its virtue is practicality, and its characteristic vice is theoretical thinness: it tells the reader what to do without explaining why the doing is justified. The anti-authorial position gives the reader a different kind of freedom, the freedom to treat the author as one voice among many in the discourse the book inhabits. Its virtue is breadth, and its characteristic vice is detachment from the particularity of the work's making. Each discipline answers a real need, which is why the debate's positions persist as practices even where their theories are disputed. A reader can adopt any of them as a working method and read Gatsby well, and the map's verdict is that no theory compels the choice among them.

A reader who works through all five disciplines in turn gains something no single position provides: a sense of how much of any interpretation comes from the text and how much comes from the theory brought to it. Read the closing pages first as an intentionalist, asking what the revisions reveal about purpose; then as an anti-intention reader, asking what the paragraph accomplishes without the archive; then as a hypothetical intentionalist, forming the best public hypothesis; then as a moderate, consulting the documents where they illuminate. The interpretations will differ at the margins, and the differences will teach more about the debate than any summary. The exercise is the map's recommended use, because it turns the theoretical disagreement into a practical education in how reading works.

## Verdict

The debate remains live, and it remains live for a reason that no exhibit can remove. The positions mapped here are coherent, well developed, and mutually incompatible. Each handles the Gatsby evidence with internal consistency. Each answers the objections standardly raised against it. None is refuted by the documents, because the documents are not what the positions dispute. What the positions dispute is the standing of authorial evidence in interpretation, and that dispute is a matter of principle rather than fact. A verdict that declared a winner would have to appeal to a standard the losing side does not accept, which is not adjudication but partisanship. The honest verdict is therefore the map itself: the disagreement is genuine, the evidence bears on each side as each side's theory says it should, and the question of whether Fitzgerald gets the last word on his novel has no answer that the field as a whole would recognize.

That verdict is not a counsel of indifference. It does not say the positions are equally good in every respect, or that the choice between them makes no difference. The choice makes a large difference to how one reads, what one admits as evidence, and what one takes interpretation to be. What the verdict says is that the difference is not the kind that evidence settles. Readers must choose a position, or work between them, on grounds that the debate itself provides: the coherence of the theory, its fit with critical practice, its picture of what reading is for. Those grounds are real, and they are the proper grounds for the choice. What they do not provide is a neutral tribunal. The map's work is to make the choice an informed one, and to show why the informed choice does not end the debate.

The wider scholarship confirms the verdict's shape. The survey of the field shows the intention question recurring without convergence, and the critical history shows it surviving every enrichment of the documentary record. The field has not failed to settle the question; the question is of a kind that does not settle by the methods that settle factual disputes. Recognizing the kind of question it is belongs to understanding it. The reader who leaves this map understanding why the evidence supports both sides, and why that support does not adjudicate, has understood the debate as it actually stands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the authorial-intention debate around Gatsby?

The authorial-intention debate around Gatsby is the dispute over whether Fitzgerald's intentions govern what the novel means. One side, the intentionalist case, holds that meaning is what the author meant, so the documented letters, drafts, and revisions are the primary evidence of what the book says. The other side, following the 1946 intentional-fallacy argument of Wimsatt and Beardsley, holds that the published novel is a public object judged by what it achieves, so the author's private designs cannot settle its meaning. The debate sharpens around Gatsby because the documentary record is unusually rich: correspondence with the editor discusses the title, structure, and proofs, and the manuscripts show deliberated revision. Both sides handle that same record consistently and reach opposite conclusions, which is why the disagreement is theoretical rather than archival. The article maps each position at its strongest without declaring a winner.

### Q: What is the case against authorial intention?

The case against authorial intention, in its canonical form the 1946 essay "The Intentional Fallacy" by W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe C. Beardsley, argues that a published work is a public linguistic object, not a private message. Once the novel is in readers' hands, its meaning is a matter of what its language accomplishes, judged by its achievement rather than by the author's design. The argument has three main strands: the author's intention as a mental state is not directly available, and surviving documents are themselves texts needing interpretation; even a known intention would not settle meaning, because works do things their makers did not plan; and language is public, so meanings are established by convention rather than private decree. Applied to Gatsby, the case admits the letters and revisions as historical evidence about the making while denying them authority over the meaning of the published text.

### Q: What is the case for authorial evidence?

The case for authorial evidence, in its canonical form E. D. Hirsch's intentionalism, holds that meaning is the author's intended meaning and that interpretations are valid when they recover it. On this view, asking what a passage means is asking what the author meant by it, and the documented record is the best access to that intention. Fitzgerald's correspondence with his editor, the deliberated titles, and the revisions in manuscript and proof are therefore not background but the primary evidence of the book's design, constraining which interpretations count as valid. The case distinguishes between crude appeals to authority and disciplined evidence-weighing: letters must be interpreted, ranked by proximity to composition, and tested against the text. Its strength is the observation that readers do appeal to authorial evidence when disputes arise, which suggests intention is the operative standard even where it is denied.

### Q: How does the debate apply to Gatsby's letters and revisions?

The debate applies to Gatsby's letters and revisions as a dispute about what the documentary record is for. The editor's correspondence records the title deliberation and the proof revisions, and the manuscripts show passages cut, added, and relocated, including the assembly of the closing pages. The intentionalist case reads these documents as the author's design made visible, evidence of purpose that constrains interpretation. The anti-intention case reads the same documents as production history, illuminating the making of the book while the published text remains the object of interpretation. Hypothetical intentionalism admits the record into the context an ideal reader would know but lets the public text govern the hypothesis. The detailed inventory belongs to the letters article and the final page composition article; this debate concerns the standing of that evidence, not its contents.

### Q: Does intention settle or is it irrelevant to meaning?

Neither assumption survives the debate as the field actually conducts it, and that is the complication this article addresses. The intentionalist case holds that intention is the standard of meaning, so it does settle interpretation when the evidence is clear, though its defenders allow that the evidence is often ambiguous and must be weighed. The anti-intention case holds that intention is not the standard, so it is not irrelevant in the sense of being uninteresting, but irrelevant in the sense of lacking authority: the letters can inform a reading without governing it. The moderate middle position, which most working critics practice, treats authorial evidence as relevant data rather than a verdict. The map's verdict is that the debate remains live precisely because both assumptions are too simple; the evidence supports each side on its own terms, and no shared standard exists to declare one assumption correct.

### Q: How does the evidence support both sides?

The evidence supports both sides because each side has a standing theory of what the evidence is evidence of. The intentionalist case approaches a documented revision as a decision revealing purpose, so the rich record of Fitzgerald's deliberations becomes testimony to his design. The anti-intention case approaches the same revision as an event in the production history that the published text has absorbed and superseded, so the same record becomes evidence of how fluid the design remained. The title deliberation, the proof changes, and the assembled ending are each consistent with both readings. The support is genuine on both sides: neither side ignores the documents or distorts them. What differs is the theory that organizes them, which is why the debate cannot be settled by pointing at the archive. The same documents genuinely support both positions because the positions ask different questions of the documents.

### Q: Who were Wimsatt and Beardsley, and what did their essay argue?

W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe C. Beardsley were mid-century American literary theorists, and their 1946 essay "The Intentional Fallacy," published in the Sewanee Review, is the canonical statement of the case against treating authorial intention as the standard of interpretation. They argued that judging a work by its author's design confuses the making with the thing made: a poem or novel, once published, belongs to the public and is judged by what it achieves in language. The essay distinguished between the author's private intention and the public evidence of the work, and it held that the author's own statements about the work are private evidence rather than public standards. The argument does not forbid interest in intentions; it forbids the critical move of letting intention govern judgment. Its influence on Gatsby criticism runs through every discussion of how the letters should be used.

### Q: What is the difference between an author's plan and a novel's meaning?

An author's plan is a private design formed before or during composition: what Fitzgerald hoped the book would do, which choices he deliberated, which effects he aimed at. A novel's meaning is what the published text accomplishes in public language for readers the author never met, including effects and resonances the plan did not anticipate. The distinction is the hinge of the whole debate. The intentionalist case holds that the plan is the meaning, so recovering the plan is recovering what the book says. The anti-intention case holds that the meaning exceeds and sometimes departs from the plan, so the plan is one context among others. Gatsby sharpens the distinction because the plan is unusually well documented while the meaning remains widely contested; the richness of the first does not dissolve the contest over the second.

### Q: Can an author misunderstand their own novel?

The debate answers this question differently depending on the position, which is what makes it a useful test of each side. The anti-intention case answers yes without hesitation: because meaning lives in what the text accomplishes publicly, an author can misjudge that accomplishment, and a later reader can understand the book better than its maker did. The intentionalist case answers with a qualification: the author cannot misunderstand what he meant, since meaning is the intended meaning, though he can be wrong about how readers will receive it or about the work's larger significance. The moderate position allows both: the author is the best witness to the design but not an infallible judge of the achievement. For Gatsby, the question matters because the documented statements sometimes sit uneasily with the readings the novel has sustained, and each side explains that unease in its own terms.

### Q: What is hypothetical intentionalism in plain terms?

Hypothetical intentionalism, associated with the philosopher Jerrold Levinson, is the position that a work's meaning is what an ideal reader would most reasonably hypothesize that the author intended, given the work and its relevant context. In plain terms: instead of asking what the author actually meant in private, ask what a well-informed reader would conclude the author meant from the public evidence. The position agrees with the anti-intention case that private intentions are not directly available and should not govern interpretation. It agrees with the intentionalist case that meaning is tied to authorship rather than floating free of it. Applied to Gatsby, the letters and revisions enter as part of the context the ideal reader knows, but the hypothesis must fit the published text, and where a private statement conflicts with the text, the text wins. It is widely regarded as the most sophisticated middle position.

### Q: What does it mean to say the evidence is theory-laden?

To say the evidence is theory-laden is to say that no document interprets itself, and that what a document shows depends on the theory the reader brings to it. A revised passage does not announce whether it reveals purpose or merely records an event in the production history; the intentionalist reads it as the first, the anti-intention reader as the second, and both readings are consistent with the physical facts of the revision. The phrase explains why the Gatsby archive cannot settle the debate: the archive is rich, but every item in it is read through a prior account of what such items are for. Recognizing theory-ladenness does not mean the evidence is useless or that all readings are equal. It means that the work of interpretation includes the work of justifying the theory, and that the debate's persistence is structural rather than a sign of insufficient research.

### Q: What does the claim that a text belongs to its readers mean?

The claim that a text belongs to its readers, central to the anti-intention case, means that once a work is published it enters a public space of language where its meanings are made in the encounter between the words and their readers. The author cannot follow the book into that space to police its readings, and the conventions that give the words their force belong to the language community rather than to any individual. For Gatsby, the claim means that the novel's meanings for successive generations of readers, including meanings Fitzgerald did not anticipate, are legitimate discoveries rather than errors to be corrected by the archive. The claim does not deny that the author made the text or that the making is interesting. It denies that ownership of the making confers ownership of the meaning, which is why the letters illuminate the book without governing it.

### Q: Why did the fallacy essay still permit interest in intentions?

The 1946 essay permitted interest in intentions because its target was a specific critical error, not curiosity about authors. Wimsatt and Beardsley distinguished between using authorial evidence and judging by it: a reader may consult the author's statements to learn what was attempted, to correct factual errors about the composition, or to find lines of attention worth testing against the text. What the essay forbids is the further step of treating intention as the standard of success, so that the work is judged by whether it matched its design. This distinction is often lost in casual summaries that present the position as indifference to history. Stated accurately, the position is disciplined rather than dismissive: the letters are admitted as data and denied the last word. For readers of the Gatsby correspondence, the permission matters, because it licenses the archive as a source of illumination while preserving the text as the object of judgment.

### Q: Does a passage cut from the drafts still carry meaning?

A passage cut from the drafts carries the meaning it had as a candidate for the novel, but it does not carry the meaning of the published work, and the positions disagree about how much that distinction matters. The intentionalist case treats cut passages as evidence of the author's deliberation, showing what was considered and rejected, which illuminates the design of what remained. The anti-intention case treats them as part of the production history that the published text superseded; they are interesting for the biography of the book but not part of its meaning. The moderate position uses them pragmatically, consulting them where they sharpen attention to a choice the final text makes. The cut passage is therefore a good test case for the debate: everyone agrees it exists and what it says, and the disagreement is entirely about its standing.

### Q: What is the role of the ideal reader in the debate?

The ideal reader is the figure hypothetical intentionalism puts at the center of interpretation: a reader who knows the work, its language, and its relevant context, and whose best hypothesis about the author's intention sets the meaning. The role matters because it relocates the standard from the author's private mind, which is inaccessible, to a public act of reasoning, which can be examined and argued about. For Gatsby, the ideal reader would know the documented letters and revisions, the conventions of the period, and the text itself, and would form the most reasonable hypothesis about what the author intended the work to mean. The other positions have their own implied readers: the intentional-fallacy position implies a judge of public achievement, intentionalism implies a seeker of communicative purpose. The debate is thus also a debate about what readers are for.

### Q: Does the debate change how the final paragraph is read?

The debate changes how the final paragraph is read by changing what the reader is allowed to appeal to when defending a reading of it. Under the intentionalist standard, a reading gains support from the documented revision history, since the assembly of the ending across drafts and proofs is evidence of the design Fitzgerald worked toward. Under the anti-intention standard, the same history is set aside as production background, and the reading must stand on what the paragraph accomplishes in the published novel. In practice, most readings of the famous closing sound similar whichever standard is invoked, because the paragraph's achievement is what draws readers to it under either theory. The difference appears at the margins: in disputes about which effects are legitimate, where the intentionalist appeals to the record and the anti-intention reader appeals to the text alone.

### Q: What do both sides agree on?

Both sides agree on the documentary facts, and that agreement is more extensive than the heat of the debate sometimes suggests. They agree that the letters exist and what they say, that the manuscripts and proofs record deliberated revision, that the title was considered under alternatives, and that the ending was assembled from relocated material. They agree that the author's statements are worth reading and that the composition history is worth knowing. They agree that interpretations must be argued for with evidence and that not every reading is equally good. What they disagree about is the standing of authorial evidence: whether it governs meaning, informs it, or merely documents the making. The agreement on facts is what makes the disagreement theoretical rather than empirical, and it is why the debate cannot be settled by producing one more letter.

### Q: Is there a middle position on authorial intention?

There are several, and the most practiced one is the moderate view that authorial evidence informs interpretation without governing it. On this view, the letters and revisions are relevant data: they suggest lines of attention, correct errors about the making, and enrich the account of the author's choices. But the text remains the arbiter, and no document closes a question the text leaves open. Hypothetical intentionalism offers a more theoretically developed middle, making the standard a public hypothesis rather than a private intention. What the middle positions share is a refusal of both extremes: they will not treat a letter as law, and they will not treat the archive as irrelevant. Their vulnerability is the charge of evasion, that they avoid the hard theoretical choice. Their defense is that critical practice has always worked this way, and that the insistence on a single governing standard as the price of seriousness is the theorist's invention.

### Q: What makes an interpretation valid if intention does not decide?

If intention does not decide, validity comes from the interpretation's fit with the public evidence of the text and its power to account for what the work does. The anti-intention case holds that a reading is valid when it is supported by the language, structure, and effects of the published novel, argued in terms other readers can assess. The author's statements may corroborate such a reading or suggest it, but they cannot validate it, because validation is a public matter and private intention is not public evidence. The intentionalist replies that without intention there is no stable object for validity to attach to, only a history of responses. The moderate position splits the difference: intention is one consideration among others in assessing a reading, relevant but not decisive. The question is the debate's deepest one, because it asks what interpretation is for.

### Q: Can new archival discoveries settle the debate?

New archival discoveries cannot settle the debate, because the debate is not about what the archive contains. Each position already has a standing account of what any document is evidence of: the intentionalist reads new letters as further testimony to design, the anti-intention reader reads them as further production history, and both readings are consistent with whatever the discovery shows. The Gatsby record is already rich enough that this pattern is visible in practice; the positions absorbed the existing correspondence, manuscripts, and proofs without shifting. A genuinely surprising discovery could change particular interpretations, correcting factual errors or opening new lines of attention, but it could not change the standing of authorial evidence, which is the theoretical question at issue. The debate's persistence is structural, and structure is not altered by exhibits.
