## The Telecommunications Act and the Irony of Its Market Legacy

![Telecom Act Market Impact: What the Big Law Did to Markets - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-93.webp)

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 was signed into law on February 8, 1996, by President Bill Clinton, becoming Public Law 104-104, according to the legislative record on Congress.gov. The signing, held at the Library of Congress, marked the first major rewrite of communications law in 62 years, as the Congressional Record noted at the time. The conference report described the measure as a pro-competitive, deregulatory national framework designed to open every telecommunications market to competition by stripping away regulatory barriers to entry. Few statutes of the decade carried larger ambitions, and few passed with such lopsided bipartisan majorities. The statute belonged to the deregulatory era surveyed in [US infrastructure legislation history](/2016/08/15/us-infrastructure-legislation-history/), which traces how Washington rewrote market rules across industries in those years. The Senate agreed to the conference report by 91 votes to 5 and the House by 414 to 16 on February 1, 1996, according to the Congress.gov legislative record. A week later the president signed the bill at the Library of Congress with a digital pen, the first bill signing ever staged there, while comedian Lily Tomlin performed her classic telephone-operator routine updated from "one ringy-dingy" to "one gigabyte," as the Congressional Record recounted. The conference report stated the objective plainly: "to provide for a pro-competitive, de-regulatory national policy framework designed to accelerate rapidly private sector deployment of advanced information technologies and services to all Americans by opening all telecommunications markets to competition." At the signing the president said the law would bring the future to every doorstep, in the account of the American Enterprise Institute's history of the statute. Congress set out to do what the courts had only begun: to shatter the remaining local telephone monopolies, to invite cable companies into telephony and telephone companies into video, and to replace a regulatory order built for monopoly with one built for rivalry. A companion [Telecommunications Act of 1996 guide](/2017/07/01/telecommunications-act-1996-guide/) sets out the statute's full provisions; what follows evaluates what those provisions did to markets.

The irony that gives this article its subject is that the companies broken apart a dozen years earlier were already on the road back together. The road to that breakup had been long. The Justice Department filed its antitrust suit against AT&T in 1974, as the Congressional Record recounted, and pursued the case through four presidential administrations before the company agreed in 1982 to the consent decree known as the Modified Final Judgment. On January 1, 1984, the divestiture took effect, splitting the Bell System into seven independent Regional Bell Operating Companies, the so-called Baby Bells, a restructuring documented in the Department of Justice telecommunications policy record and the contemporaneous legal literature. The 1996 statute promised to finish the work of 1984 by forcing genuine competition into local service. Instead, within a decade of passage, the regional companies began recombining. Wikipedia's account of the breakup's aftermath records that one Baby Bell, Southwestern Bell, eventually acquired AT&T itself and kept the name, while others merged into the firms that became Verizon and its peers. A statute meant to dissolve local monopolies presided over the reassembly of the empire dismantled in 1984.

The pattern underneath that irony is the verdict this article defends. The provisions of the 1996 law that removed restrictions took effect immediately, because they required nothing beyond the statute itself. The provisions that tried to manufacture competition, through interconnection duties, unbundling obligations, and entry permissions policed by the Federal Communications Commission and the state utility commissions, spent their lives in federal court and emerged as dead letters. Each agency attempt to define the sharing obligation drew challenges from the incumbents, and each judicial reversal restarted the clock. A contemporary Congressional Record analysis charged that the Commission had already "virtually rewritten" the statute through its early implementation choices, a complaint that captured the era's frustration whatever its legal merits. Deregulation worked at the speed of ink. Competition policy worked at the speed of litigation, which is to say it did not work at all.

Five findings carry that verdict. The first, developed below, traces radio: Congress lifted the national ownership limits outright, and the broadcast industry consolidated faster and more thoroughly than any other sector the statute touched, with the largest proprietor growing from dozens of outlets to more than a thousand in about five years. The second follows local telephone service, where the interconnection and unbundled element regime of Sections 251 and 252, the provisions the Congressional Record called the law's true significance, was undone through years of agency rulemaking and judicial reversal. The third follows long distance, where the Bell companies won reentry and the pieces of the 1984 breakup began recombining into national carriers again. The fourth measures the cross-industry invasions the statute advertised, telephone companies selling video and cable companies selling telephone service, and finds far less entry than the conference report promised. The fifth asks what households actually paid and whether capital flowed into networks at the promised rate. Across all five, the same asymmetry appears: limits that Congress erased disappeared overnight, while competition that Congress ordered into being never quite arrived.

### How were the two chambers' votes on the conference report divided?

Both chambers agreed to the conference report on February 1, 1996, the Senate by 91 votes to 5 and the House by 414 to 16. President Clinton signed the bill a week later at the Library of Congress, the first bill signing staged there, using a digital pen.

### What local ownership tiers did section 202 substitute for the national cap?

Section 202 replaced the national ceiling with tiered local limits scaled to size: up to eight stations in markets with forty-five or more outlets, no more than five in one service; up to seven in markets of thirty to forty-four; up to six in markets of fifteen to twenty-nine; and up to five in the smallest.

### How large was the largest radio group owner in 1996?

Fragmentation was nearly total. The largest group proprietor in the country held sixty-five outlets in 1996, according to a draft Commission study prepared for the 2002 biennial review. CBS, the largest owner by revenue, held thirty-nine stations and about six percent of industry revenue in the month after the law passed.

### By how much did the population of radio owners shrink in five years?

Between 1996 and 2001 the number of station owners fell from about five thousand one hundred to about three thousand eight hundred, a drop of roughly one quarter, while the count of licensed stations kept growing. Consolidation shrank the proprietor population without shrinking the dial.

### Why did radio consolidation move faster than telephone competition?

The radio change was self-executing: Congress wrote it as statutory text, the Commission conformed its rules within about a month, and a buyer needed only routine license-transfer approval to close. The telephone titles delegated the hard questions to agency rulemaking and state arbitration, where every answer could be litigated for years.

## Finding 1: Radio Ownership After the National Cap Fell

No sector of American communications changed as fast under the 1996 law as radio, and no sector illustrates the verdict above as cleanly. The ownership provisions were self-executing. Congress wrote them into the statute, the Federal Communications Commission conformed its rules about a month after passage, and buyers needed no further permission from Washington to begin acquiring. What followed was the most rapid consolidation of a national medium in the Commission's history: a handful of group owners accumulated hundreds of outlets each, the population of independent proprietors collapsed by roughly a quarter within five years, and the price of desirable properties soared as longtime operators cashed out. Every figure below is tied to a named source and period, because the scale of the change invites exaggeration and the record is strong enough without it.

### The Caps Congress Swept Away

To grasp what the statute removed, consider what preceded it. Into the early 1980s the Commission limited any single owner to seven AM stations, seven FM stations, and seven television outlets under the 7-7-7 rule, a ceiling described in a Federal Communications Law Journal article by Christopher Sterling published in 2006. Beginning in 1981, successive Commission decisions raised the radio ceilings in stages: first to twelve AM and twelve FM stations, then to eighteen of each in 1992, and finally to twenty AM and twenty FM in 1994, for a national total of forty radio properties per owner, according to the Electronic Media Law textbook chapter published by Sage. Each increase was presented as a modest modernization. Cumulatively they multiplied the original ceiling nearly sixfold, yet even the 1994 rules kept a hard national boundary that no buyer could cross. A company that wanted a forty-first station had no lawful path to it. The 1994 rules also permitted the total to reach twenty-three in a service when minority-controlled holdings were counted. A contemporaneous status-quo compilation published by the Consumer Project on Technology, dated July 5, 1995, shows the television side capped at twelve stations and a quarter of the national audience.

The local rules were tighter. Through the 1980s the Commission loosened its local limits to permit two AM and two FM outlets in a single market, a change described in a Playlist Research history of the period as a product of the Reagan administration's deregulatory turn, and the 1992 duopoly rules kept that two-plus-two structure while adding an audience-share ceiling. The rationale for all of it was the traditional one: the airwaves belonged to the public, licensees held them in trust, and dispersed control protected the diversity of voices that democratic self-government required. Douglas Gomery made the classic statement of that view in a March 2002 white paper for the Economic Policy Institute, arguing that profit maximization had never been the sole point of communications policy and that the Commission's charge under the 1934 Act was to encourage a multiplicity of voices.

That architecture made radio a deliberately fragmented business. The largest group proprietor in the country held only sixty-five outlets in 1996, according to a draft Commission study titled Review of the Radio Industry, prepared for the 2002 biennial ownership review and later surfaced by Radio Survivor. CBS stood as the largest owner by revenue with thirty-nine stations and about six percent of industry revenue in the month after the new law passed, per Sterling's account. No national chains of consequence existed because the rules forbade them. Local owners, often families or small companies holding one or two signals in a single city, dominated the trade.

The philosophical stakes of that fragmentation were stated most clearly by the historian Douglas Gomery in a March 2002 white paper for the Economic Policy Institute: "Under the Communications Act of 1934, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) is charged with allocating spectrum space to maximize 'the public interest' ... and to encourage a diversity of voices so as to promote a vibrant democracy." The caps were the operational form of that charge. By keeping the number of voices large and their owners local, the Commission sought to guarantee that the use of a scarce public resource served more than the balance sheet of its licensee.

### A Limit Erased by the Statute Itself

Section 202 of the 1996 law discarded the national ceiling entirely. The Congressional Research Service report RL31925 on media ownership rules quotes the operative language: the Commission was required to revise its rules so that no national limit would apply to commercial radio holdings at all. In place of the old duopoly structure, Congress imposed tiered local limits scaled to market size: up to eight commercial stations in markets with forty-five or more commercial outlets, no more than five in the same service; up to seven in markets of thirty to forty-four; up to six in markets of fifteen to twenty-nine; and up to five in markets of fourteen or fewer, with a fifty-percent-of-market ceiling in the smallest places.

Sterling records that the Commission implemented the national elimination about a month after the statute's passage, striking its national multiple-ownership rule to conform to the new law. The speed matters because it explains everything that followed. Nothing about the change awaited a rulemaking docket, a cost study, a state commission proceeding, or a court test. A buyer with financing could sign a purchase agreement the week the president signed the bill and close as soon as the license transfer cleared. Contrast that with the telephone provisions of the same statute, which required the Commission to define unbundled network elements, set pricing methodologies, referee interconnection negotiations, and then defend each choice through years of appellate litigation. The radio title needed none of it. The legal historian's summary in the Columbia-hosted Sterling piece is blunt: the full effects became apparent almost immediately, unintended though they may have been.

The ink was barely dry on the statute when radio deals began at a dizzying rate, in the description of the broadcast literature survey published as a conference paper. This is why radio supplies the cleanest test of what the 1996 law did when nothing stood between the statutory text and the market. The telephone titles of the law tested what happened when everything stood between the text and the market: Sections 251 and 252 expressly gave state commissions authority over interconnection arbitration, the Congressional Record noted, which meant fifty separate forums could shape the outcome before a single federal court weighed in. The radio title delegated nothing and waited on nobody. The results diverged accordingly, and the divergence is the reason this finding comes first: it shows the statute working exactly as written.

### Clear Channel and the Arithmetic of the Buyout Wave

The emblem of the wave was Clear Channel Communications of San Antonio. In 1995 the company owned forty-three radio stations, according to a September 2003 report on antimusic.com. By the time of that report it owned more than twelve hundred outlets across the United States. A contemporaneous account circulated on the ILXOR discussion board put the figure at 1,233 stations, including six in Chicago, and credited the company with about one hundred million listeners and roughly twenty percent of the industry's sixteen billion dollars in 2001 revenue. Sterling's Federal Communications Law Journal article gives the March 2002 snapshot: Clear Channel held 1,156 stations and drew nearly twenty-seven percent of total industry revenue. CorpWatch, writing in the same era, counted approximately 1,225 stations in three hundred cities, with the company leading audience share in one hundred of one hundred twelve major markets, while its closest competitors, CBS and ABC, owned only one-fifth as many outlets each. Wikipedia's history of the company, later renamed iHeartMedia, reports that Clear Channel spent about thirty billion dollars assembling the portfolio.

The pace was without precedent. Within a year of passage there had been more than a thousand radio mergers, according to the Electronic Media Law textbook chapter published by Sage. As the journalist Eric Boehlert reported in 2001, the stations Clear Channel had gobbled up to reach more than twelve hundred had previously belonged to seventy separate broadcast companies, a figure cited in the broadcast literature survey. By 2003 the Sage chapter counted more than 1,250 Clear Channel outlets nationwide, approximately one of every nine stations in the country. The industry's revenue leadership inverted almost overnight. CBS, the largest owner by revenue at thirty-nine stations and six percent of the total just after the law passed, was eclipsed by late 1998 by Infinity Broadcasting, which held 158 stations and nearly seventeen percent of revenue, per Sterling. By March 2002 the top two group owners, Clear Channel at 1,156 stations and Infinity at 184, controlled more than forty-four percent of national radio revenue between them. The FCC's own draft Review of the Radio Industry, covering 1996 through March 2003, found that Clear Channel had surpassed twelve hundred outlets while the second-ranked Cumulus Media held just over two hundred fifty, a transformation from a 1996 landscape whose largest group proprietor owned sixty-five.

To place those numbers in perspective, consider what the old rules would have permitted. Under the 1994 ceiling of twenty AM and twenty FM stations, Clear Channel's twelve-hundred-station portfolio would have required thirty separate maximum-sized companies. The statute did not merely relax the constraint; it abolished the concept of a national constraint. Locally, the tiered caps channeled the buying into clusters: in the largest cities a single firm could assemble eight outlets, five in one service, which made market-by-market dominance a lawful business plan for the first time. The joke of the era, recorded by Playlist Research, was that competition in radio used to be across the street and had moved across the hall, as the same proprietor programmed rival formats from a single building. Through the early 2000s the leading chains traded in double digits on the stock market, Playlist Research notes, and the rising share price became its own acquisition currency, rewarding each deal with the means to finance the next.

The political backlash arrived almost as fast as the deals. In 2002 the Commission denied Clear Channel's requests to buy about half a dozen stations in several markets, ruling that the additions would give the company too much power in those localities, according to the Sage chapter. Senator John McCain pushed to make new ownership restrictions retroactive to stations already acquired, arguing that the company had grown too large under the 1996 law and that multi-station local clusters hurt consumers, as reported by antimusic.com in September 2003. Senator Russell Feingold introduced legislation aimed at halting further expansion. None of these efforts unwound a single completed acquisition. The backlash confirmed the finding rather than qualifying it: by the time Washington debated whether the wave had gone too far, the wave was over and the structure it left behind was permanent.

### Fewer Owners, More Signals

The consolidation was not only a story of giants. The entire population of proprietors shrank while the count of licensed signals kept growing. Between 1996 and 2001 the number of station owners fell from about five thousand one hundred to about three thousand eight hundred, a drop of roughly one quarter, according to Sterling's article citing Commission data. A conference paper on the period's broadcast literature gives the 2002 figures in percentage terms: the number of stations nationwide rose 5.4 percent while the number of owners declined 33.6 percent. The Commission's draft Review of the Radio Industry reached the same conclusion for 1996 through March 2003: station counts up nearly six percent, owner counts down thirty-five percent, with the change driven almost entirely by mergers among existing proprietors rather than by license surrenders. An American Federation of Television and Radio Artists filing with the Commission extended the series to 2010, reporting that commercial station counts had risen around ten percent since 1996 while the number of owners had fallen forty percent.

The economics of the squeeze showed up in advertising. The Commission's draft study found that the leading firm in each local market controlled an average of forty-six percent of radio advertising revenue, and that the top two firms together controlled an average of seventy-six percent, according to Radio Survivor's summary of the surfaced document. The study concluded that this concentration was at least partly responsible for an eighty-seven percent increase in advertising rates even as audience ratings fell. Whether the rate increase reflected market power, the rising value of the medium, or both became a contested question, and the Commission's decision to suppress the draft, ordering copies destroyed after its conclusions circulated, only deepened the controversy that Radio Survivor and others documented.

By late 2005 the picture had changed only marginally, Sterling writes: Cumulus had become the second-largest proprietor with just over three hundred outlets, Infinity had slipped to fourth with 178, and Clear Channel reached nearly 107 million listeners a week, more than a third of the country's population, against 58 million for runner-up Infinity. The consolidation was essentially complete within five years of passage, and the years after merely shuffled the runners-up. The Senate report on the Media Ownership Act of 2007, citing Commission studies, recorded that the number of commercial radio proprietors fell another eight percent between 2002 and 2005, on top of the earlier collapse. A 2010 filing by the American Federation of Television and Radio Artists put the long view in stark terms: in 1996 the two largest group owners had held sixty-two and fifty-three stations respectively, while by 2010 Clear Channel alone held more than eight hundred, down from a high of about eleven hundred in 2007, and the largest two owners in the average local market controlled seventy-four percent of revenue, with the leader alone at forty-six percent.

The evidence on programming was more mixed, and the article reports both sides. The Commission's draft study suggested that the total number of formats had remained largely steady nationwide, though the larger cities saw a slight reduction in the formats on offer, according to Radio Survivor's summary. On local news the record favored the old structure: the Senate report on the Media Ownership Act of 2007 noted that studies suggested local proprietors of broadcast outlets provided more local news programming, a finding consistent with the complaints of journalists and community groups that centralized playlists and shuttered newsrooms followed the buyouts. The chains' defenders answered that group ownership funded better facilities and steadier operations than thinly capitalized independents could sustain, and that the format counts showed listeners had not lost variety in any measurable national sense. What neither side disputed was the direction of the ownership trend itself.

For sellers, the wave was a windfall. Sterling notes that station transfers expanded greatly over the four years after the law passed and that prices for good properties shot up accordingly, as many longtime operators sold out to the growing groups. The mechanism was straightforward. Buyers assembling clusters would pay premiums for the final signals needed to complete a market footprint, and the premiums drew more independents to market, which fed the next round of cluster-building. An owner who had spent decades running two outlets in one city could sell at a multiple no local buyer could match. The structure of the statute's local tiers practically guaranteed this dynamic: each additional signal had strategic value to a cluster-builder beyond its standalone cash flow.

The cultural consequences were widely reported, though harder to quantify. The broadcast literature surveyed in the conference paper describes a consistent set of findings: consolidation distanced proprietors from their communities, eliminated broadcast journalism positions, and drained local flavor and local news from programming, as playlists and newsrooms were centralized across hundreds of outlets. CorpWatch's reporting added that the largest chains leveraged airplay against concert promotion and venue control, a practice labeled negative synergy. Defenders of the change argued that group ownership brought professional management, stronger balance sheets, and investment in facilities that thinly capitalized independents could not afford, and that listeners cared about programming quality rather than the address of the proprietor. Both claims can be true at once: the balance sheets grew stronger while the voices grew fewer.

### Why Broadcasting Moved First and Fastest

Four features of the radio title explain why it outran every other part of the statute. The first was self-execution. Congress wrote the ownership change as direct statutory text rather than as a delegation to the Commission, so no rulemaking stood between the law and the deal table. A buyer needed only the ordinary license-transfer approval, a routine process, to close. The telephone titles, by contrast, delegated the hard questions, which network elements incumbents must share with entrants, at what prices, under whose arbitration, to years of agency proceedings that opponents could challenge at every step. The Congressional Record's contemporary analysis put the structural difference plainly: the local-competition provisions devolved responsibility to the state commissions, multiplying the forums in which the outcome could be contested, while the radio title asked nothing of any regulator at all.

The second was the nature of the asset. A broadcast license is a discrete, tradable property with an established sales market and a known valuation method, a multiple of broadcast cash flow. Assembling a national portfolio meant repeating a familiar transaction hundreds of times, not building new physical plant. Clear Channel's roughly thirty-billion-dollar expenditure, reported by Wikipedia, shows the scale of capital the strategy absorbed, and the late-1990s capital markets supplied it willingly until the telecommunications and information sector meltdown that began in 2000 dried up investment funds, in Sterling's description.

The third was the design of the local tiers. By permitting as many as eight outlets in the largest cities, Congress legalized the cluster strategy explicitly: dominate one locality across multiple formats, sell advertisers the bundle, and centralize back-office and programming costs across the group. The old duopoly rule had forbidden exactly this. The new rulebook made it the industry template within five years.

The fourth was the sellers' incentive. Because cluster-builders paid premiums for footprint-completing signals, the wave was self-financing in a social sense: each premium sale demonstrated the new valuations, which coaxed more independents to sell, which supplied the next round of acquisitions. Sterling's observation that longtime operators sold out as prices shot up describes a rational response to a price signal, not a conspiracy. Nobody needed to force the independents out; the statute made their licenses worth more to a consolidator than to themselves. Even the Commission's own second thoughts changed nothing: when the agency attempted to rewrite the ownership rules in its June 2003 order, the Third Circuit remanded the decision in Prometheus Radio Project v. FCC, a ruling discussed in Sterling's article, leaving the 1996 framework in place.

Radio therefore proved what the 1996 law could accomplish when its text operated directly on the market. In about five years the industry moved from a largest proprietor of sixty-five outlets to one of more than twelve hundred, from fifty-one hundred owners to roughly thirty-eight hundred, and from a fragmented local trade to a medium in which two firms took over forty-four percent of national revenue. The telephone provisions of the same law, which required regulators and judges to construct competition piece by piece, produced years of litigation and a fraction of the promised entry. The asymmetry is the article's verdict in miniature: where Congress removed a limit, the market moved at once; where Congress ordered competition into being, the courts and commissions spent a decade deciding what the order meant.

## The Unbundling Regime

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 ended the legal monopolies that states had historically granted to local telephone companies and replaced them with a federally supervised wholesale regime. Section 251 of the Act imposed on every incumbent local exchange carrier a set of duties meant to let rivals sell local service without first replicating the local network. The incumbent had to negotiate interconnection in good faith, to interconnect its network with a competitor's facilities at any technically feasible point, to provide unbundled access to its network elements, and to offer any retail telecommunications service for resale at wholesale rates. Section 252 divided the labor of making that regime work: state utility commissions would set the actual prices in arbitrated interconnection agreements, but under pricing standards Congress wrote into the statute. Rates for interconnection and for unbundled network elements had to be based on the cost of providing the element, had to be nondiscriminatory, and could include a reasonable profit. Rates for resold services had to equal the retail rate minus the portion of retail cost the incumbent would avoid when it no longer marketed, billed, or collected for the service. The Bell operating companies faced one more lever. Under Section 271, a Bell company could enter the in-region long-distance business only after satisfying a fourteen-point competitive checklist, which included furnishing interconnection, unbundled elements, and resale to competitors. Opening the local network thus became the price of admission to the long-distance market the Bell companies most wanted.

The Federal Communications Commission translated these duties into national rules in its First Report and Order on local competition, released August 8, 1996, and published at 11 FCC Rcd 15499. The order required incumbents to interconnect at any technically feasible point and to make available a minimum list of unbundled network elements in Rule 319: local loops, local switching, interoffice transmission facilities, network interface devices, signaling networks and call-related databases, operations support systems, and operator services with directory assistance. For pricing, the order prescribed TELRIC, total element long-run incremental cost. Under that methodology, an incumbent's wholesale prices for interconnection and unbundled elements were to recover, in the order's words, the forward-looking costs directly attributable to the specified element, plus a reasonable allocation of forward-looking common costs, with efficiency judged against the most advanced technology available. State commissions would hold the evidentiary hearings and set the dollar figures, but the cost standard was federal and deliberately forward-looking, untied to the incumbent's historical investment. For resale, the order set the wholesale rate as the retail rate less avoided costs, a formula that produced resale discounts near twenty percent in many states and that the order applied even to long-running promotional prices.

Two design features of the 1996 order shaped everything that followed. First, the order let a requesting carrier take elements singly or in combinations, and it barred incumbents from dismantling existing combinations or charging a fee for the act of combining them. A companion rule let an entrant select favorable terms from any other carrier's approved interconnection agreement with the same incumbent, the so-called pick and choose rule, without being forced to accept that agreement whole. Second, the economics of the regime tilted entrants toward one particular combination. A package of loop, switching, and shared transport bought together as the unbundled network element platform, UNE-P, carried the deepest effective discounts, because TELRIC-based pricing compounded across elements. A Network World regulatory primer of the period reported discounts of forty to sixty percent on UNE-P lines, against about twenty percent for resale, and identified AT&T and WorldCom as the two largest users of UNE-P. The primer also noted the consequence the Bell companies feared: without switching on the unbundling list, there could be no UNE-P at all, and the price of UNE-P did not, in their view, reflect the investment the switches represented.

Contested interpretations of the regime appeared immediately and never left. Entrant advocates argued that unbundling was the only practical path into local markets, because duplicating the last mile of copper to every home was prohibitively expensive and the bottleneck would otherwise let incumbents strangle rivals at birth. Incumbent carriers argued that the rules forced them to share their networks at rates no builder would voluntarily accept, subsidizing rivals who would then have no reason to build anything of their own. Both sides agreed on the mechanism and disagreed on its morality: the rules deliberately set wholesale prices below the cost of constructing a competing network, and the policy question was whether that discount was a bridge to facilities-based competition or a permanent crutch.

The regime's defenders described that bridge as a ladder. Entrants would begin as resellers, graduate to UNE-based service as they learned the market, and finally build their own switches and transport once customer density justified the investment, at which point the wholesale discounts could be withdrawn. The FCC's own orders echoed the premise: unbundled elements were to assist competitors in the early market-entry period, in the words of a Network World analysis of the agency's policy, but eventually had to be withdrawn. Section 271 supplied the enforcement engine for the early rungs. Because the Bell companies needed a finding of local-market openness before entering long distance, they had a powerful incentive to sign interconnection agreements and provision wholesale inputs, and a contemporary press analysis noted that as the Bell companies, particularly Verizon, opened their networks to win long-distance approval, AT&T and other competitors took about ten percent of the national local business. The ladder theory thus depended on two regulatory commitments holding at once: wholesale prices low enough to make entry profitable, and a credible eventual withdrawal that would push entrants up the rungs. The litigation of the next decade broke the first commitment repeatedly and made the second commitment's timing unknowable.

## The Litigation Chronology

The rules barely had time to operate before the courts began rewriting them. Incumbent carriers and state commissions challenged the 1996 order on multiple fronts, and in 1997 the Eighth Circuit stayed the FCC's pricing rules. The following year that court held that the FCC lacked jurisdiction to set pricing methodology for local competition, reserving that power to the states, and struck down several of the order's provisions. The Supreme Court took the case and decided AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board, 525 U.S. 366, on January 25, 1999. The Court reversed the Eighth Circuit on jurisdiction, holding that the FCC, not the states, could prescribe the methodology for pricing interconnection, unbundled elements, and resale. It held that incumbents had to furnish unbundled elements individually or in any combination a competitor requested, regardless of whether the competitor owned its own facilities. It reinstated the rule against dismantling existing combinations and the pick and choose rule. But the decision also vacated the heart of the unbundling list. Rule 319, the Court held, failed to apply the statutory test of Section 251(d)(2): for proprietary elements, whether access was "necessary," and for all others, whether denial would "impair" a competitor's ability to provide service. The FCC had effectively granted blanket access, and the Court sent the list back for a lawful determination.

The Commission's answer, the UNE Remand Order of 1999, reimposed broad unbundling obligations, including switching, under a revised impairment analysis. A companion Line Sharing Order the same year required incumbents to unbundle the high-frequency portion of the local loop so that data carriers could offer DSL service over copper the incumbent still used for voice. Entrants planned and borrowed against these rules. Then the courts intervened twice more in the spring of 2002. On May 13, 2002, the Supreme Court decided Verizon Communications Inc. v. FCC, 535 U.S. 467, and upheld TELRIC itself: the FCC could require state commissions to set wholesale rates on a forward-looking basis untied to the incumbents' past investment, and the combination rules survived as well. Eleven days later, on May 24, 2002, the D.C. Circuit decided United States Telecom Association v. FCC, 290 F.3d 415, known as USTA I, and vacated the agency's unbundling rules. The court held that the Commission had again required unbundling in markets where there was no reasonable basis for finding impairment, effectively preserving blanket nationwide unbundling for traditional facilities. The decision vacated both the UNE Remand Order and the Line Sharing Order, including the unbundling of circuit switching and the line-sharing rule for DSL. The Supreme Court denied review in 538 U.S. 940, decided March 24, 2003, and the vacatur took effect in February 2003, erasing the legal foundation on which a generation of entrant business plans had been built.

The FCC's next attempt, the Triennial Review Order, was adopted on February 20, 2003, released August 21, 2003, and published at 18 FCC Rcd 16978, with an effective date of October 2, 2003. The order made a national finding of no impairment for switching used to serve enterprise customers on high-capacity loops, ending unbundled switching for that segment. For mass-market customers, it preserved unbundled switching and the UNE-P, but delegated the impairment determination to the state commissions, which were given nine months, until July 2, 2004, to decide market by market whether competitors were impaired without access. It retained unbundling for high-capacity loops and dedicated transport, eliminated the line-sharing unbundling obligation, and gave fiber-to-the-home loops broad relief. State commissions opened dockets across the country to conduct the impairment proceedings the order required.

That delegation did not survive judicial review. On March 2, 2004, the D.C. Circuit decided United States Telecom Association v. FCC, 359 F.3d 554, known as USTA II, and vacated the Triennial Review Order's unbundling provisions. The court struck down the nationwide impairment findings for mass-market switching and dedicated transport as overbroad, and it held the subdelegation of impairment decisions to state commissions unlawful. The solicitor general announced in June 2004 that the government would not seek Supreme Court review, and the Court denied the remaining petitions that October. With the rules in limbo, the FCC issued an interim order on August 20, 2004, directing incumbents to keep providing mass-market switching, high-capacity loops, and dedicated transport until final rules took effect.

The final rules arrived in the Triennial Review Remand Order, adopted December 15, 2004, released February 4, 2005, and effective March 11, 2005. The order ended the unbundling obligation for mass-market local circuit switching, which eliminated the UNE-P as a regulatory product. It gave carriers a twelve-month transition for the embedded base of UNE-P customers, at a rate equal to the higher of the rate in effect on June 15, 2004, plus one dollar, or any state-established rate plus one dollar, with no new UNE-P orders permitted. It sharply narrowed unbundling for DS1 and DS3 loops and for dedicated transport, sorting wire centers into tiers based on competitive presence and demand, and it ended unbundling for dark fiber loops. The transition confronted a concrete operational obstacle that illustrated how deeply the old regime had embedded itself. Moving a customer from UNE-P service to a competitor's own switch required a "hot cut": a technician physically unhooked the customer loop from the incumbent's switch and cut it over to the entrant's switch while service remained active, a process competitors described as slow, expensive, and disruptive. With UNE-P, the incumbent remained the technical provider of record and no cut was needed, which a Network World analysis of the order identified as the most durable justification entrants offered for keeping the platform. The order acknowledged that hot-cut failures had impaired competition but directed the states to fix the cutover process rather than preserve UNE-P indefinitely, a large policy shift from managing entry to forcing facilities. A state commission filing from the period described the order as the culmination of nearly a decade of litigation over the unbundling regime, and the description was accurate: the 1996 rules had been vacated by the Supreme Court in 1999, the 1999 rules had been vacated by the D.C. Circuit in 2002, and the 2003 rules had been vacated by the D.C. Circuit in 2004 before the Commission finally wrote rules the courts would leave standing.

The parallel pattern of agency rules written broadly and then vacated on review also runs through [financial regulation court cases](/2014/01/15/financial-regulation-court-cases/), where courts repeatedly struck down expansive implementations of new statutory mandates.

## The Entrant Boom

Capital responded to the 1996 regime with extraordinary speed. The Heritage Foundation reported that in 1999 some 300 competitive local exchange carriers were operating in the United States, with a combined market capitalization of about 86 billion dollars. An institutional analysis published in Information Systems Frontiers put the facilities-based count at no fewer than 200 carriers by the end of 1999, and recorded the prices the boom commanded: WorldCom paid 13 billion dollars for MFS Communications in 1997, and AT&T paid 11 billion dollars for Teleport Communications Group in 1998. Equity markets were valuing local-network challengers as if the regulatory doorway would stay open indefinitely.

The operating figures tracked the investment. FCC data collected on Form 477 showed competitive carriers serving 8,194,243 end-user switched access lines in December 1999, a 4.3 percent share of the national total. By December 2000 the count reached 14,871,409 lines, a 7.7 percent share; by December 2001 it reached 19,653,441 lines, a 10.3 percent share; and by December 2002 it reached 24,863,691 lines, a 13.1 percent share. The number of reporting competitive carriers, counting only those with at least 10,000 lines in a state, rose from 81 in December 1999 to 112 in December 2002. Revenues told the same story: FCC industry figures showed competitive access providers and competitive local carriers taking in 623 million dollars in 1995, 5.65 billion dollars in 1999, 9.81 billion dollars in 2000, and 13.0 billion dollars in 2001. In six years a segment that had barely existed became a thirteen-billion-dollar business.

The composition of that growth reveals the regime's fingerprints. FCC tabulations of how competitive carriers served their lines showed the share served through unbundled network elements rising from 23.9 percent in December 1999 to 37.3 percent in December 2000, 47.5 percent in December 2001, and 55.4 percent in December 2002, while the share served through resale of the incumbent's retail services fell from 42.9 percent to 18.8 percent over the same span. Entrants were migrating exactly as the price signals directed: away from resale, with its thin avoided-cost discount, and toward UNE-based service, with its TELRIC pricing, and within UNE-based service toward the UNE-P, with its forty-to-sixty-percent discount. A parallel wave of data-only entrants built DSL businesses on unbundled loops and line sharing: Covad, NorthPoint, and Rhythms NetConnections became national names selling high-speed access over copper they did not own. The boom was real in lines, revenues, and employment, and its shape was the shape of the rules.

Not every entrant climbed the same rungs. Facilities-based carriers such as Allegiance Telecom and Time Warner Telecom built their own switches and fiber rings in business districts, using unbundled loops mainly to reach individual buildings. Data-only entrants took a narrower bet: Covad, NorthPoint, and Rhythms installed DSL equipment in incumbent central offices and sold high-speed access over unbundled loops, a business that existed only because the Line Sharing Order gave them the high-frequency portion of copper the incumbent used for voice. The largest UNE-P users, AT&T and WorldCom, took the opposite approach, reselling the incumbent's end-to-end service under their own brands with minimal facilities of their own. The regime thus supported three distinct business models at once, and the courts would later invalidate the regulatory foundations of two of them: line sharing fell in USTA I in May 2002, and mass-market switching fell in the TRRO in 2005. Only the facilities-based model rested on assets its owners controlled. The FCC's line counts carried a further caveat worth stating plainly: through December 2004, only carriers with at least 10,000 lines in a state were required to report, so the smallest entrants never appeared in the totals at all.

## The Collapse

The capital cycle turned before the legal cycle finished. In December 2000, Covad cut its revenue expectations for 2001 from 550 million dollars to 380 million dollars and announced layoffs and central-office closings, according to the EDN industry timeline, and four smaller DSL providers filed for bankruptcy protection that same month. ICG Communications, a facilities-based competitor, had already filed for Chapter 11 protection in the fall of 2000, as EE Times reported in January 2001. What followed was a roll call of the industry's most prominent names. NorthPoint Communications filed for Chapter 11 protection on January 16, 2001, after Verizon withdrew a planned majority investment, and its network was shut down that March. E.spire Communications filed on March 22, 2001, according to its own Form 10-K. Winstar Communications filed on April 18, 2001, reporting about 5 billion dollars in outstanding debt against some 30,000 business customers and more than 700 million dollars in year-2000 revenue. Rhythms NetConnections filed on August 1, 2001, according to the company's SEC filings and the bankruptcy court record, and Covad followed on August 15, 2001, according to its Form 8-K, then cut 1.4 billion dollars of bondholder debt to 257 million dollars, nineteen cents on the dollar, before emerging from bankruptcy that December. Focal Communications completed a comprehensive recapitalization on October 26, 2001, according to its Form 10-K.

The failures continued into 2002 and 2003. McLeodUSA filed for Chapter 11 protection on January 31, 2002, erasing about 3 billion dollars in debt through a pre-negotiated reorganization. Williams Communications filed in 2002 carrying roughly 6 billion dollars in debt it could not service, according to Light Reading. XO Communications filed on June 17, 2002, according to the bankruptcy court record, proposing to restructure 8.5 billion dollars in debt after a rescue investment from Forstmann Little and Telefonos de Mexico collapsed. Allegiance Telecom filed on May 14, 2003, according to its SEC monthly operating report, and XO acquired the bankrupt carrier the following year. The Heritage Foundation's post-crash accounting put the survivors at 80 to 100 carriers with a combined capitalization of about 4 billion dollars, against the 300 carriers and 86 billion dollars of 1999.

The employment figures trace the same arc as the balance sheets. Covad laid off 400 employees and closed 200 central offices in December 2000, according to the EDN timeline. Rhythms cut 450 jobs, twenty-three percent of its workforce, in January 2001. Winstar laid off 2,000 employees, nearly half its workforce, in April 2001, days before its bankruptcy filing. Behind the layoffs stood a capital market that had simply closed. Telecommunications issuers that had raised billions in high-yield debt during 1998 and 1999 found no buyers for new paper in 2001, and carriers still burning cash to finish their builds could neither complete their networks nor service the debt already incurred. The bankruptcies of 2001 and 2002 were therefore not a verdict delivered after the legal regime settled; they arrived while the regime was still being litigated, with USTA I undecided until May 2002 and the Triennial Review Order not yet written. Investors were asked to fund companies whose central input, the wholesale price list, was before the courts, and they stopped.

The mechanics of the collapse displayed the stranded-asset dynamic that defines a regulatory bust. Networks had been built, and debt had been incurred, on the assumption that a particular wholesale price list would remain available for the life of the assets. When equity markets closed to telecommunications issuers in 2000 and 2001, carriers that were still burning cash to build could not refinance, and the bankruptcy courts became the industry's clearinghouse. Assets changed hands at distress prices: according to a book-length account of the meltdown, WorldCom acquired Rhythms' assets, AT&T acquired NorthPoint's bankruptcy assets, and XO, itself freshly reorganized under new ownership, bought Allegiance. Equipment ordered for central offices that never opened moved into secondary markets at bargain prices. The entanglements ran in both directions: SBC Communications, one of the incumbent Bell companies, paid Covad 135 million dollars to walk away from a 600-million-dollar commercial agreement and erased 15 million dollars in advertising fees, according to Internet News, a transaction that showed how deeply the entrants' fate had become tied to the incumbents' strategies. The physical plant did not vanish; it was re-priced, and the losses fell on the bondholders and shareholders who had financed construction under one set of rules and watched another set take effect.

One fact disciplines every interpretation of the collapse. While the firms were failing, the lines were not. FCC data showed competitive carriers serving 19.7 million lines in December 2001, in the middle of the bankruptcy wave, and 24.9 million lines in December 2002, after most of the prominent failures. By December 2004, competitive carriers served 32.9 million lines, an 18.5 percent national share, the highest ever recorded in the FCC's series. The bust destroyed capital, not the competitive segment. Customers kept buying from someone; the question was which corporate shells would survive to serve them, and at what cost of capital.

## The Honest Causal Account

The boom and the bust share a single causal spine, and it is the regulatory framework. The investment thesis of the late 1990s assumed durable access to the incumbent's network at TELRIC wholesale rates, with the UNE-P as its most lucrative expression. Capital was committed, networks were built, and debt was layered on under the 1996 rules and the 1999 remand rules. The courts then dismantled those rules in stages: the Supreme Court vacated the unbundling list in January 1999, the D.C. Circuit vacated the remand rules and the line-sharing rule in May 2002, and the D.C. Circuit vacated the Triennial Review Order's mass-market unbundling and state-delegation provisions in March 2004. The Commission's final order in early 2005 then removed the single most valuable input, mass-market switching, ending the UNE-P outright. Business plans predicated on UNE-P arbitrage were not refuted by the marketplace; they were invalidated by judicial decisions, years after the money was spent. Plans predicated on line sharing for DSL met the same fate when the D.C. Circuit struck the rule in USTA I. The timing was unknowable in advance, because each FCC order took years to litigate, and each vacatur retroactively repriced investments made in reliance on the order it struck.

The demand evidence rules out the simplest alternative explanation. A bust driven by lack of customer demand would show competitive lines collapsing alongside the firms that served them. The opposite happened: competitive lines rose in every FCC reporting period from December 1999 through December 2004, and the competitive share of national switched access lines roughly quadrupled, from 4.3 percent to 18.5 percent. Whatever was failing, it was not the proposition that customers would buy telephone service from a company other than the incumbent. It was the proposition that a particular regulatory price list would last long enough to amortize the debt incurred to exploit it.

The two sides of the policy debate agreed on the mechanism while drawing opposite morals, and each deserves its statement. The incumbent carriers and their association argued, in a 2005 joint court brief summarized by Trade Law Daily, that the FCC had three times been told its rules were unlawful because they required sharing even where competitors could and did provide service without forced access to incumbent facilities, and that rates set on hypothetical forward-looking costs invited arbitrage rather than construction. An incumbent filing before a state commission put the charge more bluntly, quoting the D.C. Circuit's finding that the agency's underlying belief that more unbundling was always better contradicted the 1996 Act's impairment limit. Analysts at the Precursor Group, quoted in a Cato Institute assessment of the Triennial Review Order, described the regime as the de facto socialization of the Bell companies' infrastructure to drive prices down artificially, with resale competition ceasing to be a transition to facilities-based competition and becoming an end in itself. On this account, the bust was the inevitable correction of an artificial boom: capital had flowed into resale of someone else's network, not into competing networks, and the correction began when the courts forced the rules to respect the statute's impairment standard. The entrants and their advocates argued the reverse: that the incumbents used serial litigation to delay entry and to keep the rules permanently uncertain, raising the entrants' cost of capital and frightening equity markets at the worst moment. The NorthPoint episode, in which Verizon's withdrawal of a planned investment was followed within weeks by a bankruptcy filing, became their exhibit for how regulatory risk, rather than operating failure, killed viable challengers. On this account, the bust was manufactured by the legal war itself.

Both accounts locate the cause in the same place, and that convergence is the honest finding. Wholesale access priced below the cost of building drew entry on terms that could not survive the removal of that access, while a decade of litigation made the timing and terms of removal unknowable to anyone committing capital. The result was a cycle in which the rules first subsidized entry and then, through the courts, repriced it, with the losses landing on investors and the assets landing in the hands of survivors. To read the bankruptcies as proof that local competition was unwanted ignores the lines, which grew straight through the failures. To read the line growth as proof that the regime worked ignores the capital, which was incinerated by the regime's instability. The litigation chronology is the record that holds both halves together, and any diagnosis that omits it misdiagnoses everything.

## Finding Three: Structural Reassembly

### The Seven Companies of the Divestiture

On January 1, 1984, the former AT&T surrendered its local telephone operations under the 1982 Modification of Final Judgment, and those operations were reorganized as seven independent Regional Bell Operating Companies. Ameritech covered five Midwestern states, Bell Atlantic the mid-Atlantic, BellSouth nine Southeastern states, NYNEX New York and New England, Pacific Telesis California and Nevada, Southwestern Bell Arkansas, Kansas, Missouri, Oklahoma, and Texas, and US West fourteen states across the mountain and Pacific Northwest. AT&T retained long distance service, Western Electric manufacturing, and Bell Laboratories. The decree barred the seven regional companies from providing interLATA long distance service, from manufacturing telecommunications equipment, and from offering information services, and its practical effect was to prohibit combinations among them. The breakup's stated purpose was to unleash competition in long distance while leaving the local exchange monopolies intact and regulated. AT&T's own corporate history identifies the seven as the Baby Bells created by the 1984 split.

The decree's design reflected a deliberate asymmetry. Long distance was declared competitive and opened to MCI, Sprint, and other entrants, while local service was treated as a natural monopoly to be contained rather than contested. Each regional company inherited a monopoly franchise over local exchange service in its territory, regulated by state commissions, and the decree's walls were meant to keep those monopolies from leveraging into the newly competitive markets. The seven were not expected to compete with one another; their territories did not overlap, and the decree's restrictions made combination pointless. The structure was fragmentation by design, but the fragmentation was geographic, not competitive. No customer of Bell Atlantic could choose NYNEX, and no customer of Pacific Telesis could choose US West. The 1984 settlement broke one national monopoly into seven regional ones.

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 dismantled that architecture. Its section 271 created a checklist process for Bell company entry into interLATA long distance, its manufacturing provisions ended the equipment ban, and the statute as a whole superseded the decree's line-of-business restrictions. The 1996 act thus did two things at once: it opened the local exchange to competitors, and it freed the seven regional companies to expand beyond their territories and to combine with one another. The first purpose is the one the statute is remembered for. The second is the one the market acted on.

### The SBC Chain of Combinations

Southwestern Bell, renamed SBC Communications in 1995, moved first and moved the most. It acquired Pacific Telesis in 1997, gaining California and Nevada, and Ameritech in 1999, folding the five-state Midwest Bell system into its own, according to AT&T's published brand history. Along the way it added the Southern New England Telephone Company in 1998. The retiree association summary of this history records that the Pacific Telesis purchase, the Southern New England purchase, and the Ameritech acquisition together made SBC the largest telephone company in the United States. In 2005 SBC purchased its former parent, AT&T Corp., and adopted the AT&T name, launching as AT&T Inc. The transaction reunited the long distance business and the global enterprise customer base with the local networks, and the surviving company took the older brand rather than its own. In 2006 the new AT&T acquired BellSouth, the last independent regional company, a combination that also consolidated ownership of the Cingular Wireless joint venture, making it a wholly owned subsidiary later rebranded AT&T Mobility, again per AT&T's brand history. One acquirer thus absorbed four of the seven regional companies plus the long distance parent that had once owned them all, reversing the direction of the 1984 split in a single decade of transactions.

The strategy behind the chain was scale as an offensive weapon. SBC had been among the smaller regional companies, and its leadership concluded that the coming contest would be won by carriers large enough to finance national wireless networks and broadband upgrades from the cash flows of the local monopoly. Each acquisition was larger than the last: Pacific Telesis brought the California market, the largest in the country; Ameritech brought the industrial Midwest; AT&T Corp. brought the global enterprise business and the most recognized brand in the industry; BellSouth brought the Southeast and full control of the wireless joint venture. The sequence reads as a deliberate inversion of the divestiture, executed under the legal opening the 1996 act provided, and it made SBC, once a regional fragment, into the corporate heir of the company that had been broken apart.

### The Verizon Chain

Bell Atlantic announced its combination with fellow regional company NYNEX in 1996, NYNEX was consolidated into the Bell Atlantic name in 1997, and the headquarters moved from Philadelphia to New York City, according to Verizon's own historical account. Bell Atlantic then acquired GTE, a large non-Bell telephone company with a national footprint, in a transaction announced in July 1998 and valued at more than 52 billion dollars at announcement. The combination closed on June 30, 2000, at a value the regulatory record placed at 64.7 billion dollars, and the merged company began operating as Verizon Communications that day. Two months earlier, Bell Atlantic and the British carrier Vodafone had formed the Verizon Wireless joint venture, valued at approximately 70 billion dollars, combining Bell Atlantic's wireless network with Vodafone's AirTouch and PrimeCo holdings and the wireless division of GTE into a carrier with 23 million customers, with Bell Atlantic holding 55 percent and Vodafone 45 percent. The sequence also carried a long distance milestone: Bell Atlantic was the first Bell company to win approval to provide long distance service within its own territory, in New York in December 1999, following the section 271 process the 1996 act had created. Two regional companies and the largest independent telephone company had become a single coast to coast carrier with a national wireless arm and long distance authority.

The GTE combination supplied what the Bell Atlantic and NYNEX union lacked: a national wireline footprint outside the Northeast corridor. GTE was the largest telephone company never part of the Bell System, with operations spread across the country, and its acquisition gave the merged company local exchange presence far beyond the Atlantic seaboard. The wireless joint venture supplied the second half of the logic. By combining Bell Atlantic's cellular properties with Vodafone's AirTouch holdings and GTE's wireless division, the venture created a carrier with national coverage at a moment when regional wireless companies were being outspent and outbuilt. The wireline merger and the wireless merger were announced within the same two-year window, and together they expressed a single thesis: the future belonged to carriers that could sell local, long distance, and mobile service under one brand across the whole country.

### US West and the End State

The seventh regional company met a different acquirer. Qwest Communications, a long distance and data carrier built through acquisitions in the late 1990s, made an unsolicited offer for US West in the winter of 1999 to 2000, outbidding a rival proposal from Global Crossing, and the two merged on June 30, 2000, in a transaction valued at 48 billion dollars. US West ceased to exist as a separate company, and all of its subsidiaries passed directly to Qwest, whose brand replaced the US West brand. As a condition of the combination, Qwest sold off its long distance operations inside the fourteen-state US West region, which were eventually acquired by Touch America.

The contest for US West was itself a product of the period's merger logic. US West had been attempting to merge with Global Crossing when Qwest's unsolicited bid arrived, and the US West board accepted the higher Qwest offer after Qwest raised its price to 69 dollars per share. The bidding war valued a fourteen-state local monopoly franchise as the prize asset, not for its copper plant alone but for the 25 million customers and the regional networks that came with it, per the Springer case history of the deal. The combined company's market capitalization stood at approximately 85 billion dollars. Unlike the Bell-to-Bell combinations, this one absorbed a regional company into a non-Bell acquirer, which meant the Bell System's western fragment ended up outside both of the successor Bell groups. Qwest itself was acquired by CenturyLink on April 1, 2011, closing the last chapter of the seven.

By 2011, a decade and a half after the statute's passage, the seven companies of 1984 had been reassembled into essentially three wireline groups. AT&T held Ameritech, Pacific Telesis, Southwestern Bell, and BellSouth, plus the old AT&T Corp. Verizon held Bell Atlantic, NYNEX, and GTE. CenturyLink held US West. A statute designed to produce fragmented local competition had presided over the reassembly of the Bell System into fewer, larger carriers. The mechanism was the statute's own doing, since the 1996 act lifted the decree's restrictions and opened the door to combinations among the regional companies, which is what made the reassembly legally possible. The breakup of the Bell System is commonly described as ending with the 1996 act's permission for the regional companies to merge with each other or with non-Bell companies, and the subsequent decade supplied the mergers.

### The Irony, Weighed Both Ways

The reassembly invites two readings, and both deserve equal care. The investment reading holds that scale was the precondition for the capital spending the period demanded. Fiber and broadband buildouts, digital network upgrades, and the construction of national wireless carriers required balance sheets and geographic footprints that seven separate regional companies could not supply on their own. Consolidation pooled the cash flows of the local monopolies and directed them toward the networks of the next generation, and the resulting national carriers could bargain with equipment vendors, content owners, and regulators on terms no regional company could match. On this reading, the mergers were the market financing the investment the statute wanted.

The concentration reading holds that the local market the act meant to open ended up with fewer owners rather than more. Competitive local exchange carriers complained of foreclosure along the way, including the 1996 complaints reported in the Denver Post and the Rocky Mountain News that US West was neglecting or delaying the release of unbundled loops the act required it to provide. Consumers in most local markets still faced a single wireline incumbent when the decade closed, and the regional companies that had been created to be separate competitors had become divisions of two national firms. On this reading, the act traded seven regulated monopolies for two larger ones and called the result competition. Both readings describe the same transactions. The efficiency case explains why the combinations happened and what they funded; the concentration case measures what the market lost when they did. The evidence supports each in its own terms, and neither can be dismissed without distorting the record.

The shape of the market in 2006 makes the irony concrete. The statute's local competition provisions had been written for a world of many small entrants leasing unbundled network elements from the incumbents. The world that emerged instead had two dominant wireline carriers, each assembled from the fragments of the 1984 breakup, plus a third carrier holding the western states. The competitive local exchange carriers the act had envisioned did enter, and some survived, but the industry's center of gravity moved toward consolidation rather than fragmentation. Fifteen years after a statute designed to produce fragmented competition, the Bell companies had merged back into a handful of large carriers, which was the opposite of the intended market structure. The statute did not fail to permit competition; it succeeded in permitting combination, and combination was the option the market preferred.

### Wireless as the Competitive Counterweight

Against the wireline reassembly, mobile telephony grew explosively, and that growth was largely independent of the statute. CTIA's semi-annual carrier survey recorded 44,042,992 wireless connections in 1996 and 207,896,198 at year-end 2005, when penetration stood at nearly 70 percent, per CTIA statistics reported in April 2006. The industry added 25.7 million net new subscribers in 2005 alone, an 18.4 percent gain, the largest annual increase in its history to that point. Subscribers talked for almost 1.5 trillion minutes in calendar 2005, and carrier revenue from data services reached 8.58 billion dollars. The engine of that growth was spectrum, not the act. The FCC auctioned broadband personal communications service licenses in 1994 and 1995, digital networks and subsidized handsets followed, and subscriber growth compounded through the late 1990s before the act's provisions had taken practical effect. The act's own contribution to wireless was secondary: it barred states from regulating wireless rates and gave mobile carriers interconnection rights as telecommunications carriers.

The FCC's 2006 universal service order supplies the wireline contrast. Wireless service provider revenues grew from approximately 70 billion dollars in 2000 to approximately 122 billion dollars in 2004, and the number of wireless subscribers grew from approximately 101 million to 181 million, with more than 25 million added in 2005, while wireline switched access lines declined from approximately 192 million in December 2000. Competition did arrive in the period, but it arrived through a technology the 1996 framework had treated as an adjunct, not through the local wireline entry the act had placed at the center of its design. The distinction matters for attribution. Crediting the statute with the wireless boom would repeat the error of crediting it with the long distance decline: both trends were set in motion by forces outside the act, and the act at most cleared brush from their path.

## Finding Four: Prices

### Long Distance Fell on a Preexisting Trend

Long distance prices fell substantially across the period, and the statute cannot claim the decline. The FCC's Trends in Telephone Service report records average revenue per minute for long distance calls at 32 cents in 1984 and 7 cents in 2006, a decline of more than 85 percent after adjusting for inflation, with intermediate readings of 10 cents in 2001 and 9 cents in 2002. The slope was established long before the statute: AT&T's average revenue per minute fell from 32 cents in 1984 to 18 cents in 1994, a 40 percent drop recorded in the FCC's 1998 Trends report, two years before the act was signed. Adjusted for inflation, interstate and international toll rates declined 60 percent from 1991 to 2001, per the FCC's long distance market report.

The mechanism was the FCC's access charge rebalancing that followed the 1984 divestiture. Per-minute access charges paid by long distance carriers to local companies were reduced from 17.3 cents in 1984 to 1.4 cents in 2004, while the residential subscriber line charge rose from zero to 5.96 dollars per month, per the FCC Universal Service Monitoring Report figures. The policy moved the recovery of non-traffic-sensitive local costs from per-minute toll charges to flat monthly line charges, which simultaneously raised local rates and lowered toll rates. Competition among AT&T, MCI, and Sprint in long distance, competition unleashed by the divestiture rather than by the 1996 act, drove the retail side down on top of the access reform.

The rebalancing carried a cost that the long distance figures conceal. The same policy that cut per-minute access charges raised the flat monthly price of local service: the average price of a residential local line rose from 15.18 dollars per month in 1984 to 24.52 dollars in 2004, while the average long distance price fell from 32 cents to 8 cents per minute over the same period, per the FCC monitoring figures. The subscriber line charge that replaced part of the access charge appeared directly on local bills, which meant households that made few toll calls paid more while heavy long distance users paid less. The FCC's 1998 Trends report shows the retail side of the same story: AT&T's basic schedule charges for directly dialed interstate calls had been reduced about 30 percent for residential callers and 20 percent for business callers since 1984. The long distance decline was therefore not a free gain for consumers as a group; it was a transfer, engineered by the FCC over two decades, from per-minute toll pricing to flat local pricing. None of that engineering dates to 1996.

The act's contribution to long distance was the section 271 process that let Bell companies into interLATA service, and Bell Atlantic won the first such approval, in New York, in December 1999. By May 2003 the Commission had approved Bell company applications in 41 states and the District of Columbia, per the FCC's long distance report. But by then the interstate average was already in single digits, and the great majority of the inflation-adjusted decline had already occurred. The credit for falling long distance rates belongs to the 1984 divestiture and to the FCC's two-decade access reform, not to the 1996 statute, and the record should not assign the act a victory it inherited.

### Cable Rose Faster Than Inflation

Cable television followed the opposite track, and here the statute's fingerprints are direct. The Government Accountability Office's October 2003 report on the cable industry found that FCC data showed the average monthly rate for the combined basic and expanded basic tiers rising from 26.06 dollars in 1997 to 36.47 dollars in 2002, a 40 percent increase over five years against roughly 12 percent for the Consumer Price Index. The Bureau of Labor Statistics cable television subcategory index rose just under 30 percent over the same five years, a smaller increase that reflects the Bureau's adjustment for the growing number of channels. At a 2004 Senate hearing on escalating cable rates, Senator John McCain, citing the FCC's annual cable price report, stated that since 1996 cable rates had increased 56 percent, nearly three times the rate of inflation. The FCC's own price survey table shows the expanded basic tier rising from 22.35 dollars in July 1995 to 43.04 dollars in January 2005. That pricing history began under the [Cable Communications Policy Act of 1984](/2017/08/15/cable-communications-policy-act-1984/), which first gave regulators authority over monopoly cable rates.

The deregulation applied to the expanded basic tier, the package most subscribers bought, while basic tier rate regulation remained available where no effective competition existed. That asymmetry shaped the outcome. Operators could not freely raise the price of the must-carry basic tier in monopoly markets, so the expanded tier, freed by the March 1999 sunset, absorbed the increases. The FCC's survey table shows the basic tier price moving modestly, from 12.06 dollars in July 1998 to 14.30 dollars in January 2005, while the expanded basic component drove the combined bill upward. The statute thus deregulated exactly the tier where operators had the most pricing discretion and subscribers had the fewest alternatives, since the basic tier's regulated price was anchored by broadcast retransmission and public access channels that subscribers could not decline separately.

The increase rested on three forces. The first was the act itself, which sunset federal rate regulation of the cable programming services tier after March 31, 1999, exactly as the statutory text provides. Representative Edward Markey acknowledged the coming deadline in February 1998 remarks introducing legislation to extend the controls, noting that the telephone industry competition the deregulation had assumed had not materialized in any significant way. The second force was cost. GAO, drawing on data from nine cable operators, identified rising programming expenses and infrastructure investment as the primary cost factors behind the increases. An earlier GAO report on sports programming costs documented how licensing fees for cable networks, particularly sports networks, were being passed through to subscribers, and Bureau of Labor Statistics data showed cable rates rising 7.3 percent between June 1997 and June 1998 against 1.7 percent for the overall Consumer Price Index. The third force was market structure. GAO's principal finding was that competition leads to lower cable rates and improved quality, yet only about 2 percent of markets had a wired competitor, and where one existed the incumbent's rates ran 15 percent lower, with subsequent GAO work suggesting reductions as large as 41 percent in some markets, while satellite competition had minimal effect on incumbent pricing.

The investment case deserves equal care. Channel counts on expanded basic grew from 44 in July 1995 to more than 70 by January 2005, per the FCC's cable price survey table, and operators were rebuilding networks for digital video and broadband service, spending that had to be funded somewhere. The industry's position was that the rate increases paid for the programming and the plant that subscribers were receiving. The concentration case is the mirror image. Without a wired rival in 98 percent of markets, that investment could be funded through rate increases that outpaced inflation, and the act's deregulation date removed the one check on that pricing power. Cable subscribers paid for the network upgrades twice over, once as ratepayers and once as the captive funding base for them.

### The Universal Service Factor Climbed as Its Base Shrank

The third track is the universal service contribution factor, the percentage of interstate and international end-user revenues that carriers pay into the fund. FCC order 06-94 records the factor at 5.9 percent in the first quarter of 2000, 8.9 percent in the fourth quarter of 2004, and 10.9 percent for the second quarter of 2006. The FCC's Trends in Telephone Service table of contribution factors supplies the intervening steps: 7.2805 percent from the third quarter of 2002 through the first quarter of 2003, 9.1 percent in the second quarter of 2003, and 11.1 percent in the second quarter of 2005, easing to 10.2 percent by the fourth quarter of that year. The Congressional Research Service background report on the fund describes the same arc, from a low of 6.7 percent in the first quarter of 2001 to a high of 11.1 percent in the second quarter of 2005.

The arithmetic explains the climb. The assessable revenue base declined from about 79.0 billion dollars in 2000 to about 74.7 billion dollars in 2004, while fund disbursements grew from approximately 4.4 billion dollars in 2000 to approximately 5.7 billion dollars in 2004 and approximately 6.5 billion dollars in 2005, all per FCC 06-94. A shrinking denominator met a growing numerator. The denominator shrank because interstate toll rates kept falling and wireline access lines kept declining, eroding the interstate revenues the factor taxes. The numerator grew because the fund's programs, support for schools and libraries, high cost support, and low income support, kept expanding. The Commission itself stated that declines in the contribution base combined with growth in the size of the fund placed upward pressure on the percentage of assessable revenues that must be contributed.

The statute's design choice compounded the effect. Wireless carriers began contributing to the fund on January 1, 1998, but only through a safe harbor percentage of their revenues, initially 15 percent and later raised, per the Congressional Research Service report. Interconnected voice over Internet protocol providers were not required to contribute until the FCC's June 2006 decision.

The quarterly mechanics turned a gradual erosion into a visible ratchet. Each quarter the Universal Service Administrative Company projected the coming quarter's program demand and the contribution base, and the Commission set the factor as the ratio of the two, which meant every shrinkage of interstate revenues translated mechanically into a higher percentage within months. The fund's largest growth engine in these years was the schools and libraries program, whose projected demand ran into the hundreds of millions per quarter, alongside high cost support for rural carriers and low income support. The base, meanwhile, was defined as interstate and international end-user telecommunications revenues, precisely the revenue category that falling toll rates and wireless substitution were hollowing out. The numerator the statute expanded and the denominator the market eroded moved in opposite directions by design and by economics at once, and the factor recorded their divergence every quarter. The fastest growing revenue streams of the period therefore sat largely outside the contribution base while the fund's obligations grew against the shrinking interstate wireline base. The factor is computed quarterly as projected program demand divided by projected contribution revenue, as the FCC's public notice for the second quarter of 2005 demonstrates in setting the factor at 11.1 percent. The rising surcharge was produced by that funding arithmetic, a numerator the statute expanded and a denominator the market eroded, not by any general deregulatory intent of the statute.

Taken together, the three tracks refuse a single verdict on the statute's price effects, and that refusal is itself the finding. Long distance rates fell because of a divestiture and a regulatory rebalancing that predated the act by more than a decade. Cable rates rose because the act removed the price ceiling on the tier subscribers actually bought, in markets where no wired rival existed to discipline the replacement pricing. The universal service factor rose because the act enlarged the fund's obligations while leaving its revenue base tied to a shrinking interstate toll market. Each outcome has a named mechanism, a measured magnitude, and a dated record. Attributing any of them to the statute as a whole would blur the very distinctions the evidence was assembled to preserve.

## Finding Five: The School Connectivity Program

Section 254 of the 1996 statute expanded the definition of universal service beyond affordable residential telephone access to include advanced telecommunications for classrooms and libraries. Congress wrote the principle in broad terms, directing the Federal Communications Commission to establish competitively neutral rules that would enhance access to advanced services for public and nonprofit private elementary and secondary school classrooms and for libraries. The Commission implemented that directive on May 7, 1997, through Order 97-157, creating what came to be called the Schools and Libraries Program, universally known as E-Rate. Under the design the FCC adopted, eligible institutions received discounts ranging from 20 percent to 90 percent on telecommunications services, internet access, and internal connections, with the steepest discounts reserved for applicants whose students faced the deepest poverty, measured by eligibility for the national school lunch program, and with an added increment for rural locations. Schools and libraries did not receive cash. The Universal Service Administrative Company, through its Schools and Libraries Division, held the committed funds and reimbursed vendors directly for the discounted portion of approved services, a structure meant to keep the money tied to purchased services rather than general budgets. Annual disbursements were capped at 2.25 billion dollars, and applicants were required to assess their technology needs, secure state-level certification of that assessment, and select vendors through a competitive bidding process advertised nationally.

Demand for the discounts immediately outran the cap. In a report covering the first three program years, the Government Accountability Office found that requests for support rose steadily after funding began in 1998. For the third program year, covering 2000, total requests exceeded 4.2 billion dollars, nearly double the annual ceiling. The imbalance concentrated in internal connections, the wiring and equipment that carried networks inside buildings: applicants requested 3.2 billion dollars for that category, and nearly 2 billion dollars of it went unfunded because the available money ran out before reaching applicants at discount levels of 81 percent or lower. The GAO account is valuable not as a verdict on the program but as a measure of its reach. Within three years of launch, the discount mechanism had become a central financing channel for school technology spending across the country, with demand demonstrating that administrators treated the subsidy as load-bearing rather than marginal.

The category breakdown for that third year shows where the money wanted to go. Applicants requested 3.17 billion dollars for internal connections, 811 million for telecommunications services, and 247 million for internet access itself, according to the GAO's figures. The Schools and Libraries Division could fund every valid request for telecommunications and internet access but could extend internal-connections support only to applicants at discount levels of 82 percent or higher, meaning the poorest applicants were served and the middle of the distribution was rationed out. That triage was built into the program's design: when the cap bound, the neediest got priority, which is one reason the causal research later found the largest adoption effects in disadvantaged schools. The GAO's later oversight work, particularly a 2005 report on the program's management, added a less flattering administrative record. The FCC had set a fiscal year 2001 goal of connecting 90 percent of libraries, then dropped the goal from subsequent budget submissions and performance reports without replacing it. A target for private-school classroom connectivity appeared exactly once, in the fiscal year 2003 budget materials, and vanished thereafter. No performance measurement data for libraries or private schools appeared in any of the Commission's annual submissions. The GAO's funding report also documented a quieter implementation problem: committed funds frequently went unspent, as applicants struggled with the program's application complexity, competitive-bidding requirements, and documentation rules. The Commission and the Schools and Libraries Division took administrative steps to shrink the gap between commitments and disbursements, but the friction mattered for interpreting the results. The connectivity gains and the causal estimates describe a program that delivered despite a cumbersome delivery apparatus, not because the apparatus was elegant. The discounts worked through price, and price worked even through paperwork.

The headline connectivity results arrived through the National Center for Education Statistics survey series on internet access in public schools and classrooms, which tracked the period from 1994 through 2000. When NCES began measuring, 35 percent of public schools had internet access. By the fall of 2000, 98 percent did. The classroom-level measure moved even more dramatically: in 1994, 3 percent of instructional rooms had computers with internet access; by the fall of 2000, 77 percent did. The survey authors noted that the increase may have been aided by E-Rate allocations, and by February 28, 2001, the program had committed 5.8 billion dollars to applicants nationwide. The gains were not evenly distributed. In schools where at least 75 percent of students qualified for free or reduced-price lunch, 60 percent of instructional rooms were connected in 2000, compared with 77 to 82 percent in schools with lower poverty concentrations. In schools with the highest minority enrollment, 64 percent of instructional rooms had access, against 79 to 85 percent elsewhere. The trajectory mattered for interpretation: in the poorest schools, classroom connectivity had climbed from 38 percent in 1999 to 60 percent in 2000, the fastest one-year gain recorded for any school group that year. The poorest classrooms were catching up, though they had not caught up.

The trend continued past 2000. NCES follow-up surveys found that by the fall of 2002, 99 percent of public schools had internet access and 92 percent of instructional rooms were connected, and the school-level gaps that had marked the 1990s had essentially disappeared: there were virtually no differences in school internet access by school characteristics from 1999 onward. Classroom-level gaps narrowed more slowly, which is consistent with a program that funded school connections first and internal wiring under a binding cap. A 2006 case study by the Benton Foundation, examining four cities, found that E-Rate funding had a direct impact on classroom internet connectivity, corroborating the national survey pattern with local evidence. The financing behind all of this was controversial in its own right. The discounts were funded by contributions from telecommunications carriers, and a Congressional Research Service issue brief documented the political fallout when carriers began passing those contributions through to subscribers as itemized line charges on telephone bills, focusing public attention on the program's cost. The initiative had a prominent champion in Vice President Al Gore, who promoted classroom connectivity as a national goal. The controversy never stopped the disbursements, but it established the program's durable political pattern: measurable outputs, contested costs, and a constituency of connected schools.

Correlation, however, is not causation, and the strongest causal evidence comes from the 2006 study by Austan Goolsbee and Jonathan Guryan published in the Review of Economics and Statistics, based on data from every public school in California from 1996 through 2000 matched with E-Rate application records. The researchers exploited the formula-driven variation in subsidy rates, from 20 to 90 percent, to separate the discount's effect from the background trend of rising connectivity. Their finding on adoption was unambiguous: the subsidy significantly increased internet investment, with an implied first-dollar price elasticity of demand between negative 0.4 and negative 1.1. By the final year of the sample, California schools had roughly 68 percent more internet-connected classrooms per teacher than they would have had without the discounts, a difference equivalent to accelerating adoption by nearly four years. The response was strongest precisely where the need was greatest. Urban schools and schools with large Black and Hispanic student populations showed the greatest sensitivity to the subsidy, while rural and predominantly white and Asian schools responded less. This is the evidentiary core of the claim that the program narrowed the school technology gap: the measured causal effect was largest for disadvantaged students.

The researchers quantified the counterfactual in concrete terms. For the mean California school, the regression implied 14.7 internet-connected classrooms by the end of the sample period without any subsidy; the observed level with the subsidy was 24.4, some 66 percent higher, a difference equivalent to nearly four years of underlying trend growth arriving early. Those figures translate the elasticity estimates into physical classrooms, and they make the achievement results that followed harder to dismiss as a measurement artifact. The study did not find weak adoption with weak learning effects. It found strong adoption with no learning effects, which is the sharper and more consequential result.

The same study then measured what that investment bought in learning, and the answer requires honesty from everyone with a stake in the debate. Using Stanford Achievement Test results in mathematics, reading, and science across California schools, Goolsbee and Guryan found no significant effects of the E-Rate subsidy on student performance, allowing a full year for new connections to register in classroom practice and testing the result across grade levels and poverty tiers. The researchers themselves cautioned that few interventions move test scores in the short run, and Norris Dickard of the Benton Foundation argued at the time that it was too early to judge and that teacher training, not wiring alone, would determine the payoff. Education Week's 2002 reporting added that the null result held regardless of grade level or the poverty level of the school, and that Goolsbee himself described the achievement finding as weak, noting that few things influence test scores in the immediate term. But the null result has survived replication across settings and decades. Thomas Hazlett and coauthors, in a 2018 study of North Carolina public high schools from 2000 through 2013, found no gain in student test results associated with higher internet subsidy levels, and a 2017 correlational study by Violette found no association between E-Rate subsidies and graduation rates in Florida. The most extensive international evidence pointed the same way: a 2015 report from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, examining dozens of countries after accounting for social background and demographics, found no appreciable improvements in reading, mathematics, or science achievement in the countries that had invested most heavily in classroom information and communications technology, and reported that technology was of little help in narrowing the skills divide between advantaged and disadvantaged students. A 2006 Benton Foundation case study of four cities confirmed that E-Rate money moved classroom connectivity directly, which makes the achievement null more striking rather than less: the program did what it was designed to do on access, and access alone did not lift scores.

The federal oversight record adds a complication of its own. The GAO testified in 2005 that the Commission had never isolated the program's impact from other sources of school technology funding, meaning that even the impressive NCES trend lines cannot be credited to E-Rate alone with any precision. In a separate 2005 report, the GAO documented that the FCC had set connectivity goals inconsistently across beneficiary groups, dropping its library and private-school targets from successive budget submissions and performance reports, so that seven years into the program there was no systematic public accounting of what the discounts had achieved for two of the three populations they served. The honest summary, then, has two parts that partisans on both sides have resisted. The subsidy caused large, measurable increases in internet access in American classrooms, with the largest effects in poor and urban schools, which is a real policy achievement documented by the best available causal research. And it produced no detectable improvement in student achievement, documented by the same research and corroborated across states and countries. Either fact stated alone misleads. Stated together, they define the program's legacy with the precision the evidence allows. The program also endured well beyond the period studied here: a 2023 literature review estimated that annual E-Rate spending ran to roughly 2 to 3 billion dollars, which means the discount mechanism Congress created in 1996 was still financing school connectivity a quarter century after passage, long after the competition title it traveled with had been abandoned.

## The Honest Complication

The claim that the 1996 statute accomplished nothing is false, and the falsehood matters because it misattributes the genuine advances that did occur in American telecommunications after passage. Three developments are real: long-distance markets became more competitive, wireless telephony grew explosively, and school connectivity advanced dramatically. The analytical error lies in assigning all three to the statute's competition provisions. Each had a different cause, and an honest assessment assigns each outcome to the mechanism that actually produced it.

Long-distance competition did intensify after 1996, but Robert Crandall's Brookings Institution assessment, written roughly three years after passage, warned that very little of the progress could be traced to the Act. The long-distance market had been contested since the 1984 breakup of AT&T, when MCI and Sprint began taking share from the former monopoly, and prices were already falling on that trajectory before Congress acted. The carriers the statute expected to lead local entry, AT&T, MCI, and Sprint, were the same firms whose post-1984 rivalry had been disciplining long-distance prices for a decade. What the statute added to long distance was the Section 271 process allowing the regional Bell companies to enter the market after meeting a competitive checklist, yet the FCC approved no such applications in the early years and the final approvals stretched into 2003. Crandall's accounting found that competitive local exchange carriers, the new entrants the statute was built to create, had attracted fewer than one million local customers, while incumbent local carriers still served 99 percent of the country's roughly 180 million local lines. His 1998 assessment added that long-distance rates to consumers remained well above cost even as they fell, and that some Bell companies still faced little competition for in-state calls. Long-distance got more competitive; the competition came from a divestiture a dozen years old, not from the new law. The Section 271 entry process compounded the irony. Regulators delayed the Bell companies' long-distance approvals for four to seven years in an effort to perfect entry conditions for the new local carriers, yet by late 2000 and early 2001 those carriers were failing in large numbers, and the last approvals did not arrive until 2003. The delay was justified as protecting a competitive transition; the transition it protected was already collapsing.

Wireless growth belongs to a different mechanism entirely. The Cellular Telecommunications Industry Association reported that American wireless subscriptions reached 97 million by the end of June 2000, up 27.2 percent from 76.3 million a year earlier, with more than 37.8 percent of the population carrying active wireless service and digital subscribership nearly doubling year over year. The engine of that growth was spectrum policy, specifically the personal communications services auctions of the mid-1990s that created new national wireless competitors, combined with falling handset prices and the inherent appeal of mobile service. The 1996 Act's local-competition apparatus contributed essentially nothing to this expansion. The growth figures kept climbing through the turn of the century: first-half net new wireless subscribers peaked in 2000, rising 55.3 percent to 11 million, and the subscriber base reached 148 million by June 2003. By 2007, the FCC reported that 98 percent of Americans could choose from at least four wireless providers, a competitive outcome achieved without any contribution from the unbundling regime. By 2007 the base had more than quadrupled from 55 million subscribers at year-end 1997 to over 250 million, according to the CTIA's announcement that year, a growth curve that spans the Act's passage without any visible inflection attributable to its competition title. Howard Shelanski, writing in the Berkeley Technology Law Journal in 2023, made the broader point that the statute's durable contribution was not its unbundling regime but its quieter provisions: preemption of state monopoly franchises for local service, guaranteed access to rights-of-way, and interconnection duties that let new network technologies reach customers without incumbent blockage. Shelanski drew the mechanism point explicitly: growing substitution of wireless for wireline service made entry into local wireline markets less attractive for prospective competitors and made the long-distance prize less valuable for the Bell companies, diminishing both the importance of the Act's central objective and the effectiveness of its tools. The statute was fighting a war for the local loop while consumers were migrating to a different network altogether. The rate structure the Act inherited compounded the problem. Residential local rates sat below cost, subsidized by business lines and by the access charges levied on long-distance calls, which meant that the below-cost pricing regulators maintained in the name of universal service simultaneously frustrated the competitive entry the statute was meant to produce. The law's competition machinery was pushing against an economic gradient its own universal-service traditions had created.

School connectivity, the subject of the preceding finding, belongs to a third mechanism. The E-Rate discounts flowed from the universal service title of the statute, a subsidy program funded by carrier contributions, not from any provision designed to manufacture competition. Its success, such as it was, came from the oldest tool in the regulatory repertoire: lowering the price of a desired good for targeted buyers and letting demand respond, which Goolsbee and Guryan's elasticity estimates confirm it did. School connectivity deserves one clarifying sentence in this ledger: nothing about the E-Rate mechanism resembled competition policy. It was a transfer program, carrier contributions converted into discounts for favored buyers, and its effectiveness came from the price elasticity Goolsbee and Guryan measured, not from any rivalry the statute manufactured. The pattern across the three advances is therefore the opposite of what the statute's architects advertised. The elaborate machinery for engineering competition through unbundled network elements and resale produced almost nothing durable. The blunt instruments, an old subsidy mechanism and pre-1996 market structures, produced nearly everything. The statute's record looks better than the caricature of total failure, and the credit belongs to the provisions that required the least regulatory heroism to work.

## Reassembly, Not Fragmentation

The competition title of the statute did not merely underperform. It was litigated into irrelevance while the deregulatory title took effect immediately, and the industry that emerged was more concentrated than the one Congress inherited. The unbundling regime, the centerpiece of the local-competition design, required the FCC to write pricing rules, defend them through successive court challenges, and enforce them against incumbents with every incentive to delay. In 2004 the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit vacated the Commission's unbundling rules in the USTA decision, and the government declined to seek Supreme Court review, leaving the vacatur as the final word. Cost-based leasing of unbundled network elements was quickly phased out. The platform for leasing incumbents' network elements at regulated rates, the vehicle on which the entire competitive-entry strategy depended, effectively died in that year. Its collapse had a human-scale marker: in July 2004, AT&T, the largest of the would-be entrants, stopped marketing local and long-distance service to residential customers, driven out of the local market by the vertical price squeeze that the Bell companies' long-distance entry had enabled. The statute's vision of local competition through leased facilities ended not with a legislative repeal but with the dominant entrant's surrender.

Meanwhile the provisions that removed limits operated without friction. The Act freed the regional Bell companies to consolidate and to enter long-distance and other lines of business, and those freedoms executed themselves. The seven regional companies created by the 1984 divestiture merged steadily toward three national carriers, and in 2005 the reassembly reached its symbolic completion when SBC acquired AT&T and Verizon acquired MCI. The long-distance competitors the statute was supposed to discipline local monopolies ended up absorbed by the local monopolies the statute was supposed to discipline. Crandall's early warning that the proposed mergers of the remaining Bell companies should be denied went unheeded, and each combination survived review by the FCC, the Justice Department, and state commissions. Nothing in the competition title survived contact with the courts; nothing in the deregulatory title required contact with the courts at all.

This asymmetry is the strongest claim the evidence supports. Provisions that require sustained regulatory enforcement, elaborate rules, continuous monitoring, and successful defense against well-funded litigation are structurally weaker than provisions that simply remove a legal limit. The first kind of provision must win the same fight every year in every forum. The second kind must win once. When Congress writes a statute containing both kinds, the deregulatory provisions will tend to define the long-run outcome while the regulatory provisions decay under legal and political attrition. The telecommunications market of the mid-2000s, dominated by a handful of integrated carriers offering bundled local, long-distance, and wireless service, was not the fragmented competitive landscape the statute promised. It was a reassembled version of the old order, built with tools the statute itself supplied.

## Closing the Account

Measured against its own stated aims, the 1996 statute presents a ledger with entries on both sides and a net balance its drafters would not recognize. It promised competition in local telephone markets and delivered, after a decade of rulemaking and litigation, a market in which incumbents retained the overwhelming share of wireline customers while the competitive entrants it created either failed or were acquired. It promised lower prices through rivalry and delivered price declines that owed more to pre-existing competition and technological substitution than to its own mechanisms. It promised broader deployment of advanced services to schools and libraries and delivered a discount program that measurably connected millions of classrooms, disproportionately in poor districts, even as it failed to move student achievement. It promised a framework suited to the communications future and instead watched wireless and internet-based services, forces its text barely anticipated, redefine the market around its categories. It directed the Commission to encourage the deployment of advanced telecommunications capability to all Americans, and broadband did arrive at scale, but substantially through cable-modem and digital-subscriber-line competition whose dynamics the statute's drafters had not modeled. One further provision deserves acknowledgment in passing: the statute also carried the internet liability shield whose cultural and economic consequences dwarfed everything in the competition title, a subject owned by [Section 230 explained](/2017/07/15/section-230-explained/), a sibling article in this series, and mentioned here only to keep the ledger complete.

The forward-looking lesson concerns legislative craft rather than telecommunications. Future Congresses will again face the temptation to engineer competitive markets through intricate regulatory schemes that depend on agencies holding a line against concentrated private interests for years on end. The record assembled here suggests a disciplined skepticism toward that approach. Subsidy programs with clear delivery channels and observable outputs, like the school discounts, can be evaluated and judged; their successes and failures leave measurable traces. The GAO's complaint that the FCC never isolated the E-Rate effect from other funding is itself a mark in the program's favor as an object of study: the question was answerable in principle, and independent researchers answered it. Market-engineering regimes whose success depends on perpetual enforcement tend to leave a different trace: a long paper record of rules written, rules vacated, and markets that drift back toward concentration while the proceedings continue. The distinction is not between regulation and deregulation in the abstract. It is between provisions that work when the government acts once and provisions that work only if the government acts forever. The reassembly the statute produced was not an accident of implementation; it was the predictable product of limit-removing provisions surviving while enforcement-dependent provisions did not. The 1996 Act contained both, and the difference in their fates is the most instructive thing about it.

The evaluations that made this article possible share a feature worth naming in closing. The E-Rate research succeeded because the program's output was countable: connected classrooms, discount rates, dollars committed. The unbundling regime resisted evaluation for the opposite reason: its success depended on a counterfactual no dataset could supply, namely how much competition would have emerged under rules that never survived intact long enough to be tested. A legislator choosing between these design patterns in the future should prefer the countable one. Policies whose effects can be measured can be corrected; policies whose effects exist only in the space between a rule and its litigation cannot even be judged, let alone fixed.

The evidence in this article rests on named evaluations with stated periods, from the GAO's funding reports for 1998 through 2000 to the NCES connectivity surveys of 1994 through 2000, from Goolsbee and Guryan's California study of 1996 through 2000 to the North Carolina and international achievement research that followed. Readers who wish to keep their own running record of sources and findings across the series may find the [legislation study notebook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) a useful companion. The statute's ambitions were large, its mechanisms were mixed, and its outcomes were decided less by what Congress intended than by which of its provisions could survive contact with the institutions charged with carrying them out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Did the Telecommunications Act increase competition?

It increased competition in one sector and failed to produce it in the one it cared about most. Radio, where Congress simply erased ownership caps, consolidated into a few national chains within five years, which was concentration rather than competition. Local telephone service, the centerpiece of the design, saw a genuine entrant boom: competitive carriers grew from a 4.3 percent line share in late 1999 to 18.5 percent by late 2004. But the legal foundation of that entry, mandatory unbundling at regulated wholesale rates, was vacated in stages by the courts between 1999 and 2004, and the largest entrants failed or were absorbed. Long distance became more competitive, though on a trend the 1984 breakup had started. The honest answer is that the statute deregulated effectively and engineered competition poorly.

### Q: What happened to radio station ownership after the Telecommunications Act?

Section 202 erased the national ownership ceiling, and consolidation followed at unprecedented speed. Clear Channel grew from 43 stations in 1995 to more than 1,200 by the early 2000s, spending roughly 30 billion dollars assembling the portfolio. The total number of station owners fell from about 5,100 in 1996 to about 3,800 in 2001, a drop of roughly one quarter, while licensed signals kept increasing. The two largest group owners came to control more than 44 percent of national radio revenue, and the leading firm in each local market held an average of 46 percent of radio advertising revenue. Because the ownership change was self-executing, requiring no rulemaking or litigation, it took effect immediately. Radio is therefore the cleanest demonstration of what the statute accomplished when its text operated directly on a market.

### Q: Why did the CLEC industry collapse after the Telecommunications Act?

The competitive local exchange carriers built their business plans on regulated access to incumbent networks at TELRIC wholesale rates, especially the UNE-P combination of loop, switching, and transport at 40 to 60 percent discounts. The courts then dismantled that foundation in stages: the Supreme Court vacated the unbundling list in 1999, the D.C. Circuit vacated the remand rules and line sharing in 2002, and it vacated the Triennial Review Order's mass-market unbundling in 2004 before the FCC ended mass-market switching in 2005. Capital committed under one price list was repriced under another, and equity markets closed to telecom issuers in 2000 and 2001. Bankruptcies followed, from NorthPoint and Rhythms in 2001 to XO and Allegiance in 2002 and 2003. Notably, the lines survived the firms: competitive line counts rose every reporting period through 2004 even as the companies serving them failed.

### Q: Did the Telecommunications Act lower cable prices?

No. Cable prices rose substantially. FCC data showed the average monthly rate for combined basic and expanded basic tiers climbing from 26.06 dollars in 1997 to 36.47 dollars in 2002, a 40 percent increase against roughly 12 percent for the Consumer Price Index, and the expanded basic tier rose from 22.35 dollars in July 1995 to 43.04 dollars in January 2005. The statute sunset federal rate regulation of the cable programming services tier after March 31, 1999, deregulating exactly the tier most subscribers bought. Rising programming costs, especially sports licensing fees, and network rebuilds for digital video and broadband explained part of the increase, but market structure mattered most: only about 2 percent of markets had a wired competitor, and where one existed the incumbent's rates ran about 15 percent lower. With no rival to discipline pricing, operators funded investment through rate increases that outpaced inflation.

### Q: Did the Baby Bells recombine after the Telecommunications Act?

Yes. The 1996 statute lifted the Modified Final Judgment's restrictions on combinations among the seven Regional Bell Operating Companies, and the market used the opening. SBC acquired Pacific Telesis in 1997, Southern New England Telephone in 1998, and Ameritech in 1999, then bought its former parent AT&T Corp. in 2005 and adopted the AT&T name, adding BellSouth in 2006. Bell Atlantic merged with NYNEX in 1997 and acquired GTE in 2000 to become Verizon. Qwest bought US West in 2000, and CenturyLink acquired Qwest in 2011. Within a decade and a half, the seven companies of the 1984 divestiture had been reassembled into essentially three wireline groups. The statute meant to fragment local telephone markets presided over the reassembly of the empire dismantled in 1984, because its limit-removing provisions executed themselves while its competition provisions died in court.

### Q: Did E-Rate work under the Telecommunications Act?

On its own terms, yes, with a major caveat. The Schools and Libraries Program, created by the FCC in 1997 under Section 254, offered schools and libraries discounts of 20 to 90 percent on telecommunications, internet access, and internal connections. By fall 2000, 98 percent of public schools had internet access, up from 35 percent in 1994, and 77 percent of instructional rooms were connected, up from 3 percent. The best causal study, by Goolsbee and Guryan using California data, found the subsidies caused roughly 68 percent more connected classrooms per teacher than the trend alone would have produced, with the largest effects in poor and urban schools. The caveat is learning: the same study found no significant effect on student test scores, a null result replicated in North Carolina and international research. The program connected classrooms without moving achievement.

### Q: Why did the universal service fund keep growing after the Telecommunications Act?

The statute expanded the fund's obligations, especially the schools and libraries program capped at 2.25 billion dollars annually, while its revenue base was tied to interstate and international end-user telecommunications revenues, a category the market was eroding. Disbursements grew from about 4.4 billion dollars in 2000 to about 6.5 billion in 2005, while the assessable revenue base shrank from about 79.0 billion dollars to about 74.7 billion, as falling toll rates and declining wireline access lines hollowed out interstate revenues. The contribution factor, set quarterly as projected program demand divided by projected contribution revenue, therefore ratcheted upward, from 5.9 percent in early 2000 to 11.1 percent in the second quarter of 2005. Wireless carriers contributed only through a safe harbor percentage, and interconnected voice over internet protocol providers were not required to contribute until 2006, so the fastest growing revenue streams sat largely outside the base while obligations grew against the shrinking interstate wireline total.

### Q: Did the Telecommunications Act help broadband deployment?

Only indirectly. The statute directed the Commission to encourage deployment of advanced telecommunications capability, and broadband did arrive at scale, but substantially through cable-modem and digital-subscriber-line competition whose dynamics the drafters had not modeled. The competition title was written for a wireline local telephone market; the market that emerged centered on cable systems selling broadband and wireless carriers selling mobile service, forces the statute's text barely anticipated. The line-sharing unbundling that briefly supported independent DSL providers was vacated by the courts in 2002. The statute's durable contributions were its quieter provisions: preemption of state monopoly franchises for local service, guaranteed access to rights-of-way, and interconnection duties that let new network technologies reach customers without incumbent blockage. Broadband deployment happened, but the mechanisms that delivered it were largely adjacent to the competition machinery Congress advertised.

### Q: What was the unbundled network element platform and why did it matter?

The unbundled network element platform, UNE-P, let a competitive carrier buy a combination of the incumbent's local loop, local switching, and shared transport at regulated TELRIC wholesale rates and resell end-to-end local service under its own brand without owning switches. Discounts ran 40 to 60 percent below retail, deeper than the roughly 20 percent available through simple resale, which made the platform the entrants' preferred vehicle: the share of competitive lines served through unbundled elements rose from 23.9 percent in December 1999 to 55.4 percent in December 2002. AT&T and WorldCom were its largest users. The platform mattered because the entire entry strategy depended on it, and its legal foundation was the unbundling of mass-market switching. When the FCC ended that obligation in 2005, after the courts had vacated three earlier versions of the rules, the platform ceased to exist as a regulatory product and the arbitrage model built on it collapsed with it.

### Q: Which court decisions undid the local telephone competition rules?

Four decisions dismantled the regime in stages. In AT&T Corp. v. Iowa Utilities Board (1999), the Supreme Court upheld FCC jurisdiction over pricing methodology but vacated the unbundling list, Rule 319, for failing to apply the statutory necessity and impairment tests. In United States Telecom Association v. FCC (2002), the D.C. Circuit vacated the remand unbundling rules and the line-sharing order, finding unbundling required where no impairment existed. In USTA II (2004), the same court vacated the Triennial Review Order's mass-market switching and dedicated transport unbundling and struck down the delegation of impairment decisions to state commissions, and the government declined Supreme Court review. The FCC's Triennial Review Remand Order (2005) then ended mass-market switching unbundling itself. Each vacatur retroactively repriced investments made in reliance on the order it struck, which is why the litigation chronology, not any single ruling, explains the entrant collapse.

### Q: What did the Section 271 checklist require of the Bell companies?

Section 271 barred each Bell operating company from providing in-region interLATA long distance service until it satisfied a fourteen-point competitive checklist and won FCC approval. The checklist required the company to demonstrate that its local market was open to competition: furnishing interconnection, unbundled network elements, and resale to competitors, plus offering number portability, dialing parity, access to poles and rights-of-way, and nondiscriminatory access to directory assistance and operator services, among other items. Opening the local network thus became the price of admission to the long distance market the Bell companies most wanted. Bell Atlantic won the first approval, in New York, in December 1999, and the Commission had approved applications in 41 states and the District of Columbia by May 2003. The process was meant to discipline local entry, but the approvals stretched across the very years the entrants were collapsing, and the long distance prize itself was shrinking as toll rates fell.

### Q: How did the AT&T breakup relate to the later statute?

The 1984 divestiture, under the 1982 Modification of Final Judgment, split AT&T's local operations into seven Regional Bell Operating Companies, barred them from long distance, equipment manufacturing, and information services, and effectively prohibited combinations among them. The 1996 statute was meant to finish the work by forcing competition into local service. Instead it dismantled the decree's architecture: Section 271 created a path for Bell company entry into long distance, the manufacturing ban ended, and the line-of-business restrictions were superseded, which made combinations among the regional companies legally possible for the first time. The market acted on the second opening far more than the first. Within a decade, SBC had absorbed four of the seven regionals plus AT&T Corp., Bell Atlantic had merged with NYNEX and GTE into Verizon, and Qwest had taken US West. A statute sold as completing the breakup presided over its reversal.

### Q: Why did long distance prices fall if the statute did not cause it?

Long distance rates fell on a trajectory the 1984 divestiture and the FCC's access charge rebalancing had established long before 1996. Average revenue per minute dropped from 32 cents in 1984 to 18 cents in 1994, and to 7 cents by 2006, a decline of more than 85 percent after inflation adjustment. The mechanism was regulatory: per-minute access charges paid by long distance carriers to local companies fell from 17.3 cents in 1984 to 1.4 cents in 2004, while the flat residential subscriber line charge rose from zero to 5.96 dollars per month. Competition among AT&T, MCI, and Sprint, unleashed by the breakup rather than the 1996 act, drove retail prices down on top of the access reform. The statute's Section 271 Bell company entry added a late increment, with the first approval in December 1999, but by then the interstate average was already in single digits. The decline was a transfer engineered over two decades, from per-minute toll pricing to flat local pricing, not a product of the 1996 law.

### Q: What happened to the competitive carriers that went bankrupt?

Their capital was destroyed while their customers and physical plant survived. NorthPoint filed in January 2001 after Verizon withdrew a planned investment, and its network shut down that March; Rhythms and Covad followed in August 2001; Winstar, XO, McLeodUSA, Williams, Focal, and Allegiance filed through 2002 and 2003. The Heritage Foundation counted 300 carriers worth 86 billion dollars in 1999 against 80 to 100 survivors worth about 4 billion after the crash. Assets changed hands at distress prices: WorldCom took Rhythms' assets, AT&T took NorthPoint's, and the reorganized XO bought Allegiance. Tens of thousands of employees lost jobs, and bondholders absorbed the losses. Yet FCC data showed competitive lines rising in every reporting period, from 19.7 million in December 2001 to 32.9 million by December 2004. The bust destroyed the corporate shells and the capital structure, not the competitive segment: customers kept buying from someone, and the survivors bought the networks at cents on the dollar.

### Q: How did wireless growth affect the statute's competition design?

Wireless growth was largely independent of the statute and steadily undermined its premises. CTIA recorded 44 million wireless connections in 1996 and nearly 208 million by year-end 2005, with penetration near 70 percent; by 2007 the base exceeded 250 million. The engine was spectrum policy, the mid-1990s personal communications service auctions, plus falling handset prices, not the act's unbundling apparatus, which contributed essentially nothing. As Howard Shelanski later argued, growing wireless substitution made entry into local wireline markets less attractive for prospective competitors and made the long distance prize less valuable for the Bell companies, diminishing both the importance of the act's central objective and the effectiveness of its tools. Competition did arrive, but through a technology the 1996 framework had treated as an adjunct. The statute was fighting a war for the local copper loop while consumers were migrating to a different network altogether.

### Q: What was TELRIC pricing and why was it controversial?

TELRIC, total element long-run incremental cost, was the FCC's pricing methodology for interconnection and unbundled network elements under the 1996 order. Wholesale rates were to recover the forward-looking costs directly attributable to each element, plus a reasonable allocation of forward-looking common costs, with efficiency judged against the most advanced technology available, untied to the incumbent's historical investment. The Supreme Court upheld the methodology in Verizon v. FCC (2002). Entrants argued it was the only way to make entry viable, since duplicating the last mile was prohibitively expensive, and the FCC said the discounts were a temporary ladder to facilities-based competition. Incumbents argued the rates forced them to share networks at prices no builder would voluntarily accept, subsidizing rivals who would then never build. The controversy was never resolved on the merits: the courts vacated the unbundling rules the prices applied to, so TELRIC survived legally while the regime it priced died around it.

### Q: Did the statute change broadcast television ownership limits?

Less dramatically than in radio. Section 202 addressed television as well: a contemporaneous compilation shows the pre-1996 television ceiling at twelve stations and a quarter of the national audience, and the statute raised the national audience reach cap while directing the FCC to revisit the rules. But television consolidation moved more slowly than radio's, partly because the audience-reach cap remained a binding constraint and partly because television stations were far more expensive per outlet, so the buyout arithmetic that drove radio's thousand-station portfolios did not transfer directly. The radio title, which erased the national ceiling entirely and immediately, remains the statute's clearest ownership experiment. Television's experience confirmed the article's asymmetry in a quieter register: where Congress removed a limit outright, markets moved fast; where it delegated the details to the Commission, change waited on rulemaking and review.

### Q: What role did state utility commissions play under the statute?

Congress divided implementation labor: the FCC set national rules and the pricing methodology, while state utility commissions arbitrated interconnection agreements and set the actual dollar prices under the federal standard. Section 251 expressly gave state commissions authority over interconnection arbitration, which meant fifty separate forums could shape outcomes before any federal court weighed in. The Triennial Review Order of 2003 went further, delegating impairment determinations for mass-market switching to the states and giving them nine months to decide market by market whether competitors were impaired without access. That delegation did not survive: in USTA II (2004) the D.C. Circuit held the subdelegation unlawful and vacated the order's unbundling provisions. The state role thus illustrates the article's verdict in miniature: a design that multiplied the forums for contesting the rules also multiplied the litigation, and the competition the rules were meant to create never outran the proceedings.

### Q: Why did the FCC suppress its own radio ownership study?

The Commission's draft Review of the Radio Industry, covering 1996 through March 2003, documented the consolidation's scale and its costs: station counts up nearly six percent while owner counts fell 35 percent, the leading firm in each local market controlling an average of 46 percent of radio advertising revenue, and the top two firms 76 percent. The study concluded that this concentration was at least partly responsible for an 87 percent increase in advertising rates even as audience ratings fell. After its conclusions circulated, the Commission ordered copies destroyed, according to the account later surfaced by Radio Survivor. The suppression deepened the controversy rather than ending it: critics treated the destroyed draft as evidence that the agency's own research contradicted its deregulatory posture. The episode underscores the finding's evidentiary strength. The ownership trend needed no exaggeration, and the agency's discomfort with its own numbers confirmed what the numbers said.

### Q: What lesson does the statute hold for future market design legislation?

The article's closing lesson concerns legislative craft: prefer provisions that work when the government acts once over provisions that work only if the government acts forever. The ownership deregulation, the E-Rate subsidy, and the quiet preemptions of state monopoly franchises took effect and endured because they required no sustained enforcement. The unbundling regime required the FCC to write pricing rules, defend them through successive court challenges, and enforce them against incumbents with every incentive to delay, and it died under that burden. A second lesson is evaluability: subsidy programs with countable outputs, like connected classrooms, can be measured and corrected, while market-engineering regimes depend on a counterfactual no dataset can supply. Future Congresses tempted to engineer competition through intricate regulatory schemes should weigh the record of a decade of rules written, rules vacated, and markets drifting back toward concentration while the proceedings continued.
