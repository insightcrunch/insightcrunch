## Cold Open

![Nightjar: The Inert House on the Dravin Border Station - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-23.webp)

The lower floor of the Dravin border station is awake before the rest of the building is, and Lia Memmet, who has walked it at the change of shift for longer than most of the guards posted above her have held a station warrant, knows its waking by sound before she knows it by any other sign. First there is the water, moving in the culvert beneath the pump hall floor in a low, even rush that she feels through the soles of her boots as much as she hears it, and then there are the pumps themselves, three of them in a row along the northern wall, iron housings bolted to stone beds, each with its own voice in the chorus the hall makes when the night water is being lifted and sent on toward the border channel. The first pump speaks low and slow. The second answers a fraction higher. The third, the oldest, carries a roughness at the top of its stroke that the station engineer has logged, and forgiven, and logged again, through a whole session of requests for a replacement part that has not yet come down the ridge road. Together they make a pitch that a person who works beside them stops hearing as separate notes and starts hearing as the condition of a normal morning, in the way that a person stops hearing a clock in a room where she has slept for years and hears it only on the morning when it has stopped.

On this morning, at the hour when the night detail hands over to the day detail and the lamps in the lower floor are still burning against a daylight that has not yet reached the culvert grilles, Memmet hears it wrong.

She comes down the stone stair from the guard room with the shift book under her left arm and the lower floor keys on their iron ring in her right hand, and she stops, as she always stops, on the third step from the bottom, where the air changes. Above that step the air belongs to the station house, to stove heat and boot leather and the tea the night corporal makes too strong and leaves standing. Below it the air belongs to the water, cold and mineral, with the smell of wet stone and pump oil and the faint green smell that comes up from the culvert when the border channel is running high after rain in the hills that no one on the station staff calls by any name but the hills. She stands on the step and breathes the change, because standing there is how she has always given her ears time to settle, and because a guard who hurries the first minute of a watch will spend the rest of it catching up with things she should have noticed at her leisure.

The pumps are running. The water is moving. The hall below her, when she looks down into it through the stair opening, shows its ordinary arrangement in the lamplight, the three housings in their row, the gauges on their brass stems above each housing, the oiled walkway between the housings and the southern wall, the tools hung on their board in the order the engineer insists upon and the day staff rearranges and the engineer restores, each evening, without comment. And the pitch is wrong. Not loud, not ragged, not the grinding wrongness that sends a guard running for the engineer with her hand already on the alarm cord. It is higher, by a small and steady interval, across all three pumps together, as if the whole chorus had taken a breath and chosen, for reasons of its own, to sing the morning in a slightly different key. The roughness at the top of the third pump stroke is still there. The low voice of the first pump is still low, beside the others. Everything is itself, and everything is lifted, together, by the same small amount.

It is the cold, she decides. The night has been the coldest of the session so far, by the thermometer nailed beside the guard room door, whose reading she entered in the book at midnight with her own hand. Cold thickens oil. Cold contracts iron. Cold makes the water itself denser on the lift, or so the engineer has told the guards more than once when they have reported a morning note that differed from an evening one, and on those mornings the pitch has always settled as the hall warmed with work and bodies and the lamps burning down toward their reservoirs. She can picture the engineer saying it again, without looking up from his bench, in the tone he uses for facts that have been true longer than any guard presently serving. Cold, Memmet. Log it and look again in an hour. And she will log it, and she will look again in an hour, because that is the shape of a proper watch, and because she is not a woman who raises a station on a note her ears may have misjudged in the first minute of the morning.

All the same, she writes it down.

The service gate stands at the eastern end of the pump hall, where the hall narrows toward the outer bay, and it is not a door in any ordinary sense. It is a slab of riveted iron as tall as a tall man and half as wide again, set into a stone frame that divides the pump hall from the bay beyond, and behind it, on the bay side, the border channel presses its water against the iron through every hour of the day and night, held back by the gate and by the culvert works and by the whole weight of the station engineering. A counterweight on a chain once made the gate light to move, so that one guard could draw it and hold it and pass another through in a matter of moments, but the chain is old, its links stretched and its anchor plate working loose in its stone, and the renewal has been requested, and approved, and delayed, in the manner of works that everyone agrees are necessary and no one with a budget has yet called urgent. Until the renewal comes, the rule of the gate is written on a board beside it in the engineer hand, and every guard learns it before she is allowed the lower floor keys. The gate must be held on its wheel while anyone passes. A guard sets her weight to the great iron wheel on the hall side of the frame, the holding wheel, with its four spokes worn bright where hands have taken them, and she holds the gate against the pressure of the water for as long as the passage takes, and no one passes alone, and no one leaves the wheel to swing free with a person in the opening, because water that has been waiting does not wait politely when it is given a gap. Memmet has held that wheel with her shoulder set and her boots braced on the stone sill and her arms learning, again, the difference between holding a thing and merely keeping a hand on it. She respects the gate in the uncomplicated way she respects deep water and loaded carts and heights. It is the heaviest fact on the lower floor, and the station routine bends around it, morning and evening, without resentment.

Hallis waits while she reads his night entries, and signs below her entry, and takes his detail up the stair toward tea and sleep. Their boots fade. The day detail will come down in its turn, the engineer first, then the bay guards, then the clerk with the crossing papers for whatever traffic the morning brings down the ridge road to the station gate above. For this interval, which belongs to no detail fully, the lower floor is Memmet alone, and she does her round in the order she was taught by a sergeant whose name she still writes, from memory, in the back of her own notebook at the start of each session, though the order itself has long since passed into her feet.

At the eastern end she stops before the service gate and performs the check the board requires, though no passage is planned and no one waits on the bay side to come through. She sets her right hand, briefly, to the holding wheel and feels the gate through it, the way a guard feels a horse through a rein, the steady, patient pressure of the channel water transmitted through iron and stone into the wheel and into the bones of her hand. The wheel is still. The pin is in its socket. The chain hangs in its worn curve from the counterweight, slack in the manner the engineer has taught them to expect and to report if the manner changes. The gate is fast. She enters it in her mind for the log she will complete at the end of the round, and she notes, because noting is her trade, that the pitch of the pumps behind her sounds, if anything, a fraction higher here at the eastern end of the hall than it did from the stair, nearer to the third housing, nearer to the wall where the culvert turns. Or the hall is playing the trick that long rooms play with sound, gathering it at the ends. Or the cold is deeper here, where the channel presses its own cold against the gate and through the stone. She decides, again, that it is the cold, and the decision is a reasonable one, and she makes it in good faith, and the making of it is the last ordinary act of her morning.

She completes the round on the northern side, passing behind the housings where the walkway narrows and the heat of the machines makes a thin warm layer in the cold air at the height of her face. The bolt marks on the anchor plates align. In its recess beside the third housing stands the quench tank, broad and low and open, holding the cold brine the station keeps for pump seal work, its surface still and dark in the lamplight. It is ordinary equipment, as ordinary as the tool board, and she confirms that it is as it was, and moves on.

Then she returns to the desk, and completes the log, and settles to the watch.

By the second round the hall has warmed, in the small way a stone hall warms, with the lamps and the machines and her own passage through it. The tea the day corporal brings down to her at the stair foot is hot enough to steam in the culvert air. The engineer has come and gone above, calling down that he will take his readings at midday and that the cold will have the oil thick until then, which is as near as he ever comes to agreeing with a guard observation before he has made it his own. And the pitch of the pumps, when Memmet stands again on the third step from the bottom of the stair and gives her ears the same settling minute she gave them at handover, has not settled. It stands where it stood, higher than its ordinary note by the same small, steady interval, patient as the water behind the gate. She listens for a long time, longer than the round requires, with the shift book under her arm and the keys still in her hand, and then she goes to the desk and opens the log and writes, under her first entry, in the same plain hand, that the note had not settled with warming, and that she was reporting it to the day officer at the next relief, and she underlines nothing, because underlining is for officers, and she is content that the two entries, read together, say exactly what a guard can say and no more than a guard can say, which is that something in the lower floor of her station has changed its voice, and that she heard it, and that she wrote it down twice.

## Tasking

Arvo Leyden sent for Mira by the internal telephone, which in the Carrowmere offices meant that the message arrived without a voice attached to it, a clerk appearing at the door of the desk room with a folded slip and the air of a man who had been told that the slip mattered more than the errand he had been taken from. Mira read it standing, set the file she had been working on back into its tray with its marker at the page she had reached, and entered her leaving in the desk book in her own hand, because the Ninth Office kept its courtesies with itself even when it dispensed with them for everyone else. Her left hand, where two fingers still carried the burn she had taken in Valtessa, closed over the edge of the desk as she wrote, and the stiffness of the new skin pulled in the small, familiar protest that had become part of the arithmetic of her days, a cost she counted without resenting, in the way a person counts the steps of a stair she climbs every morning. The burn had closed. It had not softened. Fine work cost her a pause, firm work cost her a decision, and both costs were entered, now, in a private ledger that no one had tasked her to keep and that she kept all the same.

She had slept badly. That was not, in itself, a reportable fact, since half the Office slept badly in any given week and the other half lied about it in the returns, but the particular shape of this bad sleep belonged to the case before the case, and she carried it with her up the stairs to Leyden room as honestly as she carried the file under her right arm. In Melora, on the day before the day before this one, she had taken a second heavy read in succession, in a reading room where an absence had been retreating by measured stages toward a door, and the read had put her down for six hours in front of a junior officer, a fact that stood in the record without decoration and that she did not propose to decorate now. Six hours out of the field. Six hours in which other people had guarded a room and written a record and made the decisions that a Weigher is supposed to be present to make, while she lay in the annex quiet room with the cold of the reading working its way out of her bones at its own unhurried pace. The sleep since then had been thin and broken at its edges, and her state entering this tasking, if Leyden asked for it in the form the Office used, was reduced, and she intended to say so in exactly that word, because a handler who is told reduced can plan around it, and a handler who is told fine will plan through it, and the second kind of planning gets people hurt in rooms that neither of them has seen yet.

Leyden gave her the reason plainly. A border guard at a Dravin station had logged a change in the note of her pumps, twice, at handover and again after the hall had warmed, and the station engineer, after a day of dismissing the entries, had taken his own readings and conceded that the pumps were working against a resistance he could not locate.

He gave her the station plan first, and let her hold it in her mind before he gave her anything that might change the shape of it. The station stood where the ridge road came down out of the hills and met the border channel, a place of yards and offices and bonded stores above, and, below, a lower floor that served the water works of the crossing, a pump hall running long from west to east, an outer bay beyond a heavy service gate at the eastern end, a culvert beneath the floor and a channel beyond the bay, pressing its water against the gate through every hour. The plan marked the pumps in their row, the gauges, the desk, the quench tank in its recess, the stair at the western end, and the service gate at the eastern, drawn with the double line the station draughtsmen used for a barrier that was also a machine. Beside the gate, in the small, exact hand of the engineer who had annotated the plan for the liaison, stood the note that Leyden now read aloud without emphasis, because he knew she would hear the weight of it without help. Counterweight chain due for renewal. Gate to be held on its wheel against the water while anyone passes. No passage alone. Mira looked at the drawing of the wheel, four spokes and a hub, on the hall side of the gate frame, and thought of all the work in the world that came down, in the end, to one person holding a thing that wanted to move while another person went through the gap it made, and she marked the gate, as she marked everything Leyden gave her in a tasking, in the order in which she would need it if the case went badly, which was before she marked it in the order in which she would need it if the case went well.

Then he gave her the custody notice, and with it, the cost of the case, named up front in the manner she preferred and in the manner the Office, at its best, observed as a matter of professional honour.

The only expert available, Leyden said, was Oskar Venn.

He let the name sit. Venn had been a licensed Binder for thirty years, licensed in the full form of the licence, examined, bonded, entered on the rolls of two jurisdictions, a man whose work, for most of those thirty years, had been the ordinary, necessary, unglamorous work of his trade, seals on bonded stores, wards on strong rooms, contracts bound to hold in the physical sense that the law of the Accord recognised and enforced. He had been convicted of unlicensed work, work done outside the terms of the licence that had made him trusted, for a client whose name stood sealed in the custody papers and whose identity Leyden either did not know or did not propose to share at a tasking, and the distinction, Mira understood, was one she was not invited to pursue. Venn was held by the Accord under custody terms, in a place whose location was given to her on a separate slip that she read and returned, and he was, by every account the Office possessed, a man who knew more about the seating and path of a binding than anyone else the Office could reach inside a week. He was also a convicted practitioner whose freedom, such as it was, depended on the continued goodwill of the people who would be asking him questions, and whose every answer would therefore arrive with an interest attached to it, like a hook inside a bait.

Everything he says, Leyden said, must be verified twice. Once against the world, and once against his interest in saying it. If he tells you the hall is cold, you will check the hall, and then you will ask yourself what a man in custody gains by having the Ninth Office believe that a hall is cold. That is the cost of this case, Mira. Not the journey, not the station, not whatever may or may not be seated against a pump housing in Dravin. The cost is that your expert is your problem. You will need what he knows. You will not be able to trust the needing, or the knowing, or the man, in any of the ordinary proportions, and you will have to do the work of the case with that arithmetic running underneath every interview, every document, every conclusion you draw in his hearing or out of it.

Because the tasking required it, and because Leyden believed, as Mira did, that a capability described in a room in Carrowmere was worth very little beside a capability shown, the three of them went down to the Office yard before the papers were signed, where a test seal had been prepared on the yard strong room door by the duty Binder at first light, a modest, well made seal of the kind used on bonded stores, glowing, to a Weigher eye, with the enormous, settled weight that a binding in place always carried, a weight Mira could feel from the yard gate as a pressure at the back of her attention, steady and unmistakable. Rask stood in front of the door with his coat off and his sleeves pushed up, in the unshowy stance of a man about to lift something whose weight he already knew, and he stilled the radius. There was no gesture that a clerk could have entered in a record. There was a change in the air of the yard, as if a note that had been sounding below the threshold of hearing had been covered by a hand, and the weight of the seal, to Mira read, went blunt and muffled and far away, not gone, not broken, but suppressed, held under, in the manner of a light seen through deep water. The strong room door, under the stilling, was only a door. She walked the radius at Rask direction, counting her paces, and found its edge where he said she would find it, a matter of a few yards in every direction from where he stood, no further, because a stilling is not a wall and it is not a province, it is a man holding his breath on behalf of everyone inside a small circle, and the circle is as small as the man is honest about his own strength. Inside it, her own read was as blunted as the seal. Inside it, any practice she might have attempted would have come to her hands muffled and unreliable. A Stiller suppresses his own side too, Leyden said quietly, for the record that no one was writing, as if the point were the whole of the demonstration, which, Mira understood, it was. Rask held the stilling while she completed the circuit, and held it while she stepped back inside the radius and out again, and when he let it go at last, the weight of the seal came back into the yard like a tide returning to a rock pool, and Rask sat down, without ceremony, on the yard mounting block, and put his head back against the wall, and was, for a measurable time, good for nothing at all. His breathing was even. His hands were steady. He was simply spent, in the complete and temporary way of a man who has done exactly the work he said he could do and has no reserves left to pretend otherwise. Stilling is blunt, he said, when he could say it. It does not pick its targets. It does not last. And it costs the man who does it the whole of himself for as long as he does it, and a while after. Any plan, he said, looking at Mira rather than at Leyden, that needs the radius held while fine work is done inside it had better have the fine work ready before the stilling starts, because the stilling will not wait politely while somebody thinks.

They signed the papers back in Leyden room, in the order the Office required, tasking, custody terms for the expert, movement orders for the station, and Mira stated her state for the record in the word she had chosen on the stair. Reduced. Leyden entered it without comment, which was his way of respecting it, and added, in his own hand, below her statement, the hours she had lost in Melora, so that the file would argue with anyone who later argued with her. The ridge road, he said, as she gathered the bundle, was the other fact she should hold from the start. The station was overlooked from the road along its whole descent, and nothing that happened in its yard happened unobserved. She understood him, and she and Rask went down to make their preparations in what remained of the day.

## Ground Truth

The Dravin border station announced itself from the ridge road long before the cart reached it, and Mira, who had been warned in Carrowmere that the place was overlooked, understood the warning differently once she saw the ground with her own eyes. The road did not pass the station so much as it presided over it, coming down out of the hills in a series of long, open traverses, each traverse laid across a shoulder of bare ground from which the whole station lay visible below, its yard, its office roof, its bonded stores, the outer bay and the line of the border channel catching the light, all of it arranged as plainly as a plan on a desk. A cart that stopped on any of those traverses had a reason to stop that any traveller could name, a wheel, a horse, a view, a rest, and the people in the cart could look down for as long as looking took without a single soul on the station being able to say, with any honesty, that they had been watched. Rask, beside her on the cart bench, followed her gaze down to the yard and then back up to the empty traverse behind them, and said nothing, which was his way of entering a fact in a shared record. Below them the station went about its morning, crossings forming their patient line at the yard gate, clerks moving between the office and the stores with papers held against the wind off the channel, guards at their posts in the unhurried attitudes of people who expect to be at those posts all day. Nothing in the scene suggested that the building had a lower floor at all, except the culvert grilles at the base of the far wall and the chimney like vent that served the pump hall air. Whatever was wrong at this station was wrong underneath it, out of the sight of the road, and Mira held that thought as carefully as Leyden had intended her to hold it, because it cut in both directions at once. The lower floor was private. The lower floor was also, for anyone who had worked there unobserved across a series of visits, the one place in the whole station where privacy could be relied upon completely, provided a person could come and go by a route the yard did not see.

The station officer, Commander Sabel, received them in the office above the yard and laid out the station cooperation in numbered sentences, the lower floor at the hours the pumps allowed, the shift books and pump logs and engineer records, and access to any member of the staff whose duties touched the lower floor. The expert, she added, had not yet arrived, and when he was brought to the station under his custody terms he would be interviewed in the custody interview room and nowhere else, a condition Mira accepted without argument.

Memmet came first among the staff, because procedure in a case that begins with a document should begin with the person who wrote it, and because Mira wanted the lower floor described to her by the woman who walked it before she walked it herself in any capacity beyond that of a visitor being shown a hall. The guard who entered the office interview room was in her middle years, broad through the shoulders from years of gate work, with her hair cut for duty and her hands, resting on the table between them, showing the particular pattern of small scars and healed cracks that cold water and iron work leave on a person over time. She gave her evidence in the order of her round, as Mira had known, reading the woman log in advance, that she would. The stair. The third step, where the air changed, and where she settled her ears. The pumps sounding higher, together, by a small interval, steady across a dozen strokes. The cold of the night, entered in the book at midnight by her own hand, and offered, in good faith, as the explanation she had first allowed herself. The handover, item by item, the outer bay, the river gate, the service gate fast and its wheel pinned. The round, west to east and back along the northern side, gauges in their bands, grilles clear, gate held under her hand through its wheel with the water pressing patiently on the far side. The quench tank in its recess, brine still, seals work as usual. The second round, the hall warmed, the engineer remark about the oil, passed down with the tea. The pitch unsettled. Her second entry, written without underlining, and her report to the day officer at the next relief. She gave it all without ornament and without improvement, and when Mira asked her, gently, whether she had considered, at any point in that morning, that the changed note might indicate something seated against the machinery rather than a property of the machinery itself, Memmet considered the question with the seriousness of a person who understands that a question asked in an interview room is a thing with edges.

No, she said. The pumps were the pumps. A guard listens to them the way she listens to the building. If the building had told her it was carrying something extra, she would have written that down too, when she knew how to write it. What she knew how to write was the note, and the note not settling. She had written it twice. She paused, and then added, in the tone of a woman completing a record rather than defending herself, that she had walked that floor for years and had never yet been wrong about a sound it made, and that the station could ask the engineer, who had laughed at her entries for a full day before he took his midday readings and stopped laughing, whether her ears were an instrument the station trusted.

The engineer, examined next, confirmed the arc of that account with the reluctance of a man conceding ground to a guard in front of witnesses from Carrowmere. His name was Pell, and he kept his records in a hand so small and regular that Rask, leaning over the pump log at Mira invitation, remarked that a man who wrote like that either trusted his eyesight completely or intended no one else ever to read a word. The log itself, produced from its tin box in the lower floor office and laid out across the interview table in its sequence of bound volumes, told a story in figures that matched Memmet evidence in voice. For a run of ordinary days the pumps had drawn their ordinary load, entered in the engineer columns at morning, midday, and evening, with the small variations that weather and water and traffic imposed and that Pell could account for, line by line, with the air of a man reciting a family history. Then, across a span that began before Memmet first entry and continued, unbroken, past it, the figures changed their character. Not sharply. Nothing in the log suggested a failure, a seizure, a single dramatic event that a watch could have dated to an hour. The load rose by a small margin and stayed risen, steady in the manner of a weight added to a scale and left there, and the midday reading on the day of Memmet second entry, the reading Pell had taken after a day of laughing, stood in the book underlined three times, with a marginal note in the same small hand, enlarged for once into legibility, that said the resistance was not in the pumps, not in the culvert, and not in the channel gates, all of which he had checked with his own hands. I checked, he said, before anyone from an office tells me my trade. It is not mine. Whatever the pumps are lifting against, it came onto my floor without my leave and it is sitting somewhere I cannot see with the covers on. He looked, as he said it, less like a man defending his machinery than like a man offended on its behalf, and Mira found the offence useful, because offended engineers cooperate in the small, unrequired ways that merely obedient ones do not.

Only after the documents, the testimony, and the walks did Mira read the lower floor, and she prepared for the reading with the deliberation that her reduced state required and that the case, in her judgement, deserved. She ate what the station kitchen provided, at the hour it provided it, without tasting it particularly. She rested, seated, with her coat folded under her head, in the small room off the guard room that Sabel had assigned to the enquiry, for the full interval she had named to Rask in advance, and she did not allow the impatience that rose in her at the wasting of daylight to shorten the interval by so much as the time it took to argue about it. A Weigher who reads tired reads her own tiredness into the floor and calls it evidence. A Weigher who reads reduced, and knows it, and plans for it, can still do one thing at a time and do it cleanly. The reading she intended was a survey, nothing more. She would walk the floor as she had walked rooms and halls and lock sides before it, hand to stone and iron and wood in sequence, and she would take from it only what a survey can honestly give, the shape of what had happened there, laid down in intensity and repetition, with no names attached to it and no faces, because weight records the intensity and affect of events in places and objects, fear, violence, grief, long repetition, sustained intent, and it does not record identity, and it cannot replay a scene, and anyone who claimed a finer yield from a read of a working pump hall either did not understand the discipline or was selling something.

Around the third housing, against its southern face where the housing met its stone bed, the floor held visits.

She worked them slowly, moving her hand by inches across the housing cover, the bed stone, the anchor plate at the housing foot, the floor flags immediately before it, and she let the reading assemble itself at its own pace, resisting, as her training and her temperament both required, the temptation to complete a pattern before the pattern had finished presenting itself. What the weight gave her, when she had worked the whole of the area twice, once from the west and once from the east, so that the second working could argue with the first, was not a scene and was not a person. It was repetition with a shape to it. Four visits. She stated the number aloud for the notebook and then tested it by a third working, because a number given early in a read has a way of persuading the hand to find exactly that number and no other, and the third working held to four. Four occasions on which a person, or persons, had spent sustained, intent time at the southern face of the third housing, down at the level of the bed, in the posture that working at that level imposes, close and low and patient. The visits were spaced. Weight fades with time, days clearing, months growing faint, and the four visits did not lie at the same depth in the stone and iron, they lay at depths that increased in a regular progression, the oldest faintest, the most recent strongest, spaced as evenly as the rungs of a ladder, which spoke, as plainly as weight can speak of anything, of a schedule rather than an impulse. The affect of the visits changed across the progression, and that change was the detail Mira dictated with the most care, because it was the detail most likely to be misread later by people who had not stood where she was standing. The first visit carried caution in a quantity that bordered on fear, not terror, not panic, the controlled apprehension of a person doing a dangerous thing for the first time in a particular place, with the exits counted and the sounds of the hall above the work listened for between every movement. The second visit carried less of it, and more method. The third carried method almost entire, the settled intent of routine mastered. The fourth carried something Mira had learned, across cases, to respect more than fear, which was confidence shading into satisfaction, the affect of a worker who has inspected his own work and found it good and expects to be the only person who ever inspects it. Four visits, spaced, careful, with growing confidence. Intensity only. No identity. She said that sentence aloud as well, for the notebook, in the flat form she used when a finding needed to survive being read by hostile eyes, and Rask entered it without comment, and the hall went on lifting its water around them, higher in pitch than it should have been, steady as a held note.

The device itself she found where the visits had been made, and she found it, at first, by the failure of her hand to find anything at all.

At the southern face of the third housing, low against the bed stone, in the angle where the housing cover came down to meet the anchor plate, there was a seated shape that her eyes, adjusting to the lamplight after the reading, could make out once she knew to look for it, a bound load in the language of the practice, a compact mass no larger than a station ledger stood on end, wrapped and seated and fitted to the housing with the neatness that had characterised everything about the visits. It looked inert. It looked, in fact, like a part of the pump installation that a visitor might have passed a hundred times, a reinforcement block, a packing case for a seal assembly, something the engineer might have pointed to if he had ever been asked, except that the engineer, standing later in the doorway of his own hall with his face the colour of a man who has been shown a wasp nest in his own roof, would say that he had never seen it in his life and that nothing of that shape belonged on any housing he maintained. To her eyes it was a physical object. To her hand, resting on the housing an inch above it and then, with a care she would remember later, on the stone an inch to its side, it was something else altogether, because where the object sat, the weight that an object of that kind, bound and seated and worked on across four visits of sustained intent, should have radiated into the stone and iron around it in a great, unmistakable deposit, there was almost nothing. A bound load, fixed in place by a Binder, should have read to a Weigher as enormous, as the test seal in the Office yard had read from the gate, a pressure at the back of the attention that no discipline could mistake. This one read as a gap dressed as an object. The visits around it read clearly, the hands at work, the caution, the method, the satisfaction, all of it laid down in the stone in the ordinary way of human action. The work itself, the binding that held the load to the housing and made it a device rather than a parcel, left almost no weight where it sat. The absence touched it. Mira took her hand away and put it back, twice, at points she named aloud for the notebook, and each time the finding was the same, a shape in the world and a near silence in the read, and the cold that came off that silence was different from the cold of an ordinary read in a way she had met twice before in her life and had hoped, in the unspoken manner of people who keep such hopes, not to meet a third time so soon.

She had met it at [the rehearsal at the Corra Lock, where a charge had been placed twice and had never been meant to work](/2026/11/06/nightjar-charge-that-failed-006/), though there the absence had been partial and imperfect, a maker learning a trick and failing at its edges. She had met it in [doors in Valtessa whose perfect seals had held nothing behind them](/2026/11/11/nightjar-seal-that-wasnt-011/), where the absence had concealed bindings specifically behind seals that read as flawless, and where her burned fingers had paid part of the price of the lesson. She had met it most recently in [a reading room in Melora where an absence had been caught retreating slowly toward a door](/2026/11/12/nightjar-slow-fade-012/), measured, decaying, its edge withdrawing by stages from the room it had emptied. Three encounters, three variations, one signature, a binding that left no weight where weight should have been, which by every rule of the practice as the Office taught it and as licensed Binders practised it should have been impossible, and which was sitting, at that moment, against the third pump housing of a Dravin border station, lifting the note of three pumps by a small, steady interval and waiting, in its seated patience, for whatever completion its maker had scheduled for it.

Mira did not touch the load itself. That decision was not caution in the general sense, it was discipline in the specific sense, the discipline of a Weigher who knows that a binding read through contact is a binding interacted with, and that interactions with unknown work, in unknown states of completion, are how officers acquire the kind of injuries that do not appear in any log until the following morning. She completed the survey from a distance of inches, dictated the position of the device by measurement from the housing bolts and the bed stone joints, had Rask sketch the seating in the notebook at a scale she checked, and then she withdrew, up the hall, past the desk, to the stair, where she sat down on the third step, where the air changed, and let the cold of the reading settle and her hands stop their fine trembling before she allowed herself to think in sentences rather than in findings.

Three live possibilities presented themselves, and she set them out for Rask on the stair, and later for Sabel in the office, in the order of their surface plausibility and with the fairness that a case at this stage requires, because a possibility dismissed early for the wrong reason has a habit of returning late at a much higher price.

The first possibility was that the device was the rehearsal built properly. At the Corra Lock, a charge had been placed twice and had never been meant to work, a rehearsal in the strict sense, an exercise in placement and concealment from which a maker could learn, at low risk and at leisure, everything that a real attempt would require. A maker who had rehearsed so carefully, and who had then built the real thing with the lessons of the rehearsal incorporated into it, would produce exactly the kind of object now seated against the third housing, competent, concealed, patient, and touched by the same signature absence that the rehearsal had shown in its imperfect form. The second possibility was that the device was the work of a different hand copying the rehearsal. Methods travel. A case that had been enquired into, as the Corra Lock case had been enquired into, leaves descriptions in registers, in custody interviews, in the talk of officers and engineers and guards, and a second maker, studying those descriptions, might reproduce the method without ever having met the first maker, in the way that a tune, once heard in a market square, can be whistled by a stranger in another town. The third possibility was that the device was a decoy for something at the other end of the station. A bound load that drew the whole attention of an enquiry to the eastern end of the lower floor, to the third housing and the service gate and the culvert, would be a generous gift to anyone whose real work lay at the western end, in the stores, at the stair, along the yard wall, or in the offices above, and the care that had been spent on the concealment of the binding was, under this possibility, not a protection for the device but an advertisement of its importance, calculated to be discovered by exactly the kind of officer who had now discovered it. Mira stated all three fairly, with the evidence for each and the evidence against, and she watched Sabel face as the third possibility landed, because the third possibility was the one that converted a pump hall enquiry into a station wide emergency, and Sabel, to her credit, did not flinch from it in either direction, neither seizing it as an excuse for dramatic measures nor waving it away as an insult to her command.

The decoy possibility was tested, not waved away. That testing occupied the rest of that day and the morning of the next, and it was done properly, which in practice meant it was done slowly, by people who understood that a search conducted to confirm a conclusion already reached is worse than no search at all. The other end of the station was searched and read and cleared. The western end of the lower floor, the stair and the guard room above it, the stores and their yard wall, the offices and the strong room and the bonded cages, each was walked and examined and, where reading was appropriate, read, by Mira in survey and by station staff under her direction for the physical search, with inventories checked against their ledgers and seals checked against their registers. The culvert was inspected from both ends by lamplight, with the water lowered as far as the working of the station allowed. The outer bay was walked at low water. The ridge road end of the station, the yard gate and the crossing office and the waiting room where travellers papers were examined, received the same attention, and Rask, whose stilling had no part in a search, contributed the contribution he made best, a sceptical attention to the physical world, checking locks with his own hands, counting seals against a list he had copied himself rather than trusting the list he had been given, and asking, of every cupboard and recess and boxed store, the plain question of how long it had been since a person had opened it who was not in the room. Nothing was found. No second load, no second seating, no preparation for a second device in any stage of completion, no store of materials that the station inventory could not account for, no sign that attention had been drawn east in order to free a hand in the west. Cleared, Mira entered in her notebook, properly, and underlined the word once, because the clearing had been earned by work and she wanted the record to distinguish it from the many clearings she had seen entered, in other cases, on the strength of a glance and a reputation. The decoy possibility did not die of the search, since a decoy whose partner device had not yet been placed would also produce a clean search, and she recorded that reservation beside the entry, but it fell, in her private shorthand, to the bottom of the three, and the weight of the case settled where the weight of the visits had settled all along, at the southern face of the third housing.

While the search proceeded, the comparison that would eventually organise the whole case was being assembled, in the interview room, from paper. At Mira request, made through Leyden office by telephone with Petra Dunmore at the Carrowmere desk working the registers at the other end of the line, the case record of the Corra Lock rehearsal was summarised and sent down in a form the station clerk could copy fairly, the dimensions of the rehearsal device, its seating, its binding path as reconstructed after its discovery, the spacing of the two placements, and the nature of its contact with the lock housing against which it had been set. Dunmore read the figures twice, as was her habit with anything that would be entered in another person notebook, and Mira set them, in her own hand, in a table against the measurements Rask had sketched and the observations she had dictated at the third housing. A table is a modest instrument, and the Office did not, as a rule, lend tables any authority beyond the figures they contained, but a table has one virtue that prose lacks entirely, which is that it refuses to let a comparison blur. Set side by side, item by item, the rehearsal device and this device either agreed or they differed, line by line, and the lines on which they differed could be counted, named, and argued about without any of the softening that comes, in spoken evidence, from a witness desire to sound consistent.

| Aspect | Rehearsal device | This device |
|---|---|---|
| Anchor seating | Anchor plate set proud of the housing face, bedded on packing, held by two bolts, seating shallow and visibly worked | Anchor plate drawn into a recess cut into the bed stone, seated flush, held across its full width, no packing showing |
| Binding path | Binding carried around the outside of the housing cover, exposed at two points where it crossed the cover seam | Binding carried inside the cover line, following the housing curve, crossing no seam, exposed at no point |
| Visit spacing | Two placements close together, the second within a short interval of the first, spacing uneven | Four visits spaced evenly across a longer span, each visit complete in itself, spacing regular as a rota |
| Housing contact | Load resting against the housing on a narrow contact strip, load borne mainly by its own seating | Load seated against the housing across a broad contact face, load shared between the seating and the housing itself |

Four differences. Mira read the table aloud to Rask in the interview room, with the station plan weighted down at one corner of the desk and Memmet log, in its tin box, at the other, and she set out, as plainly as she could, what the table did and did not show at this stage of the case. Each difference, taken alone, looked like the mark of a different maker. A shallow seating against a deep one, an exposed path against a concealed one, two hurried placements against four measured visits, a narrow contact against a broad one, the whole comparison could be read, and would be read by any officer who stopped at the table, as the difference between one hand and another, the rehearsal maker and his imitator, the second possibility in her list of three standing up in documentary form and asking to be believed. She did not believe it yet, and she did not disbelieve it, and she entered the table in the case record as an artifact of the ground truth stage, complete in itself and deliberately uninterpreted beyond the observation that four differences existed and that each of them, whatever else it was, represented an improvement in the work. A deeper seating held better. A concealed path resisted inspection better. Even spacing suggested a maker no longer dependent on opportunity. A broad contact shared a load that a narrow contact had carried at a strain. Whoever had made the second device, whether the same hand learning or a different hand copying, had made a better one, and the pumps of the Dravin border station were lifting their water against its binding with a higher note and a steadier labour because the improvement was not theoretical. It was seated, in iron and stone, in a working hall, and it had been visited four times by someone who had left each time more satisfied than the time before.

It remained, before any decision about the device could be called a plan, to establish how such a device could be made inert, and to establish it in terms that belonged to the practice alone, described as the practice describes its own operations, without any passage into instruction that a person outside the discipline could carry away and use in a world where bindings do not exist. Mira set the method out for Sabel and for Rask, in the office, with the door closed and the station clerk dismissed, because even practice talk, in a border station, deserves a closed door. A bound load is held by its anchor plate. The binding completes itself through the seating, the plate seated in its recess, the path closed, the load borne between the seating and the housing. While the seating holds, the binding holds, and any attempt to move the load as it stands is an attempt to move the binding with it, on its own terms, which are the terms its maker chose. If, however, the anchor is drawn out of its seating while a Stiller holds the radius still, the position changes entirely. Under a stilling, the binding cannot complete, cannot transfer, cannot take a new hold, because the stilling suppresses all practice within its radius without choosing between one working and another. Drawn out under that suppression, the anchor comes free as inert iron, the path opens without closing elsewhere, and the load, unheld, can be lowered, by ordinary physical means, slings and hands and care, into the station quench tank, the broad, low tank of cold brine in its recess beside the third housing, kept there for the quenching of seals and the cooling of tools, where a binding deprived of its seating unravels harmlessly over hours, in cold and salt and depth, as a knot unravels in water when the standing end is cut, slowly, without force, until what remains in the tank is a mass of spent material that a Weigher can read as residue and a station engineer can remove with a hook. Every element of that method existed on the lower floor already, as ordinary equipment or ordinary staffing. The tank was there. The Stiller was there. The anchor, by the table, was seated in a recess from which it could, in principle, be drawn. What the method required beyond its elements was a decision, and the decision was the point at which the ground truth of the case gave way to the part of the case that no amount of further walking, or logging, or reading, could settle in advance, because the decision turned on the ridge road.

For the station could be evacuated. Sabel stated the option as plainly as Mira had stated the possibilities, and its merits were real. The lower floor cleared, the offices cleared, the yard cleared, the crossing line sent back up the road, and the work at the third housing done in an empty building, with no guard at a desk, no engineer at his bench, no clerk above the work with a file open and a family in the station cottages. If the device, in the drawing of its anchor, did what its maker had built it to do, an empty station would pay for the discovery in stone and iron rather than in people. But an evacuation of a border station is not a private event. It is a procession, visible from every traverse of the ridge road, carts and families and files and guards moving up the hill in the order that evacuations have used since the first station on this border was built, and it announces, to anyone watching from the shoulder of the road with the patience that watchers on that road had already demonstrated, in the matter of four evenly spaced visits, that the thing they placed has been found and that the people who found it are afraid of it. A maker who learned that his device had been found would learn it in time to use the knowledge. A network, if a network there was, behind a maker, would learn it faster. The station would be empty, and safe, and the case would be over, in every sense except the sense in which the Office had been sent to Dravin to conclude it, which was the sense that required knowing who had seated a bound load against a pump housing and taught a binding to leave no weight, and whether that knowledge was held by one frightened man in custody or by hands that had never been near a licence roll in their lives. Evacuation was safety purchased with the whole value of the discovery, and Mira, setting it out for the record in the office with the plan between them and the sound of the pumps coming up faintly through the floor, higher than they should have been and steady as a held note, did not recommend it and did not dismiss it, because ground truth is not the beat in which recommendations belong. She recorded the gate, and the tank, and the road, and the table of four improvements, and the four visits in the stone, and the silence where the binding should have been, and she closed the notebook on a case that had told her, as completely as it could, what was there, and nothing yet about what it meant.
## The Collapse

The improvement table had been a comfort while it was incomplete, and Mira understood, standing in the station office with the pump log open beside it and the lower floor still cold in her hands, that comfort was exactly what it had been offering her and that she should have distrusted it sooner. A table makes a thing look settled. Four rows, four differences, each difference named in plain Office language, anchor seating, binding path, visit spacing, housing contact, and beside each row the rehearsal at the Corra Lock set down from its case record, neat and failed and safely past, and beside that the device in the lower floor of the Dravin border station, neat in a different way, and not past at all. While the rows remained separate she could hold the reasonable reading without effort. A different hand had studied the rehearsal, or had been told of it, or had worked from a description passed along a chain she had not yet found, and had made the same kind of thing better in the ordinary way that copyists improve on what they copy, by tidying what looked untidy and strengthening what looked weak. That reading asked nothing difficult of her. It gave her two makers, one who had failed to make a charge work and one who was trying to make a charge work, and two makers could be hunted by ordinary means, by paper and by border records and by the slow accumulation of who had crossed and who had lingered, and if the second maker was never named the device could still be made inert and the station could still be held and the case could still close as a prevention, which was what the arc of the work required. She had liked that reading in the way she liked any reading that let the evidence remain evidence and did not ask her to feel her way toward a conclusion through the affect in a room. She set her palm flat on the office desk, away from the table, away from the log, on plain wood that held nothing more than clerks and tiredness, and she made herself go through the rows again in order, not as differences this time but as decisions.

Anchor seating first. The rehearsal device had been seated shallow, its anchor plate set against the housing with the kind of care that looks sufficient from a standing position and fails when the load is asked to hold against a working surface, against vibration, against the small repeated movements of a pump that never wholly rests. Whoever wrote the Corra Lock record had noted the shallowness without quite naming it as the failure, because the rehearsal had failed earlier and for larger reasons, and a record tends to spend its attention on the failure that arrived first. The device below her now was seated deep. Not merely deeper, which any copyist might manage by pushing harder or cutting wider, but seated in the place where the housing could take the depth, a place that was not obvious from the front of the housing and was not marked on the station plan that Petra Dunmore had sent down with the tasking papers, a place you found by having learned, on the earlier attempt, exactly where the shallow seating had begun to work loose under load. Mira looked at that row for a long time. A second maker, reading a report, would know the seating had been shallow. A second maker, standing at the housing with time and quiet, might find the deeper place by inspection. Either was possible. She wrote possible beside the row in her notebook, in pencil, and moved on, because one row proves nothing and she was determined not to let unease do the work of proof.

Binding path second. The rehearsal binding had taken the direct route from anchor to load, the route any practitioner draws first on paper because paper rewards straight lines and punishes nothing. In a working station the direct route crossed the part of the housing that grew warmest in operation, and warmth, in the invented and ordinary terms of licensed practice as Venn himself had once set them out for a tribunal whose record Dunmore had included, made a binding path labour, made it spend itself maintaining its own line instead of holding its load. The device below had a path that bent. It bent once, at the point where the housing ran warm, and it bent in the direction that cost the maker length and gained the binding quiet, and the bend was not elegant and was not the sort of thing a copyist adds because a report mentioned warmth. The Corra Lock record did not mention warmth at all. Mira checked, although she already knew. She turned back through the copied record page by page while Rask waited by the door with his arms folded and said nothing, which was his contribution to any process that required her to discover she was wrong at her own speed. The record spoke of the direct path. It spoke of the failure to complete. It did not connect the two, because the officer who wrote it had not been a practitioner and had not known which details belonged together. So the bend had not come from the record. The bend had come from standing at a warm housing with a binding spending itself under your hands and understanding, in the body and not on paper, why the line would not hold. She did not write possible beside the second row. She wrote learned, and then, because learned was a conclusion and she was not yet entitled to it, she crossed it out and wrote from practice instead, which was the same thing in a more cautious coat, and she knew it, and Rask knew it, and neither of them said so.

Visit spacing third, and this was the row she had trusted most when she wanted two makers, because spacing is behaviour and behaviour can be imitated by anyone patient. Four visits, spaced, careful, with growing confidence, that was what the read of the lower floor had given her and it was all the read had given her, intensity laid down in metal and stone over repeated returns, no identity, no face, no name, only the shape of a practice becoming surer of itself. A copyist could space visits. A copyist would space visits, if the report said the rehearsal had been rushed, and the report did say that, in its final paragraph, in the flat language of an officer closing a file he did not expect to be reopened. But the spacing in the lower floor was not merely even, and it was not merely patient. The intervals shortened as the confidence grew, and they shortened in proportion to the work each visit had to do, the long first visit that seated the anchor, the shorter second that set the path, the third, shorter still, that tested the housing contact, and the fourth, the briefest, that had done almost nothing except confirm. That is the rhythm of a maker returning to his own work and finding each time that less remains to be corrected. Imitation spaces visits to look careful. Making spaces visits because the work itself takes less time as it approaches its intended shape, and the difference between those two rhythms is visible only when you lay the visits beside the other three rows and ask what work each visit was for. Mira had laid them beside each other an hour earlier and had declined to ask. She asked now. The fourth visit had no work to do if a copyist was performing care for a watcher. The fourth visit had every reason to exist if the maker was checking a housing contact he had improved on the third visit and wanted to be certain about before he left the device to wait for whatever hour it had been set to wait for. She put her pencil down. The office was warm from the stove in the corner and her hands were still cold from the lower floor, and the contrast made the stiffness in the two burned fingers of her left hand announce itself each time she flexed them without thinking, a small pull, a small delay, the body keeping its own ledger underneath the one on the desk.

Housing contact fourth, and the row that ended it. The rehearsal contact had been broad and even, spread across the housing face in the approved manner, the manner taught to licensed practitioners and written into the guidance that accompanied a licence, a contact designed to distribute and to be inspected and to leave a clear record of itself for anyone who came after. The contact below was narrow and offset, set where the housing metal was thickest, where the pump stroke transmitted least, a contact that would be harder to find on inspection and harder to describe in a report and better in every practical respect that mattered to a maker who did not intend his work to be inspected at all. It contradicted the guidance. It improved on the guidance. And it improved on it in the specific direction of concealment, which was the direction the whole device leaned, the binding leaving almost no weight where it sat, the absence touching its edges, the contact placed where a routine inspection would pass over it, the path bent away from the warmth that would have made the binding labour audibly to a Weigher reading the floor at the wrong hour. Four improvements. Each one correcting a weakness the rehearsal had shown. Each one correcting it in sequence, anchor before path, path before spacing could settle, contact last because contact could only be judged once the load was seated and the path was quiet. That is not two makers. A second maker improves what he notices, in the order he notices it, and he notices the visible things first, the contact and the seating, and he leaves the invisible things, the warmth in the path, the rhythm of the visits, roughly as he found them, because he has no way to know they were weaknesses at all. This device improved the invisible things as carefully as the visible ones, and improved them in the order in which the first maker would have discovered them by failing. The reasonable reading died without drama, at a desk, in a warm office, while the pumps below kept their altered pitch and Lia Memmet waited in the corridor with her log under her arm and her ordinary care still vindicated and still not enough. Mira closed the notebook. She said to Rask that it was one maker learning, and Rask, who had reached the same place some time earlier by the shorter road of not having wanted the other answer, said only that he would tell the guard commander the other end of the station could stand down from its second search, since the decoy possibility had been tested and cleared and the device in the lower floor was, as it had always been, the whole of the case. She nodded. The collapse of a reading is not the collapse of the work. In some ways it steadied the work. One maker meant the learning had a history she could reason from. One maker meant the improvements had a logic, and a logic could be followed forward as well as back, toward the anchor, toward the seating, toward the way the load would behave when the anchor was drawn. But one maker also meant the man in custody in Carrowmere, and in a smaller interview room at the station where he had been brought under guard that morning because Leyden had insisted the only available expert should be available in person and verified twice at close quarters, had become something other than an expert. Oskar Venn had been a licensed Binder for thirty years. Oskar Venn knew the guidance that the narrow contact contradicted, because men like Venn had written the guidance. And Oskar Venn had already told the Office, on the record and without hesitation, that what was being done in the lower floor of this station could not be done at all.

The interview room at the border station had been a store room before it was an interview room and it retained the honesty of its first purpose, stone walls, a single high window wired against breakage, a table bolted in the old manner although the bolts had long since become a formality, and two chairs, one of which Oskar Venn had already assessed, from the habit of a lifetime spent assessing the places where bindings must either hold or be seen not to hold, as the less uncomfortable of the two, and had taken without asking, because custody had taken from him a great many permissions but had not yet taken the small ones and he conserved the small ones carefully. He was sixty one years old by his own reckoning, although he no longer reckoned aloud, and he had held a Binder's licence for thirty of those years before the licence was taken, and the taking still seemed to him, in the private accounting he conducted while other people arranged papers, to have been conducted by men who understood the rules of the licence very well and the practice the licence governed hardly at all, which was unjust and was also, he admitted on his better mornings, irrelevant, since a licence is the rules and nothing else, and a man who works outside them has worked unlicensed whatever his hands remember. He turned that over while he waited. The guard outside the door coughed at intervals. Water moved somewhere below the floor, in pipes or in the pump system or in the ground itself, and the sound carried the altered note that everyone at the station had been politely not naming since Memmet wrote it in her log, a pitch a fraction off true, the kind of wrongness that a man who has spent thirty years listening to bound loads settle can hear in his teeth. Venn heard it. Venn had heard it on arrival, under escort, crossing the yard with his coat held closed against the border wind, and he had filed it, professionally and against his own interest, in the part of his attention that still belonged to the work rather than to the prisoner. He was right about the licence, and he knew he was right, and it was the only clean thing he owned in this room. Inside the licence rules, within the approved seatings and the approved paths and the broad inspectable contacts that the guidance prescribed, a binding declared itself. It had weight. It could be read by any competent Weigher set down in the room with it, because the rules had been written, deliberately and after disasters whose names were taught to apprentices, to make concealment impossible for a practitioner who stayed inside them. A licensed Binder working inside the licence rules could not hide a binding. That was true. It had been true when he was apprenticed and it was true now and it would remain true whatever the Ninth Office wished to hear from him in exchange for considerations that custody made precious, a warmer cell, a letter carried, a name cleared from a list he had never seen. What the Office wanted, he understood with the clarity that fear sometimes lends instead of taking away, was for him to extend the sentence by a single step, from inside the rules to anywhere at all, and to say that hiding a binding was impossible, full stop, as a fact about the world rather than as a fact about the guidance. He rehearsed the sentence while he waited, because a sentence rehearsed is a sentence that can be delivered without the voice doing anything revealing, and he found, rehearsing it, that his hands wanted occupation, so he took the cup the guard had left, although the water in it was cold, and he set it down again in a slightly different place, and he took his old licence token from the custody envelope they allowed him to keep under supervision, a disc of dull metal worn smooth at the edges by thirty years of being carried against other metal, and he turned it once, and set it down, and laid his fingers along the edge of the table, where generations of detained and detaining hands had polished the wood to a faint shine. The fear was already there before the door opened. It was not fear of the room. It was not fear of a longer sentence, which had lost its power to frighten him at about the point where he understood that sentences end and reputations do not recover, and that he would die a convicted practitioner whatever any tribunal added or subtracted now. The fear had an address elsewhere, and he kept his eyes away from its direction the way a man keeps his eyes away from a bright window, not by effort but by long practice, and he composed his face into the expression of a difficult expert who expects to be misquoted, and he waited for the Weigher to come in and misquote him.

She came in with the cold still on her, which Venn noticed before he noticed anything else, the particular inward look that his own practice had taught him to recognise in Weighers who have read something heavy and are carrying a portion of it in the body, and behind her the Stiller, Rask, who took the wall by the door and made the room feel smaller by the simple act of declining to sit. Mira Kovac set her notebook on the table and sat in the less comfortable chair without appearing to notice that it was less comfortable, which Venn filed, also professionally, as either discipline or exhaustion, and she flexed the fingers of her left hand once against the table top, slowly, as a man might test a hinge, and the two stiff fingers answered late and she stilled them with the other hand, not hiding the gesture, and he understood that she did not hide gestures because she expected other people to be reading her hands as carefully as she read theirs. She began with the station, not with him. She described the device in the lower floor in the spare terms the Office used on the record, a seated bound load against the pump housing, and she described the read she had taken from the floor, four visits, spaced, careful, growing in confidence, and she was precise about what the read had not given her, no identity, no direction of arrival, nothing about the maker's person at all, and Venn, listening, felt the unwelcome respect that a practitioner feels for another practitioner who states her limits before she states her findings, because it meant the findings, when they came, would be exactly as large as she claimed and no larger, and could not be argued down by the usual route of arguing that the witness exaggerated everything. She laid out the improvement table then, not the paper, the reasoning, row by row, anchor, path, spacing, contact, and she said that the reading of two makers had collapsed under the order of the improvements, and that she was now proceeding on one maker learning from the rehearsal, and she asked him, as the expert the Office had brought under custody because no other Binder of his seniority could be got to a border station inside the time the device allowed, to confirm or correct the reasoning from his knowledge of practice. It was well done. It gave him every opportunity to be useful in the direction of truth and no opportunity at all to be useful in any other direction without the uselessness showing. He confirmed the path. He confirmed it at length, with the warmth of the housing and the spending of the line, because length on a safe subject is a place to put time, and while he spoke she watched his hands, openly, as he had known she would, and he gave her hands that were doing ordinary things, the cup lifted and set down, the licence token turned and set down, the fingers laid along the polished edge of the table and lifted again, and he kept his voice in the register of a man correcting a promising student's exercise, dry, a little impatient, generous with detail where detail cost nothing. Then she asked the question the whole interview had been built to reach, and she asked it plainly, without decoration, which he also respected and resented in equal measure. She asked whether a binding could be hidden from a Weigher's read, whether the near absence of weight around the load in the lower floor was a thing a maker could do deliberately, rather than a defect of her read or an accident of the seating. The room went very quiet in the way rooms do when a Stiller is present and has, perhaps without intending it, let some portion of his attention settle. Venn looked at the token rather than at her. He said that a licensed Binder working inside the licence rules could not hide a binding, and about the licence rules he was right, and she nodded as if she accepted the answer, and wrote something, and then, gently, as a person returns a cup to its exact ring on a table, she asked the question again with the licence removed from it. Not inside the rules, she said. Anywhere. Could it be done at all. His hands found the cup. His hands found the token. His hands found the edge of the table and stayed there, and the fear came up in him so specifically that he felt it as a narrowing of the afternoon to a single corridor at the end of which a door he had kept closed for longer than this young officer had been alive stood exactly as closed as he had left it and no more, because opening it by a fraction, by the width of a single true sentence in a border station interview room, would not be he who paid the whole price, and that knowledge, which should have made speaking easier, made the lie necessary instead, and he gave the lie the full weight of thirty years of licensed authority and a level voice. He said flatly that hiding a binding is impossible. He said it once, cleanly, without qualification, without the licence, without the rules, as a fact about the world. And Mira Kovac, who could not detect truth in speech, who had told tribunals and handlers and, this morning, this same room's record that weight records intensity and affect and never a proposition, that a frightened man may be frightened of prison or of weather or of his own memory and the read will not sort one from another, took his fear through the polished wood and the dull metal and the cold cup as cleanly as she had taken the fear out of the lower floor, and carried it, and knew it for fear, and knew, with the useless completeness of her practice, that it was not fear of prison at all. What he was frightened of she could not read. Who he was frightened for she could not read. Whether the flat sentence had been a lie she could not read either, in the technical sense her evidence would ever be able to bear, because speech leaves no weight and truth leaves less. She only had a man of thirty years standing whose hands had gone still on the edge of a table at the one question that mattered, whose fear had risen like water in a lock at the prospect of answering it, and who had then answered it with a smoothness that belonged to rehearsal rather than to knowledge. She thanked him for his time in the tone of an officer closing an interview that has produced nothing, and she wrote in her notebook, while he watched, a single line that he could not read upside down although he tried, and she stood, and Rask opened the door, and the altered pitch of the pumps came up the corridor underneath the ordinary sounds of the station, patient and off true, waiting for the hour when someone would have to go down the stairs and put hands on the thing that, according to the expert, could not exist in the manner of its hiding and yet was down there hiding, in plain stone and metal, from everyone except the woman who had just carried its maker's fear out of the room in her own cold hands.

## Pressure

Mira made the decision on the stairs, in full view of the people who would have to carry it, and she made it before she took the first step down, because a decision made halfway down a stair in a border station belongs to the stair and not to the officer. The guard detail stood in the lower corridor in their border coats, four of them, with Memmet at their head and her log closed under her arm, and the station commander stood behind them with the evacuation order written and unsigned in his hand, and all of them were looking at the pump hall door and at the altered pitch coming through it, a note off true that had stopped sounding like a mechanical fault some hours ago and had begun to sound like a clock. The commander put the case for signing with the plainness of a man who has rehearsed it against his own reluctance. If the device completed while the station was occupied, the lower floor would be lost and anyone in it would be lost with it, and the upper floors would follow the water wherever the water chose to go once the pumps stopped governing it. If he signed, the station emptied in good order, staff and detained persons and guard detail together, up the yard and out by the main gate, and the loss, if loss came, would be a loss of buildings and machinery, which the Accord insures, rather than of people, whom it does not. He did not say the last part. He did not need to. Mira listened with her notebook closed, because she had already written the other half of the reckoning in it upstairs and saw no reason to perform the writing twice, and when he had finished she asked him one question, whether the yard and the main gate could be seen from the ridge road. He said they could. She asked whether the ridge road was watched. He said that it was watched as a matter of routine by persons whose reports went somewhere he had never been shown, and that a full evacuation in daylight, at an hour when no drill had been announced and no relief convoy was due, would be observed, timed, and understood. She asked what it would be understood as. He said, as the discovery of the thing in his pump hall. She nodded and thanked him and then she gave her order in the same plain register he had used, because orders that borrow the tone of the room they are given in are more likely to survive contact with the room. The guard detail would leave the lower floor. Memmet would take them up, with the commander, by the inner stair, in ordinary order, at ordinary pace, as if a shift inspection had ended. The upper station would continue its routine audibly and visibly, stamps and gates and the yard traffic of a working border post, so that anyone watching from the ridge would see a station that had noticed nothing. She and Rask would remain in the lower floor. The device would be drawn and lowered to the quench tank by two people who could do the work, or it would not be drawn at all, and in either case no watcher would be given the information that the Office had found what had been placed for it to find, because that information, once given, could not be taken back, and it would travel faster than any report the Ninth Office could write about what happened next. The commander looked at the unsigned order for a moment and then folded it once and put it in his coat, which was his way of recording that he disagreed and obeyed, and Memmet, who had more to lose by the order than anyone else in the corridor since the lower floor was her floor and the log was her log, asked only whether she should leave the pump hall keys in the door or take them up with her. Mira said leave them. Memmet nodded as if that settled something larger than keys. The detail went up the inner stair in ordinary order, boots unhurried, one man still carrying the mug he had been drinking from when he was called down, because Mira had said ordinary and Memmet had understood ordinary down to the level of mugs, and the sound of them receded, and the door at the top closed without emphasis, and the lower floor became, for the first time since the device had been found in it, a place with two people in it and a bound load seated against a pump housing and a service gate between the pump hall and the outer bay that had to be held on its wheel against the water while anyone passed, and a quench tank of cold brine standing in its bay beyond the gate where the pump seal work was done, ordinary equipment, filled and kept at level by routine, waiting without knowing it had been promoted. Mira flexed her left hand once, slowly, and felt the stiff fingers answer late, and she put that knowledge where she kept all knowledge about her own state during an operation, at the front, where it could govern her choices instead of surprising them. She was reduced. She had been reduced on entering and she was reduced now, the cold of the reads still in her and the fear she had taken from Venn sitting under her own fear like a second, mismatched pulse, and she said so to Rask, aloud, on the record of the room, because he was about to still a radius for her and a Stiller is entitled to know the condition of the person inside his stilling. Rask said he had seen. He did not offer comfort and she did not want it. He asked whether she wanted the gate held first or the radius stilled first, and she considered it with the seriousness the question deserved, since the order of operations was the operation, and she said the gate first, because once the radius was stilled he would be spending himself at a rate neither of them could fully predict in a room containing an absence, and she wanted him positioned and gripping before the spending began, his feet placed, his weight set, his left hand and his right hand on the holding wheel in the places he chose for them, not arriving at the gate under pressure with the load already moving. Rask went through the service gate passage first, ducking under the low lintel that every tall man at the station ducked under without thinking, and Mira followed and noted, because noting was her discipline under fear, the ordinary things in their ordinary places, the seal tools racked by size, the spare packing coiled and oiled, the brine tank with its tide line of salt at the rim and its ladle hung on a hook worn bright, the inspection board with its chalked dates in a station hand, none of it changed by the presence of the device behind them or by the decision she had just made, all of it continuing to be a working floor in a working station while two people prepared to do a piece of licensed practice in its unlicensed form, drawing an anchor from its seating under stilling, which no guidance contemplated because the guidance contemplated making bindings safe by procedure and paperwork and time, and time was the one input the device had been built to deny them. Rask took his place at the gate. The gate stood between the pump hall and the outer bay on its heavy track, and the holding wheel governed it against the pressure of the water on the bay side, the counterweight chain that should have shared the labour being due for renewal and therefore, in the honest language of station maintenance, decorative. He set his feet. He set his hands. He turned the wheel a fraction to feel the load in it and the gate moved a fraction and the water note changed beyond it, and he let the fraction back and reported, in the flat tone he used for measurements, the weight he would be holding and the length of time he could hold it without his grip becoming a negotiation. It was long enough. It was not comfortably long enough, and Mira recorded the difference and adjusted the plan she carried in her head by the specific amount it required, which was this, that there would be no pause between the drawing of the anchor and the lowering toward the tank, no inspection of the freed load on the floor, no moment in which she stood back to confirm what her hands already knew, because pauses are paid for by the person holding the gate. She told him so. He said that was the first sensible economy he had heard from the Office all week. Then she went back through the passage alone and stood in the pump hall in front of the device, and the pitch of the pumps filled the space where instructions would have gone if there had been anyone left to give them to, and she laid out on the housing top, in order, the few tools the work allowed, all of them station tools, all of them ordinary, and she named the sequence aloud once, for the record of her own hearing, anchor first, path quiet under stilling, load freed but not completed, load carried through the gate passage at the pace the gate allowed, load to the tank, and she stopped there because the tank belonged to the next part of the work and she had learned, at cost, not to spend attention past the step her hands were on. She called through the passage that she was ready. Rask answered that the gate was his. She said, still the radius now, and she felt the stilling come up around her like weather arriving from a direction weather does not usually use, a pressure on the practice in the room, indiscriminate, blunt, exactly as he had demonstrated it in the Office yard on a test seal what felt like a year of her life ago and was only days, the seal dimming under his attention and her own sense of the weight in the floor dimming with it, the device and the housing and the old accumulated affect of the lower floor all pressed down together under a hand that could not choose among them. That was the limit of stilling and she had been told it and she had seen it and it still cost her a breath to experience it from inside, her one instrument muffled precisely when the work began, so that she would draw the anchor substantially by touch and by knowledge and by the reasoning she had done at the desk upstairs, and not by read. She said his name once to confirm he could hear her. He said he could, and that the spending had begun, and that she should be about the work. She set her hands to the anchor.

The anchor resisted in the manner of a thing seated by a man who had learned from a shallower seating, which is to say it resisted evenly, without the small give at one corner that tells the hands a plate was set in haste, and Mira worked it by increments she counted under her breath, not for Rask and not for any record, but because counting was how she kept the work at the speed the gate allowed instead of the speed her fear preferred. Her left hand was the problem she had named in advance and it behaved exactly as named, the two stiff fingers late on each adjustment, so that twice she had to stop and reset her grip with deliberation while the stilling pressed on the room and Rask held his wheel in the passage and said nothing at all, not even when the pause lengthened, because he understood that commentary is also a spending. The housing was warm where the binding path bent away from the warmth, and cool at the narrow contact, and she read the device with her palms in the only language left to her under stilling, temperature and resistance and the minute tremor that a working pump lends to everything bolted to it, and she found the maker's fourth visit in her hands, the confirming visit, because the seating had been finished with a final quarter turn of attention that a copyist would never think to add and that her fingers recognised as the gesture of a man checking his own work before leaving it to wait. She worked past it. The plate moved. It moved by the width of a fingernail and the pitch of the pumps altered under the movement, not toward true, not yet, but sideways, a grinding interval in which every sound in the hall seemed to be deciding which note to become, and she called through the passage that the anchor was moving and that he should set himself. Rask answered with one word. The plate moved again. The path, which should have lain quiet under the stilling while its anchor was drawn, gathered itself instead along the bend the maker had given it, and Mira understood in her hands, a half breath before the room understood it in sound, that the improvement she had admired at the desk had a second purpose she had not priced, that a path bent for quietness was also a path stored under tension, and that drawing its anchor would release the tension along the line of the housing and out through the nearest heavy thing that shared a frame with it, and the nearest heavy thing was the service gate. She shouted. The binding released. The gate slammed on its track with a sound that took the pump noise and the water noise and her own voice and folded them into a single concussion that she felt in her teeth and in the floor and in the plate coming free in her hands all at once, and the holding wheel spun as the gate took the water load sideways, spokes blurring past the place where a man's hands govern a gate, and then the spin stopped, which was worse than the spin, because the spin stopping meant the hands had caught it. Rask had caught it. He had held the gate as she asked him to hold it, against the order she had given upstairs and against the plan she had adjusted at the housing, held it through the slam and through the spin and through the moment when letting it run would have been the decision every instinct in a body makes on its own behalf, and the gate stayed, and the water stayed, and the passage stayed open by exactly the width the work required, and his voice came through the passage flattened and shortened by effort, telling her the gate was held and that she should not stop. She did not stop. The load was out of its seating. The anchor plate hung free in her hands with the bound load suspended from its seating in the manner of practice that she would not have described to anyone outside the work, inert looking still, uncompleted still, held from completing by the stilling that pressed the whole room down and by the anchor being out of the place the maker had chosen for it, and it was heavier than its size allowed for and colder, and her stiff fingers screamed their small delayed protest at the grip she required of them and she reordered the grip so the stronger fingers carried and the stiff ones guided, the economy she had practised in corridors and on rails now spent in earnest. She moved. The passage was low and narrow and the gate at its end was a held thing now rather than a mechanism, held by a man whose breathing she could hear changing as the minutes of his holding accumulated, each breath costing more of the total he had named to her before the stilling began, and she understood that his total was being revised downward by what the slam had taken out of his left hand, although he had not reported it and would not report it, because Rask reported what governed the work and he had decided, in the half second of the spin, that his hand was his to spend and the gate was hers to rely on, and the narrowing of his capacity was arriving instead as delay, as the small sounds a man makes when a task that should hold steady must be reheld, again and again, at shorter intervals. She said his name. He said, bring it through. She brought it through, under the lintel, past the racked seal tools and the coiled packing, into the bay where the quench tank stood, and she set the load down on the tank boards with a care that felt absurd and was not, because a bound load unseated is a bound load negotiating, and the boards took its weight with a creak she felt through her knees. Then she went back to the gate because the work was not done and because he could not leave it. She put her hands on the wheel above his and took her share of the holding, which was less than half for reasons of simple strength that neither of them commented on, and together they eased the gate through the small distance that let the water pressure settle back into the shape the gate was built to govern, a fraction at a time, his left hand failing to arrive at its spoke twice and arriving the third time more slowly, the delay written into the movement of the gate itself so that the gate moved in a rhythm neither of them chose. When the gate was seated again and the wheel could be pinned, she pinned it, with her own hands, fumbling once, and the pin held, and the water note beyond the gate steadied. Rask took his hands off the wheel one at a time, the right first, and he looked at the left as a man looks at a tool that has stopped answering in the expected way, and he said, in the flat tone, that the left would not be much use for the carrying, and that the tank work would have to be hers on that side. She said she understood. She asked whether he could walk. He said walking was not the hand. The stilling was still up, thin now, his spending audible in it the way a note thins when a string is overtired, and she told him to let it go in stages because the load was out and seated on the boards and the completion it had been built for had nowhere left to stand. He let it go. The weight of the lower floor came back into her all at once, the old affect, the device cooling toward ordinary metal, the fear she carried that was hers and the fear that was not, and she stood in the bay with her hands shaking from work rather than cold for once, and she looked at the load on the tank boards and at the gate pinned and quiet and at Rask holding his left hand against his coat with his right, not cradling it, just keeping it out of the work, and she made the call up the inner stair in a voice she kept deliberately ordinary, load out, gate held, lower floor secure, send Memmet down with the tank crew and no one else, and the pitch of the pumps, behind her, had begun, very slightly, to climb back toward true.

## The Silence

The tank crew had not yet come down, and in the interval Mira stood in the outer bay with the load resting on the boards and Rask seated, at her insistence and against his preference, on an upturned seal crate with his left hand kept still against his coat, and she did nothing, deliberately and completely, for the length of time it took the pumps to climb another fraction toward their true note. Stillness after work of that kind is not rest. It is the part of the work in which the evidence is allowed to assemble itself without the interference of her hands, and she had learned to protect it the way she protected the order of a read, by naming in advance what she was not going to do. She was not going to touch the load again until the crew stood ready at the tank. She was not going to question Rask about the hand until a medical officer had seen it, because any answer he gave her now would be an answer designed to keep the operation moving and she had no right to spend it. She was not going to compose the message to Leyden in her head, although the message was already composing itself in the phrases handlers prefer, because composing a message is a way of deciding what a thing meant before you have finished looking at it. She looked instead. The load sat where she had set it, inert to the eye, its anchor plate drawn, its path uncompleted, and under the returning weight of the room, as the stilling thinned and faded out of the bay, it read as almost nothing, which was the whole of the difficulty and had been from the beginning. Almost nothing, seated against a housing in a lower floor, in a station overlooked from a ridge road, built by one maker who had learned his lessons in the order the rehearsal taught them. She laid the four improvements out in her mind in their order and let each one stand without commentary. The anchor seated deep, in the place the housing could take depth. The path bent away from the warmth, at the cost of length, for the gain of quiet. The visits spaced by the work each visit had to do, shortening as the making approached its shape, four returns, the last of them barely a touch, a confirmation. The contact narrow and offset, against the guidance, where the metal was thickest and an inspection would pass over it. Any reader who had followed her this far had everything she had. That was the fairness of it, and she honoured it by not reaching past it in the silence. The pump pitch. Memmet hearing it first, at a change of shift, in the ordinary course of a careful round, and writing it in the log although she expected to be told it was the cold, and being right in the one particular that opened the case, that a working station knows its own note and a change in the note is information whatever its cause. The Binder's refusal. Venn in the interview room, right about the licence, fluent about the path, generous with everything that cost nothing, and then the flat sentence, delivered with the smoothness of rehearsal, his hands still on the edge of the table, his fear rising through wood and metal into her palms, fear with an address elsewhere, fear that was not about prison and not about this room and not about the sentence a tribunal might add to the one he already served. She could not call the sentence a lie on any evidence her practice would bear. She held that limitation in the silence alongside everything else, because a silence that quietly discards the inconvenient limits is only an argument wearing quieter clothes. Speech leaves no weight. Truth leaves less. A frightened expert is not a convicted liar, and a collapsed reading about two makers does not, by its collapsing, convict the man who profited from it. And yet the four improvements stood in their order, and the pitch stood off true behind her, climbing, and the gate stood pinned after the slam that the bent path had stored for whoever drew the anchor, and somewhere above her a station went on performing ordinariness for watchers on a ridge because she had ordered it to, and a man who had held a gate for her sat on a crate conserving a hand. The silence did not resolve these things into a charge. It resolved them into the next lawful act, which was smaller and harder to argue with. The load would go into the brine. The binding would unravel over hours, harmlessly, in cold salt water kept for seal work, until the device was a piece of metal that a station fitter could lift with one hand and a report could describe without adjectives. Venn would be asked once more, on the record, with the result known to both of them, and whatever he did with that question would be his, recorded, and left where a later reader could weigh it. She heard the tank crew on the inner stair, Memmet's voice among them, unhurried. Mira breathed once, felt the cold settle back into her shoulders where it lived, and spoke.

## Close

The lowering into the quench tank was, by design and by relief, the least dramatic piece of work done in the lower floor that day, and Mira supervised it with the attention that undramatic work deserves precisely because nobody's fear is helping them concentrate on it. The tank crew were station people, a senior fitter and two younger hands, and Memmet stood at the bay door with her log open again, recording times in her round, unembarrassed hand, as if the log were a rope the station could use to pull the afternoon back into its ordinary shape. The senior fitter inspected the boards, the tank rim, the salt tide line, the ladle on its bright hook, and pronounced the brine at level and cold, in the tone of a man reciting the conditions of his own competence. The load could not be carried on Rask's side, which rearranged the lift. Mira took that side herself. Her stiff fingers were assigned, by her own decision stated aloud so the crew would not politely rearrange it, to guidance rather than grip, the stronger fingers and the heel of the hand carrying, and the fitter, who had worked with injured men all his professional life and had the courtesy of his trade, adjusted the count so that the lift rose on his word and not hers, giving her hands one less thing to be on time for. The load went up off the boards evenly. It crossed the short distance to the tank at waist height, shedding nothing, declaring nothing, a bound thing with its anchor drawn and its completion denied, held in the interval between making and unmaking by nothing more mystical than brine and procedure. At the rim they paused on the count, because the fitter always paused at the rim, he said, so that a load went into cold water as a decision and not as a drop. Then it went down, slowly, on the tank tackle, into the brine, and the brine took it with a small, dense sound and a rising of cold vapour that smelled of salt and of the mineral cleanliness of the station's working water, and the surface closed over the housing contact, over the narrow offset seating, over the bent path and the drawn plate, and the thing that four visits had built went out of sight into ordinary station equipment kept for pump seal work. Nothing happened. That was the success, and Mira made the crew stay and witness the nothing for the full count the procedure required, because a binding unravelling harmlessly over hours announces itself mostly by continuing not to happen, by the absence of completion, by pitch and temperature and the readings of the room settling, minute by minute, toward the unremarkable. She read the bay once, lightly, with a hand on the tank rim, and what came back was cold brine and old stone and the honest tiredness of the people standing in it, and underneath, fading as a footprint fades in a corridor after rain, the last of the near nothing where the binding had been, losing its edges, becoming indistinguishable from the general quiet of a place where something had been prevented rather than done. The pumps completed the sentence the afternoon had been trying to finish since Memmet's shift change. Their pitch climbed the final fraction and settled, true, unremarkable, the note the station made when it was governing its water as it had governed it every working day of Memmet's service, and the senior fitter, without being asked and without looking up from the tank, said that was them back, in the tone of a man confirming weather. Memmet wrote it in the log with the time. Her log, which began the case with a note nobody above her had wanted to act on, now closed its account with the same note reversed, entered in the same hand, and the vindication, when it came, was entirely procedural and therefore entirely real: the commander countersigned the entry before he left the bay, in her presence, without qualifying language. The station reopened by stages that were designed, like the refusal to evacuate, to tell the ridge road nothing. The lower floor resumed its watches. The yard traffic, which had performed ordinariness all afternoon, was permitted to become ordinary in fact, gates and stamps and the evening relief arriving across the yard at the usual hour with the usual complaints about the wind off the border hills. Rask went up the inner stair under his own power, refusing the fitter's arm with a courtesy that cost him visible effort, to where the station medical officer kept a room off the upper corridor, and Mira did not follow him, because the remaining business of the case was hers and it was waiting in the interview room with a guard outside its door and cold water in its cup.

Venn had been told, as custody required, that the device was inert and the station held. He received the news, when she entered, with the composed nod of an expert whose advice has been taken, and he arranged his expression around a satisfaction that would have been convincing if she had not already carried his fear once that day and learned its shape. She sat in the less comfortable chair. She put the record between them in the formal sense, the notebook open, the custody witness at the door where the witness stood for all statements taken under the Accord's terms, and she told him, plainly and in order, what had been done in the lower floor: the anchor drawn under stilling, the load lowered into the station quench tank, the binding unravelling in brine, the station intact, the pumps at their true pitch, no person harmed in the device itself, and she watched him receive each item with small, correct acknowledgements, the nods of a man ticking a procedure he could have recited himself. Then she asked him once more. She asked it in the same words she had used before, with the licence removed, because a question repeated in new words invites a man to answer the wording, and she wanted him answering the question. Could a binding be hidden from a Weigher's read. Could it be done at all. His hands began their circuit as she had known they would, the cup lifted and set down in a place fractionally different from its ring, the licence token turned on the table, the fingers laid along the polished edge and held there, and she put her own hand, openly, on the table's edge on her side, not reaching, not performing, simply present in the way her practice required an anchor to be present, and the fear came through the wood again, immediate and specific and undisguised by the composure he was maintaining everywhere above the level of his hands. It was not fear of prison. It had the same address as before, elsewhere, in a direction he kept his eyes away from, and it rose at the question and not at the news, at the prospect of the sentence and not at the prospect of any consequence this room could offer him, and she took it and carried it and felt her own cold deepen around it like water closing over a stone. He said that hiding a binding is impossible. He said it badly this time. The rehearsal that had carried the sentence in the morning had been spent somewhere in the hours between, or the knowledge of the brine and the tank and the drawn anchor had taken the smoothness out of it, because the words arrived with a catch before them and a haste after them, the haste of a man closing a door he can hear opening behind him, and he added, unasked, a sentence about the guidance, about the rules, about what licensed practice contemplated, piling the true thing on top of the false one as if enough truth, stacked carefully, could be made to weigh the same as honesty. She let him finish. She wrote the answer in the notebook in his words, both sentences, the flat one and the piled one, and she read them back to him as the record required, and he confirmed them with a nod that his hands did not participate in. There was no triumph in it anywhere. She had a prevented device, a held station, a partner upstairs with a medical officer, and an expert whose fear she could read and whose lie she could not prove, and the case, which the arc table had assigned as a sabotage prevention closing with the device made inert, closed exactly there, on its outcome, full success, with its cost itemised elsewhere and its central human fact sitting unprovable in a notebook, in the handwriting of the only witness whose evidence on the point was, by the rules of her own practice, inadmissible even to herself. She thanked him. The guard signed the custody transfer. Venn was returned to the cell the station kept for detained persons in transit, with a note placed on his file that evening, in her wording, checked by Rask from a chair in the medical room with the pen in his right hand: the expert had been asked twice, the expert had answered twice, the Office could not yet read the answer. Dunmore, reached by telephone at the Carrowmere desk before the light went, received the report in her usual order, outcome first, cost second, questions last, and she repeated the cost back without softening it and said the Office would carry it on the case record in full. Leyden, reached after her, said only that a prevention nobody outside the ridge road would ever hear of was the best kind the Ninth Office was ever offered, and that she should sleep before she wrote the ledger. She said she would write the ledger first. It was the truest thing she said all day, and she could tell, from the pause on the line, that he could not tell whether it was true, which was, she reflected, putting the telephone down in the cooling office while the true pitch of the pumps held steady under the floor, the ordinary condition of everyone who had ever had to take her word for anything.

## The Notch

The ledger took her longer than it should have, because she wrote it at the station office desk with her left hand protesting at the pen and the cold of three reads settled so deep in her shoulders that the stove in the corner seemed to be warming a room she was only visiting. She wrote the case as the Office required it written, prevention, device inert, station held, and she wrote the spine entry with the care she had been taught for entries that move a long matter by exactly one step and no further. One notch only. A Binder of thirty years, licensed for all of them, a man who had helped write the guidance his profession worked under, had been asked on the record, twice, whether a binding could be hidden, and had said flatly that hiding a binding is impossible, and had been lying, and the lie had cost him something. She wrote the cost without naming it, because she did not know its name. She knew its evidence. She had watched him ask, at the end, after the custody transfer had been signed and the interview was formally over and nothing he said could be entered as testimony, whether his statement would be shown to anyone outside the Office. He had asked it casually, as a detained man asks about the handling of paperwork, and he had watched her hands while he asked it, as if the answer might be written there before she spoke it. She had not answered. Not from cruelty, and not from tactics, although both were available to her in the moment and she declined them in turn. She had not answered because any answer she gave him, yes or no or the Office formula about records, would have told him something about who he was afraid of, and she was not willing to trade information with a fear she could not identify, in either direction, at the end of a day in which information had been the whole currency. He had received the silence as a man receives a verdict, with a small, slow nod, as if she had confirmed the worse of two expectations, and the guard had taken him out past the desk where Rask would later sit, and the corridor had absorbed him in the ordinary direction of cells and locked doors. The question she carried forward out of the case was exactly one, and she wrote it in the ledger in the form the ledger demanded, as a question and not as a suspicion dressed in a question's clothing. Venn is afraid of something that is not the Accord. Everything else the case had opened, the Office had closed inside the work: the maker and his learning, the device and its unmaking, the station and its vindication, the gate and its holding. That one remained, and it would remain, she understood, until the fear found its address in some later room, on some later floor, in the presence of some later device whose binding left almost no weight where it sat. She closed the ledger. Upstairs, the medical officer had finished with the formalities that precede a transfer, and the last business of the evening, which was nobody's business on any form, was the walking of a detained expert past the medical room door at the hour when the station moved its prisoners to the night cells, a matter of corridors and timing that no one had arranged and everyone afterward would remember as if it had been arranged. Mira stood in the doorway with Rask beside her, come to say, in person and standing, that the report had his name spelled correctly in it, which was his whole contribution to the evening's paperwork and his whole refusal of the chair they kept offering him. The guard brought Venn along the corridor at the pace custody sets. Rask's hand was in a splint.

Venn in the interview room doorway, brought there a final time because the night cells were reached through the room where his statement had been taken and the station had no kinder geography to offer any of them, looked neither at the guard nor at the officer who had twice asked him the question he could not afford to answer with the truth. He looked at Rask's hand in its splint, held still against Rask's coat where he kept it out of the work, and he went on looking at the splint rather than at Mira as the guard moved him on, as if the price of the day, itemised at last in a form a Binder could read, were the only honest statement left in the building, and as if reading it were safer, by far, than meeting the eyes of the woman who had already read him.
## Case File

### Q: How is a Dravin border station staffed across a change of shift?

Station staffing in Dravin follows a fixed overlap in which the outgoing guard detail remains on the floor until the incoming detail has walked the full circuit and signed for each post in turn. The lower floor, the outer bay and the ridge approach are signed separately, so that no single signature covers ground the signer has not seen. A guard who has reported an irregularity during a shift stays available for questioning until the report has been copied into the station book and read back. Relief is not complete while any post remains unsigned, and an unsigned post is treated as unheld for record purposes. The book records each signature with the hour, and a late signature is marked as late rather than entered as if made in sequence.

### Q: Who may close a Dravin border station, and on what showing must the closure rest?

Closure of a border station rests with the station master acting on a written showing, countersigned where an Accord interest is engaged. The showing must name the hazard, the part of the station affected and the expected duration, and it must state what traffic will be turned back and where that traffic will be held. A verbal closure has no standing beyond the hour in which it is given. Where closure would strand bonded goods between posts, the master must also record the custody arrangement for those goods. Reopening requires a fresh entry stating that the ground named in the showing has been inspected. A closure entered without the required showing is treated as a local precaution only, and it confers no authority to hold goods or persons.

### Q: How are watchers observed on the ridge road above a station entered in the station record?

External observation of a station is recorded as a station fact, not as an accusation against any person seen on the road. The entry notes the position held, the duration of the presence, whether the party moved with passing traffic or remained after traffic had cleared, and whether the same position was taken again on a later shift. No entry may name a watcher unless identity has been established by a separate means. Entries of this kind are copied to the desk that tasks field officers, because a pattern of observation changes what a visible response at the station would disclose. Copies are compared at the desk for repetition of position and hour, since a single presence on a public road proves nothing by itself.

### Q: What standing does a station book hold when a guard report entered in it is later doubted?

A station book entry made at the time of the event carries the standing of a contemporaneous record, and a later doubt does not reduce that standing by itself. The officer who made the entry may be asked to speak to it, but the entry is not rewritten to match later recollection. If a subsequent inspection supports the entry, the book is marked as vindicated on that point and the doubting memorandum is retained alongside it. If inspection does not support the entry, the correction is appended as a separate entry. Erasure is not permitted in either case. A vindicated entry restores the maker to full credit for later entries, and the doubt is not carried forward as a general caution against the name.

### Q: Does a Binder licence extend to work on Accord infrastructure, or is it confined to private commissions?

The licence is confined to commissions that a private holder may lawfully offer, unless a separate infrastructure warrant has been issued for named work at a named place. Accord pumps, gates, locks and bonded stores are infrastructure for this purpose. A Binder who accepts private payment for work touching such fabric acts outside the licence even where the technique used was ordinary and the result harmless. The warrant, where granted, is read narrowly. It covers the structure named and no other, and it lapses when the named work is signed off or abandoned. A warrant obtained after the work has begun does not cure the defect, and the work remains outside the licence for inspection and payment purposes.

### Q: May a licensed Binder refuse a commission, and must a refusal be entered anywhere?

A Binder may refuse any commission without giving a reason to the person offering it, and refusal alone is no offence and no ground for review of the licence. A refusal must be entered, however, where the commission was offered through an Accord desk or where the Binder had already inspected the object and formed a view of its condition. The entry records the offer, the inspection if any, and the fact of refusal. It does not record the reason unless the Binder chooses to state one. The rule protects the client from quiet shopping after an adverse inspection. An unentered refusal after inspection may be treated as concealment where a later Binder accepts the same commission and a fault is then found in the object.

### Q: What must a Binder show before sealing a load that is to cross a border between members?

Before sealing a load for cross border carriage, the Binder must show the load manifest, the seating prepared for the anchor, and the name of the holder who will answer for the load at receipt. The seal is then applied in the presence of a witness who signs for the condition of the seating, not for the soundness of the binding itself. A seal applied to an unshown load is void for Accord purposes, and carriage under it is treated as unsealed carriage. The showing is recorded at the station of departure and copied to the station of receipt. The receipt station may refuse the load where the copy of the showing has not arrived, and refusal on that ground is not a finding against the Binder.

### Q: How does a Binder licence lapse through non use, and how does that differ from suspension?

Lapse through non use follows a continuous period in which the Binder has taken no commission, given no supervised instruction and submitted no work for inspection. Lapse is administrative. It restores nothing and condemns nothing, and return to practice requires a fresh showing of competence before the licensing desk. Suspension is different in kind. It follows a finding, it is entered with reasons, and it bars practice until the finding is lifted or expires. A lapsed Binder who works is unlicensed. A suspended Binder who works has also defied a finding, and that defiance is recorded separately. During lapse the Binder may witness the work of others but may not sign for it, supervise it formally, or hold out a token as if current.

### Q: Where is a convicted practitioner held, and how do the custody terms differ from those for ordinary prisoners?

A convicted practitioner is held in a designated custody wing rather than in the general prison population, under terms set by the sentencing court and administered by the Accord. The separation is not a privilege. It prevents unsupervised contact in which practice could be taught, commissioned or performed through another prisoner, and it protects the practitioner from being pressed for work that no order has authorised. Visits are logged by name and purpose. Correspondence is held for inspection where the sentence so provides. The wing register is a custody document and is produced to oversight on demand. The wing register also records refusals of food, medicine or visits, since welfare and security are administered together and neither account is complete without the other.

### Q: What practice, if any, may a convicted practitioner perform while in custody?

The default position is none. A convicted practitioner performs no binding, carrying or stilling work for any person, including custody staff, and a request made informally has no authority. A narrow exception exists for supervised demonstration required by a court or by an investigation, where the work is described in advance, witnessed throughout, and undone or secured at the close. Demonstration under this exception confers no payment, no credit against sentence and no restoration of licence. Any object handled is listed before and after, and the supervising officer signs for its condition on return. An application for a demonstration that does not describe the work in advance is returned without consideration, and the return is entered on the custody file.

### Q: How is the custody of a convicted practitioner reviewed, and who must sign the review?

Custody is reviewed at fixed intervals by a panel that includes a custody officer, a licensing representative and a member with no prior involvement in the case. The review considers conduct, health, any approach made to the practitioner by outside parties, and whether the original reasons for separation still hold. It does not rehear the conviction. The prisoner may submit a written statement, which is attached unread in substance to the review record, though its length and receipt are noted. No review takes effect until all three members have signed, and a dissent is recorded rather than resolved by majority note. A review that finds the separation no longer justified must still state where the practitioner will be held instead, and transfer does not follow from the finding alone.

### Q: What is taken from a convicted practitioner at intake, and how is each item accounted for?

At intake the practitioner surrenders the licence token, any seals or dies held in the licence name, prepared anchors in personal possession, and any commission book covering work not yet inspected. Each item is listed, described and sealed in the presence of the practitioner, who signs the list as an account of what was taken rather than as agreement with the sentence. Items that are ordinary personal property are dealt with under general prison rules and kept apart from practice material. Nothing surrendered at intake is destroyed during the sentence. Return, retention or forfeiture is decided at release by separate order. A practitioner who disputes the list signs the dispute beside the item concerned, and the sealed item is retained unopened until the dispute has been resolved.

### Q: Why does a change in pump pitch matter at a border station, and how is it entered in the pump log?

Pitch is treated as a working indication of load, flow and wear in the pump train, and a sustained change from the established note for a given setting requires an entry even where pressure readings remain within limits. The entry records the setting, the hour the change was first noticed, who noticed it, and whether the change held after the pumps were rested and restarted. A pitch report is not a finding of damage. It is a trigger for inspection, and failure to enter a noticed change is a log offence separate from any fault later found in the machinery itself. A pitch change that clears when a single pump is isolated is entered against that pump, and the train as a whole is not condemned on the report.

### Q: What is a station quench tank for, and why is cold brine the medium kept in it?

The quench tank is ordinary station equipment kept for pump seal work, in which heated or stressed components are lowered to cool without shock or distortion. Cold brine is kept because it holds a steady low temperature, resists sudden warming when a hot component is entered, and is already stocked for seal maintenance. The tank, its position and its state of readiness are part of the station inventory and are checked on the inspection round. Use of the tank for any other purpose must be entered in the station book, with the time of entry and the name of the officer who authorised the use. Brine drawn off for any reason is replaced and the replacement entered, because a tank found short at need is treated as an inventory failure.

### Q: How does the holding wheel of a service gate fail in service, and what renewal duty applies to its counterweight chain?

The service gate between the pump hall and the outer bay is held against water pressure by its wheel while any person passes, because the counterweight that should assist the gate cannot be trusted once its chain has worn. Wear shows first as uneven travel and delayed take up, then as a gate that creeps when left held. The chain is subject to a renewal duty at a fixed interval or on a recorded finding of wear, whichever comes first. Until renewal is signed off, the gate is worked as an unassisted gate, the wheel is staffed throughout any passage, and that staffing is entered in the log. A gate log that omits the staffing entry is incomplete even where no harm followed, and the omission is charged to the officer responsible for the passage.

### Q: How are pump logs and shift books compared after an incident at a station?

The comparison is made entry by entry against the clock, with each book read by an officer who did not write it. Pump entries establish what the machinery was doing. Shift entries establish who was present, which posts were held and what was reported at the time. A gap in one book that is covered by the other is noted as a gap, not filled from it. Where the books disagree on an hour or a sequence, both versions stand and the disagreement is referred upward with the books themselves. No officer who served during the incident may certify the comparison as complete. The comparison certificate names the officers who read the books and the hours at which the reading was done, and it is filed with both books.

### Q: How is the evidence of an expert held in custody weighed when it cannot be corroborated?

Uncorroborated evidence from a custodial expert is admitted to the file but given no operative weight until a second source or a physical check supports the part on which a decision would turn. The expert standing of the witness does not supply the missing support, and neither does long experience in the trade. Officers are directed to separate what the expert knows as technique from what the expert asserts as fact about the case in hand. Technique may guide an inspection. Assertion may not replace one. The file must show, for any step taken, which corroboration was obtained and when. Where corroboration supports only part of an assertion, only that part acquires weight, and the remainder stays on file under the same caution as before.

### Q: What limits govern showing a custodial expert material from a live case?

Material is shown only under a written direction that names the items, the purpose of the showing and the questions the expert is being asked to address. The expert sees no wider file, hears no account of other evidence, and is not told what answer the Office expects. A supervising officer is present throughout, and the showing is recorded item by item. Anything the expert handles is listed before and after. Comment volunteered beyond the questions asked is recorded in full and marked as volunteered, because volunteered comment from custody may be offered to shape the case rather than to assist it. A record of the showing is placed on the custody file as well as the case file, so the expert history of contact with evidence remains complete.

### Q: What entry is made when a custodial expert is believed to have lied, but the lie cannot yet be proved?

The entry is one of unresolved concern, stated in neutral terms, and it records the exact words used, the question that prompted them, and the ground for concern held at the time. It does not record a finding of lying, because no finding has been made. The entry is placed on the custody file and cross referenced on the case file, so that later readers see both the statement and the caution together. Access to the entry is limited to officers with a stated need. If proof later emerges, a further entry is added. The original entry is not amended to read as if proof had existed earlier. The ground for concern must be a stated ground, such as a conflict with a physical check, and unease about manner or attitude is not sufficient.

### Q: Who decides whether a custodial expert statement may be shown outside the Office, and on what terms?

Disclosure outside the Office is decided by the controller responsible for the case together with the custody authority, and neither may authorise it alone. The decision considers whether disclosure would expose a live method, prejudice a proceeding, or place the expert at risk from parties whose interest in the statement is not known. Where disclosure is authorised, it is of the exact words recorded, with the custody status of the speaker stated plainly alongside them. A summary prepared for outside readers has no standing as the statement. Refusal to answer a disclosure request is itself recorded, with the date of the request. Where the two authorities disagree, disclosure does not proceed, the disagreement is recorded, and the requesting party is told only that disclosure has not been authorised.
