Stephen Greenblatt never wrote the famous new historicist reading of Romeo and Juliet, and that fact, properly understood, is the most instructive thing about the method he founded. New historicism, the approach Greenblatt named in 1982 and practised across Renaissance Self-Fashioning (1980) and Shakespearean Negotiations (1988), treats a play as one move inside a culture's continuous traffic of power, ritual, fear, and desire. Applied to the lovers of Verona, the method refuses the familiar picture of a love story floating free of its moment and asks harder questions instead. What civic machinery does the feud serve, and whose authority does its violence feed? What did a secret marriage mean in a society where marriage was property law, sacrament, and police matter at once? Why does the catastrophe arrive not by fate but through a plague order, a sealed door, and an undelivered letter? The verdict argued through this article is measured. New historicism reveals the play's public skeleton, the systems of princely authority, household discipline, imported honour, and epidemic administration inside which the private passion has to move, and it explains features of the text that purely literary readings leave as decoration or accident. Its price is real: in careless hands the method flattens the poem into a document among documents and trades the play's verbal life for a neighbouring anecdote. Disciplined by the documentary habits of editors, the method earns its place. Undisciplined, it becomes the flattening its critics describe.

One distinction governs everything that follows. The school is the subject here, not any single essay about the play, because the school never produced a canonical essay about the play from its founder's hand. Greenblatt's direct encounters with Romeo and Juliet are an appreciative introduction written for The Norton Shakespeare and a biographical use of the play inside Will in the World (2004), where the tragedy is mined as evidence about a marriage rather than read as an object in its culture. Everything else called a new historicist reading of the play is the method at work in other scholars' hands, or the method extended with care. That is not an embarrassment to be hidden. It is the precise shape of the evidence, and an account of Greenblatt and this play that inflates a landmark the record does not contain would fail the first test the method itself sets: stay answerable to the documents.

## What Is New Historicism, and What Does Greenblatt's Method Claim?

New historicism begins from a refusal. The older literary history of the mid twentieth century, associated with E. M. W. Tillyard and his picture of an Elizabethan world order, treated history as a settled backdrop: the critic first reconstructed the period's beliefs from sermons, chronicles, and homilies, then set the play in front of that painted scenery and read its meaning off the contrast. Greenblatt's generation objected that the backdrop model made history inert and the play a mere illustration of doctrines already known from other sources. Against it, the new historicists proposed that text and context make each other. A play is not a reflection of its culture standing outside it. A play is a participant in the culture, drawing materials from courts, churches, streets, and sickrooms, reworking them under the pressure of performance, and returning them to circulation altered. Greenblatt gave the practice its durable metaphors in the opening chapter of Shakespearean Negotiations, "The Circulation of Social Energy." Social energy, in his paraphrase-resistant formulation, names the charge carried by certain words, gestures, rituals, and images as they pass between the theatre and the other institutions of a society: the capacity of a staged exorcism to borrow terror from a real one, of a stage prince's edict to borrow weight from a real proclamation. The theatre, on this account, is an exchanger inside the culture's economy of representations, negotiating for materials, transforming them, and paying some of them back with interest.

### Why will new historicism not let history sit in the background?

Because a background, by definition, cannot act. The Tillyard model assigned history the role of scenery, fixed before the play began and unchanged by it, so every reading became an exercise in matching the drama to doctrines assembled elsewhere. History as scenery can explain nothing that the play does.

New historicism insists that the traffic runs in both directions at once: the sermon shapes the soliloquy, and the soliloquy, performed nightly before thousands, reshapes what the sermon can mean to its hearers. Treating history as an active medium rather than a painted flat is the method's founding gesture, and every later feature of the school follows from it.

The method's other instruments follow from that founding gesture. The anecdote, a small reported incident drawn from a diary, a court record, a traveller's tale, becomes a privileged form of evidence, because the anecdote resists the smoothing of grand narrative and lets a whole network of assumptions flare up inside a single scene. Self-fashioning, the title concept of Greenblatt's 1980 book, names the period's newly intense project of constructing an identity, always, he argued, in relation to an authority and an alien other that the self both needs and opposes. Subversion and containment, the paired terms of the essay "Invisible Bullets," pose the school's most debated question: when a play stages disorder, does it release pressure against the ruling order, or does authority produce and license that disorder the better to absorb it? And the exchange model of the theatre insists that plays are made out of negotiated transfers: a chronicle's narrative, a ritual's gestures, a scandal's notoriety, converted into stage capital by a commercial company that had to please a paying crowd. Greenblatt himself resisted the idea that any of this amounted to a doctrine. In the introduction to The Power of Forms in the English Renaissance (1982), the volume where the phrase new historicism first appeared, and again in later prefaces, he presented the approach as a practice rather than a system, a set of questions brought to the archive rather than a machine for producing identical readings. That modesty matters for the verdict reached below, because the method's strengths and its characteristic failures both grow from the same root: it is a way of hunting, not a way of concluding.

The method also relocates the author, and the relocation matters for a play so often read as its author's purest lyric overflow. In the negotiation model the playwright is neither the solitary genius of the formalist account nor the passive register of his age in the old historicist one. He is a tradesman inside the exchange, and in this case an unusually literal one: a sharer in his playing company, entitled to a portion of its takings, writing for fellows whose strengths he knew, in venues whose licences he depended on, and by the end of the decade a part-owner of the Globe playhouse itself. The plays that came out of that position are, among other things, the products of a man with equity in the means of production, and the new historicist finds that fact legible in the work's habits: its canny distribution of great parts across the company's strengths, its alternation of crowd-pleasing spectacle with the verbal fireworks that built reputations, its instinct for materials already warm in the culture. Romeo and Juliet, with its balanced double plot of street and orchard, its feast, its fights, its friar, its nurse, reads like the work of a shareholder who knew exactly what his house could sell and what his moment was hungry to feel. Genius, in this account, is not denied. It is given a business address, and the address explains features of the masterpiece that reverence leaves as mysteries.

The quarrel with formalism defines the other boundary. The New Criticism that dominated Anglo-American reading at mid century treated the poem as a self-sufficient verbal object whose meaning could be recovered by close attention to paradox, irony, and image, with history admitted only as a footnote. New historicism accepts the closeness and rejects the sufficiency. For Greenblatt's school, the very features the formalist prizes, the pun that turns a compliment into a threat, the ritual speech that misfires, are charged because they circulate. A formal pattern inside the play points outward to the ceremonies, legal formulas, and street codes that taught an audience how to feel it. Harry Levin's great formal reading of the play, which counts its sonnets, aubades, and epitaph machinery as a closed system of form, stands in this series as the method's opposite pole, and the two readings will be set against each other when the verdict is weighed: Levin explains how the play is built, and the new historicist asks what the building was for, in that city, in that decade.

## The World Picture It Replaced: Old Historicism and the Background Trade

To feel the force of the new school, the old one has to be given its due first, because the old historicism was not foolishness. Tillyard's The Elizabethan World Picture (1943) and his companion study of the history plays offered readers a coherent account of the period's official cosmology: a universe of degree and correspondence, in which order in the state mirrored order in the heavens and disorder anywhere threatened order everywhere. Lily B. Campbell's Shakespeare's Histories (1947) read the chronicle plays as mirrors of Elizabethan political doctrine, patiently recovered from Tudor homilies, chronicles, and treatises. The school's archive was real and its recovery work permanent. The homily against disobedience and rebellion was read in churches; the chain of being was a commonplace of educated speech; a critic who ignores such materials will misread the period's vocabulary of order, and the Prince's speeches in this play draw on that vocabulary in every scene he enters. Greenblatt's generation did not discard the archive. It discarded the architecture.

### What did the old historicism get right that its replacement kept?

It kept the period's own words. The old school established that sermons, homilies, chronicles, and conduct books are indispensable evidence for what a play's language meant to its first hearers, and every contextual reading of this play still proceeds on the documents those scholars assembled.

No new historicist account of the edicts, the marriage doctrine, or the funeral rites can proceed without that archive. What the replacement rejected was never the archive. It was the assumption that the archive spoke with one voice and that the play's duty was to repeat it.

The flaws were structural. A world picture assembled from official texts mistakes the loudest voices for the whole culture, since homilies were instruments for producing the obedience they described, not neutral reports of a consensus already achieved. Worse, the model froze the picture: beliefs recovered from treatises were treated as a fixed mental furniture that every playgoer carried into the theatre, so the drama could only illustrate, never interrogate, the doctrine. Applied to this play, old historicism produced a familiar reading, in which the lovers figure as disorder punished and the Prince's closing judgment as the world picture reasserting itself, the feud a breach in degree healed by sacrifice. The reading is not false, and the new school retains its skeleton. What the new school adds is the question the old one could not ask: whose interests the picture served, what it cost to maintain, and what the play's audience, which included servants, apprentices, and the unpropertied young, might have felt while the doctrine was being pronounced over two bodies their own age. The homily against rebellion was preached because rebellion was imaginable. The world picture was painted because the wall kept cracking. A method that reads the official text as an anxious performance rather than a settled fact can hear, in the Prince's magnificent final speech, both the doctrine and the strain of pronouncing it.

The institutional contrast mattered too. Old historicism was a scholarship of the library carrel, its ideal product a monograph establishing the background once and for all. New historicism arrived with the manners of a different academy: collaborative, journal-centred, essayistic, frankly interested in the present arrangements of power that made some questions askable and others invisible. Its founding documents are programmatic essays rather than summae, and its characteristic unit is the article that sets a play beside an unexpected document and watches both change colour. That essayistic scale is the method's glory and its vulnerability, since an essay can be brilliant about a juxtaposition it never has to defend at monograph length. The verdict on the play will keep returning to that asymmetry: the old school built foundations it mistook for the building, and the new school builds brilliant rooms that sometimes float a few inches above the ground the editors surveyed.

## Did Stephen Greenblatt Ever Write a New Historicist Reading of Romeo and Juliet?

The honest answer is no, and the inventory is short enough to give in full. Shakespearean Negotiations, the 1988 book that made the method famous, contains five chapters: the programmatic account of social energy, "Invisible Bullets" on the Henry plays and Thomas Harriot's report from Virginia, "Fiction and Friction" on Twelfth Night and cross-dressing, "Shakespeare and the Exorcists" on King Lear and Samuel Harsnett's attack on fraudulent exorcisms, and "Martial Law in the Land of Cockaigne." Romeo and Juliet is not among its subjects. Renaissance Self-Fashioning (1980) closes with Othello, in the chapter on the improvisation of power, and gives its other chapters to More, Tyndale, Wyatt, Spenser, and Marlowe. The essays collected in Learning to Curse (1990) circle the same territory of culture and power without settling on the lovers. That volume also holds his most compact methodological statement, "Towards a Poetics of Culture," where the exchange model receives its clearest formulation: works of art are made inside a network of trades between the theatre and the other social institutions of their moment, and the critic's task is to reconstruct the trades, neither dissolving the artwork into them nor sealing it against them. The essay matters for this play because it supplies the standard by which the applications are judged below. A poetics of culture, honestly practised, must name the trades it alleges, and the trades alleged for Verona, plague administration, marriage settlement, retaining, the fencing fashion, the suppressed shrine, are exactly the ones for which documents survive in quantity. A reader who goes to Greenblatt's shelf looking for the great set-piece reading of this play will find the shelf arranged around it, rich on the histories, the late comedies, and Lear, and silent here.

What Greenblatt did write about the play falls into two kinds, neither of them a new historicist reading. The first is his introduction to Romeo and Juliet in The Norton Shakespeare, the edition he general-edited, where the play receives a few pages of the appreciative attention an editor owes a text he is presenting. The Norton introduction is, strikingly, a lover's document rather than a theorist's. Greenblatt praises the orchard scene for language so intense it seems to remake the world for its speakers, and he closes on the play's afterlife, observing that audiences across four centuries have in effect granted Juliet's wish, cutting Romeo out in little stars and setting him in the face of heaven. The argumentative energy of the piece goes to the poetry and to the audience's enduring surrender, not to plague orders, retaining statutes, or the marriage market. The second kind is biographical. In Will in the World, the chapter "Wooing, Wedding, and Repenting" recruits the plays, Romeo and Juliet among them, as evidence in the mystery of the playwright's own hurried marriage to Anne Hathaway, asking what the tragedy's vision of clandestine desire might confess about its author's experience. Whatever the merits of that speculation, and its critics have been severe, its direction is the reverse of the method's: the play is treated as a trace of a life, not as a negotiation within a culture.

The consequence for any serious account is clarifying. When teachers and survey courses speak of the new historicist Romeo and Juliet, they mean a reading assembled from the method's tools by many hands: historically minded scholars who took the feud into the records of civic violence, the marriage into the law of consent, the letter into the plague administration of the 1590s. The reading is real, but its authorship is collective, and its quality varies with the discipline of the hand applying it. Greenblatt supplied the questions, the vocabulary, and the prestige. He also, in his own practice on other plays, supplied the standard the application must meet, since his best chapters earn their juxtapositions in the archive: Harriot's report really was written for the Raleigh circle that also patronised the stage, and Harsnett's exorcism pamphlet really does share language with Lear's mad speeches. Where a reading of this play can meet that standard of documented contact, it deserves the school's name. Where it trades on atmosphere and resemblance, it deserves the critics' charge, examined below, that the method flatters ingenuity at the expense of proof. Keeping that standard in view is what separates the argument of this article from the inflation the record forbids: the landmark is the method, and the play is its test case, not its trophy.

A small emblem of the relationship sits in the front matter of Shakespearean Negotiations itself. In his note on texts, Greenblatt records that his Shakespeare quotations throughout the book are taken from The Riverside Shakespeare, edited by G. Blakemore Evans. The theorist of circulation built his arguments on an editor's disciplined text, and Evans also edited Romeo and Juliet for the New Cambridge series, an edition whose introduction models the older virtues: date, sources, stage history, and textual descent, each claim tied to a document a reader can check. The emblem will matter when the disagreement is adjudicated at the end of this article, because it shows the two practices were never in practice separable. The boldest contextual reading still stands on editorial ground, and the most austere edition still chooses which contexts to admit. The question is never whether a reading will use history, but whether its history can survive being checked.

## A School, Not a Solo: The Berkeley Circle and the Method's Spread

The singular name in this article's title is itself a small distortion the record corrects. New historicism was from the start a collective enterprise with a mailing address: the Berkeley English department and the journal Representations, founded in 1983 as the school's house organ, with Greenblatt among its founding editors. Its most quoted formula belongs not to Greenblatt but to Louis Montrose, who named the practice's double commitment as a reciprocal concern with the historicity of texts and the textuality of history: texts are made by their moment, and the moment itself reaches later ages only through texts, each side of the formula correcting the other's naivety. Catherine Gallagher brought the method to the novel and, with Greenblatt, to its most reflective self-account in Practicing New Historicism. Jean E. Howard's early survey of the movement, published in 1986 while the school was still young enough to need one, already identified its signature moves and its characteristic risk, the leap from a resonant document to a totalised culture. A school with a journal, a formula, and friendly internal critics within five years of its naming is not a man's opinion. It is an institution, and its readings of plays are institutional products, which is one reason a reading of this play exists in the culture without existing in the founder's bibliography: the institution produced it, distributed across essays, lectures, and classrooms, in the way institutions produce most of their real effects.

The institutional history also explains the method's classroom triumph, which is part of its claim on this play. The Norton Shakespeare, with Greenblatt as general editor, placed the plays inside a apparatus of contexts, documents, and historical introductions that carried the school's assumptions into every undergraduate syllabus in the English-speaking world, including the assumption that a tragedy of young love should be introduced with attention to the world that regulated marriage, policed the streets, and closed the theatres in plague time. Generations of students met Romeo and Juliet through that apparatus before they met any single new historicist essay, and the introduction Greenblatt wrote for the play, with its surrender to the poetry, sits inside an edition whose architecture is the method institutionalised. The founder's restraint at the level of the essay and his ambition at the level of the institution are the same fact seen from two sides: the method conquered by becoming the default setting, the water in which other readings swim, rather than by winning a single famous interpretation of this play. Any verdict on Greenblatt and Romeo and Juliet that misses the institutional story will mistake the absence of an essay for an absence of influence, when the influence is precisely what made the essay unnecessary.

## The Circulation of Social Energy in Verona: How the Method Turns

To watch the method turn, take the play's first minutes. The Prologue announces "two households, both alike in dignity," and the curtain rises not on the lovers but on servants trading insults in the street: Sampson boasting that he will "take the wall of any man or maid of Montague's," Gregory correcting his courage, and the two of them agreeing that "the quarrel is between our masters and us their men" (1.1). A formalist reading hears exposition. A new historicist reading hears a transaction. The scene takes the street codes of Elizabethan London, the etiquette of the wall, the thumb-biting insult, the servant's borrowed honour, and converts them into theatrical pleasure, while the playhouse itself, a commercial institution operating under licence and suspicion, converts the crowd's appetite for violence into a safe spectacle and a profitable afternoon. Energy has circulated: from the street into the fiction, from the fiction back into the audience's habits of feeling about honour, service, and rank. Greenblatt's term for the theatre's part in this traffic is negotiation, and the word is chosen with care. The company does not passively receive its culture's materials. It bargains for them, prices them, heightens some and suppresses others, and the resulting play bears the marks of the bargain in its very structure: this tragedy keeps handing its love story back to the public world that keeps interrupting it.

### Why does the anecdote carry so much weight in this method?

Because the anecdote is where the system shows its seams. Grand narratives smooth an age into a worldview, and Greenblatt's school distrusts the smoothing. A single reported incident, a diary entry about a sealed house, a fencing master's boast in a printed manual, preserves the friction between profession and practice.

In Practicing New Historicism (2000), written with Catherine Gallagher, the anecdote is defended as counterhistory: a small, stubborn fact set against the large story until the large story has to change shape around it. The method's readings stand or fall by whether the anecdote genuinely touches the play, and the friction it preserves is the friction between what a culture professes and what it does.

The strongest anecdotal contacts for this play are not exotic. They are administrative. Consider the instrument on which the whole catastrophe turns: a letter. Friar Laurence sends his plan to Mantua by Friar John, and Friar John returns with the report that he could not deliver it, because the searchers of the town, "suspecting that we both were in a house where the infectious pestilence did reign, sealed up the doors, and would not let us forth" (5.2). The speech is usually read as fate wearing a disguise. The new historicist asks what the disguise is made of, and the answer lies in records any Londoner of the mid 1590s could have supplied from experience: plague orders under which suspected houses were shut up with their inhabitants inside, watchmen set at doors, movement between towns treated as a vector of infection. The play's first audiences had lived through the plague years of 1592 to 1594, when the London theatres themselves stood closed for long stretches and the playing companies survived by touring or not at all. When Friar John describes the sealed door, the energy in the line is not decorative. The audience's own memories of quarantine, of letters that miscarried because a road was watched or a house was shut, flow into the fiction and give the accident its terrible credibility. What older criticism called chance, and moralising criticism called providence, the method redescribes as a system: the catastrophe is routed through the period's public-health machinery, and it convinces because that machinery was real, familiar, and known to fail in exactly this way.

Greenblatt gave the method its own internal check in the pair of terms he developed for the behaviour of powerful objects, wonder and resonance. Wonder names the arrest, the capacity of a singular made thing to stop a beholder in admiration or terror before any context has been assembled. Resonance names the object's answering capacity to reach beyond its frame and set the surrounding culture sounding, the documents, rituals, and memories vibrating in sympathy. A reading that pursues resonance alone produces the flattened document the critics complain of, a play dissolved into its period. A reading that rests in wonder alone produces the sealed formal object the school was founded to refuse. The discipline of the pair is the discipline of the whole method at its best: every contextual claim must survive the object's resistance, and every admiration must be made to explain the charge it feels. Applied to the present case, the orchard scene is the play's wonder, the passage where even the school's founder surrendered to the language, and the sealed house of Friar John's report is its resonance at maximum, a dozen words that set the entire plague administration of the 1590s sounding behind the fiction. The play needs both registers to be itself, and so, the verdict will argue, does the criticism that would do it justice.

The same redescription works across the play's public machinery. The Prince's interventions, three times repeated, follow the rhythm of proclamation and assize: an edict with a capital penalty, a sentence moderated after inquiry, a final judgment that distributes blame across the whole community, including the judge himself. The feast at the Capulets', where the love story ignites, is a piece of household statecraft, staged by a father who has just been bargaining with the County Paris over the terms on which his heiress may be viewed. Even the play's most inward moment is built from public materials. When Juliet, alone on the balcony she does not know is a stage, reasons "What's in a name? That which we call a rose by any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2), she is conducting a small experiment in the period's philosophy of language and law at once: names in her world are not labels but instruments, carrying inheritance, allegiance, and honour, and her wish to unmake them is the wish to step outside the system that assigns every person a house. The method's claim is that the speech's pathos depends on the audience's knowledge that the system cannot be stepped out of. A rose may be renamed in a garden. A Capulet cannot be renamed in Verona, because the name is the mechanism by which property descends, servants are fed, and old men are obeyed. Social energy is the name for that dependence of feeling on institution, and the play is saturated with it. The saturation had a material condition in the playhouse itself, a wooden amphitheatre where a penny bought standing room in the yard and sixpence bought a gallery seat, so that the fiction's whole social range, from prince to serving-man, played nightly to a room containing its own likeness at every price. When the servants open the tragedy by weighing their masters' dignity against their own, the yard heard its own idiom given the stage's first minutes, and when the Prince closes it by distributing praise and blame among the great, the galleries heard government performed in the idiom of the court. The circulation the method describes was therefore not an abstraction imposed by later critics. It was the daily experience of a mixed room watching a mixed city, each rank in the fiction finding its counterpart in the room and its feelings repriced by the exchange.

The play's rituals show the traffic at its most legible, because ritual is social energy in its stored form. Verona's ceremonies keep changing places with their opposites. The feast where the lovers meet is a marriage market dressed as a dance. The wedding the Friar performs is a treaty disguised as a sacrament. The funeral that replaces the second wedding is described by Capulet in the play's great inventory of inversion: "All things that we ordained festival turn from their office to black funeral; our instruments to melancholy bells, our wedding cheer to a sad burial feast" (4.5). A new historicist lingers over that speech because it thinks in the method's own terms. The objects stay the same, the food, the music, the flowers, the gathered household, while their charge reverses, festival energy rerouted into mourning without a single prop changing hands. The play understands, as the method does, that a culture's feelings are carried by forms that can be reloaded, and that tragedy can be built by reloading them in public. The tomb scene completes the pattern: the monument the fathers promise is the funeral form reloaded again, this time as statecraft, grief converted into civic architecture. Three times the same stock of ceremonial energy changes hands and changes sign, and the audience that had itself lived through the period's great reloadings, its churches stripped and refitted, its saints' days abolished and its maypoles defended, was trained by experience to read the conversions.

The playhouse's own position inside this economy deserves a section of the analysis, because the institution performing the feud was itself built on the feud's model. Under the statute of 1572, players without a noble patron were classed in law with rogues and vagabonds, liable to whipping; the companies survived by wearing a great household's livery and name, the Chamberlain's Men, the Admiral's Men, licensed servants of a lord whose protection converted their vagrancy into service. The men who first acted Sampson and Gregory's boasts about serving a good master were themselves liveried servants performing service, in a playhouse their patron's name shielded from the magistrates who periodically tried to close it. New historicism savours such recursions, and for once the savouring is earned, because the recursion is documentary rather than decorative: the same legal machinery that made the feud's retinues a problem for the Tudor state made the theatre's retinue a solution for the players, and the play's opening meditation on what servants owe their masters, and what masters owe the peace, was spoken by men whose livelihood depended on the audience's answer. When Sampson insists on the dignity of his service and Gregory corrects his courage, the joke runs on the performers' own contracts. The energy in the scene is the company's own, spent and replenished nightly.

## The Feud as Civic Ritual: What the Brawls Do for Verona

A new historicist reading of the feud starts by declining to treat it as a given, a piece of Italian colour inherited from the sources. The question the method asks is functional: what does this violence do, for the houses that practise it, for the city that tolerates it, and for the ruler who three times fails to end it? The play gives the feud no origin, no stated cause, no first injury to avenge. Its emptiness is the point. The feud persists because it organises loyalty: servants who have nothing else to be proud of can be proud of the quarrel, as Sampson and Gregory demonstrate before their masters appear, and young men with no estate to defend can defend the house's name as a substitute estate. Readings of the play that concentrate on its household politics, such as the companion study of [two households and the struggle for power and class standing in Verona](/2022/04/22/two-households-class-power-verona/), document how completely the feud structures rank and service in the city. The new historicist frame adds the circulation: this economy of honour was not Verona's alone. Elizabethan London legislated repeatedly against the liveried retainers through whom great households had once made private war, and the statutes against unlawful retaining testify to a state still nervous, a century after the Wars of the Roses, about aristocratic affinity as a rival to public order. A play in which two great houses keep bodies of fighting servants, and in which the prince must bargain with the patriarchs rather than simply command them, stages the Tudor state's own unfinished business in Italian dress.

The decade of the play's first performances sharpens the contact. The middle 1590s brought failed harvests, high bread prices, and disorder in London itself, including the apprentice riots of the summer of 1595, when crowds of young servants attacked officers and markets and the ringleaders were executed as traitors. A tragedy that opens with servants brawling in a public place, and whose ruler enters crying "Rebellious subjects, enemies to peace" before threatening the patriarchs with death if the streets are stained again (1.1), does not need to allegorise those events to draw energy from them. The audience's anxiety about masterless young men, about violence escaping the household into the street, about authority that proclaims more than it can enforce, is the charge the scene borrows. Greenblatt's school calls this borrowing negotiation rather than reflection, because the play does something with the anxiety besides mirror it. It makes the brawl delightful. The opening scene is funny, its violence choreographed as wit, and only when Tybalt enters does the temperature change. The theatre thus performs the culture's ambivalence exactly: a society that feared street violence also consumed it as entertainment, in the bear garden as in the playhouse, and the play's structure, which begins by making disorder charming and ends by counting its corpses, is that ambivalence given form.

One voice in the opening scene deserves the method's particular attention, because the play raises it once and then suppresses it for four acts. When the brawl generalises, the citizens of Verona pour in with clubs, crying "Down with the Capulets! Down with the Montagues!" (1.1), and the Prince's first address is to them as much as to the patriarchs: "You men, you beasts, that quench the fire of your pernicious rage with purple fountains issuing from your veins." The commons of Verona have their own account with the feud, whose private wars tax their streets, their markets, and their safety, and for a dozen lines the play lets the taxed speak. Then the crowd vanishes from the tragedy, its grievance absorbed into the Prince's proceedings, its members reappearing only as the anonymous watch that arrests friars and servants in the final scene. A new historicist reading does not invent a popular revolution the play declines to stage. It notices the licensing: whose anger the fiction permits itself to voice, for how long, and under whose presidency it is then conducted to a conclusion. The citizens' cry is the feud's energy as felt from below, and its swift disappearance into the machinery of princely judgment is the containment pattern in miniature, staged in the play's first two hundred lines for any reader the method has taught to watch the doors through which voices enter and leave.

One cost of the reading should be charged to it openly, because the documentary check catches the method here as surely as it vindicates it elsewhere. The new historicist Verona is transparent: the critic reads straight through the Italian city to the London street, treating the setting as a licensed disguise for the audience's own arrangements. But the play's Italy is not a nullity. It came to the stage through a documented traffic of its own, the Italian novella tradition Englished by way of a French intermediary into Arthur Brooke's long poem of 1562 and William Painter's prose version, and the fiction's Verona, a city of signorial rule and factional houses, had a real political physiognomy in the della Scala past that the sources carried with them. A method alert to circulation might be expected to track that traffic too, the energy of the translated tale, the English appetite for Italian stories of love and vendetta, Italy as the period's imagined laboratory of passion and policy. In practice the school's readings hurry past the Mediterranean to the Thames, and something is lost in the hurry: the play's foreignness, its status as an import reworked, which is part of what its first audiences paid for. The honest verdict keeps both ledgers. The London contacts are documented and load-bearing. The Italian mediation is also documented, and the method's relative silence about it marks a preference, for the domestic and the institutional, that its practitioners seldom acknowledge as a choice.

The Prince himself is the reading's central exhibit. Escalus is no tyrant and no cipher. He is a ruler whose authority is real but negotiated, dependent on the cooperation of the very houses he judges, and the play marks the dependence with unusual precision. His first edict threatens death and is ignored within the day. His second appearance, after Mercutio and Tybalt lie dead, produces not the promised execution but a moderated sentence, banishment, reached after hearing Benvolio's partial narrative and the pleas of both houses: "Mercy but murders, pardoning those that kill" (3.1), he concludes, in a line that states the containment problem from the ruler's side, since mercy extended to keep the peace becomes an instrument of further killing. His third appearance finds him confessing complicity: "And I for winking at your discords too have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punished" (5.3). A new historicist reads this arc as the play's anatomy of early modern governance, in which power works less by command than by theatre, pardon, spectacle, and the management of aristocratic honour, and in which the ruler's final success, the reconciliation of the houses over the bodies, is achieved by the catastrophe rather than by his authority. The ending's famous stage picture, the fathers clasping hands and promising golden statues, is from this angle a piece of statecraft performed by grief: the feud's energy is not abolished but converted, melted down and recast as public monument under the Prince's presidency. Whether that conversion should be celebrated as order restored or distrusted as power feeding on the young is the subversion and containment question, and the play, characteristically, gives the evidence for both answers.

## Marriage as Negotiation: Consent, Property, and the Secret Wedding

No part of the play gains more from the method than its marriage plot, because no part of the play is more densely institutional. The companion analysis of [patriarchy and the marriage market in Verona](/2024/02/23/patriarchy-marriage-market-verona/) establishes the market's rules inside the fiction: an heiress of thirteen, a suitor who petitions her father before he speaks to her, a dowry implied in every negotiation. New historicism sets the fiction beside the period's actual law and custom, and the fit is close enough to be clarifying. In the England of the 1590s, a marriage made by the exchange of consent between the parties, especially when solemnised by a priest, was valid in law even when it was clandestine, contracted without banns, licence, or parental approval. The same act that made the lovers husband and wife in the eyes of the Church made them offenders against household government, public order, and the economics of alliance. Reformers and conduct writers argued both sides of the question with real passion: marriage as a companionate union founded on the couple's consent, and marriage as a settlement negotiated by families in which a child's obedience was a religious duty. The play stages the argument as a family at war with itself, and Capulet is given both positions to speak, in sequence, to devastating effect.

In the first act, petitioned by Paris, Capulet is the very model of the restrained patriarch. Juliet "is yet a stranger in the world," he answers; "she hath not seen the change of fourteen years. Let two more summers wither in their pride ere we may think her ripe to be a bride," and he adds the period's most humane doctrine, "And too soon marred are those so early made" (1.2). He even concedes the consent principle: his will is that Paris win her heart, since "my will to her consent is but a part." By the third act, with Tybalt dead and the family's honour bleeding, the same man is arranging the wedding for Thursday and meeting his daughter's refusal with the language of property and the street: "Hang thee, young baggage! Disobedient wretch! I tell thee what: get thee to church o' Thursday, or never after look me in the face" (3.5). A character-centred reading explains the reversal by temperament or grief, and both are present. The new historicist reading adds the system: Capulet's two voices are the two voices of his culture's marriage ideology, consent and command, and the play's structure assigns them to the moments when each is useful to the house. While the market is calm, consent costs nothing and ornaments the father's authority. When the house is threatened, the daughter's marriage becomes an instrument for repairing alliances, and consent is withdrawn like credit. Juliet is not caught between a kind father and a cruel one. She is caught between two clauses of the same settlement, and her tragedy is that she acted on the first clause in a world that reserved the right to enforce the second.

The demographic record sharpens the scene further, and it is the kind of evidence the method exists to admit. Juliet is not yet fourteen, a fact the play repeats with almost bureaucratic insistence, in the Nurse's calculations, in her mother's reminiscence, in her father's bargaining. Historical demography, in the tradition running from John Hajnal's account of the European marriage pattern to E. A. Wrigley and R. S. Schofield's reconstruction of England's population, establishes that Englishwomen of the period typically married in their middle twenties, and that a bride of thirteen was exceptional in the audience's own country, familiar mainly from aristocratic settlements and from the Italian world the play represents. The audience therefore watched the marriage plot with double vision: Juliet's age marked her as foreign and extreme, a fact of Verona, and simultaneously raised the domestic question of how early was too early in a system where fathers disposed of daughters. Capulet's line about brides too soon marred is the play handing that question to the house in the father's own voice, before the plot answers it with a corpse. A reading that treats the age as a mere datum of the source misses the transaction: the play imports an Italian extremity precisely to test, at a safe distance, the English argument about consent, ripeness, and the price of haste.

The Friar's part in the marriage shows the method working on motive. Laurence agrees to marry the lovers in a speech that announces not pastoral sympathy but policy: "For this alliance may so happy prove to turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3). The marriage is, for him, an instrument of civic reconciliation, a treaty disguised as a sacrament, and the play's church thus behaves as the period's church was expected to behave, as an arm of public order whose spiritual acts carry political weight. His later instruments, the potion, the staged funeral, the letter, are likewise institutional before they are personal: each borrows the authority of ritual, of the tomb, of the sealed and carried message, and each fails when the institutions around it, the wedding timetable, the quarantine, the watch, move faster than his design. Coppelia Kahn's classic reading of the play, examined in this series as [Coppelia Kahn on coming of age in Verona](/2026/12/18/coppelia-kahn-coming-of-age-verona/), approaches the same ground from the psychology of masculine initiation, arguing that Romeo must pass through the feud's school of manhood to become a husband. The methods differ, Kahn's lens being psychoanalytic where Greenblatt's is institutional, but the new historicist can absorb her insight as evidence: a culture's rituals of manhood are among the energies a play circulates, and the duel that interrupts the wedding day is the feud's initiation rite reclaiming its pupil at the very moment the marriage rite has discharged him.

### Why does the name speech mark the limit of self-fashioning?

Because the speech tries to unmake by declaration what only institutions can unmake. Juliet reasons that a name is only a word and urges Romeo to refuse his, as if a self could be fashioned by an act of will (2.2). The play grants the reasoning its full brilliance and then lets the names hold.

Renaissance Self-Fashioning argued that identity in the period was made, not born, but made always against an authority and an alien power that set the terms, and Juliet's great speech is the play's test of that thesis. The speech is the most inward passage in the play and the most institutionally literate: she knows that the name is not a word but a mechanism, carrying the feud, the inheritance, and the obedience owed to the house. Her tragedy is that the insight changes nothing. The play thereby stages both the period's new fantasy of the self-authored life and the documentary truth that households, not declarations, assigned persons their places.

## Plague, Quarantine, and the Institutions of Chance

The catastrophe of Romeo and Juliet has embarrassed moralising criticism for centuries, because it runs on accident: a letter miscarries, a friar is delayed, a waking comes minutes late. Bradley, whose exclusion of the play from the great tragedies this series has weighed, rested part of his case on that machinery, finding the disaster imposed from outside rather than grown from character. New historicism dissolves the embarrassment by historicising the machinery. Each accident in the chain is an institution behaving as institutions did. The letter miscarries because plague administration sealed a house and stopped a traveller, an event so routine in the 1590s that the audience needed no explanation beyond Friar John's few lines. The potion plot is hostage to a wedding date advanced by a father's command, the household's timetable overriding the church's. The tomb scene is policed by a watch that arrives, as watches did, in time to arrest the survivors and report to the Prince. Even the feud's final brawl is set in motion by the conventions of the duello, the imported Italian science of honour that had become, in the London of Vincentio Saviolo's fencing school and his printed Practise of 1595, a fashionable technology of masculine reputation. The play's Verona runs on the same systems that ran its audience's city, and the tragedy's celebrated speed is the speed of institutions interacting: household, church, market, sickroom, and street, each transmitting the lovers' secret at the pace of its own procedures until the procedures collide.

This is the point at which the method most plainly outperforms the language of fate. The Prologue calls the lovers "star-crossed" and promises that their deaths will bury their parents' strife, and generations of readers have taken the astrology at face value, as the play's own philosophy. The new historicist notes who is speaking and in what genre: a Chorus, performing the conventional office of a prologue, retailing the commonplaces of fortune that the ensuing action will translate into documented mechanisms. What the Chorus calls the stars, the play shows as the quarantine, the timetable, the edict, and the code of honour. The translation is the reading. It does not deny that the play believes in fortune; it observes that the play keeps cashing fortune out into institutions, as if fortune were the name a culture gives to the systems it cannot see whole. For a school founded on the suspicion that the largest abstractions are made of small transactions, there could hardly be a more congenial text, and the congeniality should itself be registered as a warning, since a method that finds its perfect specimen everywhere it looks may be describing its own preferences. The adjudication below returns to that warning with the critics who made it a charge.

The apothecary of Mantua extends the catalogue of institutions by one more, the policed market in dangerous goods. Romeo, banished and desperate, remembers a tattered retailer whose shop displayed "a tortoise hung, an alligator stuffed, and other skins of ill-shaped fishes," and buys from him a dram of poison under a law the seller recites as he breaks it: Mantua's law, the apothecary agrees, is death to any he that utters such drugs (5.1). Romeo's answer is the play's most naked piece of political economy: "The world is not thy friend nor the world's law; the world affords no law to make thee rich." The transaction takes four exchanges. Poverty outsells statute, gold proves the stronger poison, and the lovers' fate, so often assigned to the stars, is here purchased over a counter from a man the market has starved into compliance. The new historicist does not claim the scene as an allegory of capitalism avant la lettre. The claim is smaller and harder: that the play persistently routes its catastrophe through regulated exchanges, letters stopped by health officers, marriages arranged as settlements, poisons sold against statute, and that a criticism attending to the regulations understands the tragedy's machinery better than a criticism attending only to its sentiments. Chance in this play wears a uniform every time it appears.

## Saints, Shrines, and a Sonnet: Suppressed Devotion at the Feast

The lovers' first meeting is the play's most famous passage and, for this method, its most instructive transaction. Romeo crosses the feast, takes Juliet's hand, and the two of them speak a sonnet together, built entire from the liturgy of pilgrimage: "If I profane with my unworthiest hand this holy shrine, the gentle sin is this: my lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand to smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5). Juliet answers inside the same rite, correcting his theology with a flirt's precision: "For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch, and palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss." The exchange ends in the rite's consummation, negotiated like a prayer being granted: "Saints do not move, though grant for prayers' sake." "Then move not, while my prayer's effect I take." Every term is devotional. Shrine, pilgrim, palmer, sin, prayer, saint: the vocabulary belongs to the cult that the English Reformation had spent sixty years dismantling, the pilgrimage roads emptied, the shrines at Walsingham and Canterbury destroyed, the saints' images burned or whitewashed, the intercession of saints condemned as superstition by the state church. Eamon Duffy's The Stripping of the Altars (1992), the great modern reconstruction of that devotional world and its destruction, documents what was lost: a whole economy of sacred touch, of journeys made to holy places, of hands laid on relics and lips on shrines, through which ordinary people had conducted their most intense transactions with the sacred.

Greenblatt's school reads the sonnet as that economy's afterlife. The energy of suppressed devotion did not vanish when the shrines came down. It circulated, and one of its destinations was the commercial stage, where the gestures of pilgrimage could be performed again under the licence of fiction, their charge intact and their object replaced. The sonnet at the feast is, on this reading, a piece of devotional machinery reloaded with erotic content, exactly as Capulet's funeral speech reloads the wedding feast: the form persists because the feeling it was built to carry still needs carrying, and the theatre becomes the shrine that the parish can no longer maintain, with a boy actor's hand as the relic and a kiss as the granted prayer. The reading gains support from the founder's own later work. In Hamlet in Purgatory (2001), Greenblatt traced how the abolition of purgatory, a doctrine that had organised immense traffic between the living and the dead, drove that traffic onto the stage, where a ghost could still demand remembrance from a culture forbidden to pray for him. The mechanism is the same one the sonnet exhibits: an institution is dismantled, its energies are displaced, and the playhouse, licensed and suspect and packed, collects them. That Greenblatt developed the mechanism on Hamlet rather than on this play returns to the record established above. The tool is his. The feast sonnet is where a reader of this play finds it working at highest pressure, in fourteen lines that two generations of formalist commentary admired as a perfect miniature without asking where a Protestant audience learned to feel a kiss as a rite.

The reading also disciplines itself at the obvious objection, which the documentary check requires to be stated. Pilgrimage language survived in the period's love poetry independent of any theatrical transaction, Petrarch's Laura being the standing shrine of the sonnet tradition, and the lovers' exchange may draw on that literary reservoir rather than on suppressed devotion directly. The new historicist answer distinguishes the reservoirs. Petrarchism explains the conceit's availability in verse. It does not explain the scene's staging, the touch negotiated palm to palm, the emphasis on hands, lips, and granting, which reproduces the choreography of the shrine visit rather than the page's address to an absent lady. The energy reading survives the check because its channel is choreographic as well as verbal: a culture that had queued to touch holy things recognised the queue when it saw one forming across a dance floor.

## The Friar's Cell: Confession, Counsel, and the Plot's Private Machinery

A second suppressed institution powers the plot as surely as the shrine powers the feast scene, and the method's attention catches it where devotional readings see only a helpful priest. The tragedy's machinery runs on confession. Juliet repairs to Friar Laurence's cell under the warrant of shrift, the sacramental confession in which a penitent disclosed sins in private to a priest and received counsel and absolution under seal. Her mother accepts the errand without a blink, since the girl goes, as she tells her parents, to make confession and be absolved (3.3, 4.1). Inside that licensed privacy the entire counter-plot is built: the potion proposed, the feigned death arranged, the messages entrusted. The cell is the play's one room where a daughter of the house can speak unobserved to an authority who is not her father, and the tragedy is, in a strict sense, what that room made possible and what its privacy could not protect. For the play's first English audience the institution carried a charge that a modern audience has to recover. Auricular confession had been abolished in the Church of England, its compulsory practice ended at the Reformation, its privacy surviving in the culture as a memory at once valued and suspect, associated with the old religion's machinery of secrets. The play imports the institution from Catholic Italy along with its friars and its shrines, and then does with it what it did with the pilgrimage liturgy: it runs modern-feeling business, adolescent desperation, parental evasion, clandestine planning, through the old machinery and draws pathos from the friction.

The Friar himself, read through the method, is less a character than a junction box. Into his cell flow the period's wires: sacramental authority from the church, medical and herbal knowledge from the physick tradition he announces in his first speech on the earth's grave and womb, civic policy in his hope that the marriage will reconcile the houses, and the postal system in his letters to Mantua. His failure is correspondingly systemic. Each wire he depends on is cut by another institution acting on schedule: the wedding advanced by the father, the letter stopped by the health officers, the tomb visited by the bridegroom he could not warn. Biographical and moral criticism asked whether Laurence is guilty, and the play's Prince sentences him with the period's own ambivalence, "we still have known thee for a holy man," before the machinery of pardon absorbs him. The new historicist asks the prior question the guilt debate skips: what made one friar the load-bearing point of so many systems, and the answer is the confessional itself, the single institution through which a household's secrets could legally pass to an outsider. Where secrets can travel, plots can be built. Where the channel is one man walking between two towns in plague season, plots fail as this one fails, not by fate but by bandwidth.

## The Duello and the Fashioning of Men

Mercutio's mockery of Tybalt is usually anthologised as wit, and it is also a document. Tybalt, he reports, is "the very butcher of a silk button, a duellist, a duellist," a gentleman who fights "as you sing prick-song, keeps time, distance, and proportion," whose art reduces to "the immortal passado, the punto reverso, the hai!" (2.4). The speech assumes an audience fluent in the new fencing culture, the Italian masters teaching in London, the printed manuals with their diagrams of measure and tempo, the young gallants who had made the rapier and its grammar a badge of fashion. New historicism reads the duel plot through that fluency. Tybalt is not simply a hothead. He is a technician of an imported honour system, a man whose identity is fashioned by a foreign art, and his quarrel with Romeo is a collision between two technologies of the self: the duellist's arithmetic and the lover's improvisation. Romeo, entering the scene fresh from a marriage that has reclassified Tybalt as his kinsman, refuses the arithmetic: "I do protest I never injured thee, but love thee better than thou canst devise" (3.1). The refusal is unintelligible inside the code, which has no category for a love that cancels enmity without a settlement of honour, and Mercutio steps into the gap with the code's own logic, dying of the intervention with the play's most famous piece of social analysis on his lips: "A plague o' both your houses!" (3.1).

Mercutio's other set piece repays the attention from the opposite direction. The Queen Mab speech, usually read as a firework of pure fancy, is organised as a census: the fairy visits the sleepers of Verona by profession, and each profession dreams its own appetite. Courtiers dream on curtsies, lawyers on fees, soldiers on cutting foreign throats, parsons on benefices, a lady on kisses, a tithe-pig's tail and a drab's lips each receiving their due (1.4). The catalogue is the play's smallest complete social survey, desire distributed by occupation and rank, and it makes the method's point in the mouth of the play's wittiest sceptic: there is no appetite in this city that is not wearing a uniform. Even dreams, the last private territory, are socially allocated. When Romeo interrupts, "Peace, peace, Mercutio, peace! Thou talk'st of nothing," the nothing he names is the sum of the city's something, its entire economy of wanting, dismissed by the one young man whose own wanting is about to be reallocated by a glance across a dance floor. The speech is not decoration on the feud's world. It is the feud's world dreaming.

The curse repays the method's attention, because it distributes blame exactly as the reading does: not to a star, not to a villain, but to the houses, the institutions that manufacture the violence and consume the young men who serve it. Mercutio, the Prince's kinsman and neither house's servant, is the one figure positioned to see the system from outside, and the play kills him at the moment of his clearest sight, converting his curse into the tragedy's thesis statement. Romeo's self-fashioning completes the pattern. The young man who began the play performing a borrowed identity, the Petrarchan lover sighing for Rosaline in set oxymorons, attempts in the orchard to fashion a new self by refusing his name, and discovers in the street that the old assignments, kinsman, enemy, banished man, are enforced by others regardless of his declarations. Greenblatt's 1980 book argued that self-fashioning in the period always required an alien against which the self was built and an authority by which it was licensed. Romeo builds himself against the feud and is licensed by the Friar, and the play shows the licence revoked by every institution that had issued it: the church's plan quarantined, the Prince's sentence exiling, the father's timetable advancing. The self he fashioned survives exactly as long as the systems permit, which is to say until Thursday.

## Subversion and Containment: Does the Ending Restore the Prince or Indict Him?

The sharpest instrument the school produced is the question Greenblatt posed in "Invisible Bullets": whether the disorder a Renaissance play stages is a genuine threat to authority or a safety valve authority itself provides, subversion produced in order to be contained. Applied to this play, the question divides its ending against itself. The containment answer is easy to document. Order is restored. The Prince presides over the final scene, hears the evidence in due form, assigns the pardons and punishments, and closes the proceedings with the state's monopoly on meaning: "For never was a story of more woe than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3). The reconciled fathers are absorbed into the settlement as monument builders, their grief taxed into golden statues that will stand as the city's official memory of the feud. The lovers, whose secret marriage defied household and market alike, end as public property, their story nationalised by the ruler who failed to protect them. On this reading the play performs the classic containing operation: it lets an audience feel the full force of rebellion against arranged marriage and household tyranny, then buries the rebels and converts their energy into an endorsement of the order that required the sacrifice.

### Does the ending subvert the Prince or serve him?

It serves him in form and indicts him in content, and the form does not cancel the content. The ceremony of judgment is his, the pardons are his, the closing couplet is his, yet the judgment he pronounces includes his own conviction among the sentences it hands down.

He has lost a brace of kinsmen for winking at the discords, and all, himself named in the reckoning, are punished (5.3). A contained rebellion would not put the confession in the ruler's mouth at the moment of his triumph. The play gives authority the last word and makes the last word an admission that the word came too late.

The question has a reflexive edge the method itself invites, since the object asking it was a contained object. Every play performed in London passed under the eye of the Master of the Revels, whose office licensed texts for the stage and could require alterations, and the companies played in venues the civic authorities repeatedly petitioned to suppress. The tragedy that wonders whether staged rebellion serves the ruler was itself staged by permission, its script a licensed commodity, its company a lord's retained servants. Greenblatt's school treats this not as an embarrassment but as the normal condition of Renaissance art: negotiation all the way down, with the licence fee and the censor's pen among the trades a poetics of culture must reconstruct. For the present play the reflexion is unusually pointed. A fiction in which authority fails three times to govern the streets was performed, without recorded incident, before an authority that governed the stage itself, and drew its largest energies from the gap between the Prince's Verona and the magistrates' London, two regimes of order, each theatrical, each incomplete, commenting on each other nightly without either having to acknowledge the commentary. Containment, on this showing, is not a thesis imposed on the play. It is the air the play was built to breathe.

The subversion answer draws its evidence from the price tag the play refuses to hide. Containment, in the strong form of the thesis, requires the disorder to be exposed as hollow, its energies revealed as authority's own creatures. Nothing in the tomb scene is hollow. The love the state now monumentalises was real, the institutions that killed it are named by their own officers, the Friar confessing his policy, the Prince confessing his negligence, the fathers confessing their enmity in the very act of renouncing it, and the audience's judgment is left in possession of the field: Capulet's own verdict on the dead is "poor sacrifices of our enmity" (5.3), a sentence that assigns the guilt to the system in the system's own voice. The adjudication offered here is that the play performs containment and audits it in the same gesture. Verona's order is restored, visibly, ceremonially, and at a cost the restoration cannot recoup, since the heirs of both houses and of the Prince's own blood are the price. That double movement is more interesting than either half of the debate alone, and it is the kind of doubleness the method, at its best, was built to catch: a culture staging its own anxieties in a form that consoles it and accuses it in the same performance. Whether the doubleness belongs to the play or to the method's habits of finding it is the question the critics take up next.

## The Charge of Flattening: Critics, Editors, and the Documentary Check

The complication the brief requires to be faced squarely is by now a literature of its own: the charge that new historicism flattens the literary, dissolving poems into the documentary soup of their period until the play becomes interchangeable with the pamphlet beside it. Carolyn Porter gave the charge an early and durable form in "Are We Being Historical Yet?" (1988), arguing that the school's characteristic essay lets a vivid anecdote stand in for a history it never actually reconstructs, so that the play and the document illuminate each other in a circle with no outside. Edward Pechter, writing on the new historicism and its discontents, pressed the evidentiary point: the juxtapositions that generate the readings are chosen by the critic, and a method that cannot say what would count against its reading cannot claim to have discovered anything. Walter Cohen's political criticism of the school added that its favourite conclusion, containment, is suspiciously convenient, since a theory that finds every rebellion pre-absorbed by power has excused itself from ever being surprised by a text. David Scott Kastan, in Shakespeare After Theory (1999), carried the case into the next generation, defending a chastened historicism that would let the play's literariness, its difference from the documents it resembles, count as evidence in its own right. The charges converge on a single demand: that the traffic between text and context be documented, priced, and allowed to fail, rather than asserted by resonance.

### Why do editors insist on documents where theorists follow energy?

Because an editor's claims can be checked against objects that survive, and a beautiful connection cannot. When G. Blakemore Evans edited this play for the New Cambridge series, his contexts were a date argued from records, sources identified by verbal parallel, a stage history built from documents, each exposed to disproof.

The anecdotal method, by contrast, can drift toward connections that feel true because they are beautiful, and beauty is not a control. The editor's insistence is therefore not a rival theory of the play but a discipline imposed on all theories: any context claimed for a passage should be one the passage's first audience could plausibly have brought to it, through a channel that can be named.

The adjudication this article defends applies that discipline as a filter and keeps what passes. The play's strongest contextual readings all survive the documentary test, because their channels are concrete: plague orders were printed, proclaimed, and enforced on the audience's own streets within three years of the first performances; the statutes against retaining and the chronicles of civic riot were public knowledge; the law of clandestine marriage was argued in every contested wedding the period records; Saviolo's manual was printed in 1595, the play's own moment, and Mercutio's vocabulary proves the contact from inside the text. Where a reading can show the channel, the energy metaphor is a precise instrument, naming the verified movement of material between institution and stage. Where it cannot, the resonance is decoration, and the critics' charge lands. Greenblatt's own practice supplies both the model and the warning. His exorcism chapter works because Harsnett's pamphlet and Lear's speeches share phrases a collator can list. His biographical use of this very play in Will in the World falters, by the same standard, because no document connects the playwright's marriage to Juliet's speeches except the critic's sense of a fit, and the fit is doing all the work. The school's founder thus furnishes, in his own two modes, the demonstration of where the method ends: at the edge of the checkable.

It is equally part of fairness to record what the flattening charge misses. The method never claimed that a play is only its contexts, and Greenblatt's writing at its best is conspicuously attentive to the aesthetic charge of the object, to what his essay on museums called wonder, the power of a singular thing to stop a viewer in wonder before resonance sets it vibrating among its neighbours. The Norton introduction to this play, with its surrender to the orchard scene's language, is not an aberration in his work but the other pole of it, the pole the flattening charge pretends not to see. A school that produced both the exorcism collation and that introduction cannot fairly be described as indifferent to the literary. The accurate charge is narrower and more damaging where it applies: the method's middle practitioners, working at a distance from its founder's archival habits, sometimes let the anecdote float free, and the school gave them no internal mechanism for telling the difference. The editor's discipline, imported from outside, is that mechanism, which is why the verdict below sentences the method to be practised under editorial supervision rather than acquitting or condemning it whole.

A second line of criticism, mounted from the school's own political side, deserves its place in the record because it shaped how the method was applied to plays of rebellion like this one. If power always contains subversion in advance, the objection ran, then the method has built despair into its foundations and taught a generation of readers to find every resistance already absorbed, every rebel already a licensed fool. The charge of quietism stung because it used the school's own evidence: the essays did keep concluding that the disorder served the order. Greenblatt's responses, across the introductions and interviews of the following decades, conceded the pattern while disputing the moral, arguing that to describe a mechanism of containment is not to endorse it and that the plays themselves, which keep showing authority succeeding at ruinous cost, are less comfortable than the thesis extracted from them. This article's adjudication of the ending followed that concession: the Prince's restoration is real, and the play prices it in heirs. It is also worth recording that the school's founder proved willing to revise the brand itself, remarking more than once that he preferred cultural poetics to the label that had made him famous, as if the method's maker shared the critics' suspicion of methods. A practice that began by refusing to be a doctrine kept trying, honourably and without complete success, to refuse.

## Neighbours and Rivals: Formalism, Cultural Materialism, Presentism, and the Marxist Reading

The method's identity sharpens against its neighbours. Against formalism, the contrast has already been drawn: Levin's play is a system of forms whose tensions resolve inside the poem, and the new historicist honours the description while denying that the system is closed, since the forms themselves, the sonnet of the first meeting, the aubade of the parting, the epitaph of the close, are social instruments borrowed from courtship, ceremony, and monument building, each carrying its institution's energy into the fiction. Against old historicism, the contrast is between backdrop and traffic, already drawn in the account of Tillyard. The nearest neighbour is the most instructive. British cultural materialism, the school of Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield, shares the archive, the suspicion of consensus, and the interest in power, and differs in the conclusion it is willing to draw: where Greenblatt's containment thesis tends to find dissidence absorbed in advance by the order it challenges, the cultural materialists insist on faultlines where genuine opposition survives and can be taken up by later readers for their own struggles. Applied to this play, the difference is a difference about the ending's statues: containment reads them as the state taxing grief into legitimacy, the faultline reading finds in the fathers' ruin and the Prince's confession a permanent exhibit against household tyranny, available to every generation that has used the play against arranged marriage and inherited hatred. The evidence of the play's afterlife, its centuries of service as an argument for the young against the old, supports the faultline account of its effects, while the ending's ceremonial form supports containment's account of its design. The two schools divide the play between them, each strongest where the other is silent.

The formalist contrast can be made exact on a single sequence. Levin's reading of the aubade, the lovers' dawn parting in the third act, hears a formal debate inherited from medieval lyric, nightingale against lark, conducted inside a pattern of light and dark imagery that the whole play sustains. The description is superb and, within its terms, complete. The new historicist asks one question more: whose time is it? The lark is the household's clock, the herald of the working day on which the Nurse will knock, the marriage will be bargained, and the banishment will be executed, while the nightingale sings in the lovers' stolen interval, time experienced as private property. The formal pattern, on this account, is a border dispute between two regimes of time, the institution's and the couple's, and the scene hurts because the audience knows which regime owns the morning. Neither account cancels the other. Levin's explains the scene's construction, and the historicist's explains its pressure, and a reader equipped with both reads the aubade twice as well as a reader equipped with either. That doubleness is the practical form of this article's verdict: the schools are instruments, and the play is larger than any single instrument's range, which is an argument for carrying several rather than for melting them into one.

Two further neighbours are treated at full length elsewhere in this run and are placed here only to fix the map. Presentism, the declared rival that reads the play frankly from the concerns of the present rather than disavowing them, turns the new historicist accusation around and asks whether the recovered past is ever more than the present in costume; that quarrel belongs to the presentism article and is joined there. The Marxist readings, which take the feud into the economics of household production, dowry, and class alliance, share the new historicist archive while grounding its energies in material relations the cultural vocabulary can leave vague; the companion study of [Marxist readings of Verona's class conflict](/2027/02/12/marxist-readings-romeo-and-juliet/) gives that tradition its own hearing. New historicism's distinct contribution, against all its neighbours, is the circulation model itself: the insistence that the play is neither a mirror of its moment, nor a weapon against it, nor a timeless form above it, but a transaction inside it, priced in the currencies of honour, consent, fear, and spectacle that the moment supplied.

## The Verdict: What New Historicism Reveals in This Play, and What It Cannot See

The verdict, argued through the evidence assembled here, is that new historicism is the strongest available account of the play's public life and an incomplete account of its private one, and that the two findings together fix its rank among the schools this run surveys. On the credit side, the method converts the play's embarrassments into explanations. The feud without a cause becomes an institution with functions, organising service, honour, and masculine credit for men who have nothing else to organise. The accidents of the catastrophe become the period's administrative systems interacting, plague control against church timetable against household command, and the audience's documented experience of those systems supplies the tragedy's credibility. The Prince becomes a study in negotiated authority that illuminates every scene he touches and several he does not. Capulet's reversal on the marriage becomes the sound of a culture's two marriage doctrines succeeding each other under pressure. Each of these readings passes the documentary check the critics demanded, because each names its channel: statute, plague order, marriage law, fencing manual, chronicle. A school that can be audited and survives the audit on this much of the play has earned more than tolerance.

One more credit belongs in the ledger, because it answers a standing puzzle about the play's construction. Romeo and Juliet is unusually full of servants who talk. The tragedy opens in its servants' mouths, gives its longest prose to a nurse, pauses after its heroine's apparent death for a scene of hired musicians haggling over instruments, names, and the price of their attendance, and lets a servant's glimpse of a ring carry its catastrophe to Mantua. Formalist criticism tended to apologise for the musicians as comic relief indecorously placed. The historicist reading claims them as structure: the fiction's energy circulates vertically through the whole household, from patriarch to pot-boy, and the grief of the great house is registered, in the musicians' scene, in the only terms the hired men own, the terms of their trade and their fee. The audience, itself vertically mixed from groundlings to gallants, found its own ranks distributed across the fiction's, and the play's social bandwidth is part of its design, the household rendered as a complete economy of feeling and labour rather than a backdrop for two principals. A method that begins by asking whose voices a text licenses is the first method that had a use for everything in this play.

On the debit side, the method's account of the lovers themselves runs thin, and the thinness is structural. Juliet's reasoning on the balcony, the astonishing sequence in which a girl of thirteen dismantles the name system in soliloquy, is illuminated by the method only at its edges, as a comment on patronymics, while the thing itself, the speed and gaiety and terror of a mind inventing freedom in real time, belongs to orders of explanation the school brackets: the formal, the psychological, the poetic. Greenblatt's own Norton introduction tacitly concedes the boundary by praising the orchard scene in the language of wonder, and the concession is the honest centre of his whole practice, the acknowledgment that the circulation of energies presupposes a charge worth circulating, which the energies themselves do not explain. The flattening charge is therefore true of the method's programme and false of its founder's best work, and students of the play should hold both truths in the arrangement the evidence supports. Read the play with the new historicists to understand what Verona's systems did to the lovers. Read it with the formalists, the editors, and the psychologists to understand what the lovers did with the systems, in speeches no institution composed for them. The cliche this series exists to retire, the love story outside time, does not survive the first reading. The poem does survive the second, and a method honest about its own limits, as its founder intermittently was, makes the best guide to the border between the two.

One last exhibit belongs to the verdict, because the method predicts it. If plays are transactions in a culture's energies, a successful play should go on transacting long after its first moment, and this play's afterlife is a four-century ledger of exactly such exchanges. The tragedy that anatomised the conversion of private passion into public monument has itself been monumentalised on an industrial scale: the city of Verona maintains a house for Juliet, complete with a balcony assembled in the twentieth century for the pilgrims the play sends there, and receives letters addressed to a girl who never lived from correspondents asking her counsel in love. The shrine economy this article traced in the feast sonnet here completes its longest circuit, out of the fiction and into real estate, real tourism, and a real secretariat answering the mail. A formalist can call the phenomenon kitsch and a historian can call it heritage. The new historicist calls it evidence, the strongest the play affords that its energies were never its author's private property, and that the circulation Greenblatt named has not stopped. The school that taught readers to watch the traffic can fairly claim the traffic as its vindication, even where its founder, who never wrote the reading, is concerned. Its quietest victory is visible in the classroom editions through which the play is mostly read, which now routinely print the period's documents beside the text, homilies on obedience, orders for the plague, treatises on marriage and the duel, so that a student meets the sealed house and the marriage settlement with the evidence on the facing page. That apparatus is the method become furniture, unremarked because universal, and it frames the play in its moment for readers who will never hear the school's name.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch historicism primer, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch historicism primer is the table in this article that sets each working part of the new historicist method, the anecdote, circulation, negotiation, self-fashioning, subversion and containment, against the passage of the play where that part earns its keep, with act, scene, and line cited for every claim. The pattern it exposes is that the method succeeds exactly where a channel between institution and stage can be named and documented, in plague orders, marriage law, retaining statutes, and fencing manuals, and fails where it asks a reader to accept a resemblance as a connection.

### Q: Who coined the term new historicism, and when?

Stephen Greenblatt coined the term in 1982, in his introduction to The Power of Forms in the English Renaissance, a special issue of the journal Genre that he edited. He had been practising the approach without the label since Renaissance Self-Fashioning in 1980, and he later admitted a preference for his other name for it, cultural poetics, which he felt described the practice more accurately than a term implying a single method. The label stuck anyway, and the school that gathered under it, centred at Berkeley and in the journal Representations, defined literary study's historical turn for a generation.

### Q: What does the circulation of social energy mean in plain terms?

It means that the feelings, fears, and charges attached to real rituals, scandals, and institutions do not stay where they start, but pass into plays and out again, changed by the passage. A stage edict borrows its weight from real proclamations the audience has heard cried in the street. A staged quarantine borrows its dread from real sealed houses. The play, in return, changes how its audience feels about edicts and quarantines the next time it meets them. Social energy is Greenblatt's name for whatever is being exchanged in that two-way traffic, which the theatre negotiates rather than merely reflects.

### Q: Did Stephen Greenblatt write a new historicist reading of Romeo and Juliet?

No. His 1988 manifesto Shakespearean Negotiations gives its chapters to the Henry plays, Twelfth Night, King Lear, and the culture of exorcism and colonial report, and Renaissance Self-Fashioning closes with Othello. The play this school is so often used to teach never received a set-piece reading from the school's founder. What exists under his name is an appreciative introduction for The Norton Shakespeare and a biographical use of the play in Will in the World, where the tragedy is treated as evidence about its author's marriage. The celebrated new historicist reading of the play is the method applied by other hands.

### Q: How does Greenblatt use Romeo and Juliet in Will in the World?

He uses it as evidence in a biographical mystery rather than as an object of study in itself. The chapter "Wooing, Wedding, and Repenting" asks what the playwright's hurried marriage to Anne Hathaway, contracted when she was already pregnant and he was eighteen, might explain about the plays, and it reads the tragedy's vision of secret desire and catastrophic haste as a kind of confession. Critics of the biography objected that the inference runs in a circle, since the life is reconstructed from the plays and then used to explain them. The episode matters here because it shows the founder, on this play, working in the biographical mode his method had displaced.

### Q: How does new historicism read the feud between the Montagues and Capulets?

It reads the feud as an institution with functions rather than a grudge with a cause the play never supplies. The quarrel organises loyalty for servants who own nothing else, supplies young men with a substitute estate in the house's honour, and gives both patriarchs a theatre for authority, which is why it survives every attempt to end it. The reading sets the fiction beside Elizabethan England's statutes against liveried retainers and its anxiety about private retinues, and finds the play staging the state's unfinished business: households that still keep fighting men, and a prince who must bargain with them instead of commanding them.

### Q: What does the Prince's Verona show about ideas of order in the period?

It shows authority working by theatre, pardon, and negotiation rather than by simple command. Escalus proclaims a capital edict and is disobeyed within the day, moderates his sentence after the killings because the peace needs the houses' cooperation, and ends by confessing that his own leniency has cost him a brace of kinsmen. A new historicist reading takes that arc as the period's political realism: power in a city of great households is a performance that must be renewed at each crisis, and the play's ruler succeeds only when catastrophe does for him what his proclamations could not, reconciling the factions over their children's bodies.

### Q: How does new historicism reinterpret the undelivered letter to Romeo?

It reinterprets accident as administration. Friar John's report is precise about the mechanism: the searchers suspected the house where he lodged of infection, sealed up the doors, and would not let the friars forth, so the letter could not even be returned, let alone delivered (5.2). Plague orders did exactly this to suspected houses in the London of the early 1590s, when the theatres themselves stood closed by the same emergency. The method therefore declines to call the miscarriage fate. It is a public-health system performing as designed, indifferent to the private catastrophe its correct operation causes.

### Q: How does new historicism read Capulet's two voices on Juliet's marriage?

It reads them as the two doctrines of the period's marriage system succeeding each other under pressure. In the calm of the first act Capulet speaks the consent doctrine, postponing the match, calling early brides too soon marred, and making his will merely a part of his daughter's consent (1.2). After Tybalt's death he speaks the command doctrine, fixing the wedding for Thursday and threatening disownment at a refusal (3.5). The new historicist point is that neither voice is the mask and neither the man: both are clauses of one settlement, and the tragedy is that Juliet acted on the clause her culture advertised and met the clause it enforced.

### Q: What is self-fashioning, and where does the play test it?

Self-fashioning is Greenblatt's term, from his 1980 book, for the period's project of constructing an identity rather than simply inheriting one, a project he argues always proceeds against an alien other and under the eye of a licensing authority. The play tests it in the orchard, where Juliet reasons that a name is only a word and urges Romeo to refuse his, as if a self could be declared into existence (2.2). The test fails. The household enforces the name through the marriage market, the street code, and the Prince's sentence, and the lovers' self-authored identities survive only as long as the institutions that assigned their old ones permit.

### Q: What is the difference between resonance and wonder in Greenblatt's account of art?

Wonder is the power of a singular object to stop a beholder, the arrest a reader feels before the orchard scene's language before any context has been supplied. Resonance is the same object's power to vibrate outward, to set neighbouring documents, rituals, and memories sounding in sympathy. Greenblatt set the pair out in his writing on museums and collecting, collected in Learning to Curse, and the distinction is his own safeguard against the flattening charge: a criticism that pursues only resonance dissolves the work into its surroundings, while wonder marks the point where the work exceeds every context assembled around it. His Norton introduction to this play is written from the wonder side of the line.

### Q: Where did the idea of subversion and containment come from in Greenblatt's work?

It came from "Invisible Bullets," the essay on the Henry plays in Shakespearean Negotiations, where Greenblatt argued, through Thomas Harriot's report from Virginia and the figure of Prince Hal, that authority in the period characteristically produced the very disorder it then punished, testing and displaying its power in the act of suppression. Transferred to Verona, the question becomes whether the feud and the secret marriage are rebellions the Prince's order absorbs, converting them at the close into statues, pardons, and a state-approved story, or whether the play's accounting of the price, the heirs of both houses and the Prince's own kin, leaves the order indicted by its own ceremony of restoration.

### Q: How does new historicism differ from formalism in reading this play?

Formalism reads the play as a self-sufficient verbal structure whose meanings are generated internally, by image, paradox, and pattern, in the manner of Harry Levin's account of its sonnets, aubades, and formal machinery. New historicism accepts the patterns and denies the self-sufficiency. It asks where the forms were borrowed from and what they carried with them: the sonnet from courtship ritual, the epitaph from monument culture, the prologue's fortune language from the commonplaces the action proceeds to translate into quarantine orders and wedding timetables. For the formalist the pattern is the meaning. For the new historicist the pattern is a transaction, and the meaning includes the price.

### Q: How does new historicism differ from British cultural materialism?

The two schools share an archive, a suspicion of consensus, and a politics, and divide over what staged dissent amounts to. Greenblatt's containment thesis tends to find rebellion produced and absorbed by the order it challenges, so that the ending's reconciliation would count as power converting grief into legitimacy. Cultural materialism, in the work of Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield, insists on faultlines: points where a text's contradictions remain open and usable by later readers against the order that produced them. The play's afterlife as an argument against arranged marriage and inherited hatred is the cultural materialists' standing evidence that the lovers were never fully contained.

### Q: What do critics of new historicism accuse the method of doing to literature?

They accuse it of flattening literature into documentation, and of proving its readings by juxtaposition rather than evidence. Carolyn Porter charged that the characteristic essay lets a vivid anecdote substitute for a history never reconstructed. Edward Pechter pressed the falsifiability point, that a method unable to say what would count against a reading discovers only what it planted. Walter Cohen found the containment conclusion too convenient, since power that always absorbs rebellion in advance can never be surprised by a text. David Scott Kastan later defended a chastened historicism in which the play's difference from the documents it resembles counts as evidence too.

### Q: What did Elizabethan law and custom say about secret marriages like the lovers'?

They said two contradictory things at once, which is why the Friar's ceremony is both efficacious and scandalous. A marriage contracted by present consent and solemnised by a priest was valid in law even when clandestine, without banns, licence, or parental approval, though the parties and the priest could be punished for the irregularity. At the same time, household government treated a child's unlicensed marriage as theft of the family's chief asset, and reforming opinion argued the question from both sides, companionate consent against filial obedience. Juliet is therefore married in law and ruined in standing by the same act, and the play's catastrophe grows in the gap between the law's two answers.

### Q: What do the golden statues show a new historicist about the Prince's power?

They show power completing its characteristic operation, the conversion of dangerous private energy into public monument. The fathers, reconciled over the bodies, promise each other gold: Montague will raise Juliet's statue in pure gold, and Capulet answers with Romeo's, "poor sacrifices of our enmity" (5.3). A new historicist notes who presides over the exchange and who keeps the story. The rebellion that defied household, market, and edict is recast as the city's treasure, displayed under princely auspices, its meaning fixed by the ceremony that closes the play. Whether the statues honour the lovers or appropriate them is the containment question in its most durable form.

### Q: What role does the audience play in the circulation of social energy?

The audience is the exchange on which the whole traffic clears. Its members brought the culture's charges into the playhouse, memories of plague closures, proclamations, duels, and contested weddings, and the performance borrowed against that capital, which is why Friar John's few lines about a sealed house needed no footnote in 1595. They then carried the play's versions of those things back out, its picture of princely mercy, paternal tyranny, and young love, into the streets where the originals lived. Greenblatt's theatre is not a mirror held up to its audience's world but a market inside it, and the audience is the medium in which both currencies, the world's and the play's, are exchanged.

### Q: Why does new historicism treat the theatre as an institution and not only an art?

Because the theatre the method studies was a business operating under licence, censorship, and plague orders, buying its materials in a market and selling them to a crowd it had to please to survive. A playing company negotiated for plots, borrowed the prestige of rituals and the notoriety of scandals, and trimmed its products to the authorities who could close it. Treating the playhouse as an institution explains features the art-only account leaves as mysteries: why this tragedy keeps interrupting its love story with proclamations, feasts, funerals, and trials, the public ceremonies its audience knew from life and paid to see reworked, and why its ending hands the story to a presiding magistrate.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch historicism primer to argue that the play belongs to its moment?

An essay should start from the primer's strongest rows, the ones whose channels can be documented, and build outward. Open with the plague: Friar John's sealed house against the orders that closed houses and theatres in the early 1590s. Add the marriage system, where Capulet speaks consent in one act and command in another, and the feud as the state's unfinished business with armed households. Cite the passages by act, scene, and line, name the documents each passage touches, and close by granting the wonder objection, that the orchard scene exceeds every context, since an argument that states its own limit is the one a marker, like a method, can trust.
