The Domestic Violence Act, formally the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, is built on a single deliberate refusal. It refuses to treat violence inside a home as a problem that punishment alone can solve. A prosecution can send a violent husband to prison and still leave his wife with no house to live in, no money for medical treatment, no order keeping him away from her workplace and no legal grip on the earnings and property she was driven from. The statute answers that gap by creating a civil proceeding in which a magistrate can order a respondent to stop, to leave, to pay and to let the applicant stay, and it does so without requiring her to prove a crime beyond reasonable doubt or to wait for a criminal trial to end.

![A magistrate's courtroom and the shared household at the centre of the Domestic Violence Act's civil remedy - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-110.webp)

The distinction sounds technical and is anything but. A criminal statute asks the state to prove guilt and then imposes a sentence; the person harmed is a witness in the state's case, and the outcome belongs to the state. This statute asks a different question: what does the woman in front of the court need in order to be safe and to live with dignity from the day the order is passed? Its answers are practical. She may need the violence to stop and be kept stopped. She may need to remain in the house she has lived in, even though the house stands in someone else's name. She may need money for the earnings she lost, the injuries she carries and the household goods that were taken from her. She may need temporary custody of her children and compensation for the harm itself. The Act collects those needs into five kinds of order and builds a small administrative machine, centred on an officer called the Protection Officer, to carry a woman from her first approach to a magistrate's courtroom with her safety planned for rather than left to chance.

## The Domestic Violence Act as a civil remedy

The civil character of the statute is not an accident of drafting but its organising idea, and everything else in it follows from that idea. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 is Act 43 of 2005. It received the assent of the President on 13 September 2005 and was brought into force on 26 October 2006, together with the rules framed under it, once the state governments had been given time to put the supporting machinery in place. It contains five chapters and thirty-seven sections. It extends, as enacted, to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, and the reorganisation of 2019 carried the central protective statutes into the successor Union territories, so the Act forms part of the law across the country.

A proceeding under the Act begins with an application, not a charge sheet. The applicant is called the aggrieved person, the person against whom relief is sought is called the respondent, and the magistrate who hears the matter is a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class or a Metropolitan Magistrate. No one is convicted at the end of such a proceeding and no one is acquitted. The magistrate either grants the orders sought, grants some of them, or declines to grant them, and the orders operate on conduct and property rather than on liberty. The Act creates exactly one offence, and it is not the violence itself: Section 31 makes the breach of a protection order, or of an interim protection order, an offence punishable with imprisonment that may extend to one year, or with a fine that may extend to twenty thousand rupees, or with both. The violence is addressed by restraint, residence, money and compensation; only disobedience of the court's own order is answered with punishment.

That design has consequences for proof and for pace. Because the proceeding is civil in character, the magistrate acts on satisfaction reached on the material before the court, and Section 23 empowers the court to grant interim and even ex parte orders on the strength of an affidavit where a prima facie case is disclosed, so that protection does not have to wait for a contested hearing. Because the relief is meant to arrive while it can still change the applicant's situation, the Act writes speed into its own text, requiring the first hearing to be fixed ordinarily within three days of the application reaching the court and requiring the magistrate to endeavour to dispose of the application within sixty days of that first hearing. Whether those timelines hold in practice is a separate and harder question, taken up later in this article; the design itself is unambiguous about the urgency it intends.

### Why does the Act protect only women as applicants?

The Act reserves the position of aggrieved person to a woman, and the choice was deliberate. Its drafters proceeded from the recorded pattern of violence inside Indian homes, in which the overwhelming weight of physical, sexual, emotional and economic abuse within domestic relationships falls on women, and from the economic dependence that traps them.

A remedy aimed at that pattern was framed for the class that bears it, in the same way that protective legislation elsewhere in Indian law addresses the group a documented harm concentrates upon. The economic dependence in question is not an abstraction: a woman whose earnings are taken, whose ornaments are held by others and who has no title to the house she lives in cannot convert a legal right into a course of action without a statute that brings the court to her situation, which is the conversion this Act attempts.

Critics of the design argue that violence within a home can be suffered by men as well, and that a statute closed to male applicants leaves such cases to the general criminal law alone. Defenders of the design, including the women's organisations that campaigned for the statute, answer that the general law remains open to every complainant, while this Act was built to correct a specific and evidenced asymmetry: the woman who is beaten or impoverished inside her own home and who, without a residence right and monetary relief, has no practical remedy at all. Parliament accepted that reasoning, and the courts have treated the women-only frame as a considered legislative classification rather than an oversight. The argument continues in public debate, and this article returns to it when the counter-reading of the statute is stated in full.

## The architecture of the Act

The thirty-seven sections fall into five chapters, and the sequence of the chapters is itself a statement of the design. The first chapter is preliminary: the short title, the extent of the Act, and the definitions, which are placed at the front because the definitions do the statute's real work. The second chapter contains the definition of domestic violence in Section 3 and a short set of provisions about information and immediate assistance, the duties to inform the aggrieved person of her rights under Section 5, the obligation of shelter homes under Section 6 and of medical facilities under Section 7. The third chapter builds the machinery, covering the appointment and duties of Protection Officers and the registration and duties of service providers. The fourth chapter is the procedural and remedial core, running from the application under Section 12 through the five reliefs to the alteration of orders, reliefs in other proceedings, jurisdiction, procedure and appeal. The fifth chapter is miscellaneous: the status of Protection Officers as public servants, the single offence and its character, the penalty for an officer's dereliction, the protection of good faith action, the rule that the Act operates in addition to other laws, and the Central Government's power to make rules.

Two structural choices in that arrangement repay attention. The definitions precede everything, which means the Act argues about scope at the level of meaning rather than at the level of remedy: whether a woman is protected is decided by the words "domestic relationship", "shared household" and "domestic violence", and the case law of the statute is predictably a case law of those three definitions. And the machinery chapters precede the procedure chapter, which means Parliament legislated the delivery system before the courtroom steps, a sequence that reflects the drafters' judgment that the applicant's problem was less the absence of rights than the absence of a route to them. The rules framed by the Central Government under Section 37, notified with the Act's commencement, supply the prescribed forms, including the domestic incident report, and the qualifications and terms for Protection Officers and service providers, so the working detail of the system sits in subordinate legislation that states administer.

The constitutional ground of the design is Article 15(3) of the Constitution, which provides that nothing in the equality article shall prevent the state from making any special provision for women and children. A statute that reserves its applicant class to women is the kind of provision that clause exists to permit, and the classification has not been held against the Act; when the equality guarantee was invoked in Harsora, it was invoked to widen the respondent class, not to strike the applicant class. The Act also draws quietly on the directive in Article 21 jurisprudence that treats dignity and bodily integrity as components of the right to life, a line of authority the courts have used in reading the statute's protections broadly, and on India's obligations under the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, which the preamble tradition of the drafting years treated as part of the statute's purpose. The Act is therefore best understood as protective legislation with a constitutional shelter for its asymmetry, and its litigation history has tested its definitions far more than its validity.

## The harm the statute was built to answer

Violence inside a home differs from violence between strangers in ways that decide what a useful law looks like. The parties share a roof, a kitchen, a bank account and often children. The harm is rarely a single incident that can be photographed and prosecuted; it is a pattern that can run for years, combining blows with control of money, confiscation of documents and ornaments, threats, humiliation and the slow closing of every exit. A woman in that position frequently cannot leave, because the house is not hers on paper, because her earnings have been taken or never permitted, because her natal family may not take her back, and because the children are in the same house. A law that offers only the punishment of the man she lives with offers her a choice between enduring the violence and destroying the household she depends on, which is why so many complaints were withdrawn and why the criminal route, though necessary, was never sufficient.

The scale of the harm is documented in the National Family Health Survey. In the fifth round of that survey, NFHS-5, conducted from 2019 to 2021, 29.3 per cent of ever-married women aged eighteen to forty-nine reported having experienced spousal violence in physical or sexual form, and the figure rose to about thirty-two per cent when emotional violence was counted alongside. The survey also recorded how rarely that violence reaches any institution: only about fourteen per cent of the women who had experienced physical or sexual violence had ever sought help from any source. Those two findings together describe the terrain the statute works on. The violence is common, it is concentrated within marriage and the household, and the formal legal system is the last resort of a small minority, reached only when private endurance has failed.

A statute designed for that terrain has to lower the cost of approaching the law. It has to work without a lawyer being indispensable, without a police case being a precondition, without the applicant having to leave the house as the price of safety, and without demanding that she first prove a serious crime to a criminal standard before anyone will order the violence to stop. Each of those requirements shaped the Act: the application route through a Protection Officer, the freedom to approach the magistrate directly, the residence right that protects occupation rather than ownership, and the civil standard on which interim orders can be granted within days. The statute is best read as an attempt to redesign the point of contact between a woman in a violent home and the authority of the state.

## Reading the evidence on prevalence

The survey figures used in this article need their limits stated, because the Act's constituency is wider than the survey's frame. NFHS-5 measures spousal violence against ever-married women between eighteen and forty-nine, which is the population a health survey can reach with a domestic violence module administered under privacy protocols. The Act, by contrast, protects any woman in a domestic relationship: the never-married partner in a relationship in the nature of marriage, the mother complaining against a son or a daughter-in-law, the sister in a joint family household, the widow in her matrimonial home. None of those applicants appears in a spousal violence figure, and the Act's true constituency is therefore larger and differently composed from the 29.3 per cent headline. The survey also records experience, not adjudicated fact, and its help-seeking figure records approaches to any source, in which family predominates, so the share of affected women who reach a legal institution of any kind is smaller than fourteen per cent. None of this weakens the figure's use for the purpose to which this article puts it, which is to establish the scale of the documented harm and the rarity of institutional contact. It does mean that arguments conducted by trading the headline percentage, in either direction, are arguments about a proxy, and the statute's own litigation record, its applications, orders and breaches, remains the evidence that would describe the Act itself, in a form that no published national series yet assembles.

## How the statute came to be made

The civil model did not emerge from a committee room in a single step. For two decades before 2005, the principal legal response to cruelty inside marriage was Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code, inserted in 1983, which made cruelty by a husband or his relatives a criminal offence. That provision mattered, and it gave police and prosecutors a tool against conduct that had earlier been treated as a private matter. But its limits were structural. A prosecution, even a successful one, produced a conviction and a sentence; it produced no residence order, no maintenance tailored to the violence, no custody arrangement and no compensation, and it moved at the pace of a criminal trial while the applicant's daily situation remained untouched. Women's organisations documented case after case in which a woman had won the criminal argument and lost her home.

The demand for a civil statute was pressed through the nineteen nineties by women's groups and by the National Commission for Women, and a draft bill was prepared with the Lawyers Collective, the organisation associated with the advocate Indira Jaising, playing a leading role in shaping its architecture. A bill was introduced in 2002, but it drew sustained criticism from women's organisations for departures from the draft they had developed, it was examined by a Parliamentary Standing Committee, and it lapsed when the thirteenth Lok Sabha was dissolved in February 2004. The government that took office after the 2004 election returned to the subject, consulted the women's movement on the contested points, and introduced a revised bill in the Lok Sabha on 22 August 2005. The bill passed the Lok Sabha on 24 August 2005 and the Rajya Sabha on 29 August 2005, a passage measured in days that reflected how much of the argument had been settled during the long drafting years before it.

Commencement waited a further year, and the interval is instructive. The Act could not operate on signature alone, because its promises run through Protection Officers to be appointed, service providers to be registered, shelter homes and medical facilities to be notified, and magistrates to be oriented to a new kind of application. The Act and its rules were brought into force together on 26 October 2006, once the frame for that machinery existed. The gap between enactment and commencement, and the unevenness of the machinery that followed, form the background to the implementation record discussed later: the statute was designed as a delivered service, and services depend on the state that delivers them.

The year's interval also carried a quieter lesson that the monitoring reports would later document from the other side. Commencement was a single national event, but appointment, registration and notification were acts of thirty-odd state and Union territory administrations, each moving at its own pace and with its own budget, so the Act began its life as a uniform text laid over an ununiform apparatus. A woman in a district of Delhi or Andhra Pradesh, where the first report found full-time officers, met a different statute in practice from a woman in a district where the charge had been handed to an already burdened official as an additional duty, although the sections each of them invoked were identical. That divergence between the law's text and its local embodiment is not a drafting failure, and the drafters had anticipated it by writing the duties in imperative terms; it is the standing condition of welfare legislation in a federal system, and the Act's history is the record of how far imperative drafting can go toward equalising it.

## The drafting choices that were argued over

Four choices in the drafting were contested at the time and explain the statute's present shape, and each left a mark that the courts later had to interpret. The first was the choice of forum. The bill's architects placed the remedy before magistrates rather than before the family courts or a new tribunal. Magistrates existed in every district, worked to criminal procedure timelines, and stood in an established relation with the police who would have to enforce protection orders and act on breaches; a family court route would have concentrated the remedy in fewer places and in a system designed for matrimonial causes rather than emergency protection. The cost of the choice is the one the implementation record shows: applications under the Act compete for the attention of courts carrying heavy criminal dockets, and the summary character the drafters intended depends on listing practices the magistracy does not always sustain.

The second choice was the width of the definition. A narrower bill, confined to physical violence, was available as a model, and the 2002 bill's critics from the women's movement charged it with diluting the draft that the movement had developed with the Lawyers Collective, including on the reach of the definition and the machinery. The enacted definition's four classes, and the detail of the economic abuse Explanation, are the residue of that argument: the movement's position, that violence in a household is exercised through money and threats as much as through blows, prevailed in the text. The third choice was the residence right. Giving a woman a statutory right to remain in a house she does not own was the provision property lawyers found hardest, and it is the provision whose judicial narrowing and restoration dominate the case law; the drafters accepted the friction deliberately, judging that a protective statute without a residence right would order everything except the thing the applicant most needed. The fourth choice was the abstention from offences. A version of the statute that criminalised the violence itself was possible and was not taken, because criminalisation would have imported the criminal trial, its standard of proof and its pace into a remedy designed for immediacy, and because Section 498A already occupied the punitive ground. The single offence of breach is the hinge between the two systems: the civil system restrains, and only disobedience of the restraint is handed to the criminal system.

## What the Act counts as domestic violence

Section 3 of the Act contains the definition on which everything else rests, and the definition is written as conduct rather than as a list of crimes. An act, omission, commission or conduct of the respondent amounts to domestic violence in four situations. The first is conduct that harms or injures or endangers the health, safety, life, limb or well-being of the aggrieved person, whether the well-being affected is mental or physical, and the section states expressly that this includes physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse and economic abuse. The second is harassment of the aggrieved person with a view to coercing her, or any person related to her, to meet an unlawful demand for dowry or for other property or valuable security. The third covers conduct that has the effect of threatening the aggrieved person, or anyone related to her, by conduct of either of those two kinds. The fourth is a residuary clause reaching conduct that otherwise injures or causes harm, physical or mental, to the aggrieved person.

Two features of that drafting deserve attention. The definition is organised around effect on the woman rather than around the penal category the conduct might also fit, so an act that no criminal provision describes precisely, such as the persistent confiscation of wages or the destruction of household property to discipline her, can still ground an application. And the definition reaches omissions and threats as well as completed acts, which reflects the lived structure of abuse inside a home, where a threat believed is a restraint obeyed and where neglect of medical care or food can injure as reliably as a blow.

The statute then unpacks the four named classes of abuse in an Explanation, and the Explanation is where the definition earns its width. Physical abuse is any act or conduct of a nature that causes bodily pain, harm or danger to life, limb or health, or impairs the health or development of the aggrieved person, and it expressly includes assault, criminal intimidation and criminal force, so the criminal vocabulary is borrowed to describe conduct rather than to require prosecution of it. Sexual abuse is any conduct of a sexual nature that abuses, humiliates, degrades or otherwise violates the dignity of a woman. Verbal and emotional abuse is defined through insults, ridicule, humiliation and name-calling, through insults directed at her for not having a child or not having a male child, and through repeated threats to cause physical pain to any person in whom she is interested. Economic abuse receives the longest treatment of all, and its detail matters because money is the mechanism by which much domestic control is exercised.

Economic abuse, as the Explanation writes it, covers the deprivation of economic or financial resources to which the aggrieved person is entitled under any law or custom, or which she requires out of necessity, including household necessities for her and her children and her stridhan, the property given to a woman that is hers in her own right. It covers the disposal of household effects and the alienation of assets, movable or immovable, in which she has an interest or is entitled to use by virtue of the domestic relationship. It covers the restriction or prohibition of access to resources and facilities she is entitled to use or enjoy, including access to the shared household itself. Retention of stridhan has been treated by the courts as economic abuse of a continuing character, so that a woman deprived of her ornaments and property does not lose her remedy with the passage of time while the deprivation continues.

The four classes differ in how they present to a court, and the differences shape the evidence an application assembles. Physical abuse leaves the most legible record, which is one reason the Act builds medical examination into its machinery: a woman taken to a notified medical facility under Section 7 acquires a contemporaneous record from a source outside the household. Sexual abuse within marriage sits in a distinct legal position in Indian law more broadly, since the general criminal law has historically treated the marital relationship as an exception in its central sexual offence, and the Act's inclusion of sexual abuse as a ground of civil relief gives a magistrate purchase on conduct that a criminal court may not reach in the same form; the relief is restraint and protection rather than conviction, but the conduct is named and restrained, which the applicant may experience as the operative difference. Verbal and emotional abuse is proved by pattern, by the testimony of the applicant and of those who witnessed the insults, the ridicule or the threats, and its inclusion in the definition is the statute's recognition that the purpose of such conduct is dominion and its effect is injury, whether or not a hand is raised. Economic abuse is proved by documents and their absence: the account she cannot operate, the salary that is collected from her, the stridhan that is withheld, the household necessities that are denied. A definition that had confined itself to the first class would have missed the mechanisms by which dependence is manufactured and maintained inside a household, which is why the Explanation gives the fourth class its length.

It follows that a single act can suffice. The definition speaks of any act of domestic violence, and an application is not required to plead a course of conduct, although the material placed before the magistrate will usually show one. The breadth is functional rather than rhetorical: the applicant comes to court at the point of crisis, frequently after one incident that follows years of lesser coercion, and a statute that demanded proof of a settled pattern as a threshold would withhold interim protection from the cases whose urgency is greatest. The magistrate's task is then to assess credibility and risk on the whole material, affidavit, incident report, medical record and testimony, and to fit the orders to what is shown, which is the discretionary work the later sections equip the court to do.

The verbal and emotional class repays a moment's attention for the specificity of its examples, because they disclose the social world the drafters were describing. The Explanation names insults and ridicule, and then names insults or ridicule directed at the woman for not having a child, or for not having a male child, a specification that would be unintelligible in a society without son preference and that locates the statute in the households it governs. It names, likewise, repeated threats to cause physical pain to any person in whom the aggrieved person is interested, which reaches the technique of control exercised through her parents, her siblings or her children rather than through her own body. A definition borrowed from a foreign model code would not have contained either clause; both are evidence that the width of Section 3 was argued from Indian material, by the movement organisations that had documented these patterns in case work over the preceding decades.

The dowry limb of the definition performs a junction inside the statute. Harassment aimed at extracting dowry or other property is part of domestic violence under Section 3, while the giving and taking of dowry is separately an offence under the Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961, whose prohibition and penalty scheme is set out in the companion study of the [dowry prohibition statute](/2015/11/15/dowry-prohibition-act-1961-explained/). The same demand can therefore open proceedings under both statutes at once, one civil and one criminal, and Section 36 of this Act confirms that its remedies stand in addition to, and not in derogation of, the provisions of any other law. The applicant is not asked to elect between protection and prosecution; the design assumes the two may be needed together.

The placement of property inside the definition also explains the statute's connection to women's property rights more generally. A generation of reform, including the changes made to succession law by the [Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005](/2016/10/15/hindu-succession-amendment-2005/), enlarged the property a woman owns in her own right, and the economic abuse provisions of this Act protect her enjoyment of that property inside the household, where ownership on paper and control in fact often part company. A woman may be a coparcener by law and still be denied access to the income of the property, or hold stridhan that is kept from her; Section 3 treats that denial itself as domestic violence, without waiting for it to be accompanied by physical harm.

## Who is protected: the aggrieved person and the domestic relationship

The Act's protection is organised by relationship rather than by place or by marital status alone. Section 2(a) defines the aggrieved person as any woman who is, or has been, in a domestic relationship with the respondent and who alleges to have been subjected to any act of domestic violence by the respondent. Two elements carry the weight. The applicant must be a woman, and she must stand, or must have stood, in a domestic relationship with the person against whom she complains. The words "has been" do quiet but important work: a woman does not lose the Act's protection because the relationship has ended, because she has left the house, or because she has been driven out of it, and the courts have confirmed that neither a decree of judicial separation nor the simple fact of living apart extinguishes her status under the statute.

Section 2(f) defines the domestic relationship as a relationship between two persons who live, or have at any point of time lived, together in a shared household, where they are related by consanguinity, by marriage, by a relationship in the nature of marriage, by adoption, or as family members living together as a joint family. The drafting again reaches backward in time: cohabitation at any point suffices, and the Supreme Court in Prabha Tyagi v Kamlesh Devi, decided in 2022, held that an aggrieved person need not actually be residing with the respondents at the time the violence is alleged or the application filed, because the definition is satisfied by having lived together at any point and by her right to live in the shared household. The relationship net is wide. It covers wives and widows, mothers, sisters, daughters and daughters-in-law, women in adoptive relationships, and members of a joint family, provided the shared household and the relationship are both made out.

Two applicant profiles within that net are less widely understood than the wife's, and they matter to the statute's reach. A mother may be an aggrieved person against her own son, or against a daughter-in-law after Harsora, where the violence or the economic deprivation, the withholding of maintenance, the pressure to transfer property, the denial of access to her own rooms, occurs within the household they share; the Act gives her a route that does not require her to invoke the senior citizens legislation or to sue her child in a civil court, and it explains the otherwise puzzling overlap between this Act and the senior citizens statute that Vanitha had to untangle. A widow in her matrimonial home is the second profile: her domestic relationship with her husband has ended by death, but her relationships with the members of the household continue, her residence is in the shared household, and her exposure to dispossession classically begins at widowhood, which is precisely when the Section 17 right, operating against the relatives who share the house with her, does its work. The definition's inclusion of relationships by consanguinity and of family members living together as a joint family is what carries both profiles inside the Act, and litigation by mothers and widows forms a substantial and comparatively unpublicised part of the Act's docket.

The phrase "relationship in the nature of marriage" carries the definition beyond formal marriage, and it is the phrase the courts have had to construe with the most care, because Parliament extended protection to women in live-in relationships without defining where such a relationship begins. In D. Velusamy v D. Patchaiammal, decided in 2010, the Supreme Court held that the expression bears a meaning akin to a common law marriage. The court set out the indicia it would look for: the couple must hold themselves out to society as being akin to spouses; they must be of legal age to marry; they must be otherwise qualified to enter a legal marriage, including being unmarried; and they must have voluntarily cohabited and held themselves out as akin to spouses for a significant period of time in a shared household. The court was explicit about what falls outside: a relationship in which a man keeps a woman largely for sexual purposes or as a servant does not qualify, and neither do weekend arrangements or passing liaisons, because no shared household in the statutory sense is formed.

In Indra Sarma v V.K.V. Sarma, decided in 2013, the Supreme Court returned to the question and offered a broader set of guidelines, stating that the factors it listed were indicative rather than exhaustive. The guidelines direct attention to the duration of the relationship, the existence of a shared household, the pooling of resources and financial arrangements, the domestic arrangements of the household, the sexual relationship, the presence of children, the couple's socialisation in public, and their intention and conduct in holding themselves out. Applying those factors, the court held that a woman who had lived with a married man for some eighteen years, knowing throughout that he was married, was not in a relationship in the nature of marriage within the Act, because one of the Velusamy conditions, that the parties be qualified to marry, was absent. The judgment is equally remembered for what the court asked of the legislature: it recorded that women in relationships falling outside the Act's protection, and the children of such relationships, stood exposed, and it called on Parliament to consider legislation addressed to them.

Read together, the two decisions draw a deliberate boundary. Protection follows the outward and inward substance of a marital household, its duration, its shared resources and its public presentation, rather than the mere fact of cohabitation or intimacy. A third decision, Chanmuniya v Virendra Kumar Singh Kushwaha, decided in 2011, pressed for an even wider reading of the phrase and referred aspects of the question to a larger bench, so the boundary has been argued from both directions; the Velusamy and Indra Sarma framework remains the operative statement of the test. For the applicant in a live-in relationship, the practical lesson of the case law is that her application should plead and prove the indicia: duration, a shared household, pooled finances, shared domestic life and public holding out, rather than relying on the relationship's existence alone.

## Who can be made to answer: the respondent

Section 2(q) defines the respondent as any adult male person who is, or has been, in a domestic relationship with the aggrieved person and against whom relief has been sought, and a proviso added that a complaint could also be filed against a relative of the husband or of the male partner. As enacted, therefore, the definition carried the words "adult male" at its centre, and for the Act's first decade applications against women, most often mothers-in-law and sisters-in-law alleged to have joined in the abuse, ran into the argument that only adult males could be respondents.

That limitation ended in Hiral P. Harsora v Kusum Narottamdas Harsora. The litigation itself arose between women, in a dispute in which the respondents were the applicant's mother-in-law and sisters-in-law, and the question reached the Supreme Court in 2016. The Court held that confining respondents to adult males could not be reconciled with the object of the Act or with the equality guarantee of Article 14 of the Constitution. Its reasoning followed the mischief: domestic violence, as Section 3 defines it, can be inflicted by women as well as by men, and complaints in actual households are frequently made against female relatives; a definition that immunised them would leave the applicant protected against only part of the conduct the statute exists to restrain. The Court accordingly struck the words "adult male" from Section 2(q), holding that the proviso to the definition became otiose once those words were removed. Since that judgment, a respondent under the Act may be any person in a domestic relationship with the aggrieved person, male or female, adult in the sense the court's reading leaves operative through the definition's remaining structure, and the applications the Act receives reflect that reality, with mothers-in-law among the most common respondents after husbands.

One qualification survives intact inside the residence provisions: Section 19 carries a proviso that no order directing a respondent to remove himself from the shared household shall be passed against a woman. The proviso sits in visible tension with the widened definition of respondent, and the courts have treated it as a specific protection written into the residence power itself rather than as a limit on who may be a respondent for other orders. A female respondent may therefore face protection orders, monetary relief and compensation, while the particular direction that she vacate the household is withheld by the statute's own text.

The Harsora reasoning is worth following for what it reveals about how the courts read this statute. The bench did not treat the words "adult male" as an accidental survival to be excised on a technicality; it reasoned from the statute's protective purpose and from the facts of Indian households, in which a mother-in-law may control the kitchen, the keys and the money, and may be alleged to have joined in or directed the conduct of which complaint is made. A definition that allowed orders against the son but none against the mother, on identical allegations, would have produced proceedings in which the magistrate could restrain part of a household's conduct and not the rest, and the Court refused that incoherence. The judgment also illustrates the Act's equality geometry: the applicant class is closed to men by design and sustained as a special provision for women, while the respondent class, once the case was examined, was required by Article 14 to track the conduct the Act restrains rather than the sex of the person who commits it. Protection is asymmetric; liability is not.

## The shared household and the right to reside

Section 2(s) defines the shared household as a household where the aggrieved person lives, or at any stage has lived, in a domestic relationship, either singly or along with the respondent, and it includes a household owned or tenanted either jointly by the parties or by either of them, in respect of which either party has any right, title, interest or equity, as well as a household that forms part of a joint family of which the respondent is a member, irrespective of whether the respondent or the aggrieved person has any right, title or interest in it. Section 17 then states the right the definition supports, in words that deserve to be read without abbreviation: every woman in a domestic relationship shall have the right to reside in the shared household, whether or not she has any right, title or beneficial interest in it, and she shall not be evicted or excluded from it by the respondent or any of his relatives except in accordance with the procedure established by law.

That right is the statute's most distinctive provision, and its meaning was fought over for fourteen years. The first major construction came in S.R. Batra v Taruna Batra, decided by the Supreme Court in December 2006, within weeks of the Act's commencement. The house in dispute belonged to the husband's mother. The Court read the definition narrowly, holding that a shared household could only be a house belonging to or taken on rent by the husband, or a house belonging to the joint family of which the husband is a member, and that a daughter-in-law's claim to alternate accommodation could be made against her husband alone and not against her parents-in-law. On that reading, the great majority of Indian households, in which a married woman lives in a house owned by her husband's parents, fell outside the residence right altogether, and the provision's promise shrank to the minority of cases in which the husband himself held title or tenancy.

### Can the home of the husband's parents be a shared household?

Yes. In Satish Chander Ahuja v Sneha Ahuja, decided on 15 October 2020, a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court overruled the narrower reading given in Batra and held that a shared household includes a house belonging to a relative of the husband in which the woman has lived in a domestic relationship. Title is not the test.

The questions are whether the house was the household in which the parties lived together in a domestic relationship, and whether the woman lived there in that relationship, not whose name stands on the deed. The litigation itself illustrates the class of case at stake: it arose from a suit by a father-in-law seeking possession of the family house in Delhi from his daughter-in-law, and it reached the Supreme Court because the narrower reading in Batra would have ended her claim at the threshold, on the single ground that the house was his.

The fourteen years between the two decisions were the years in which the Act was most heavily used, and the Batra reading governed throughout them, so its consequences are part of the statute's history and not a footnote. Daughters-in-law in the commonest Indian housing arrangement, the parental home of the husband, were told that the house was not a shared household at all; applications were dismissed at the threshold on title documents rather than heard on the facts of the violence; and the protection the residence order could still give was confined to restraining dispossession from houses the husband owned or tenanted, or to claims for alternate accommodation against the husband personally. The monitoring literature and the High Court decisions of the period record magistrates and judges straining against the reading in cases where its results were starkest, and the professional consensus by the time Ahuja was argued was that Batra had misread the definition, however respectable the property-law instincts behind it. The case for correction was put on the text: Section 2(s) expressly includes a household that forms part of a joint family of which the respondent is a member, irrespective of whether either party has any right, title or interest in it, and a construction that made title decisive rendered those words decoration.

The Ahuja bench, comprising Justices Ashok Bhushan, R. Subhash Reddy and M.R. Shah, reached that conclusion from the text of Section 2(s) itself, observing that the definition Batra had narrowed contains no requirement that the respondent own or tenant the house, and that reading such a requirement into it defeated the definition's express inclusion of households in which neither party has any right, title or interest. The bench was equally careful about the right's limits. The right to reside is not indefeasible, and a claim under the Act does not end the inquiry: courts must weigh the competing claims of everyone in the household, and the judgment recorded that aged parents-in-law living in their own house are entitled to live peacefully, with the circumstances of each case deciding how residence, alternate accommodation and rent are to be arranged between the parties. The holding widens the door; it does not predetermine what order is just once the applicant is through it.

Two months later, in S. Vanitha v Deputy Commissioner, Bengaluru Urban District, decided on 15 December 2020, a three-judge bench comprising Justices D.Y. Chandrachud, Indu Malhotra and Indira Banerjee addressed the collision between the residence right and the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007, under which tribunals may order the eviction of children from a senior citizen's property. The case arose from an eviction order obtained against a daughter-in-law who had pending claims under this Act. The Court held that the two statutes, each a welfare measure, must be construed harmoniously, and that the right to residence under Section 17 cannot be defeated by the simple expedient of securing an order of eviction through the summary procedure of the senior citizens legislation. The tribunal or court faced with competing claims must consider both, and Section 36 of this Act, which keeps its remedies additional to other law, does not subordinate the residence right to the other statute's machinery. The practical consequence is that a Section 17 claim neither automatically evicts the in-laws nor is automatically extinguished by their seniority; it must be heard.

## The support architecture: Protection Officers, reports and service providers

A civil remedy fails at the doorstep if the applicant must find the court, frame the application and arrange her own safety without assistance. The Act therefore builds a support structure around the magistrate, and its central figure is the Protection Officer. Section 8 requires every state government to appoint, by notification, as many Protection Officers in each district as it considers necessary, for the areas the notification specifies. Section 9 lists the officer's duties, and the list is the operational heart of the statute: to assist the magistrate in the discharge of the magistrate's functions; to make a domestic incident report to the magistrate on receiving a complaint of domestic violence, and to forward copies to the police officer in charge of the local police station and to the service providers in the area; to move the application on the aggrieved person's behalf where she so desires; to ensure that she is provided legal aid under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, a statute whose free legal aid machinery is examined in the study of the [legal services authorities framework](/2021/06/15/legal-services-authorities-act-1987/); to maintain a list of the service providers, shelter homes and medical facilities available in the area; to have the aggrieved person sheltered and medically examined where she requires it; and to ensure that orders for monetary relief are complied with and executed in accordance with the Code of Criminal Procedure.

The domestic incident report is the document around which this machinery turns. It is the record made when a complaint of domestic violence is received, prepared by the Protection Officer, or by a service provider the woman approaches first, in the form the rules prescribe, and forwarded to the magistrate and the police. Its function is evidentiary and practical at once: it captures the complaint close to the event, identifies the parties and the household, records injuries and the assistance sought, and gives the magistrate a structured starting point for interim orders. Section 12 adds that where a domestic incident report has been received, the magistrate shall take it into consideration before passing an order on the application. In Prabha Tyagi v Kamlesh Devi, the Supreme Court clarified the weight of that instruction: consideration of a report that exists is required, but the existence of a report is not a precondition to relief, and a magistrate may pass interim, ex parte and final orders without one, since many applicants reach the court without any report having been made.

Other doors stand open beside the Protection Officer. Any person who has reason to believe that an act of domestic violence has been, or is being, or is likely to be committed may give information about it to the Protection Officer or the police, and Section 4 protects a person who acts in good faith from any civil or criminal liability for giving that information. Police officers, Protection Officers, service providers and magistrates who receive a complaint carry a duty under Section 5 to inform the aggrieved person, in a language she understands, of her right to apply under the Act, of the services available to her including shelter and medical aid, of her right to free legal services, and of her right to file a criminal complaint for cruelty where the facts support one. Service providers, which are voluntary organisations and entities registered with the state government under Section 10, may record a domestic incident report, arrange medical examination and secure shelter for a woman who approaches them. Shelter homes must provide shelter on the request of a Protection Officer or service provider under Section 6, and notified medical facilities must provide medical aid under Section 7. Section 11 lays corresponding duties on governments: wide publicity for the Act, periodic sensitisation and training of police and judicial officers, and coordination between the ministries and departments concerned with its delivery.

The paper trail this machinery generates is substantial: incident reports, applications, medical records, affidavits, service reports and copies of orders moving between the officer, the police station, the service provider and the court. Practitioners and applicants assembling that record for filing or for appeal frequently work from scanned bundles and presentation-style compilations of exhibits, and a [browser-based PowerPoint viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) that opens such presentation files directly in the browser is one practical route to reviewing the compiled record without the originating software installed.

### Who helps a woman reach the magistrate?

She does not have to find the court alone. The Act routes her through a Protection Officer appointed for her district, who records a domestic incident report, arranges shelter and medical examination, secures her free legal aid and can move the application on her behalf.

A registered service provider can do the same groundwork: it may record the incident report, arrange her medical examination and see her sheltered. A police officer who receives her complaint must inform her of these rights under Section 5, in a language she understands, and any person who has reason to believe that domestic violence has been or is likely to be committed may report it to the officer or the police in good faith without incurring civil or criminal liability for doing so.

The design deserves one further observation. Protection Officers and members of service providers are treated as public servants under Section 30, and Section 33 attaches a penalty to an officer who fails or refuses to discharge the duties the magistrate directs, without sufficient cause: imprisonment that may extend to one year, or a fine that may extend to twenty thousand rupees, or both. Section 35 protects action taken in good faith under the Act. The statute thus disciplines its own machinery, at least on paper, recognising that a relief system dependent on officials must give the applicant some answer when the official does nothing.

The officer's work, followed through a single application, shows why the office carries the statute. Before any application exists, the officer is the recipient of information under Section 4 and the maker of the domestic incident report, which means the officer is the first state official to hear the account in a structured form and to record it. When the application is filed, the officer assists the magistrate, serves the notice under Section 13, and is the person the court holds responsible for knowing where the applicant is sheltered and whether she has been medically examined. When orders are passed, copies travel to the officer under Section 24, and the execution of monetary relief is the officer's charge under Section 9. At each stage the officer is the connective tissue between institutions that do not ordinarily communicate: the police station, the hospital, the shelter home, the legal services authority and the court. A system designed this way is exactly as strong as the officer's time and standing, which is why the monitoring finding that the charge is so often additional, held by an official whose substantive post lies elsewhere, is not an administrative detail but the central fact of the Act's working life. The sixth Staying Alive report's launch discussion, at which the hundred crore budgetary provision was announced, treated the conversion of these additional charges into a dedicated cadre as the reform on which the rest depends, and subsequent assessments have repeated the point in substantially the same terms.

The duties Section 11 lays on governments complete the architecture, and they are the provisions least often read. The Central and state governments are to give the Act wide publicity through public media, to take steps for periodic sensitisation and awareness training of police officers and members of the judicial services on the issues the Act addresses, to promote coordination between the ministries and departments dealing with law, home affairs, health and human resources, and to have protocols for the pre-litigation and post-litigation stages prepared and placed in the hands of every service provider. The sensitisation duty matters because the Act asks of its operators a series of unfamiliar judgments: a police officer trained on offences must learn that the applicant may not want a prosecution, a magistrate trained on trials must learn to assemble civil relief on an affidavit within three days, and a Protection Officer drawn from a revenue or welfare cadre must learn the execution of court orders. Where that training is delivered, the machinery's other defects are partly compensated by the operators' understanding; where it is not, the statute is administered by officials reasoning from the habits of other laws, which is one documented source of the preconditions, demands for police complaints and insistence on reports that the Supreme Court's later judgments had to strike down.

## The application to the magistrate

Section 12 is the doorway into the court. An application for one or more of the Act's reliefs may be presented to the magistrate by the aggrieved person herself, or by a Protection Officer on her behalf, or by any other person on her behalf, so a relative, a friend or a service provider may set the proceeding in motion where the woman cannot. The application states the reliefs sought, and the magistrate's jurisdiction under Section 27 follows the geography of the applicant's life rather than the respondent's convenience: the court within whose local limits the aggrieved person permanently or temporarily resides, carries on business or is employed may hear her, as may the court where the respondent resides or where the cause of action has arisen. A woman who has fled to her parents' home or to a shelter in another district is not sent back to the respondent's district to seek protection.

The timelines the section prescribes have already been noticed, but their arrangement is worth stating as a sequence, because the sequence is the statute's promise of pace. The magistrate shall fix the first date of hearing, which shall not ordinarily be beyond three days from the date of receipt of the application. Notice of that hearing goes to the respondent through the Protection Officer under Section 13, to be served within the time the section prescribes, ordinarily a maximum of two days from receipt of the notice. At the other end, Section 12(5) directs that the magistrate shall endeavour to dispose of the application within sixty days from the date of its first hearing. Three days, two days, sixty days: the numbers are the legislature's own measure against which the system's performance can be judged, and they are framed deliberately, the first two as rules with a narrow margin and the third as an endeavour, a direction of effort that courts treat as directory rather than as a condition whose breach voids the proceeding.

The temporary residence limb of Section 27 deserves its own emphasis, because it reverses the ordinary geography of litigation for the person least able to travel. Jurisdiction follows the place where the aggrieved person permanently or temporarily resides, and the words carry a woman who has fled to a shelter, a relative's house or her natal home in another district: she applies where she is, and the respondent travels. The same section takes in the place where she carries on business or is employed, recognising that the workplace district may be the safe district, the one where her days are spent and her protection order will operate. Jurisdiction also lies where the respondent resides and where the cause of action arose, so the applicant holds a choice among forums rather than a single assigned court, and the choice is exercisable at the moment of crisis without any inquiry into which forum the respondent would prefer. In a statute whose recurring theme is the removal of thresholds between the woman and the court, the jurisdiction section is of a piece with the information rights in Section 5 and the reporting protection in Section 4: each removes one errand, one journey or one permission that a more conventional design would have interposed.

### What happens at the first hearing?

The first hearing is the point at which urgency is converted into orders. It is fixed ordinarily within three days of the application reaching the court. The magistrate has the application, the domestic incident report where one exists, and any affidavit filed with it, and may on that material grant interim orders at once, including ex parte.

Where a prima facie case of violence or its likelihood is shown on that material, protection need not wait for the respondent to appear. Notice is served on the respondent through the Protection Officer, ordinarily within two days, and the respondent is then heard before final orders are made, with the interim position holding the household steady in the meantime.

Section 23 supplies the interim machinery. The magistrate may pass such interim orders as the court deems just and proper at any stage of the proceeding, and where satisfied, on the applicant's affidavit, that the application prima facie discloses that the respondent is committing, has committed or is likely to commit an act of domestic violence, the court may grant an ex parte order on that affidavit before the respondent is heard. The ex parte route is the statute's answer to the interval problem: the days between application and contested hearing are precisely the days in which retaliation and eviction are most likely, and an order that had to wait for both sides to be heard would arrive after the harm it exists to prevent. Because such orders rest on one side's affidavit, they are interim by nature; the respondent is heard, and the orders are confirmed, varied or vacated on the full material.

The Act equips the magistrate with two further instruments for managing the proceeding itself. Under Section 14, the court may at any stage direct the respondent or the aggrieved person, singly or jointly, to undergo counselling with a member of a service provider who possesses the prescribed qualifications and experience, and when such a direction is made the next date of hearing is fixed within a period not exceeding two months. Under Section 15, the court may secure the services of a welfare expert, preferably a woman, including a person experienced in family matters, to assist in discharging its functions. Section 16 permits proceedings to be held in camera where either party so desires, a protection that matters in litigation where the material is intimate and the parties frequently live within the same extended family and neighbourhood. Procedure otherwise follows the Code of Criminal Procedure save as the Act provides, under Section 28, with a saving that lets the court lay down its own procedure for disposing of applications under Section 12 and ex parte applications under Section 23, a flexibility meant to keep the proceeding summary in character. When orders are passed, Section 24 requires copies to be given free of cost to the parties, the Protection Officer, the service provider concerned and the police station within the court's jurisdiction.

One clarification from the case law belongs to this route. In Kamatchi v Lakshmi Narayanan, decided in 2022, the Supreme Court held that the limitation period governing complaints of offences under Section 468 of the Code of Criminal Procedure does not apply to an application under Section 12. The reasoning follows the statute's character: an application for relief is neither a complaint nor the initiation of a prosecution, and the only proceeding under the Act to which the Code's limitation chapter speaks is a prosecution for the offence of breach under Section 31, where limitation runs from the date of the breach. An applicant is therefore not shut out because the violence she describes began, or even ended, more than three years before she applied, and magistrates who had been dismissing applications as time-barred were disapproved.

At the final hearing the magistrate's inquiry is structured by the character of the proceeding. The court is not trying an offence, so the applicant does not prove her case beyond reasonable doubt; the court reaches satisfaction on the material, which characteristically comprises her testimony, the incident report, medical records, the welfare expert's assistance where it has been taken, and such documents of the household as either side produces. The respondent is heard, may test the applicant's account and may dispute the relationship, the household, the conduct and the quantum, and the orders that emerge are reasoned orders, appealable under Section 29, which disciplines the first hearing's informality. The interim stage and the final stage thus run on different materials and different intensities of scrutiny, and much of the practical litigation under the Act consists of respondents seeking the vacation of ex parte orders at the first contested hearing and applicants seeking their conversion into final orders of the same tenor. A magistrate's management of that conversion, neither treating the interim order as a presumption nor discarding it as a formality, is where the statute's balance between urgency and fairness is actually struck, case by case.

## The first weeks of a case, as the statute arranges them

The provisions read separately in the earlier sections compose a single chronology when a case is followed through, and the chronology shows the design working as one machine. It begins away from the court: a complaint reaches a Protection Officer, a service provider or the police, a domestic incident report records it, and the duties to inform attach, so the woman learns of the application route, the shelter and medical provisions and the legal aid entitlement at the point of first contact rather than at the court door. If she is injured, the medical facility's obligation gives her treatment and a record; if she cannot safely go home, the shelter home's obligation gives her a roof that is not the respondent's to withdraw. The application then goes to the magistrate of the district where she is, and the three-day rule fixes the first hearing while the events are fresh. At or before that hearing, the affidavit supports interim and ex parte orders, so the protection order, the restraint on dispossession and interim monetary relief can all be in force within days of filing. The Protection Officer serves the notice, ordinarily within two days, and the respondent's contest begins on a household whose interim arrangements the court has already set.

From there the statute contemplates a short contested phase. The magistrate may deploy the welfare expert, may direct counselling within its two-month frame, may hold proceedings in camera, and must endeavour to conclude within sixty days of the first hearing. At disposal, the orders fit the case: prohibitions tailored to the applicant's exposure, residence arranged by restraint, partition, removal or rent, money assessed on the disclosed means under the discipline Rajnesh v Neha supplies, custody settled with the child's safety as its stated test, compensation assessed for the injuries including the mental and emotional. Copies go free of cost to the parties, the officer, the provider and the police, so that every institution holding a duty under the orders holds the text of them. Thereafter the orders live: the protection order until discharge is sought, all orders open to alteration on changed circumstances, the breach offence standing behind the protection order, the sessions appeal open for thirty days from service, and the Protection Officer charged with the execution of the monetary directions. Described at this length, the chronology exposes the statute's wager plainly. Every stage has an owner, a timeline and a document; the failure modes the monitoring reports record are failures of owners to act, timelines to be kept and documents to be made, not failures of the scheme to specify them.

## How an application is assembled

An application under Section 12 is a document with a practical anatomy, and describing that anatomy explains several of the Act's other provisions. It identifies the parties and the domestic relationship between them, because jurisdiction over the respondent depends on that relationship and the court must be able to see it on the face of the pleading. It describes the shared household, for the same reason: the residence right and the residence order both fasten on the household in which the parties lived together, and after Ahuja the pleading addresses who owns the house only to make the factual case that the parties lived there in a domestic relationship, not to establish title in the applicant. It sets out the acts of domestic violence relied on, organised under the classes of Section 3, with dates and particulars where they can be given, and it states the reliefs sought, order by order, so that the magistrate can grant a fitted combination rather than a general direction.

The supporting material follows the same logic. The domestic incident report, where one exists, supplies the contemporaneous record made by the Protection Officer or the service provider. A medical record supplies the external evidence of injury where Section 7 examination was obtained. An affidavit supports the request for interim and ex parte orders under Section 23, and its adequacy decides the interim stage, since the magistrate acts on it before the respondent is heard. Documents of the household economy, bank records, salary particulars, lists of stridhan and property, support the monetary heads under Section 20, whose quantum must be related to expenses incurred and losses suffered and to the standard of living the section names. The applicant who cannot produce documents is not barred; much domestic violence occurs in households where the paperwork is itself controlled by the respondent, which the definition of economic abuse recognises, and the magistrate proceeds on testimony and such material as exists. But the structure of the Act rewards the assembly of a record, and the Protection Officer's duties, the service provider's recording function and the free legal aid route all exist to make that assembly possible for an applicant without resources of her own.

Service of the proceeding on the respondent is the step on which contested litigation turns, and the Act assigns it to the Protection Officer under Section 13 rather than leaving it to the applicant's own efforts. The officer serves the notice of the hearing date, in the manner the section prescribes, and reports service to the court, which protects the applicant from having to approach the respondent herself at the moment of maximum friction and gives the court a neutral record of whether the respondent was informed. Where service is evaded, the procedural framework the Act borrows supplies the usual substituted modes, and the ex parte power ensures that evasion cannot be converted into immunity from interim orders.

## The five reliefs

The orders a magistrate may grant are the statute's operative core, and they are best understood as a set of distinct instruments, each aimed at a different component of the applicant's situation. The Act's defenders describe the set as the point of the civil model: a criminal court convicts or acquits, while this court assembles the combination of restraint, housing, money, custody and damages that a particular household requires. The five reliefs follow.

The protection order, under Section 18, is the restraining instrument. Where the magistrate is satisfied that domestic violence has taken place or is likely to take place, the court may prohibit the respondent from committing any act of domestic violence, from aiding or abetting its commission, from entering the place of employment of the aggrieved person or the school of a child, from attempting to communicate with her in any form including personal, oral, written, electronic or telephonic contact, from alienating any assets, including bank accounts and lockers held jointly or solely by the respondent, from causing violence to her dependants, other relatives or any person who gives her assistance, and from committing any other act specified in the order. The order is framed around the applicant's actual exposure: the workplace she must attend, the school run she must make, the account she must be able to use.

The residence order, under Section 19, is the housing instrument, and it operates on the shared household defined earlier. The magistrate may restrain the respondent from dispossessing or in any other manner disturbing the possession of the aggrieved person from the shared household, whether or not the respondent has a legal or equitable interest in it; may direct the respondent to remove himself from the shared household; may restrain the respondent or any of his relatives from entering any portion of the household in which she resides; may restrain the respondent from alienating, disposing of or encumbering the household; may restrain the respondent from renouncing his rights in it; and, where the household cannot justly be shared, may direct the respondent to secure the same level of alternate accommodation for her, or to pay rent for it. The court may impose additional conditions and may require the respondent to execute a bond, with or without sureties, for preventing the commission of domestic violence, and may direct the officer in charge of the nearest police station to give protection to the applicant or to assist in implementing the order. The same section authorises a direction that the respondent return her stridhan and any other property or valuable security to which she is entitled. The proviso noted earlier, that no direction to remove himself shall be passed against a respondent who is a woman, applies to the removal limb.

Monetary relief, under Section 20, is the instrument for the economic consequences of the violence. The magistrate may direct the respondent to pay monetary relief to meet the expenses incurred and losses suffered by the aggrieved person and her children as a result of the domestic violence, and the section names the heads: loss of earnings; medical expenses; the loss caused due to the destruction, damage or removal of any property from her control; and maintenance for the aggrieved person and her children, including an order under or in addition to an order of maintenance under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure. The relief must be adequate, fair and reasonable and consistent with the standard of living to which she is accustomed, and it may be paid as a lump sum or in monthly payments as the nature and circumstances of the case require. If the respondent defaults, the magistrate may direct an employer or a debtor of the respondent to pay the applicant directly a portion of the wages, salaries or debt due, to be adjusted against the amount payable, a garnishment route that gives the order a collection mechanism inside the statute itself.

The custody order, under Section 21, empowers the magistrate to grant temporary custody of any child or children to the aggrieved person or to the person making the application on her behalf, and to specify arrangements for visit by the respondent. The power carries its own safeguard: if the magistrate considers that any visit of the respondent may be harmful to the interests of the child, the court shall refuse to allow the visit. The compensation order, under Section 22, directs the respondent to pay compensation and damages for the injuries, including mental torture and emotional distress, caused by the acts of domestic violence committed by him, a head of relief that recognises harm a criminal fine, paid to the state, never reaches.

The Section 31 offence is defined by reference to breach of a protection order or an interim protection order, and applicants and advisers should note that the penal consequence attaches to that instrument, which is one reason protection orders are sought even where residence and money are the applicant's main concerns. And interim availability across the table flows from Section 23, which empowers the magistrate to grant such interim and ex parte orders as the court considers just and proper in respect of the reliefs the Act provides.

### What about the children while a case is pending?

Section 21 gives the magistrate power to grant temporary custody of any child to the aggrieved person and to settle the respondent's visits, and the section contains its own child-protection rule: where a visit by the respondent may be harmful to the interests of the child, the court shall refuse to allow the visit.

Children enter the Act in a second way as well, since monetary relief under Section 20 extends to the children's expenses and maintenance, and the definition of verbal and emotional abuse reaches insults directed at a woman for not having a child or a male child. Custody under the Act is temporary by design: it holds the child's position while the proceeding and its orders operate, and it neither decides permanent guardianship nor displaces the family court's authority over the child's long-term arrangements, which Section 26 keeps in view by allowing the reliefs to be sought within those proceedings as well.

Section 17 deserves a final statement in its own right, separate from the residence order that implements it, because the two are often run together. The right to reside is conferred by the section itself on every woman in a domestic relationship; it does not depend on an application, on an order, or on any share in the property. A residence order is what the court grants when that right is resisted, directing the persons and the police who must respect it. A woman who remains in the shared household without any order is not a trespasser awaiting regularisation; she is exercising a statutory right, and Section 17(2) makes her eviction or exclusion lawful only through the procedure established by law. That is the provision the brief for this statute singles out as distinguishing it from everything else in Indian law, and the description is accurate: no other protective statute separates the right to stay from the ownership of the roof in this way.

Orders under the Act are not frozen at the moment they are made. A protection order remains in force until the aggrieved person applies for its discharge, and under Section 25(2) the magistrate, on an application by either party and on being satisfied that there is a change in the circumstances requiring alteration, modification or revocation of an order, may do so for reasons recorded in writing. Section 26 lets the reliefs travel: protection, residence, monetary, custody and compensation relief may be sought in any legal proceeding before a civil court, family court or criminal court affecting the parties, whether that proceeding began before or after this Act commenced, and a party who has obtained relief under this Act must inform the court of it in any other proceeding, so that overlapping orders are reconciled rather than accumulated in ignorance of one another.

## The protection and residence orders in operation

The protection order and the residence order do most of the statute's daily work, and their operation repays a closer look than the section summaries allow. A protection order is a set of prohibitions fitted to the applicant's exposure, and its drafting is an exercise in anticipating contact. The prohibition on entering her place of employment answers the pattern in which a respondent appears at the workplace to create scenes that cost the applicant her job, which is also an attack on the economic independence the Act elsewhere protects. The prohibition on attempting to communicate, which the section spells out across personal, oral, written, electronic and telephonic forms, answers the channel through which threats and pressure travel when physical access is barred, and its breach is correspondingly provable from records. The prohibition on alienating assets and operating accounts answers the interval risk that property will be moved beyond the reach of monetary relief while the proceeding is pending. Each prohibition, once granted, is a term whose breach is the Section 31 offence, which is why the order's precision matters: a respondent can only be punished for disobeying a direction that was actually made, and an applicant's adviser frames the prayer accordingly.

The residence order operates on a household rather than on conduct, and its limbs are graduated by intensity. The least intrusive direction restrains dispossession and disturbance of possession, leaving the household's composition untouched while freezing the applicant's position in it. The direction restraining the respondent and his relatives from entering a specified portion of the house partitions the household internally, a remedy suited to large joint family houses in which separation under one roof is physically possible. The direction that the respondent remove himself is the strongest interference with his occupation, and the proviso protects a female respondent from it, while the alternative accommodation limb lets the court avoid it altogether by transferring the cost of separation to the respondent in money: he secures accommodation of the same level for her, or pays the rent for it, and the household he remains in is left undisturbed. The bond the court may require, with or without sureties, converts the respondent's future conduct into a financial stake held by the court, and the direction to the police to give protection or assist in implementation connects the paper order to the only force that can attend at the door. The stridhan direction within the same section, ordering the return of her property and valuable security, resolves in the same proceeding a species of dispute that would otherwise require separate civil litigation, and its presence in the residence section reflects the drafters' understanding that the fight over the house and the fight over her property are usually one fight.

The interaction of the residence order with the ordinary civil law of property is the point on which Vanitha and Ahuja together have settled the approach, and it generalises beyond senior citizens. A respondent or a relative who owns the house may sue in the civil court for possession or injunction, and such suits are the standard counter-move to an application under the Act. The civil suit is not barred, and the Act does not extinguish ownership; what the two judgments establish is that the applicant's statutory residence claim must be placed before the court deciding possession, and that a decree or a tribunal order obtained without that claim being considered does not conclude her right. The residence claim is thus best understood as a statutory equity inside property litigation: it does not defeat title, it conditions the timing and manner in which title may be asserted against a woman in a domestic relationship, requiring her circumstances, and the availability of alternate accommodation, to be weighed before possession changes hands.

## Monetary relief and the maintenance overlap

Monetary relief under Section 20 sits inside a crowded field of maintenance law, and the overlaps have generated their own jurisprudence. A wife may hold, at the same time, a maintenance order under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, interim maintenance in a matrimonial proceeding under the personal law statutes, and monetary relief under this Act, and Section 20 itself contemplates the accumulation by providing for maintenance under this Act including an order under or in addition to an order under Section 125. The risk the accumulation creates is double counting of the same needs, and the courts' response has been to require coordination rather than election: orders are to be adjusted against one another, the amounts payable under each head are to be disclosed to the court fixing the next, and the duty of disclosure in Section 26(3) of this Act serves the same discipline across proceedings.

The governing synthesis is Rajnesh v Neha, decided by the Supreme Court in 2020, which laid down guidelines for the payment of maintenance across the overlapping statutes, including this one. The Court directed that parties in maintenance proceedings file affidavits of disclosure of their assets and liabilities, so that quantum is fixed on declared means rather than on assertion; it addressed the criteria for determining quantum, the date from which maintenance is payable, and the enforcement of orders, and it held that maintenance granted under one statute is to be adjusted or set off against amounts granted under another, with the applicant under a duty to disclose previous maintenance proceedings and orders. For proceedings under this Act, the significance is practical: a Section 20 application is decided within a disclosure discipline that the applicant's pleadings should anticipate, and the magistrate's assessment of what is adequate, fair and reasonable proceeds on the parties' declared means. The judgment belongs to maintenance law generally rather than to this Act alone, but no account of the monetary relief is complete without it, since Section 20 is one of the principal routes by which maintenance reaches women who have left, or been driven from, the matrimonial home.

## The single offence, and the appeal

The offence created by Section 31 is deliberately narrow, and its narrowness is the proof of the statute's civil design. A breach of a protection order, or of an interim protection order, by the respondent is an offence punishable with imprisonment that may extend to one year, or with a fine that may extend to twenty thousand rupees, or with both. Nothing else in the Act is an offence: not the violence that grounds the application, not disobedience of a monetary direction as such, and not the failure of a respondent to attend. Section 32 makes the breach offence cognizable and non-bailable, so the police may arrest without a warrant when a protection order is disobeyed, and it permits the court to conclude that the offence has been committed on the sole testimony of the aggrieved person. The same court trying a breach may also frame charges for offences under the general criminal law that the facts disclose, so a single episode of disobedience that itself involves cruelty or assault can be tried together with the breach.

The severity is calibrated to its function. The offence exists to give the protection order teeth, because a restraining order that costs nothing to disobey restrains nothing, and the arrest question that dominates public discussion of the statute is answered by this architecture: an application under the Act does not by itself lead to anyone's arrest, since there is no offence to arrest for until a court order exists and is breached. Critics who describe the Act as a route to automatic arrest misdescribe it, and applicants who expect an arrest on filing misunderstand it in the opposite direction. What the Act gives on filing is a court, an officer and a timetable; what it gives on breach is a criminal consequence.

The calibration deserves one comparison. The maximum of one year's imprisonment is well below the punishment the criminal law attaches to the cruelty that may underlie the application, and deliberately so: the offence punishes disobedience of the court, an affront to the order's authority, rather than re-punishing the underlying violence, which the criminal law addresses on its own charges where a complaint is made. A respondent who breaches a protection order by committing a serious assault faces both the breach prosecution and the prosecution for the assault, and Section 31's provision for framing the other charges in the same trial prevents the two from being artificially separated.

Section 29 provides the appeal. An appeal lies to the Court of Session within thirty days from the date on which the order made by the magistrate is served on the aggrieved person or the respondent, as the case may be, whichever is later. Either side may appeal: the applicant against a refusal or an inadequate order, the respondent against an order granted. Beyond the statutory appeal, the constitutional writ jurisdiction of the High Courts and the revisional powers under the criminal procedure framework remain available in the background, as they do across magistracy litigation, but the Act's own channel is the sessions appeal, and its thirty-day window is short enough that the service of the order, for which Section 24 provides free copies, starts a clock both sides must respect.

## Enforcement after the order

An order under the Act is the beginning of enforcement rather than its end, and the statute distributes the enforcement work among the same actors who built the case. The protection order and any residence order carry the Section 31 offence behind them, and the usual sequence on disobedience is a report to the police or to the magistrate, the registration of the breach as a cognizable offence, and trial of the breach before the magistrate, who may also frame charges for the underlying conduct the breach involved. The design keeps the breach prosecution in the court that knows the file, rather than sending the applicant to begin again elsewhere, and Section 32's permission to act on her sole testimony recognises that breaches of orders inside a home characteristically occur without independent witnesses.

Monetary orders are enforced differently, by execution rather than by prosecution. The Protection Officer's Section 9 duty to ensure compliance and execution of monetary reliefs under the Code of Criminal Procedure makes the officer the applicant's agent in recovery, and the Section 20 direction to an employer or debtor converts the respondent's salary or receivables into the payment source where he defaults. The weakness the monitoring literature identifies in this machinery is not the absence of powers but their activation: execution is application-driven, each default must be brought back before the magistrate, and an applicant without continuing legal aid may not return, so that monetary orders can decay into paper entitlements in districts where the officer does not pursue them. The Act's answer, imperfect but deliberate, was to assign the pursuit to a named public officer rather than to the applicant alone, which returns the assessment of enforcement to the same question as the rest of the statute: the design is coherent, and its yield depends on the cadre that staffs it.

## Beside the criminal law: coexistence, not election

The Act does not stand alone in its field, and its relations with the criminal provisions are a designed feature rather than an overlap to be apologised for. Section 36 states the rule: the provisions of this Act shall be in addition to, and not in derogation of, the provisions of any other law for the time being in force. A woman subjected to cruelty may file a criminal complaint under Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code, the provision against cruelty by a husband or his relatives, which since the coming into force of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 is carried in that code's corresponding Section 85, and she may simultaneously pursue an application under this Act for protection, residence and money. The two proceedings ask different questions, apply different standards and deliver different outcomes, and the pendency of one is no bar to the other. The course the courts have taken in supervising the criminal provision, including its arrest safeguards and its contested record, is traced in the account of [Section 498A and the judicial response](/2016/03/01/section-498a-judicial-response/).

The division of labour between the two routes is the cluster's central lesson. The criminal route punishes past cruelty after proof beyond reasonable doubt at the end of a trial; it can vindicate, deter and imprison, and it can do none of those things quickly, nor can it order a respondent to vacate a room, hand over stridhan or pay for a hospital stay. The civil route restrains future harm on a magistrate's satisfaction, within days at the interim stage, and it assembles the practical conditions of a safer life; it cannot convict, and it is not meant to. Survivors, advisers and courts treat the two as complements, and the field as a whole, including the prohibition statutes, the offences against women in the penal code and the workplace legislation, is mapped in the guide to [women's rights laws in India](/2015/11/01/womens-rights-laws-india-guide/).

Proceedings under the Act also run alongside matrimonial litigation rather than inside its shadow. A divorce petition, a maintenance claim under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, a custody dispute and an application under this Act may all be pending between the same parties, and Section 26, discussed with the reliefs, is the statute's answer to the resulting risk of inconsistent orders: relief under this Act may be sought within those proceedings, and relief already granted under it must be disclosed to the other court. The Supreme Court's treatment of stridhan retention as continuing economic abuse, in Krishna Bhattacharjee v Sarathi Choudhury, illustrates the interlock: a judicially separated wife remained an aggrieved person under the Act, and the retention of her property by her husband's family was treated as a wrong that continued for as long as the property was withheld, connecting the Act's monetary reliefs to disputes that family courts were already seised of.

## The statute among its neighbours

Comparison with the statutes around it sharpens what is distinctive here. The Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961 is a prohibition statute: it defines an offence and attaches penalties, and its working depends on prosecution, so a woman harassed for dowry receives from it the possibility of the offenders' punishment and nothing for her housing or maintenance. Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure is a maintenance jurisdiction: summary, welfare-oriented and available across religions, but confined to the single question of monthly support, with no power to restrain violence, protect residence or award compensation for injury. The Hindu Marriage Act and the other personal law statutes supply maintenance and custody within matrimonial proceedings that a wife may not wish to begin, since they are oriented to the dissolution or adjustment of the marriage itself. The workplace legislation against sexual harassment, the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013, which belongs to the same family of protective law, addresses a different site of harm and works through committees and employers rather than through a magistrate, although it preserves the complainant's access to the criminal law alongside its internal process in a recognisably similar spirit of cumulation.

The Domestic Violence Act is the only instrument in that company that begins from the household as it is, without requiring the applicant to leave it, dissolve it or prosecute its members, and that can respond to the whole of her situation in one proceeding: the violence restrained, the roof secured, the money ordered, the children placed, the injury compensated. That comprehensiveness is the analytical core of the brief's claim that this is the only major protective statute for women in India that gives relief rather than punishment, and it also explains the statute's exposure. A single proceeding that touches residence, property, maintenance and custody will necessarily collide with every other proceeding touching the same subjects, which is why Section 26's traffic rules between courts matter as much in practice as the reliefs themselves, and why the quality of the magistracy's coordination with family courts has become one of the quiet determinants of the Act's value in contested cases.

## The implementation record

The statute's text assumes a district-level machinery, and the monitoring of that machinery is the least comfortable part of its record. The principal source is the "Staying Alive" series of monitoring and evaluation reports produced by the Lawyers Collective Women's Rights Initiative, which tracked the Act's working from its first year of operation. The first report, covering the first year, recorded that all states except five had appointed Protection Officers, but that except in the National Capital Territory of Delhi and Andhra Pradesh none of the appointments were of full-time officers; the pattern, repeated across the later reports in the series, was of revenue and child development officials holding the Protection Officer's charge in addition to their own posts, with the caseloads of both. The sixth report in the series, evaluating court orders from the period it covered in 2011 and 2012, continued the documentation of part-time appointments and thin infrastructure, and recorded cases in which disposal ran far beyond the sixty-day endeavour of Section 12(5). In the first monitoring cycle, only five states had registered service providers, and the notification of medical facilities and shelter homes lagged the appointments of officers.

The structural findings can be summarised without exaggeration. A Protection Officer holding additional charge cannot make domestic incident reports promptly, cannot shepherd applications through a three-day first hearing, and cannot maintain the list of shelter homes and medical facilities that Section 9 requires, for the elementary reason that the officer's working day is already spoken for. A district without registered service providers or a notified shelter home leaves the applicant with orders on paper and nowhere to sleep. A magistracy carrying heavy criminal dockets hears the Act's applications in the interstices of those dockets, and the sixty-day endeavour yields to the queue. None of this is an argument about the statute's design; it is an argument about the distance between the design and the budget lines that were meant to carry it, a distance the Ministry of Women and Child Development acknowledged when it announced an annual budgetary provision of one hundred crore rupees for the office of Protection Officers at the launch of the sixth Staying Alive report.

The usage figures point the same way. The NFHS-5 finding that only about fourteen per cent of women who experienced physical or sexual violence had sought help from any source describes all sources together, police, family, institutions and this Act's machinery among them; the Act's own application counts are not published as a single settled national series, and this article does not construct one. What the survey establishes is the size of the population the statute exists for, and what the monitoring reports establish is the thinness of the provision made for reaching them. Between those two documented facts lies the statute's practical history.

The state has added adjacent infrastructure since the Act's first decade. The One Stop Centre scheme of the Ministry of Women and Child Development, rolled out from 2015, places centres, commonly known as Sakhi centres, in districts to offer women affected by violence an integrated point of medical aid, police facilitation, legal aid and counselling, and women's helplines have been integrated with emergency response systems in a number of states. These schemes are not machinery created by the Act, and they do not answer for its own officers and registers, but they alter the terrain on which an applicant moves: a woman who reaches a One Stop Centre is, in the design of that scheme, connected to the legal aid and the Protection Officer whom the Act makes responsible for her application. The honest assessment remains the one the monitoring record supports. Where the district machinery functions, with a full-time officer, registered providers, a notified shelter home and a magistrate who lists the applications promptly, the Act approximates its text. Where it does not, the text is a description of a system that exists elsewhere.

## The counter-reading, stated at full strength

The resource criticism is the first of the two serious challenges, and on the evidence summarised in the previous section it is substantially made out: the reliefs are only as real as the Protection Officers, shelter homes and prompt hearings that deliver them, and in many districts those do not exist in the form the Act prescribes. The statute's defenders, including the Lawyers Collective, which drafted much of the original bill and then monitored its delivery, do not dispute the findings; they direct them at the governments that under-appointed and under-funded the machinery, arguing that a monitoring record of this kind is an indictment of implementation rather than of design. That exchange has run for the life of the Act, and the honest summary is that both propositions are true at once: the design is unusually complete, and the completeness has not been matched by provision.

The scope criticism comes from the opposite direction and is pressed most organisedly by men's rights groups, among them the Save Indian Family Foundation. Its strongest form runs as follows. The Act is available to women alone, so a man subjected to violence in his home has no equivalent civil remedy and must rely on the general criminal law. The definitions of verbal, emotional and economic abuse are wide enough to cover quarrels, financial disagreements and the ordinary frictions of a joint family, and a complaint can therefore be assembled out of the material of an unhappy household rather than a violent one. Residence orders, particularly after the widening of the shared household in Ahuja, can be directed at houses owned by elderly parents-in-law who are not alleged to have committed any act of violence, subjecting them to litigation over their own home and, in the removal and dispossession limbs, to the prospect of arrangements imposed on their property. Supporters of the criticism add that proceedings under the Act are frequently filed together with criminal complaints in matrimonial breakdown, and read that conjunction as tactical pressure in a marital dispute.

The debate over misuse labours under an evidential difficulty that both sides acknowledge when pressed. There is no official statistical series that measures the proportion of applications under the Act that are false, exaggerated or well founded, and the figures traded in public argument are drawn from conviction rates in the neighbouring criminal law, from acquittal rates that conflate failure of proof with falsity, or from the experience of particular courts and organisations. Acquittal in a prosecution under Section 498A, for instance, establishes that the charge was not proved beyond reasonable doubt; it does not establish that the underlying complaint was fabricated, and it says nothing directly about applications under this Act, which are decided on a different standard for a different purpose. The defenders of the statute press that distinction hard, and add that the remedies themselves are a safeguard of a kind: a magistrate who has heard both sides may refuse, trim or time-limit orders, and the alteration power in Section 25 exists for orders that events have overtaken. The critics answer that interim and ex parte orders, granted on one side's affidavit, impose the burden first and test it later, and that for a respondent wrongly accused the interval is the injury. Both descriptions correspond to real cases, which is why the argument has not been settled by evidence and continues to be conducted as an argument about which error a legal system should fear more, the unprotected applicant or the overborne respondent.

The defenders' answer, given by advocates and scholars of the Act including Flavia Agnes, who has written on the statute with both commitment and criticism of its delivery, is that the breadth of the definition reflects the phenomenon it addresses. Abuse in a household is characteristically a pattern of control in which economic deprivation, threats and humiliation are the instruments and physical assault the enforcement of last resort; a definition confined to assault would protect women only at the point where the pattern had already done its work. On residence, the answer is that the orders are discretionary and interim in character: Section 19 confers a choice of directions of varying intensity, from restraining dispossession to ordering removal or alternate accommodation, and Ahuja itself instructs courts to balance the claims of aged occupants. On the conjunction with criminal complaints, the answer is Section 36: Parliament made the remedies cumulative deliberately, because protection and punishment are different needs, and the same facts can ground both without either being an abuse of the other.

A third criticism is heard from within the women's movement itself, and it is directed at the statute's operation rather than its existence. The counselling power in Section 14 was designed as assistance, a route by which a household might be stabilised without adversarial orders. Critics of its working argue that in practice a counselling direction can tilt a proceeding toward reconciliation as the expected outcome, returning a woman to the household on assurances rather than on enforceable orders, and that the two-month interval the section allows before the next hearing can dissipate the urgency the Act elsewhere cultivates. The answer available within the statute is that counselling is a discretionary direction, not a precondition to relief, that interim orders under Section 23 may be granted and kept in force alongside it, and that a magistrate alert to the risk will sequence the two so that protection precedes conciliation. The exchange identifies a genuine design tension that recurs across protective law: the same flexibility that lets a court fit its response to a household that may continue can be used, by a court so inclined, to prefer the household's continuation to the applicant's safety, and the Act's text gives the applicant's side the better of that argument only where the magistrate is willing to use the interim powers first.

This article does not adjudicate between those cases, because the record supports parts of each and the contest between them is a live one about emphasis: how much weight to give the documented pattern of unremedied violence against women, against the documented instances of overbroad complaints and burdens on third parties within households. What can be said without taking sides is that the Supreme Court's own interventions have moved the statute toward balance at the points of sharpest friction, narrowing neither the definition nor the applicant class, but insisting, in Ahuja and Vanitha, that residence claims be decided with the rights of every occupant in view, and, in Harsora, widening the respondent class in the name of the statute's own logic. The design Parliament enacted has proved capacious enough to hold both the criticism and the answer, which is why litigation under it continues to refine rather than to repudiate it.

## The judicial life of the statute

The statute's meaning has been made in four waves of litigation, and the sequence is the shortest route to its present shape. The first wave fixed the boundaries of the household. Batra in 2006 confined the shared household to the husband's property or the joint family house, and that confinement governed the Act's first fourteen years, shaping what applicants could hope for and what magistrates believed they could order. The second wave fixed the boundaries of the relationship. Velusamy in 2010 and Indra Sarma in 2013 built the test for relationships in the nature of marriage, admitting the durable, marriage-like household and excluding the merely cohabiting or knowing arrangement with a married man, while Chanmuniya in 2011 recorded the pressure for a wider reading and Bhanot in 2012 confirmed that the Act reaches conduct from before its own commencement, so that its protection is not confined to relationships formed after October 2006. The third wave fixed the boundaries of the parties. Harsora in 2016 removed the adult male limitation on respondents, and Krishna Bhattacharjee in the same year held that a judicially separated wife remains an aggrieved person and that retention of her stridhan is a continuing economic abuse. The fourth wave, in the years from 2020, consolidated the residence right and the procedure together: Rajnesh v Neha disciplined the maintenance overlap in 2020; Ahuja in the same year overruled Batra and restored the definition's width; Vanitha two months later protected the residence claim against summary eviction under the senior citizens legislation; and in 2022 Prabha Tyagi freed the orders from dependence on a domestic incident report and confirmed that the applicant need not be residing with the respondents when she applies, while Kamatchi freed the application from the limitation period that governs criminal complaints.

Read as a whole, the four waves moved in one direction with one exception. The definition of violence was never narrowed. The applicant class was never widened beyond women, and the relationship test was deliberately kept demanding. The respondent class was widened, the residence right was widened after its long narrowing, and the procedural path was cleared of preconditions that had accumulated in the magistracy's practice. The courts' consistent technique has been fidelity to the enacted text read in light of the statute's protective purpose, which is why the two great corrections, Harsora and Ahuja, were both argued as recoveries of the text from readings that had displaced it, the first from a limitation the equality clause could not support and the second from a title requirement the definition never contained.

## What the statute changed

The Act's legacy is best measured by the legal ideas it made ordinary. It established that a woman's right to remain in her home does not depend on owning it, an idea Section 17 states in a sentence and the courts spent fourteen years working out. It established that the violence a protective law must answer includes the control of money, the confiscation of property and the threat believed, and not assault alone. It established that a magistrate's court can be a place where a household is rearranged for safety, by orders about rooms, rents, accounts, school gates and hospital bills, and not only a place where offences are tried. And it established a named public officer whose statutory job is the journey of the applicant, an institutional idea that the monitoring reports show to have been honoured unevenly and that no subsequent debate has proposed abandoning.

The Act also changed the vocabulary in which Indian households are discussed in law. It made "domestic violence" a defined term of art with four specified classes, where before it had been a journalistic phrase; it put "shared household" and "aggrieved person" into the working language of magistrates, police officers and family lawyers; and it taught a generation of litigants that residence, maintenance and protection are questions a single application can ask together. Statutes that alter the vocabulary of a field alter what can be claimed within it, because a grievance that has a statutory name and a named officer to receive it is a grievance that can be filed, counted and argued about, and the Act's terms have passed into the drafting of later protective measures and into the pleadings of the neighbouring jurisdictions described earlier in this article.

Its limits are the mirror of its design. A statute that works through officers, shelters and hearings inherits the capacity of the district it operates in, and the sixty-day endeavour in its text stands as a permanent standard against which its own delivery is measured and found slower. A statute that protects by relationship invites boundary litigation about which relationships count, and the live-in cases show the courts holding a line that leaves some women, particularly those in relationships with married men, outside the door they most need opened. A statute that separates relief from punishment is permanently exposed to misunderstanding from both directions, described as toothless by those who want arrests and as oppressive by those who mistake its orders for convictions.

Questions remain open, and an account of the statute should end its analysis by naming them rather than by implying a completeness the law does not have. The reference made in Chanmuniya toward a wider reading of relationships in the nature of marriage has never displaced the Velusamy and Indra Sarma framework, and the position of women in long relationships with married men, which Indra Sarma itself commended to Parliament, awaits the legislation the Court invited. The measure of compensation under Section 22 remains comparatively undeveloped, without the settled scales that other compensatory jurisdictions have produced, so that awards for mental torture and emotional distress vary widely between courts on similar facts. The quantification of monetary relief has been disciplined by the disclosure regime of Rajnesh v Neha, but the alteration jurisdiction in Section 25 has generated little appellate guidance on the standards for revisiting orders as households change. And the largest question is not doctrinal at all: whether the district machinery will be funded into the form the third chapter of the Act describes, a question that has been open since the first Staying Alive report and on which the statute's second twenty years will be judged as its first twenty have been.

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 remains the only major protective statute in its field that gives relief rather than punishment, and that singularity is the fact about it from which all informed argument proceeds. This article describes the law as it stands in the statute and the judgments; it is not advice on any individual situation, and a person in immediate danger should contact the police or a recognised helpline without waiting for any proceeding to begin.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is the Domestic Violence Act a criminal or a civil law?

It is a civil law. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 creates a proceeding in which an aggrieved person applies to a magistrate for protective relief, and the magistrate grants or refuses orders about conduct, residence, money, custody and compensation. Nobody is convicted or acquitted in that proceeding, and the violence itself is not made an offence by the Act. The single offence the statute creates is the breach of a protection order or an interim protection order under Section 31, punishable with imprisonment up to one year or a fine up to twenty thousand rupees or both. The Act's civil character also explains its speed: interim and ex parte orders can be granted on an affidavit under Section 23, because the court is restraining harm rather than determining guilt after a criminal trial.

### Q: What counts as domestic violence under the 2005 Act?

Section 3 defines domestic violence as conduct that harms, injures or endangers the health, safety, life, limb or well-being of the aggrieved person, mental or physical, and it names four classes of abuse: physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse, and economic abuse. The definition also covers harassment meant to coerce the woman or anyone related to her into meeting an unlawful demand for dowry or other property or valuable security, conduct that threatens her through any of those means, and other conduct that injures or causes her harm. The Explanation to the section details each class: economic abuse includes depriving her of resources she is entitled to, disposing of household effects and assets, and barring her access to the shared household, while verbal and emotional abuse includes insults, ridicule, humiliation and repeated threats against persons she cares about.

### Q: Who can apply for relief under the Domestic Violence Act?

The applicant is the aggrieved person, defined in Section 2(a) as any woman who is, or has been, in a domestic relationship with the respondent and who alleges that she has been subjected to domestic violence. She may present the application under Section 12 herself, or a Protection Officer may present it on her behalf, or any other person, such as a relative, a friend or a service provider, may present it on her behalf. Because the definition reaches a woman who has been in the relationship, leaving the house, being driven out of it or being judicially separated does not by itself end her eligibility. The relief she applies for may be a protection order, a residence order, monetary relief, a custody order or a compensation order, or a combination of them suited to her situation.

### Q: What is the right to reside in a shared household?

It is the right conferred by Section 17 of the Act. Every woman in a domestic relationship has the right to reside in the shared household whether or not she has any right, title or beneficial interest in the house, and she may not be evicted or excluded from it by the respondent or his relatives except in accordance with the procedure established by law. A shared household, under Section 2(s), is a household where she lives or has lived in a domestic relationship, and it includes a house belonging to a joint family of which the respondent is a member. In Satish Chander Ahuja v Sneha Ahuja in 2020 the Supreme Court held that a house belonging to a relative of the husband in which the woman has lived in a domestic relationship is a shared household, overruling the narrower reading in Batra. The right does not transfer ownership; it protects her residence.

### Q: What orders can a magistrate pass under the Domestic Violence Act?

Five kinds. A protection order under Section 18 prohibits the respondent from committing or abetting domestic violence, entering the applicant's workplace or a child's school, attempting to communicate with her, alienating assets or accounts, and harming those who assist her. A residence order under Section 19 can restrain her dispossession from the shared household, direct the respondent to remove himself, restrain entry or alienation, or direct alternate accommodation or rent. Monetary relief under Section 20 covers loss of earnings, medical expenses, loss from destruction or removal of property, and maintenance. A custody order under Section 21 grants temporary custody of a child and settles visits, which must be refused if harmful to the child. A compensation order under Section 22 awards damages for injuries, including mental torture and emotional distress. Interim and ex parte orders may be granted under Section 23.

### Q: Can female relatives be named as respondents under the Domestic Violence Act?

Yes. As enacted, Section 2(q) defined the respondent as an adult male person, and applications against female relatives faced that textual objection for the Act's first decade. In Hiral P. Harsora v Kusum Narottamdas Harsora, decided in 2016, the Supreme Court struck the words adult male from the definition, holding that the limitation could not be reconciled with the Act's object or with Article 14 of the Constitution, since domestic violence as the Act defines it can be inflicted by women as well as men, and complaints commonly name mothers-in-law and sisters-in-law. The proviso to the definition was treated as otiose once the words were removed. One qualification remains in the residence power itself: Section 19 carries a proviso that no order directing a respondent to remove himself from the shared household shall be passed against a woman.

### Q: What is a Protection Officer required to do?

Section 9 of the Act lists the duties. The Protection Officer, appointed by the state government for each district under Section 8, must assist the magistrate in discharging the court's functions, make a domestic incident report on receiving a complaint of domestic violence and forward copies to the local police and to the service providers in the area, and move the application before the magistrate where the aggrieved person so desires. The officer must ensure that she receives free legal aid under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, maintain a list of the service providers, shelter homes and medical facilities available in the area, arrange shelter and medical examination where she needs them, and ensure that orders for monetary relief are complied with and executed in accordance with the Code of Criminal Procedure. Protection Officers are public servants, and Section 33 penalises an officer who fails to discharge directed duties without sufficient cause.

### Q: How long does a Domestic Violence Act case take?

The statute prescribes the timetable in Section 12. The magistrate must fix the first date of hearing, which shall not ordinarily be beyond three days from the date the application is received by the court, and notice is served on the respondent through the Protection Officer ordinarily within two days. Section 12(5) directs the magistrate to endeavour to dispose of the application within sixty days from the date of its first hearing. That sixty-day direction is framed as an endeavour, and the monitoring reports on the Act's working, including the Staying Alive series, have recorded disposals running far beyond it in practice, in courts carrying heavy dockets and in districts where Protection Officers hold the charge in addition to other posts. Interim orders under Section 23 are the statute's answer to the gap, since they can be granted at the first hearing or ex parte before it.

### Q: What is a Domestic Incident Report, and must a magistrate have one before passing orders?

A domestic incident report is the record made when a complaint of domestic violence is received, prepared by the Protection Officer, or by a registered service provider the woman approaches first, in the form prescribed by the rules under the Act, and forwarded to the magistrate and to the police officer in charge of the local police station. It captures the complaint, the parties, the household and the assistance sought, close to the event. Section 12 directs the magistrate to take a report into consideration before passing an order where one has been received, but in Prabha Tyagi v Kamlesh Devi the Supreme Court held that consideration of a report is not the same as requiring one: a magistrate may pass interim, ex parte and final orders without a domestic incident report, because many applicants reach the court without any report having been made, and relief is not to wait on paperwork.

### Q: Is there a time limit for filing an application under the Domestic Violence Act?

The Act prescribes no limitation period for an application under Section 12, and in Kamatchi v Lakshmi Narayanan, decided in 2022, the Supreme Court held that Section 468 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, which limits the time for taking cognizance of offences, does not apply to such an application. The reasoning is that the application is neither a complaint nor the start of a prosecution; it is a request for civil relief. Limitation under that chapter applies only where the Act itself creates an offence, which is the breach of a protection order under Section 31, and there the period runs from the date of the breach. Magistrates had in some cases dismissed applications as time-barred because the violence alleged was old, and that approach was disapproved. Delay can still affect the court's assessment of the material, but it is not a statutory bar.

### Q: Does the Domestic Violence Act protect women in live-in relationships?

Yes, where the relationship is a relationship in the nature of marriage within Section 2(f). In D. Velusamy v D. Patchaiammal the Supreme Court held that such a relationship is akin to a common law marriage: the parties must hold themselves out as akin to spouses, be of legal age to marry, be otherwise qualified to marry including being unmarried, and have voluntarily cohabited for a significant period in a shared household. Weekend arrangements and relationships in which a woman is kept largely for sexual purposes were held not to qualify. In Indra Sarma v V.K.V. Sarma the Court listed indicative factors, including duration, a shared household, pooling of resources, domestic arrangements, a sexual relationship, children, socialisation in public and intention and conduct, and held that a woman who knowingly lived with a married man was outside the Act because the parties were not qualified to marry, while calling on Parliament to legislate for women in such positions.

### Q: Can a magistrate consider violence that happened before the Act came into force in 2006?

Yes. The Act came into force on 26 October 2006, and in V.D. Bhanot v Savita Bhanot the Supreme Court held that conduct predating its commencement may be taken into consideration on an application under it. A woman who lived with the respondent in a shared household before the Act commenced is not barred from applying, and the magistrate may look at behaviour before commencement in deciding whether domestic violence has taken place and what relief to grant. The reasoning reflects the statute's character: the application seeks present relief for a continuing situation, and the pattern of a domestic relationship does not divide neatly at the commencement date. This reading sits with the definition of the domestic relationship in Section 2(f), which is satisfied where the parties have at any point of time lived together in a shared household.

### Q: What monetary relief can a magistrate award under Section 20?

The magistrate may direct the respondent to pay monetary relief to meet the expenses incurred and losses suffered by the aggrieved person and her children as a result of the domestic violence. The section names the heads: loss of earnings, medical expenses, the loss caused due to the destruction, damage or removal of any property from her control, and maintenance for the aggrieved person as well as her children, including an order under or in addition to an order of maintenance under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure. The relief must be adequate, fair and reasonable and consistent with the standard of living to which she is accustomed, and it may be ordered as a lump sum or in monthly payments as the circumstances require. If the respondent defaults, the magistrate may direct his employer or a person indebted to him to pay the amount directly out of wages or the debt due.

### Q: Can a magistrate pass orders without hearing the respondent first?

Yes, at the interim stage. Section 23 empowers the magistrate to grant interim orders the court considers just and proper, and where the application is supported by an affidavit that prima facie discloses that the respondent is committing, has committed or is likely to commit an act of domestic violence, the court may grant an ex parte order on the basis of that affidavit before the respondent is heard. The design answers the interval between filing and a contested hearing, which is when eviction or retaliation is most likely. Such orders are interim: notice is then served on the respondent through the Protection Officer, ordinarily within two days, and the respondent is heard before final orders are passed, when the interim orders may be confirmed, varied or vacated. The first hearing itself is to be fixed ordinarily within three days of the application reaching the court.

### Q: What happens if a respondent disobeys a protection order?

Disobedience of a protection order, or of an interim protection order, is the one offence the Act creates. Section 31 makes the breach punishable with imprisonment that may extend to one year, or with a fine that may extend to twenty thousand rupees, or with both, and Section 32 makes the offence cognizable and non-bailable, so the police may arrest without a warrant. The court may conclude that the offence has been committed on the sole testimony of the aggrieved person, and the court trying the breach may also frame charges for offences under the general criminal law disclosed by the same conduct. This is the only point at which an application under the Act leads to an arrest: filing the application itself is not an offence and arrests nobody, and it is the violation of the court's own order that converts the matter into a prosecution.

### Q: Can a divorced or separated woman still apply under the Domestic Violence Act?

Yes, in the situations the courts have recognised. The aggrieved person is a woman who is or has been in a domestic relationship with the respondent, and the past tense is part of the definition. In Krishna Bhattacharjee v Sarathi Choudhury the Supreme Court treated a judicially separated wife as an aggrieved person and held that her husband's family's retention of her stridhan was economic abuse of a continuing character, so the wrong continued while the property was withheld. In Prabha Tyagi v Kamlesh Devi the Court added that an applicant need not be residing with the respondents when the application is filed, since the domestic relationship is satisfied by having lived together in the shared household at any point of time. The relief remains tied to the relationship and the violence alleged; what separation changes is the applicant's address, not her standing under the Act.

### Q: Can relief under the Domestic Violence Act be claimed inside another case?

Yes. Section 26 provides that the reliefs under the Act, protection, residence, monetary relief, custody and compensation, may also be sought in any legal proceeding before a civil court, a family court or a criminal court affecting the aggrieved person and the respondent, whether that proceeding was instituted before or after the Act commenced. A woman engaged in divorce or maintenance litigation is therefore not confined to a separate application under Section 12, although that route remains open. Section 26 also imposes a duty of disclosure: a party who has obtained relief in any proceeding under the Act must inform the court of that relief in any other proceeding, so that courts dealing with the same household do not pass orders in ignorance of one another. Monetary relief under Section 20 may likewise be granted in addition to maintenance under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure.

### Q: How is the Domestic Violence Act different from Section 498A of the Indian Penal Code?

They do different work on the same facts. Section 498A, inserted in the Indian Penal Code in 1983 and carried into Section 85 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, is a criminal offence of cruelty by a husband or his relatives: the state prosecutes, guilt must be proved beyond reasonable doubt, and the outcome is punishment. The Domestic Violence Act is a civil proceeding the woman herself brings: a magistrate acts on satisfaction reached on the material before the court and orders protection, residence, monetary relief, custody and compensation, with interim orders available in days. Section 36 of the Act provides that its provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law, so the two proceedings may run together and the pendency of a criminal complaint is no bar to an application, nor the reverse. A prosecution cannot order a respondent out of a room; an application cannot convict him.

### Q: Can an order under the Domestic Violence Act be changed or cancelled later?

Yes. Under Section 25, a protection order remains in force until the aggrieved person applies for its discharge, and Section 25(2) empowers the magistrate, on an application by the aggrieved person or by the respondent, to alter, modify or revoke any order made under the Act where the court is satisfied that there has been a change in the circumstances requiring it, recording reasons in writing. Changed employment, a respondent's compliance over time, a new household arrangement or fresh violence can each ground such an application, and interim orders are in any event open to variation once the respondent has been heard. The mechanism matters because the orders govern a living household rather than a concluded transaction, and the statute assumes that arrangements fitted to one set of facts may cease to fit another.

### Q: Can a magistrate's order under the Domestic Violence Act be appealed?

Yes. Section 29 provides that an appeal lies to the Court of Session against an order of the magistrate, within thirty days from the date on which the order is served on the aggrieved person or on the respondent, as the case may be, whichever is later. Either side may appeal: an applicant against a refusal of relief or an order she considers inadequate, and a respondent against an order passed against him. The thirty-day window runs from service, and Section 24 requires that copies of the order be given free of cost to the parties, the Protection Officer, the service provider concerned and the local police station, so the date of service can be established from the record. Orders under the Act are not revisable through the Act itself beyond this appeal, and interim orders are ordinarily taken up within the main proceeding rather than by separate appeal.
