Every reader of Romeo and Juliet reads a text that two early quartos disagree about, line by line, speech by speech, and direction by direction. The comparison of Q1 and Q2, the shorter quarto of 1597 against the fuller quarto of 1599, is the single most instructive exercise in the play's whole textual history, because it exposes the editorial choices hidden inside every modern edition. Set side by side, the two witnesses tell different kinds of truth. Q2 preserves the verse at something close to its full authorial reach: the long soliloquies, the dense conceits, the complete architecture of speeches that modern editions print. Q1 preserves the play as a working stage event: compressed, reordered in places, paraphrased in others, and furnished with stage directions that record bodies moving through a performance. The verdict argued through this article is plain. Neither quarto is a corrupt copy of the other in any simple sense. Each is evidence for a different thing, and the editor's task is not to crown one and discard the other but to collate them with a clear account of what each witness can and cannot testify to.

![The two early quartos of Romeo and Juliet compared side by side - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-81.webp)

That verdict has consequences for reading. A line that exists only in Q2, a direction that exists only in Q1, and a reading where the two texts split over a single word are three different kinds of fact, and they carry three different weights. The collation method, the pattern of the cuts, the transposed passages, the reassigned speeches, and the famous cruxes where one word divides the witnesses all repay close attention, because together they show how the play's text was made twice: once in the writing and once in the playing. The familiar reading text, the one quoted in essays and printed in school editions, is a third making, assembled by editors who take the verse mainly from Q2 and the staging partly from Q1. Understanding that assembly is the real subject of the Q1 against Q2 comparison.

## Two Quartos of Romeo and Juliet: The Scale of the Difference

The first fact of the comparison is size, and size governs everything else. Q2 prints roughly three thousand lines of the play; Q1 prints roughly twenty-two hundred. The standard modern counts, as Jill Levenson's Oxford edition sets them out, put the shorter text some eight hundred lines below the fuller one, a loss of more than a quarter of the whole. No other pair of early texts for a major Shakespeare tragedy diverges at quite this scale while remaining recognisably the same play in the same order for most of its length. The two quartos share the plot entire, the same sequence of scenes for long stretches, hundreds of identical or near-identical lines, and the same ending. Yet across that shared frame the shorter witness cuts, compresses, paraphrases, and occasionally rewrites, and it does so unevenly: some scenes pass almost untouched, while others lose a third or more of their lines.

The unevenness is the diagnostic heart of the matter. A text shortened by a censor or a careless printer would lose material in a different pattern, evenly or arbitrarily. Q1 loses material selectively, and the selection has a profile. Extended lyricism goes first: soliloquies shrink, conceits are thinned, reflective passages that delay the action are paraphrased into a few serviceable lines. Plot-bearing dialogue survives at a far higher rate. Entrances, exits, fights, deaths, and discoveries keep their shape, and the stage directions that manage them grow fuller rather than thinner. The pattern is the profile of a performance text, a version shaped by the economics of the stage, where playing time is finite, doubling is a necessity, and a speech that cannot be cut without breaking the story is protected while a speech that exists for its verbal richness is exposed.

### What is the largest single difference between the two quartos?

The largest single difference is length with a pattern: the first quarto prints eight hundred fewer lines than the second, and the missing lines cluster in soliloquy, lyric expansion, and reflective dialogue rather than in plot. The shorter text is not a random sample; it is the longer play with its wealth taxed and its action spared.

That pattern must be stated before any theory of origins, because every theory of Q1 stands or falls on whether it can explain the pattern. Memorial reconstruction, the theory that actors rebuilt the play from memory for a pirate printer, predicts paraphrase where memory failed and accuracy where a reporting actor's own part was concerned. Performance abridgement predicts exactly the observed profile of cuts: lyric wealth taxed, action spared, directions enriched by stage practice. An early-draft theory predicts something else again, a shorter play that the author later expanded, which would show immature versions of passages rather than flattened paraphrases of mature ones. The collation, pursued scene by scene in the sections that follow, keeps returning to the same distribution of loss, and the distribution is what any verdict on the two texts must answer to.

The scale difference also has a quieter consequence for citation and study. Line numbers, speech lengths, and imagery counts computed on a Q2-based edition describe a play that the Q1 audience, if Q1 records performance, never heard in full. When a critic counts the lines allotted to each character, or measures how long a soliloquy runs, the count is edition-bound in a way that the Q1 and Q2 comparison makes visible. The eight hundred missing lines are not evenly drawn from every speaker. They fall heaviest on the great set pieces: the Friar's speeches, the lovers' extended arias, Mercutio's flights. A character's share of the play is therefore partly a function of which witness an editor trusted, and the comparison restores that contingency to view.

## Where the Missing Lines Went: The Pattern of the Cuts

The eight hundred lines absent from Q1 are not missing at random, and tracing their distribution is the fastest way to understand what kind of text Q1 is. The heaviest losses fall in the play's reflective and lyrical registers. The Friar's long speeches contract sharply: his opening soliloquy on the virtues and poisons of plants, the speech that begins "The grey-eyed morn smiles on the frowning night" (2.3.1), survives in Q1 in a reduced form that keeps the moral and sheds much of the botanical meditation. Juliet's potion soliloquy in 4.3, the great aria of fear that begins "I have a faint cold fear thrills through my veins" (4.3.15), is similarly compressed, its catalogue of horrors thinned. Romeo's speeches of wonder and despair lose lines throughout. Where the fuller text lingers, the shorter text hurries.

By contrast, the machinery of the plot passes through the compression almost intact. The street brawl that opens the play, the ball, the balcony assignation in its outline, the secret marriage, the duels, the potion plot, the tomb: every load-bearing event stands in Q1 in its Q2 order and at near Q2 fullness of dialogue. Even scenes built from pure talk survive when the talk does plot work. The negotiations in 2.4, the Nurse's teasing delay of her news, keep their comic structure because the delay is the scene's action. What the compression removes is delay that exists for its own verbal sake. The pattern has a name in the scholarship of performance: it is the profile of a text cut for playing time by people who knew which speeches an audience could lose without losing the story.

### Which kinds of passage does the first quarto cut first?

Lyric and reflective passage goes first: soliloquies, conceits, catalogues, and speeches that pause the action to contemplate it. Plot-bearing dialogue, entrances, fights, and discoveries are cut last and least. The order of vulnerability in the first quarto is a playing company's order, in which the story must survive the evening intact while verbal richness is the expendable luxury.

A second feature of the cuts sharpens the diagnosis. Q1 frequently does not delete a long speech outright; it paraphrases the speech into fewer, flatter lines that preserve its information and discard its figure. The paraphrase is most visible where Q2 is most densely written. A conceit that runs to six lines in the fuller text returns in the shorter as two lines of plain statement. A catalogue of parallel clauses returns with half its members. This behaviour matters because simple abridgement by a playing company usually cuts whole speeches or clean blocks within them; sustained paraphrase at the level of the phrase suggests a transmitting memory or a revising hand rewording as it went, not only scissors. The two mechanisms are not exclusive. A performance text can be both cut for the road and garbled in transmission, and the collation shows passages where a clean cut explains the evidence sitting beside passages where only paraphrase explains it. Any single-mechanism theory of Q1 has to strain somewhere, and the strain points are where the scholarly disagreement, examined in a later section, takes hold.

There are also whole presences and absences at the level of the set piece. The musicians' scene after the discovery of the apparently dead Juliet, the prose comedy of Peter and the musicians in 4.5, contracts severely in Q1, as comedy that depends on verbal fooling tends to when a text is shortened. The Chorus speeches stand in both witnesses, framing the action in each. The Prince's closing speech stands in both, though trimmed in the shorter text. Romeo's dream speech in Mantua and the apothecary episode survive in outline with their verse thinned. The map of survival is consistent enough to state as a rule: in Q1, whatever an audience needed in order to follow the tragedy is present, and whatever existed to be savoured is at risk.

## Material Moved, Not Cut: Transpositions and Scene Order

Compression is only one of the ways the two quartos part company. A second class of difference is transposition: material present in both texts but placed differently, and this class is small in bulk and large in significance. The clearest instance stands in the final scene. In Q2, and in every modern edition that follows it, the testimony before the Prince in 5.3 unfolds with Balthasar's account and the Friar's long narrative in a settled order, the servant's evidence and the letter framing the Friar's full explanation of the potion plot. Q1 brings the same witnesses forward in a different order, rearranging who speaks before whom at the tragedy's moment of public accounting. The words are largely the play's words; the sequence is not. A transposition of this kind is hard to explain as a printer's error, since it involves whole blocks of dialogue. It reads either as a memory's natural reordering of a scene whose events mattered more than their sequence, or as a staging adjustment, made because a company found the scene played better with the explanations marshalled differently.

Local transpositions occur elsewhere at smaller scale. Within the Queen Mab speech, Q1 reorders elements of Mercutio's catalogue, a matter treated at passage length in the dedicated section below. Within speeches, couplets change places, and half-lines migrate. The balcony scene contains small displacements around the vow exchanges, where the order of pledges in Q1 does not match the Q2 sequence that editions print. Each instance is minor alone. Together they establish that Q1's relationship to Q2 is not that of a shortened copy, because a copyist shortening a text does not usually move its blocks around. The shorter witness behaves like a text that passed through performance, where scenes are reassembled nightly by memory, cue, and habit, and through a transmission that preserved the substance of episodes more faithfully than their architecture.

### Where does the first quarto move material rather than cut it?

The clearest transposition stands in the final scene, where the first quarto reorders the testimony laid before the Prince, bringing the witnesses forward in a different sequence from the second quarto while keeping their substance. Smaller moves occur inside the Queen Mab catalogue and around the balcony vows, where couplets and pledges change places between the two witnesses.

Scene-level order, by contrast, is stable between the witnesses for almost the whole play, and the stability deserves emphasis because it disciplines the comparison. Q1 does not present a differently plotted play. The scenes follow the same succession from the brawl to the tomb, with the same entrances of the same persons in the same situations. Whoever abridged or reported the shorter text treated the scene architecture as fixed, and altered the filling rather than the frame. That conservatism is itself evidence about origins: improvisation or wholesale rewriting would have disturbed the frame somewhere, while transmission through performance, which depends on a stable sequence of cues, preserves it. The comparison therefore yields a two-level picture. At the level of the scene, the witnesses agree. At the level of the line, the speech, and occasionally the episode's internal order, they diverge constantly, and the divergence is where editors earn their keep.

## Speeches Reassigned: When the Quartos Give a Line to Different Speakers

A third class of difference changes not how much is said or in what order, but who says it. Speech assignments differ between the quartos at a scatter of points across the play, and each difference, however small, alters the local meaning, because a line's sense in drama is half carried by its speaker. In the lamentation over the supposedly dead Juliet in 4.5, the distribution of grief among Capulet, his wife, the Nurse, and Paris is not identical in the two witnesses; lines of lament that Q2 assigns to one mourner appear under another's name in Q1, and the balance of the scene shifts accordingly, from formal antiphony toward a different distribution of voices. In the opening brawl and its aftermath, small assignments in the servants' and citizens' exchanges vary. Around the duels in 3.1, the allocation of cries and interjections differs at points that affect how the catastrophe's blame is voiced in the moment.

Editors faced with a reassignment must decide which witness to follow, and the working rule that emerges from modern practice is instructive. Where Q2's assignment makes sense, it stands, because Q2 is the copy-text and its speech headings descend from the author's draft. Where Q2's assignment is impossible or manifestly confused, and Q1's is coherent, editors have sometimes crossed to Q1, reasoning that a performance text can preserve the true allocation where a draft's heading was ambiguous. The draft behind Q2 is known to be untidy in its headings at exactly this kind of point: characters enter the author's papers under varying names and descriptions, and a heading misread by a compositor can move a line from one speaker to another without leaving any other trace. A reassignment is therefore one of the places where the two witnesses genuinely compete, because each has a plausible channel to the truth: Q2 through the author's hand, Q1 through the company's practice.

The stakes of reassignment rise with the importance of the line. A moved interjection in a crowd scene changes little. A moved line in the lovers' exchanges changes the erotics of a scene, and a moved line in the final testimony changes the tragedy's public verdict on its own events. The collation shows Q1 and Q2 diverging most consequentially where voices crowd together: laments, brawls, discoveries, the tomb. Those are the scenes where a reporting memory would most easily confuse speakers, and also the scenes where a playing company's settled practice would be most authoritative. The same evidence supports two opposite morals, and the honest editorial response, visible in the best modern editions, is case-by-case judgment recorded in the apparatus rather than a blanket rule for either witness.

Keeping the two texts' variants straight across hundreds of pages of collation is the kind of work that rewards a private research system, and editors and students alike increasingly keep parallel notes, variant lists, and scene files in a local-first workspace such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the collation for one scene can sit beside the reading text and grow with the work. The tool matters less than the habit: the comparison is cumulative, its evidence scattered, and its verdicts depend on holding many small facts in view at once.

## The Cruxes: Variant Readings Where One Word Divides the Witnesses

Beyond cuts, moves, and reassignments stands the class of difference that readers meet most often without knowing it: the variant reading, where both texts print a line but print it differently. Most variants are small, a synonym, a tense, an inverted phrase, the kind of drift that any transmission produces. A few are famous, because the word at stake carries meaning out of proportion to its size, and the most famous of all stands in the lovers' first exchange at the ball. As Romeo takes Juliet's hand and begins the shared sonnet, Q2 reads "This holy shrine, the gentle fine is this" (1.5.92 in modern numbering), with "fine" understood as a penalty or payment for the profaning touch. Q1 reads "the gentle sin is this". Editors have divided over the pair for centuries. "Sin" completes the sonnet's religious conceit with perfect economy: the touch is a sin, her lips must purge it, the kiss becomes an act of absolution. "Fine" carries a legal colouring that some editors judge the harder and therefore the likelier authorial reading, since transmission tends to smooth difficulties into ease. Most modern editions print Q2's "fine", and record Q1's "sin" in the apparatus, where it waits as a standing reminder that the most quoted love lines in English survive in two forms.

The balcony scene supplies a run of smaller variants that show paraphrase and variant blurring into one another. Where Q2's Romeo swears "Lady, by yonder blessed moon I swear, / That tips with silver all these fruit-tree tops" (2.2.107-108), the shorter text gives a flattened version of the oath with wording displaced, and Juliet's answering protest against moon-swearing arrives in compressed form. Where Q2's Juliet coins the play's economics of generosity, "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2.133-135), Q1 keeps the thought in fewer and plainer words. Read in parallel, the scene in Q1 is recognisably the balcony scene and recognisably not the text that anthologies quote: its skeleton is the same, its music reduced. Editors print Q2 here without hesitation, and the Q1 variants serve scholarship rather than the reading text, as evidence of how the scene sounded when transmission rather than the draft carried it.

Romeo's dying speech offers the gravest test of the variant class. In Q2 the speech runs at full length, through the address to the "detestable maw" of the tomb (5.3.45) and the toast "Here's to my love" before the drug is drunk (5.3.119). In Q1 the same beats occur in drastically fewer lines, the speech compressed toward its outline, with phrasing that sometimes matches Q2 closely and sometimes drifts into approximation. No editor builds a reading text from Q1 here. Yet the Q1 outline is precious to theatre history, because it shows the speech as a playable unit of a certain length, and it disciplines speculation about the full text's performance: the fuller speech was either cut in the playing Q1 records, or played by a Romeo whose company accepted a longer final scene than the shorter witness preserves. Either way, the variant pattern at the play's climax is evidence about the early stage life of the tragedy that no single-witness account could supply.

### How does the comparison change the meaning of a familiar line?

At the cruxes, a single word carries the difference: the first quarto's "gentle sin" against the second quarto's "gentle fine" in the lovers' first sonnet turns the kiss from an absolution into a payment. Parallel reading teaches that familiar lines are editorial settlements, not certainties, and that the losing reading often preserves the conceit's logic as sharply as the winner.

A methodological point follows from the cruxes, and editors state it in various forms. A variant is not automatically an error. Where two readings both make sense, the choice between them is a judgment about transmission: which reading is more likely to have been corrupted into the other. The principle that the harder reading deserves respect, because copyists and memories simplify, is the textual critic's old rule, and it operates throughout the Q1 and Q2 collation, usually in Q2's favour and occasionally, where Q2's reading looks like a compositor's guess at a difficult draft, in Q1's. The apparatus of a good edition is the record of those judgments, and reading it against the two quartos in parallel is the only way to see how much of the familiar play is decision rather than datum.

## Stage Directions: The First Quarto's Performance Record

On one axis of the comparison the shorter text does not merely hold its own against the fuller; it wins. Q1's stage directions are fuller, more frequent, and more practical than Q2's, and they record the play as a sequence of physical events in a way the authorial draft behind Q2 often does not. The fuller text's directions can be literary or permissive, written by a dramatist imagining the scene rather than a company executing it. Q1's directions read like the residue of execution. They specify who enters with what, where bodies go, and how the stage picture is composed at moments where Q2 leaves the staging to inference. In the final scene, the shorter text is explicit about the forcing of the tomb and the disposition of the bodies. Around Juliet's waking, Q1's directions track her movement from the bier into the scene's closing business with a clerk's care. At the ball, in the fights, and at the discovery of the bodies, the same fullness recurs.

Editors have acted on this evidence for generations, and their practice states the comparison's verdict in its purest form: modern editions take stage directions from Q1 at points where Q2 has none or has one too vague to stage. The dagger business at Juliet's death, the handling of the watch's discoveries, the management of entrances where the draft is silent: at such points the apparatus records a direction adopted from the 1597 text into a reading text otherwise built on 1599. The crossings are individually small and collectively decisive for how the play reads on the page, because directions are the reader's only staging. A fuller account of how the early texts differ in their directions as a class belongs to the dedicated study of [stage directions in the early texts](/2026/06/26/stage-directions-early-texts/), which treats the directions witness by witness. The comparative point stands here: Q1's directions are the strongest internal evidence that the shorter text passed through a playhouse, since no one inventing a text for the press alone bothers to record how a tomb was opened or where a body fell with such consistency.

### Which quarto gives fuller stage directions, and why does the gap matter?

The first quarto gives the fuller directions, records of entrances, properties, fights, and bodies, where the second quarto is often sparse or literary. The gap matters because editors stage the printed play partly from the shorter text: directions adopted from the first quarto govern how readers picture scenes that the fuller text leaves unstageable on the page.

Two cautions govern the use of Q1's directions, and the comparison is incomplete without them. First, fullness is not the same as authorial intention. A direction can record what a company did without recording what the dramatist wrote, and companies adapt business to their stage, their actors, and their properties. Second, Q1's directions occasionally describe business that the dialogue contradicts or that seems designed for a different disposition of the stage, reminders that performance evidence is evidence of a performance, not of the play in the abstract. The best editorial practice threads between credulity and dismissal: Q1's directions are admitted where Q2 is silent and the business they describe is consonant with the dialogue, and they are recorded in the apparatus where they are not admitted, so that the reader can reconstruct the staging the first audience may have seen. That threading is editorial judgment at its most exposed, and it is one of the places where the Q1 and Q2 comparison stops being bibliography and becomes interpretation.

The directions also contribute to the origins debate from an unexpected angle. A text reconstructed from memory by actors would naturally carry the directions those actors' bodies remembered, and a text abridged for performance would naturally accumulate directions as the abridgement was fitted to the stage. Both theories predict Q1's directional fullness, so the fullness cannot choose between them. What the directions do exclude is the notion that Q1 is a merely literary corruption, a bookseller's botch assembled at a desk. Its pages know where the bodies were. Whatever else Q1 is, it is a text that has been on a stage, and every account of it has to begin from that knowledge.

## Queen Mab Across the Quartos: A Passage-Length Comparison

The Queen Mab speech is the comparison in miniature, and for that reason it serves as the test passage for every theory of the two texts. Mercutio's catalogue of the fairies' midwife, who "gallops night by night / Through lovers' brains, and then they dream of love" (1.4.70-71), runs in Q2 to a full, intricately ordered flight: the coach of an empty hazelnut, the spoked wheels of spiders' legs, the catalogue of sleepers from courtiers to the parson, and the darkening close in which the hag teaches maids to bear. Q1 prints the speech shorter. Elements of the catalogue change places; some of the sleepers vanish; phrasing that Q2 keeps at a high polish arrives flattened at points, as if the conceit had been carried in a memory that retained its structure and its best lines while shedding connective tissue. The speech's arc survives. Its proportions do not.

At passage length, three comparative facts stand out. First, Q1's Mab is performable as it stands: it begins, builds, darkens, and is cut off by Romeo's "Thou talk'st of nothing" (1.4.96) at the same structural joint as Q2's. Second, the lines Q1 keeps are disproportionately the famous ones, the hazelnut coach, the lovers' brains, the blisters on ladies' lips, which is the retention pattern of memory rather than of editing, since an editor cutting for length would keep the structure and sacrifice the ornament evenly. Third, the reordering inside the catalogue touches sequence without touching sense, the signature of a speech recalled as a set piece rather than transcribed from a page. None of this settles the speech's transmission alone, and the microscopic collation of the speech line by line belongs to the separate study of [the Queen Mab speech across the quartos](/2026/05/08/queen-mab-speech-across-quartos/), which owns that detail. The passage-level verdict is enough for the comparison: Mab shows Q1 behaving exactly as it behaves across the whole play, keeping the architecture, taxing the ornament, and drifting in the wording, and a theory of Q1 that cannot explain Mab cannot explain the shorter text.

Editors print Q2's Mab entire, and no serious alternative exists, because the speech's meaning lives in its accumulation and Q1's accumulation is partial. Yet the Q1 Mab retains a scholarly use that goes beyond evidence about transmission. It demonstrates that the speech was always detachable in performance, a set piece whose length a company could tune, and it gives directors a period precedent for the cuts that nearly every production makes. The comparison thus returns a dividend in the theatre as well as the study: the shorter quarto is the first recorded production decision about the play's most famous flight of fancy, and its decision was to keep the frame and trim the flight.

## How the Shorter Text Came to Be: Memorial Reconstruction and Its Challengers

Every comparison of the quartos eventually becomes an argument about origins, because the pattern of differences demands a cause. The classical account descends from A. W. Pollard, who in 1909 grouped Q1 with the so-called bad quartos, and from W. W. Greg, who gave the group its standard explanation: memorial reconstruction. On Greg's account, a text like Q1 was assembled from memory, typically by actors from the company that owned the play, and sold to a printer when the company was on tour or in financial straits. The theory explains the collation's odd mixture with elegance. A reporting actor remembers his own part and the scenes he stood in with near accuracy; scenes he watched from the tiring house or never appeared in come back paraphrased; cues blur; speeches migrate between speakers; whole lyric passages collapse into their gist. Harry R. Hoppe's study of this play's shorter text pressed the theory to its most specific form, hunting for the reporting actor in the pattern of accuracy itself, and finding the text most exact where particular roles were concerned and vaguest where those roles were offstage.

The challengers have been formidable. Kathleen Irace, in Reforming the "Bad" Quartos, argued that texts of this class are better understood as performance adaptations, deliberate reworkings for the stage, shortened and adjusted by companies for touring conditions and smaller casts, and that their differences from the fuller texts show design where the reconstruction theory sees decay. Laurie E. Maguire's Shakespearean Suspect Texts subjected the diagnostic features of memorial reconstruction to systematic test and found them unreliable: the symptoms said to prove a reported text appear in texts known to be good, and fail to appear in texts known to be reported, so the diagnosis rests on circularity more often than the theory's confidence allows. Paul Werstine pressed the bibliographical side of the scepticism, arguing that the bad quarto narrative says more about twentieth-century editorial desires for a pure authorial original than about the early book trade, and that the evidence for piracy and reconstruction dissolves under scrutiny of the records.

### Why was the shorter quarto branded a bad quarto?

Pollard's label of 1909 sorted the first quarto among the bad quartos because it is shorter, paraphrased in stretches, and not the author's draft, and Greg's memorial reconstruction supplied the story making those faults coherent: actors rebuilding a play from memory for a printer. The brand stuck for a century; its fairness is what the challengers reopened.

The single-witness facts behind the label, the printing, the title page, and the text's physical character, belong to the study of [the First Quarto of 1597: the 'bad' quarto](/2026/03/27/first-quarto-1597-bad-quarto/), which owns that ground. The comparative adjudication belongs here, and it runs as follows. Memorial reconstruction in its strong form, one or two actors dictating the whole play from memory, explains the paraphrase and the speaker confusion but strains at the collation's contrary evidence: long stretches of Q1 are too accurate, too close to Q2's wording, for unaided memory of a three-hour play, and the cuts are too intelligently patterned, taxing lyric and sparing plot with a consistency that memory loss does not produce. Pure adaptation explains the pattern of cuts and the enriched directions but strains at the paraphrase, since a company adapting its own promptbook has the true words in front of it and no need to flatten "gentle fine" into gist or to mangle a conceit it could simply shorten. The verdict the collation supports is a composite. Q1 descends from the play in performance, most plausibly through an abridgement made for the stage and transmitted through channels that included memory, whether of actors reporting their parts or of a text reconstructed around a performance. The shorter quarto is a performance text twice over, shaped by the stage and degraded, in its wording, by the route it travelled to the printer. Each theory names one of its parents correctly and errs in claiming sole paternity.

That composite verdict also disposes of the moral colouring in Pollard's label. A performance-derived text is not a failed copy of the author's draft; it is a successful record of a different thing. The twentieth century's editors needed Q2 to be the play and Q1 to be its corruption, because copy-text editing requires a hierarchy. The comparison, read without that need, shows two artefacts with different evidentiary gifts, and the modern shift in the scholarship, from verdicts of badness to accounts of function, is the comparison's own conclusion arriving a century late.

## What Editors Actually Do When the Quartos Disagree

The editorial settlement that the comparison produces can be stated as a working method, and it is best seen in the practice of the major editions. The foundation is the copy-text principle associated with Greg's rationale of copy-text: one witness is chosen to govern the edition's accidentals, its spelling and punctuation texture, and to serve as the default for substantives, and for this play that witness is Q2, printed from copy close to the author's draft. Brian Gibbons's Arden edition built on Q2 while recording Q1's variants; G. Blakemore Evans's Cambridge edition did the same with a fuller textual essay; Jill Levenson's Oxford edition prints a Q2-based text with the comparison woven through its introduction; René Weis's Arden edition likewise takes Q2 as copy-text and emends from Q1 at chosen points. The facts of the fuller witness as a physical and bibliographical object belong to the study of [the Second Quarto of 1599: the 'good' quarto](/2026/04/03/second-quarto-1599-good-quarto/); the comparative doctrine is what concerns this article.

### Which quarto should a first-time reader open?

A first-time reader should open an edition built on the second quarto, because that text carries the play at full authorial length, and should treat the first quarto as the second book on the desk: the record of the play in performance, consulted for staging, for variants, and for the knowledge that the familiar text was assembled, not simply found.

The method has three moving parts. First, default to Q2: where Q2 gives a coherent reading, it stands, because its channel to the author is the shortest. Second, emend from Q1 where Q2 is manifestly wrong and Q1 is right, a small class of cases in which the draft's difficulty defeated its compositors while the performance tradition carried the true reading through; each such emendation is an argument, printed in the apparatus with its evidence. Third, supplement the staging from Q1: directions the draft lacks are admitted from the performance text where the dialogue welcomes them, marked as editorial in any honest edition. The three parts embody a single principle, which answers the brief's complication of which text to trust where they differ. Trust is not a property of a witness; it is a judgment per reading, governed by each witness's known channel of transmission. Q2 is trusted for words because it descends from the writing; Q1 is trusted, more cautiously, for staging and occasionally for sense, because it descends from the playing. An editor who trusted either witness wholesale would produce either a draft's confusions dignified as a text or a performance's abbreviations dignified as the play.

The First Folio's place in the settlement confirms the doctrine by inheritance. The Folio text of the tragedy descends through the third quarto from Q2, corrected in places, so the Folio is a grandchild of the fuller quarto rather than an independent witness, and its agreements with Q2 count for little while its disagreements are mostly the noise of reprinting. Editors therefore collate principally between the two quartos, and the Folio enters the apparatus as a derivative voice. The comparison's binary shape, two witnesses, two channels, one authorial and one theatrical, is unusually clean for a Shakespeare play, and it is why this play's textual problem teaches the general method so well: every editorial decision can be traced to a judgment about which channel a given reading travelled.

There remains the question of what the edition does to the reader's play, and the comparison gives that question a sharp edge. A Q2-based edition with Q1 directions silently naturalises a hybrid: words from the desk, bodies from the stage. Readers who never open the apparatus never learn which parts of their text were decided at cruxes like "gentle sin" and "gentle fine", or that the Friar's closing explanation exists in a version a third of its printed length, or that the testimony in the final scene once ran in a different order. The hybrid is the right text to read; the comparison insists only that it be read knowingly. That is the deepest sense in which the two quartos' differences reveal the play: they reveal that the play, as a printed object, is an editorial achievement built on two partial witnesses, and that every quotation from it is a small act of trust in judgments a reader can inspect but has usually not.

## Passages the Fuller Text Keeps Alone: Reading Q2 Against the Gaps

The most efficient way to feel the comparison is to read a Q2 set piece and then look for it in Q1. Juliet's invocation over the coming night, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2.1-2), runs in the fuller text through the wish for the "waggoner Phaeton", the plea for a cloud curtain, the address to "love-performing night", and the prayer for Romeo's scattering into stars, a soliloquy of more than thirty lines that sets the erotic and fatal temperature of the whole scene. The shorter text carries the speech in reduced form, its opening impulse present and its elaboration taxed. The same shape repeats at the apothecary's shop in Mantua, where Q2's Romeo inventories the poverty of the place, "And in his needy shop a tortoise hung, / An alligator stuff'd, and other skins / Of ill-shaped fishes" (5.1.41-43), in a description whose grotesque plenty makes the purchase of poison feel like an entry into death's own storeroom. Q1 keeps the transaction and thins the inventory. In each case the plot loses nothing and the play loses a stratum: the imagery systems by which the tragedy thinks.

These Q2-only strata matter to criticism in a way that is easy to miss. Arguments about the play's imagery, its patterning of light and dark, its animal figures, its economics of bounty and debt, are built overwhelmingly from passages that Q1 attenuates or omits. The famous density of the lovers' language is a Q2 phenomenon at full strength. A performance tradition that played something closer to the shorter text heard a leaner, faster, more anecdotal tragedy, in which the story's bones carried more of the evening and the verbal flesh less. Neither experience is the true one in isolation; they are the play's two early modes of existence, and the comparison is the only instrument that registers both. Critics who write about the tragedy as a verbal artefact are writing about Q2's play, whether they say so or not, and the collation is what entitles a reader to add that qualification.

### How much of Juliet's potion soliloquy survives in the shorter text?

The shorter text keeps the potion soliloquy's situation and its spine, Juliet alone with the vial, weighing the fears that the fuller text unfolds, but compresses the unfolding severely: the catalogue of horrors, the waking among the dead, the terror of madness, survive as a reduced sequence rather than at the second quarto's full, accumulating length.

The gaps also expose the shorter text's priorities at the level of the individual speaker. The Friar's share shrinks disproportionately, because his speeches carry the play's exposition and its moralising, and a performance text can compress exposition once the audience has the plot. The lovers lose lines in absolute terms but keep the highest proportion of their great speeches' openings and closings, the parts a memory retains and an audience waits for. Mercutio loses catalogue members but keeps his set pieces' frames. The Nurse loses fooling. The distribution reads like the residue of evenings actually played, in which what survived was what landed, and what landed was beginnings, endings, jokes with a payoff in action, and anything the story could not proceed without. As a record of early modern theatrical taste operating on this specific play, the pattern of the gaps is evidence of a kind no preface or account book supplies.

## What the Shorter Text Alone Preserves

The traffic is not all one way. Q1 holds material that Q2 lacks, and although the bulk is small, its existence disciplines any account that treats the shorter text as a mere subtraction. Its stage directions are the largest class, treated in their own section above: directions for business, movement, and the handling of the tomb scene that the fuller text never prints. Beyond the directions, Q1 preserves a scattering of lines and half-lines found nowhere else, connective tissue in dialogue, extra phrases in crowd and servant exchanges, and occasional readings where its wording has persuaded editors against the copy-text. The class is miscellaneous because its origins are miscellaneous: a company's settled business, a reporter's accurate memory of a passage the draft set down ambiguously, a genuine authorial phrase that the draft behind Q2 happened to lose or that the fuller text's compositors corrupted.

The mechanism by which Q1 can beat Q2 at individual points deserves a precise statement, since it justifies the eclectic element in modern editing. Q2's copy was a working draft, and drafts contain slips: a word miswritten, a heading misplaced, a phrase left in a state the author would have corrected at the next stage. Transmission through performance can, at such points, preserve the corrected or intended reading, because the company played the line in a form that worked and the shorter text descends from that playing. When Q2's reading is nonsense or limps and Q1's is clean, editors infer that the performance channel carried the truth past the draft's blemish. The inference is never certain, since a reporter can also tidy a difficulty into sense by accident, and the apparatus exists precisely to mark the difference between a reading an editor adopted on this reasoning and a reading the copy-text simply gives. But the class is real, and its reality is the standing refutation of any rule that would seal Q2 against correction from its shorter rival.

There is also a theatrical category of Q1-only value: pacing evidence. Where the shorter text reorders a joke, shortens a delay, or brings a witness on sooner, it may record solutions that playing discovered, and directors have mined it for exactly that. The Nurse's scenes, the musicians' fooling, the management of the final discoveries, all exist in Q1 in stage-tested proportions. A modern production that cuts the fuller text, as nearly all do, is repeating the shorter quarto's founding act, and some directors consult it openly as the first cut of the play ever preserved. The comparison thus gives the theatre a genealogy: the abridged Romeo and Juliet is not a modern vandalism but a form the play took within two years of its first performances, printed while the company that created it still held the stage.

One more class of the shorter text's sole preservations belongs to the study of names and forms. Across the play, small verbal forms differ between the witnesses in ways that no argument can elevate into cruxes and no editor can afford to ignore: the shapes of names in headings and dialogue, the handling of oaths and interjections, the wording of the watch's and servants' functional lines, the minor connective phrases by which scenes are joined. Individually these are the small change of collation. Collectively they form the background texture against which the large differences are measured, and they carry a specific evidentiary use. A transmission channel reveals itself in its small change more honestly than in its set pieces, because no one, neither reporter nor abridger nor compositor, bothers to falsify or to polish the functional words. Where the shorter text's small change runs consistently toward the idiom of the playhouse, colloquial, cue-conscious, and economical, the channel argument gains the kind of support that statistics gives and rhetoric cannot: hundreds of tiny agreements in tendency, each worthless alone, decisive in aggregate.

The same discipline governs the shorter text's unique connective lines, the brief phrases and half-lines it supplies where the fuller text passes directly between beats. Some of these are a stage's carpentry, the words a company found necessary to get a body across a stage or a scene out of a door. Some are a memory filling a seam it could feel but not recall. An editor prints almost none of them, and a reader of the reading text never meets them, yet they complete the comparison's portrait of its shorter witness as a working object, a text that was used, mended, and kept playable, bearing the marks of use the way a prompt copy bears them. The fuller text was preserved by being printed well once. The shorter text was preserved by being useful, and its unique scraps are the fingerprints of that usefulness.

## Accidentals and Substantives: The Texture Beneath the Words

Beneath the level of wording lies a stratum of difference that readers of modern editions never see, because modern editions replace it wholesale: the accidentals, the spelling, punctuation, capitalisation, and lineation of the early pages. Greg's distinction between substantives, the readings that affect meaning, and accidentals, the texture in which readings are clothed, organises the comparison at this depth. Q2's accidentals descend from an author's draft filtered through compositors; its punctuation is the light, rhetorical pointing of the period, its spellings partly the dramatist's own, its verse sometimes mislined where the draft's layout misled the print shop. Q1's accidentals descend through more hands and show it: rougher pointing, more irregular lineation, prose and verse set awry at points where transmission lost the shape of the verse. The shorter text's mangled lineation in lyric passages is itself one of the collation's quiet arguments for a memorial channel, since a copyist with a written text before him rarely destroys verse shape so thoroughly, while a dictating memory loses line endings first.

Editors modernise the accidentals of both witnesses in a reading text, and the comparison explains why that modernisation is not a neutral act. To repunctuate a Q2 speech is to decide where its clauses turn, and at cruxes the decision steers interpretation as surely as a variant word does. To reline a passage is to decide its metre and therefore its pace. The modern page, with its clean act and scene numbers, its regularised headings, its locations supplied for scenes the early texts leave unplaced, is a third artefact built over both quartos, and the scholarly edition's collation is the only place where the building shows. For the comparison's purposes, the lesson is that Q1 and Q2 differ not only in what they say but in what kind of object each is: Q2 a draft's print, bearing an author's habits under a print shop's; Q1 a performance's print, bearing a company's habits under a reporter's and a print shop's together. Every stratum of each page is evidence about the channel it came through, which is why bibliographers read paper, type, and headings with the same care that critics read speeches.

Speech headings supply a neat instance at this stratum. The fuller text's headings vary as a draft's headings vary, a character designated now by name, now by role or description, as the author's pen kept pace with invention; compositors and editors regularise what the draft left fluid. The shorter text's headings have their own irregularities, including designations drawn from the playhouse rather than the study. Neither witness gives the tidy system of a modern dramatis personae, and the comparison of headings is one of the tools by which scholars identify the copy behind each print: fluid, inconsistent naming points to papers close to composition, while the playhouse flavour of certain headings in Q1 points to its theatrical channel. Small evidence, patiently accumulated across a whole collation, is what turns the Q1 and Q2 problem from a contest of impressions into a discipline.

## The Verdict of the Comparison: What Each Text Is For

The sections above converge on a settlement that can now be stated whole. Q2 is the text of the play as written: the copy-text for editors, the source of the verse as criticism quotes it, the witness whose authority rests on its descent from the author's papers. Q1 is the text of the play as played: shorter by a quarter, patterned by performance, paraphrased where transmission wore the wording, enriched where the stage left its marks in directions and business, and capable, at scattered points, of correcting the draft that outranks it. The differences between them reveal that the early modern play existed in both modes at once, that print preserved each mode once, imperfectly, and that the modern text is a negotiated settlement between the two survivals.

Three practical consequences follow for anyone who reads, teaches, stages, or cites the tragedy. First, quotation is Q2 quotation: the lines the culture remembers descend from the fuller text, and the shorter text's versions, however interesting, are evidence about those lines rather than rivals for the memory. Second, staging is partly Q1 staging: the directions embedded in modern editions include the performance text's contribution, and a reader picturing the scenes is picturing a hybrid. Third, certainty is apparatus-deep: wherever a line matters to an argument, the honest procedure is to check the collation, because the comparison shows how often a famous reading was a choice, and how respectable the rejected alternative can be. The cliché of the timeless love story rests on a single smoothed text. The two quartos, read against each other, restore the choices, the labour, and the theatre inside it.

The comparison's final lesson is about evidence itself. Two partial witnesses, each compromised in a different way, yield more knowledge in collation than either yields alone, because their compromises are different and their agreements can therefore be trusted where they occur. Where Q1 and Q2 agree, which is across most of the play's substance, the text stands on two independent channels, the desk and the stage, and agreement between channels is the strongest warrant early modern drama can offer. Where they disagree, the disagreement is itself information, about writing, about playing, about memory, about print. That is what the differences reveal, and it is why the collation of two small books from the last years of the sixteenth century remains the foundation on which every modern Romeo and Juliet, on the page and on the stage, is built. No edition escapes the comparison; every edition is its answer.

## How Scholars Collate: The Working Method Behind the Comparison

The comparison rests on a discipline with its own tools and habits, and a reader who understands the method can judge the verdicts it produces. Collation begins with the physical books: surviving copies of each quarto are compared against one another first, because copies of the same edition can differ where a print shop corrected type mid-run, and a variant between copies must not be mistaken for a variant between editions. The mechanical collator associated with Charlton Hinman, which superimposes images of two pages so that differences flicker into visibility, was built for the First Folio but embodies the principle for all early texts: establish what each edition actually reads before arguing about what the readings mean. Only then does the substantive collation start, the two texts set in parallel, a scene at a time, every difference of wording, assignment, order, and direction recorded without yet being explained.

The recording stage is deliberately atheoretical, and its discipline explains the authority of the results. A collator logs that the shorter text lacks a couplet, gives a speech to another speaker, prints a synonym, spells a name otherwise, and the log makes no distinction between the trivial and the momentous. Explanation comes afterward and must account for the whole log, not for selected highlights. This is why single striking variants, however famous, cannot carry a theory of Q1 by themselves: "gentle sin" is one entry in a register of thousands, and the theories live or die on the register's overall pattern, the distribution of paraphrase, the geography of accuracy and error across scenes and speakers. Modern parallel-text presentations, printing the two quartos on facing pages or in aligned columns, extend the same discipline to any reader's desk, and they have changed the comparison's sociology: judgments that once required a research library's rare book room can now be checked by any student willing to read two columns slowly.

A third stage follows explanation, and it is the editor's: the construction of a reading text from the collated witnesses, with every departure from the copy-text and every adoption from the rival witness declared in the apparatus. The method's honesty is procedural. An editor may be wrong about any given reading, but the collation and the apparatus expose the reasoning to correction, and later editors re-argue the same entries with better accounts of transmission. The Q1 and Q2 literature reads, across a century, like a single long audit of this kind: Pollard's classification, Greg's mechanism, Hoppe's identifications, the sceptics' counter-tests, the performance account, each generation re-sorting the same thousands of logged differences under a better explanation. The comparison in this article stands in that line, and its composite verdict, a performance-shaped text transmitted partly through memory, is offered as the explanation that wastes least of the evidence.

## Comedy and Prose in the Two Texts

The comparison has a comic register as well as a tragic one, and it confirms the pattern from an unexpected side. Prose, which carries the play's servants, its fooling, and much of the Nurse, behaves differently in transmission from verse. Verse has metre as a preservative: a half-remembered pentameter can be rebuilt around its rhythm, and its errors show against the measure. Prose has no such skeleton, and in the shorter text the prose passages drift further from the fuller text's wording than the verse does, expanding and contracting around their jokes, keeping the laugh and losing the phrasing. The musicians' scene is the extreme case, its banter reduced hard in Q1, but the Nurse's great rambling speeches show the same physics at a gentler pressure: her anecdote of Juliet's childhood arrives with its story intact and its verbal surface reworked, the weaning, the earthquake, the husband's jest all present, the sentences re-knit.

Mercutio stands astride the verse and prose boundary, and his treatment in the shorter text is among the collation's most telling results. His verse flights are taxed like all lyric wealth, the Mab catalogue reordered and thinned, the wit combats compressed. His function, however, survives entire: the provocations, the bawdy, the fatal gaiety of "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1.99), all stand in Q1 at their posts. The performance text keeps what Mercutio does and trims what he says beyond the doing, which is precisely the opposite of what a reading culture does with him. For the page, Mercutio is his language; for the stage of the shorter text, he is his effect. The two quartos preserve those two Mercutios respectively, and productions ever since have had to choose, nightly, which of the two to weight.

The comic evidence also sharpens the origins argument at a point where the tragic evidence is ambiguous. Deliberate abridgement for performance cuts jokes that need time and keeps jokes that land fast, and Q1's comic cuts fit that logic well. Memorial transmission, by contrast, garbles jokes in a characteristic way, preserving punchlines and losing setups, because laughter fixes the end of a routine in memory while its approach fades. Both signatures appear in the shorter text's comedy, in different scenes, which is one more reason the composite verdict outperforms either pure theory. The collation does not force a choice between a cut text and a misremembered one when the evidence describes a text that was both, cut in the playhouse and carried to the printer by a route that memory had already edited.

## Frame Passages: The Prologue and the Closing Speech Compared

A play's frame, its first and last speeches, concentrates its textual problems, because frame passages are the most likely to be revised, memorised, and quoted. The Prologue sonnet stands in both quartos, and their agreement across its fourteen lines, "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1) through to the appeal for the audience's patient ears, is nearly complete, a reminder that the witnesses converge wherever transmission found a fixed, formal, detachable unit. Sonnets travel well. The lovers' shared sonnet at the ball converges in structure and diverges at its famous crux; the Chorus sonnet before the second act stands in both texts; and the frame's stability throws the interior's instability into relief. Where the play speaks in set forms, the witnesses agree. Where it speaks in the flowing, revisable medium of dialogue, they part.

The closing speech divides them in the opposite manner. The Prince's summing up in the fuller text, beginning "A glooming peace this morning with it brings" (5.3.304), runs through the pardons and punishments to the epitaph couplet, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). The shorter text reaches the same couplet by a shorter road: the speech is present, its office discharged, its proportions reduced, and it arrives after a testimony sequence whose internal order, as the transposition section recorded, differs from the fuller text's arrangement. The frame comparison thus ends the collation on its characteristic note. The play's most public words, the Prince's verdict on the feud, are stable in destination and unstable in extent, known by heart in a form whose fullness only one witness guarantees.

There is a fittingness in that result which the scholarship has not been slow to notice. A tragedy about messages that miscarry, letters that never arrive, and accounts given too late has come down to posterity in two accounts that disagree, each partial, each shaped by the channel that carried it. The Friar's letter to Mantua failed inside the play; outside it, the play itself reached print twice by two routes, and neither route delivered the whole. Editors stand where the Prince stands in the final scene, piecing a true story out of witnesses whose testimony overlaps, conflicts, and must be weighed rather than merely counted. The comparison of the quartos is the weighing, and it never entirely ends, because each generation of scholarship brings a new account of the channels, memory, performance, print, against which the old entries in the collation register must be sorted again.

## The Comparison in the Theatre: What Directors Take from Each Text

The stage has its own use for the collation, distinct from the study's. A director working from a modern edition inherits the editorial settlement invisibly: Q2's words, a staging partly derived from Q1, and no obligation to think about either. The comparison becomes practical the moment cuts are contemplated, and cuts are contemplated in every production, because the fuller text at full length runs long in the theatre. At that point the shorter quarto stands as the one period document that shows where a playing text of this tragedy can lose a quarter of its lines and keep its story, its scenes, and its effect. Its choices are not binding, and its paraphrases are not usable, but its proportions are evidence: the set pieces it halves, the fooling it drops, the exposition it compresses, mark the places where the play's own first interpreters found the text elastic.

Directors take three further things from the comparison. The first is licence with precedent: a production criticised for cutting the musicians or halving a soliloquy can point to a text printed in 1597 that did the same, and the argument changes from vandalism to tradition. The second is staging: the directions the shorter text preserves, for the tomb, the waking, the disposition of bodies, offer solutions tested in the play's own period, and a director blocked on the mechanics of the final scene can consult the witness that watched the scene played rather than the one that imagined it. The third is a caution about the words themselves. A company that knows the "gentle sin" variant exists rehearses the ball scene differently, aware that the sonnet was a site of instability from the beginning, and some productions have restored the shorter text's reading for a night or a run as an experiment in the play's other life. The comparison, in the theatre, is not antiquarian. It is a second promptbook, four centuries old.

### Do the two quartos end the play with the same speech?

Both quartos end with the Prince's closing speech and its epitaph couplet on Juliet and her Romeo, and the destination of the final scene is identical in the two witnesses. The road differs: the first quarto reaches the speech through a reordered testimony and prints the speech itself in shorter compass than the second quarto's full version.

Actors meet the comparison in a different part of their work, the part, in the theatrical sense. A Romeo who learns the dying speech from a modern edition learns Q2's full arc; the shorter text shows that part as it existed when the role was new, compressed toward its beats, and the contrast is a lesson in how roles grow in the study and shrink in the playhouse. Role books and cue scripts in the early theatre gave each actor only his own lines and cues, and the memorial theory of Q1's origin rests on exactly that material fact: that the play existed, for its first performers, as parts rather than as a whole, and that any reconstruction from those parts would bear the marks the collation finds. Whether or not the theory holds for this text, the comparison preserves the distinction between the play as a whole that readers hold and the play as a set of parts that its first actors held, and no account of the tragedy is complete without both objects in view.

## Beyond This Play: The Comparison as a Method for Early Drama

The Q1 and Q2 problem belongs to a class, and the class gives the comparison its final reach. Other plays of the period survive in a short early quarto and a fuller later text: Hamlet most famously, where the 1603 quarto stands to the 1604 quarto roughly as this play's 1597 text stands to its 1599 text, and the Henry V and Merry Wives quartos present versions of the same puzzle. Pollard's bad quarto label was coined for the class, and the century of argument that followed, reconstruction against adaptation against revision, rehearsed itself on each member in turn, with the evidence of each collation disciplining the theories applied to the others. This play's pair has a special standing in the class because of its date and its clarity: the two-year gap between the witnesses, the fuller text's title page advertising correction and augmentation against the shorter, and the sheer scale of the divergence make it the cleanest case in which to learn the method.

The title page of Q2 deserves its comparative moment here, since it is the only place where one witness comments on the other. "Newly corrected, augmented, and amended" is a publisher's claim that a previous text existed, was faulty, and has now been superseded, and it confirms from inside the period what the collation establishes analytically: the shorter text came first, was known to be defective as a record of the words, and was replaced by a fuller text drawn from better copy. What the advertisement cannot tell, and what it took the collation to discover, is that the defective text was not worthless: that its defects are patterned, its staging precious, and its readings occasionally right against its successor. The period's own verdict and the modern verdict differ exactly there, and the distance between the two verdicts is the history of textual scholarship in miniature.

For students of editing beyond this play, the pair teaches the general lesson with unusual economy. Authority is not a property that a document has; it is a relation between a document and a question. Asked for the words, the fuller quarto answers with authority and the shorter with interference. Asked for the staging, the relation inverts. Asked for the play as its first audiences experienced it, both answer partially and neither completely, and the honest edition or production is the one that knows which question it is asking at each moment. That discipline, learned on two small books about two households in Verona, transfers to every edited text a reader will ever open, which is the widest sense in which the differences between the quartos reveal more than the play: they reveal the conditions under which any old text reaches new readers at all.

## The Balcony Scene in Parallel: A Worked Collation

Abstract claims about paraphrase and compression earn their meaning only at the level of the scene, and the balcony scene is the fairest test the play offers, since it is the passage every reader knows and the passage where the two witnesses' characters diverge most instructively. Set the openings side by side. The fuller text begins with Romeo's rapt aside, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3), and builds through the sun and moon conceit to Juliet's entrance into speech, "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33). The shorter text reaches the same beats by a shorter road. The architecture is identical: the orchard, the window, the overhearing, the name speech, the vows, the parting. The verbal surface within that architecture is where the witnesses part, the fuller text's conceits arriving in the shorter text thinned, reordered at points, and occasionally flattened into statement.

The name speech shows the process at its most delicate. In the fuller text Juliet reasons her way through the philosophy of names: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44), an argument built from a definition, an instance, and a conclusion, and capped by the offer to surrender her own name for his. The shorter text preserves the argument's skeleton and its destination while spending fewer lines on the reasoning. A reader meeting the scene first in the shorter text would understand everything Juliet means and miss much of how the meaning is made, because the making, in this scene, is the meaning's emotional content: the lovers think in figures, and the figures are the fullness that transmission taxed. Editors print the fuller text, and the parallel reading explains both why they must and what the alternative witness is for. The shorter balcony scene is not a failed copy of the famous one. It is the same scene at the temperature of performance, where the argument has to land at speaking speed and the philosophy is carried by situation more than by elaboration.

### Which balcony lines does the shorter text keep closest to the fuller text?

The shorter text keeps the scene's hinges closest: the window opening, the name speech's destination, the vow exchanges' turning points, and the parting. Between the hinges, elaboration thins. The pattern is a memory's or a cutting company's pattern alike, preserving what the scene cannot proceed without and what an audience waits for, while the connective conceits are taxed.

The vows and the parting extend the lesson. The fuller text's exchange of oaths, with Juliet's refusal of moon-swearing and her bounty speech, runs in the shorter text in compressed form, its pledges intact and its imagery reduced, and the farewell, "Good night, good night! Parting is such sweet sorrow / That I shall say good night till it be morrow" (2.2.184-185), survives in both witnesses because closural couplets, like sonnets, travel well through every channel. The worked collation of this one scene thus returns the whole comparison in small: form and plot converge, elaboration diverges, wording holds at the hinges and drifts between them, and the editorial task is to print the fuller text while keeping the shorter text's evidence in view. A scene that exists in two such states is not a problem to be solved but a fact to be read, and the balcony scene, read in parallel, is the best single lesson in what the two quartos are.

## The Final Scene in Parallel: Order, Testimony, and the Bodies

The tomb scene tests the comparison at the opposite pole, where action outweighs language and the shorter text's strengths come forward. The sequence of events is common to both witnesses: Paris keeping watch and scattering flowers, Romeo's arrival with Balthasar and the instruments for forcing the vault, the letter of dismissal, the fight, Paris's dying plea to be laid with Juliet, Romeo's speech over the body and over Juliet, the poison, the waking, the Friar's arrival too late, the dagger, the watch, the Prince, the testimony, the reconciliation of the houses over the bodies. Within that common sequence the witnesses divide their labour exactly as the general pattern predicts. The fuller text gives the language at length: Romeo's address to the tomb, his farewell to Juliet's lips, the full narrative the Friar delivers at the end. The shorter text gives the action its clearest written form: directions for the forcing of the monument, the disposition of Paris's body, the waking and rising of Juliet, business that the fuller text leaves the reader to infer from dialogue.

The testimony sequence, where the witnesses transpose material rather than merely differing in fullness, has been noted in its own section, and the parallel reading shows why the transposition matters beyond bibliography. The order in which Balthasar and the Friar lay their accounts before the Prince shapes the scene's jurisprudence: whose evidence frames whose, how the letter corroborates the narrative, where the audience's superior knowledge is discharged into the characters' ignorance. A company that reordered that sequence, or a memory that reassembled it differently, changed the tragedy's public meaning at its moment of accounting, and the difference between the orders is the largest interpretive divergence in the whole collation that does not involve a single word's wording. Editors retain the fuller text's order, as copy-text discipline requires, and the apparatus preserves the alternative as the record of a stage that arranged its explanations otherwise.

### What does the shorter text's final scene reveal about playing the ending?

It reveals an ending engineered for momentum: testimony marshalled for speed, business specified so that bodies, torch, and tomb compose a legible picture, and speeches held to their beats. The shorter final scene plays like a company protecting the tragedy's climax from fatigue, spending its remaining time on discovery and grief rather than on explanation at full length.

Read whole, the final scene in parallel settles the comparison's tone. Neither witness embarrasses the other. The fuller text's Friar explains more than any audience needs and exactly as much as a reader wants; the shorter text's stage knows how the scene moves and says so; the transposed testimony shows the episode being thought about by practitioners; the closing couplet arrives in both. An editor assembling the modern ending takes words from one channel and movement from the other and calls the result the play, and the parallel reading is what makes that assembly honest. The tragedy's last scene, in which partial accounts are pieced into a true story over two bodies, is the play's own image of its textual condition, and the two quartos are the witnesses whose conflicting testimony the reader, like the Prince, must hear in full before judging.

## Two Routes to Print: The Witnesses' Channels Compared

Behind every difference catalogued above stands a difference of journey, and comparing the journeys is the comparison's explanatory core. The fuller text travelled a short road: from the author's working papers, the foul papers in the scholarship's term, to a print shop, where compositors set it with the draft's untidiness still visible in its pages, its variable speech headings, its occasional duplicated or tangled lines, its directions that read like a writer thinking about staging rather than a company recording it. The shorter text travelled a long road with more hands on it: from the stage, where the play existed as parts, cues, and a book of the play held by the company, through abridgement or report, into a form that a printer could set, with the journey's wear showing in paraphrase, transposition, reassignment, and the compensating fullness of its directions. Each witness's characteristic virtues are the virtues of its road. The draft's road preserves wording because nothing intervened between composition and print except a print shop. The stage's road preserves business because everything it carried had been embodied, nightly, by actors whose movements were the text's other memory.

The comparison of channels converts the collation's data into judgments. A variant in wording is weighed according to the probability that each road would produce it. Where the fuller text's reading is difficult and the shorter text's is smooth, the stage road explains the smoothness as wear, and the draft's reading stands. Where the fuller text's reading fails and the shorter text's works, the draft road explains the failure as a slip of the pen preserved by a print shop, while the stage road, which had to speak the line nightly, explains the working reading as the play's own correction of its papers. A variant in direction is weighed in the opposite direction, the stage road being the credible carrier of business and the draft road the credible carrier only of intention. The method is probabilistic through and through, and its conclusions are therefore always revisable, but it is not arbitrary: it ties every editorial choice to a claim about how texts travel, and those claims can be tested against the whole register of differences at once. The two quartos reward the method because their roads are so distinct. Few early plays offer an authorial channel and a theatrical channel in such clean separation, and none offers them for a text that the culture has memorised so completely.

One further traveller must be placed on the map to keep it honest: the third quarto of 1609 and the First Folio text that descends from the fuller line. Their existence tempts a false arithmetic in which three later witnesses outvote the shorter one, but descent is not independence, and a reprint's agreement with its ancestor adds no new evidence. The stemma, the family tree of the texts, keeps the comparison binary at its root: two independent channels from the 1590s, and everything else downstream. Editors who cite the Folio in the apparatus do so as a record of early editorial behaviour, not as a third witness with a vote, and the comparison's discipline on this point is among its most exportable lessons. Counting witnesses is a beginner's method. Weighing channels is the discipline itself.

## What the Comparison Cannot Settle

A method's authority includes an account of its limits, and the collation has four that deserve record. First, no authorial manuscript survives against which either witness could be checked directly, so the fuller text's closeness to the draft is an inference from internal evidence, strong but never demonstrable line by line. Second, the company's promptbook does not survive either, so the shorter text's relation to the actual staging, whether through report, abridgement, or a copy of the book itself degraded in transmission, cannot be fixed with certainty, and the composite verdict offered in this article is a best explanation rather than a proof. Third, the collation cannot recover performances: it can show that a text is performance-shaped, but the voices, the pacing, the casting, and the theatre itself are gone beyond recall, and the shorter text's directions are a skeleton of the event, not the event. Fourth, the comparison cannot settle the small class of variants where both readings are authorially plausible, passages the dramatist may himself have written twice in the course of composition and revision, since revision leaves the same signature as corruption at many points and no channel argument can separate them.

Those limits do not weaken the verdicts the comparison does sustain. The scale and pattern of the divergence, the direction of the staging evidence, the copy-text settlement, the occasional correction of the draft by the stage text, and the composite account of the shorter text's origin all rest on the distribution of thousands of logged differences, not on any single crux, and distributions are the kind of evidence that survives the loss of manuscripts. What the comparison finally teaches is a habit rather than a result: to ask of every reading, in every edition, which channel carried it, what that channel preserves and what it wears away, and whether the trust being placed in it matches the question being asked. The two households of the play feuded over honour they could not define. The two quartos of the play disagree over lines their readers can compare, and the comparison, unlike the feud, makes its participants wiser: the text as written and the text as played, held in view together, each explaining the other's gaps.

## Case Study: The Dawn Parting Compared

One scene deserves a final worked treatment because it concentrates every class of difference in a page of dialogue: the dawn parting in 3.5, the lovers' aubade above the orchard. The fuller text opens with Juliet's resistance to the light, "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5.1), and the fiction the lovers build together, "It was the nightingale, and not the lark, / That pierc'd the fearful hollow of thine ear" (3.5.2-3), sustained through the lark's counter-claim, the dividing clouds, Romeo's acceptance of death as the price of staying, "Come, death, and welcome! Juliet wills it so" (3.5.24), and the reversal in which Juliet, seeing him against the growing light, expels the fiction herself and sends him down. The scene is an argument conducted in images, and its length is its meaning: the longer the lovers can keep the fiction standing, the more the audience feels the day as an enemy.

The shorter text performs the same scene at a gallop. The beats stand in their order, the nightingale and the lark dispute their claims, the reversal arrives, and the descent follows, but the argument's steps are fewer and its images plainer, as though the scene had been carried by its outcomes rather than by its language. Nothing in the plot changes. Everything in the experience changes, and the parallel reading isolates exactly what a performance loses when it taxes lyric wealth at the scene where lyric wealth is doing the tragic work: the audience of the shorter text saw a parting efficiently conducted, while the readers of the fuller text inhabit a parting prolonged until its pain becomes the point. Editors print the fuller dawn without a contest, and the apparatus's record of the shorter scene is among the collation's most cited exhibits for the performance character of its witness, since no scribe shortening a manuscript at a desk chooses this, of all scenes, to hurry, while a company watching the light of its own playing afternoon has every reason to.

The scene also contributes a small, sharp instance of the variant class. Within the compressed exchange, individual lines survive nearly word-perfect beside lines that drift, and the distribution of survival is not random: the lines that carry the fiction's terms, nightingale, lark, day, death, hold firm, while the connective reasoning between them loosens. That is the signature found across the whole collation, lexical anchors retained and syntax re-knit around them, and finding it in miniature, in the play's most famous morning, confirms that the pattern is a property of the shorter text's transmission rather than of any one scene's history. The dawn parting thus closes the worked comparisons where the balcony scene opened them, at the window, with the same lesson read in a darker light: two witnesses, one keeping what the words were, the other keeping what the scene did, and an editor's text that must answer to both.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Roughly how much of the play is missing from the first quarto compared with the second?

About a quarter of it. The second quarto prints roughly three thousand lines and the first roughly twenty-two hundred, a gap of some eight hundred lines, as the counts in Jill Levenson's Oxford edition set them out. The missing quarter is not spread evenly. Soliloquies, lyric expansions, and reflective dialogue lose the most, while plot-bearing scenes survive nearly whole. The proportion matters because it rules out casual explanations: printers do not lose a quarter of a text by accident, and the patterned shape of the loss is the chief evidence that the shorter quarto descends from the play in performance rather than from the author's papers.

### Q: What is collation, and how is it performed on the two quartos?

Collation is the systematic comparison of two or more witnesses to a text, recording every difference between them. Performed on the quartos, it begins with the physical books, whose surviving copies are checked against each other to separate print-shop corrections from true variants. The two texts are then set in parallel, scene by scene, and every divergence of wording, speech assignment, order, and stage direction is logged without interpretation. Explanation follows the log, not the other way round, and theories of the shorter text stand or fall on whether they can account for the whole register of differences rather than for a few famous entries.

### Q: Where does the first quarto compress the play most heavily?

The compression falls heaviest on the great reflective set pieces. The Friar's speeches contract sharply, Juliet's potion soliloquy in the fourth act is thinned to its spine, Romeo's wonder and despair lose lines throughout, and the musicians' prose comedy after the discovery of the body shrinks severely. By contrast, the brawl, the ball, the marriage, the duels, and the tomb keep their structure and most of their dialogue. The geography of the cuts is the comparison's most eloquent result: whatever an audience needed in order to follow the story survives, and whatever existed to be savoured verbally is placed at risk.

### Q: Does the first quarto preserve any lines that the second quarto lacks?

Yes, though the class is small beside the eight hundred lines it loses. The shorter text holds a scattering of lines and half-lines found nowhere in the fuller text, connective phrases in dialogue, extra material in crowd and servant exchanges, and, most importantly, a body of stage directions the authorial text never prints. A few of its unique readings have persuaded editors that the performance tradition carried the true wording at points where the draft behind the second quarto slipped. The class matters out of proportion to its size, because it proves the shorter text is not a mere subtraction from the longer one.

### Q: When the quartos assign the same speech to different speakers, which assignment do editors follow?

Editors follow the second quarto by default and cross to the first only on argument. The fuller text is the copy-text, and its headings descend from the author's draft, so its assignments stand wherever they make sense. Where the draft's assignment is impossible or confused and the shorter text's is coherent, editors have sometimes adopted the first quarto's allocation, reasoning that a performance text can preserve who actually spoke a line where a draft heading was ambiguous. Every such decision is declared in the apparatus. The governing principle is that trust attaches to readings one at a time, judged against each witness's channel of transmission, not to either book wholesale.

### Q: How does the Friar's closing explanation differ between the quartos?

In the second quarto the Friar's final narrative, the speech in which he lays the whole potion plot before the Prince, runs at full explanatory length, accounting for the marriage, the potion, the failed letter, and the waking in the tomb. In the first quarto the same office is discharged in drastically fewer lines: the facts arrive, the sequence is preserved, and the elaboration is gone. The difference is a perfect sample of the shorter text's method, which protects information and taxes expression. Editors print the fuller narrative, and the compressed version survives as evidence of how much explanation a playing audience was asked to absorb at the tragedy's end.

### Q: Why do editors import stage directions from the first quarto into a second-quarto text?

Because the fuller text, descending from a working draft, often fails to direct the action it imagines, while the shorter text, descending from performance, records business the stage worked out: the forcing of the tomb, the handling of bodies, the management of discoveries and entrances. An edition built only on the second quarto would leave scenes unstageable on the page at exactly their most physical moments. Editors therefore admit the first quarto's directions where the dialogue welcomes them, marking the borrowing in the apparatus. The practice is the comparison's verdict in miniature: verse from the text of the writing, staging from the text of the playing.

### Q: What separates a substantive variant from an accidental one in this comparison?

A substantive variant changes meaning: a different word, a reassigned speech, a moved passage, the first quarto's "gentle sin" against the second's "gentle fine". An accidental concerns the texture in which words are clothed: spelling, punctuation, capitalisation, lineation. W. W. Greg's rationale of copy-text assigns the two classes different treatment, taking accidentals from the chosen copy-text, here the second quarto, while substantives are judged one by one across both witnesses. Modern editions then modernise the accidentals of whichever text they print, which is why the distinction is invisible to most readers even though it governs every page they read.

### Q: How does the memorial reconstruction theory read the pattern of differences?

The theory, developed from A. W. Pollard's classification by W. W. Greg, holds that the shorter text was rebuilt from memory, most plausibly by actors of the company, and sold to its printer. It reads the collation as a memory's map. Passages a reporting actor played or stood within are accurate; scenes he watched from offstage or never entered come back paraphrased; speeches migrate between speakers; lyric wealth collapses into gist while plot survives, because plot is what memory must keep. Harry Hoppe's study of this play pressed the reading furthest, locating the reporter's own roles in the pattern of accuracy the comparison reveals.

### Q: What in the differences made later scholars doubt memorial reconstruction?

Two kinds of evidence strained the theory. First, scale and quality: long stretches of the shorter text run too close to the fuller text's exact wording for unaided memory of a long play, and its cuts are too intelligently patterned, taxing lyric and sparing plot with a consistency that forgetting does not produce. Second, method: Laurie Maguire's systematic tests found the symptoms said to diagnose a reported text occurring in texts known to be sound, and missing from texts known to be reported, which made the diagnosis circular. Paul Werstine added the bibliographical objection that the piracy narrative outruns anything the records of the book trade show.

### Q: How does the performance abridgement theory explain the differences differently?

Kathleen Irace's account treats the shorter text as a deliberate adaptation for the stage: a company's reworking of its own play, shortened for playing time, touring conditions, or a smaller cast. Where reconstruction sees decay, abridgement sees design, and it explains the collation's pattern of cuts directly, since a company cutting its play taxes lyric expansion and protects plot exactly as the shorter text does. It also explains the enriched stage directions, which an adaptation accumulates as it is fitted to performance. The theory strains instead at the paraphrase, since a company holding a written text has no need to flatten wording it could simply shorten.

### Q: Why can the first quarto sometimes hold the better reading where the second errs?

Because the second quarto's copy was a working draft, and drafts contain slips: words miswritten, headings misplaced, phrases left in states the author would later have corrected. The shorter text descends through performance, and performance can carry a corrected or intended reading past a blemish in the papers, since the company played the line in a form that worked and the playing was transmitted. Where the fuller text's reading limps or fails sense and the shorter text's is clean, editors infer exactly this. The cases are few and each is argued in the apparatus, but their existence refutes any rule sealing the copy-text against correction.

### Q: How do modern editions show a reading imported from the first quarto?

Honest editions show it in the apparatus, the register of variants and editorial decisions printed with the text, where an entry records the reading adopted, the witness it came from, and often the reading rejected. Stage directions borrowed from the shorter text are typically bracketed or noted as editorial in origin. René Weis's Arden edition and Jill Levenson's Oxford edition both keep the crossings visible in this way, so a reader can reconstruct the two witnesses' division of labour on any page. Editions without an apparatus make the same borrowings silently, which is why the scholarly editions remain the comparison's proper home.

### Q: When both quartos give a plausible reading, how does an editor choose between them?

By asking which reading better explains the other. Transmission tends to simplify, so the harder, stranger reading is often the likelier original, since a copyist or a memory smooths difficulties into ease while rarely inventing them. The editor also weighs each witness's channel: the second quarto descends from the author's draft and starts with the advantage on wording, while the first descends from performance and can prevail where the draft looks corrupt. Context, metre, the author's known habits, and the direction of likely error all enter the judgment, and the apparatus records it so that later editors can reverse it.

### Q: What does a parallel-text reading of the quartos reveal that a single edited text conceals?

It reveals that the familiar play is a settlement. A single edited text presents every line with the same authority, while parallel reading shows lines descending from the author's draft, directions descending from performance, and cruxes where the printed reading won an argument against a respectable rival. It also reveals the shorter play inside the longer one: the faster, leaner tragedy of plot that the first quarto preserves. Perhaps most valuably, it restores contingency to quotation. A reader who has watched "gentle sin" and "gentle fine" divide the witnesses never again mistakes a famous line for a fact of nature rather than an editorial decision.

### Q: How do the quartos disagree over Romeo's dying speech?

Both texts give Romeo a dying speech over Juliet's body in the tomb, but they disagree over its extent and finish. The second quarto's speech runs at full length, through the address to the tomb as a detestable maw, the farewell to Paris, and the toast to love before the poison is drunk. The first quarto compresses the same beats into far fewer lines, keeping the speech's outline and its closing act while shedding much of its rhetoric, with phrasing that sometimes matches the fuller text closely and sometimes drifts into approximation. Editors print the full speech; the compressed version stands as the earliest record of the role's playable shape.

### Q: How do spelling and punctuation differences between the quartos affect an editor's work?

The second quarto's spelling and light rhetorical punctuation descend, through compositors, from an author's draft, and they form the texture a copy-text edition respects before modernising. The first quarto's page is rougher: pointing is sparser and more erratic, verse is mislined where transmission lost its shape, and prose and verse are occasionally set awry. An editor must therefore repunctuate and reline the shorter text more aggressively wherever it is used, and every repunctuation is an interpretation, deciding where clauses turn and how speeches breathe. The differences also serve diagnosis, since ruined lineation in lyric passages is among the collation's quiet arguments for a memorial channel.

### Q: What verdict does the comparison support about which text records the play as written and which as played?

The comparison supports the division plainly. The second quarto records the play as written: fuller, denser, closer to the author's papers, and the rightful copy-text for editions. The first quarto records the play as played: a quarter shorter, patterned by performance priorities, paraphrased where its transmission wore the words, and enriched with the staging the draft omits. Neither verdict demotes its witness. The written text explains what the dramatist composed; the played text explains what the first audiences received, and the whole truth about the tragedy's early life requires both records, collated, with their disagreements treated as evidence rather than embarrassment.

### Q: How should a student cite a line that exists in only one of the quartos?

By naming the witness, not merely the play. A line found only in the first quarto should be cited to that text, by act and scene as modern parallel editions number it, with the quarto identified, so that no reader mistakes a performance-text survival for part of the standard reading text. The same discipline applies in reverse to the fuller text's unique passages. Scholarly editions and parallel-text presentations supply the lineation and the apparatus for such citations. The habit matters because the comparison's central finding is that the two quartos are different objects, and a citation that hides which object a line lives in conceals the evidence it rests on.

### Q: Why does the pattern of what the first quarto cuts matter more than the amount?

Because the amount alone is compatible with almost any story, while the pattern discriminates between them. A quarter of the play is missing, and that fact by itself could reflect censorship, damage, haste, or theft. The pattern, lyric taxed and plot spared, directions enriched while soliloquies shrink, accuracy tracking certain roles and scenes, is what lets scholars test theories against evidence: it fits a performance origin, strains a careless-copy account, and divides the memorial and adaptation explanations between them. In textual scholarship generally, distributions convict and acquit where totals merely describe, and this collation is the discipline's favourite demonstration of the principle.
