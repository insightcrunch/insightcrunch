## Cold Open

![Nightjar: The Slow Fade in a Locked Melora Reading Room - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-72.webp)

The reading room ran long from east to west along the south side of the courts annex, longer than any clerk needed and narrower than its length promised, with a tall window cut into its long southern wall from near the floor to near the ceiling, so that the room fell naturally into two halves that everyone who worked in it understood without naming them. There was the window half, bright even on a dull morning, where the light fell across the reading tables and warmed the wood, and there was the door half, quieter and dimmer, where the door from the corridor entered at the western end and where the key register stood on its narrow stand beside the door, its cover worn at the corners from hands. Three reading tables stood in a row down the middle of the room, each with two chairs, each with a lamp on a brass stem and a shallow tray for paper that must not leave the annex. Along the northern wall, opposite the window, shallow shelves held bound volumes of procedure and the boxed files that were brought up from below only when a case required them, and the air in the room, when Faun Grist unlocked it each morning, always smelled first of paper dust and lamp oil and the faint mineral smell of the stone sill under the window.

Grist came to the door at seven by the annex bell, as he had come to it on every working morning of the Twenty Ninth Session that the courts had sat, with the key on its cord around his wrist and the morning list folded in his left hand. He was a careful man in the way that men who open other people rooms for a living learn to be careful, not from virtue alone but from the knowledge that any small disorder found later would be laid, fairly or not, at the door of the first person who entered. He had been an usher of the annex for eleven years, first in the hearing rooms below and then, when his knees began to trouble him on the stairs, in the quieter rooms above, and he liked the reading room because it asked little of anyone except attention. Clerks came there to read files that could not be copied. Advocates came there, under supervision, to read what the court allowed them to read and nothing else. At night the room was locked and empty, and it stayed locked and empty under observation, and that plain fact was the foundation on which all its use rested.

He put the key in the lock and felt the familiar resistance and then the familiar give. The lock was a good one, deep and clean, serviced at the start of each session and checked at its middle, and it turned without scraping. On the small board beside the door, in the covered slot that only the night watch and the morning usher handled, the observation log lay open to the night page. Grist set his list down for a moment, as his routine required, and read the log before he pushed the door wide, because the routine was the point and because a routine performed out of order was only a memory of one. The night watch had signed at each hour. The hand was the same throughout the night, a watchman named Corl who wrote his letters large and pressed hard enough to leave a mark on the page below, and each entry said the same thing in the same few words: door locked, corridor clear, no entry, no exit, window fastened from within as checked through the glass of the corridor viewing slit at the start of the watch. That slit, a narrow vertical pane set into the wall beside the door and angled so that a person in the corridor could see the window latch and a part of the nearest table without entering, was an old feature of the annex, built for rooms that held paper under restriction, and Grist trusted it in the modest way he trusted any device that had never yet given him reason not to. The last entry stood at six, an hour before. Below it the space for the morning opening waited empty.

The seal on the door frame, a strip of waxed paper placed by the evening usher across the join of door and frame and initialled at locking, was unbroken. Grist looked at it closely, as he always did, not bending so near that his breath might be blamed for anything, and saw the evening initial whole and the paper untorn, its edges still adhering. Only then did he break it himself, with the flat of his thumbnail, and note the time of breaking in his own mind for the entry he would write when the room had been prepared. He pushed the door open and stepped inside and left the door wide behind him, as the rules for opening required, so that anyone in the corridor could see that the room was being opened and by whom.

Item one on the morning list was the lamps.

He went down the row of tables from west to east, as the list ordered, and tended each lamp in turn. He checked the reservoir, trimmed nothing that did not need trimming, wiped the glass where soot had gathered at its rim, and set each wick to the height marked by the small notch that gave a reading light without smoke. His hands knew the work and did it without asking his eyes for much help, which was why, perhaps, his eyes stayed where the list kept them, on the work immediately before them. The first lamp had burned low the day before and needed oil. He filled it from the small can kept in the cupboard by the door, taking care not to spill, and wiped the stem clean. The second lamp was in good order. The third, nearest the window at the eastern end, needed its glass wiped twice before the soot came clear, and he held it up to the window light to check his work, and the light that came through the tall window was grey and even and without warmth yet, the kind of morning light in Melora that made paper look whiter than it was.

Item two was the register.

Not the key register by the door, which belonged to locking and unlocking and to the record of who held which key and when, but the room register, the bound book that lived on the stand at the eastern end of the room and in which every file brought into the room was entered, and every person admitted was entered, and every departure was entered, all in the hand of the usher on duty or the clerk in charge. Grist carried it, still closed, to the nearest table and opened it to the page for the new day and ruled, with a straight edge kept in the stand drawer for the purpose, the lines that would hold the day entries. He wrote the date in the form the annex required, by session and by sitting day, and he wrote his own name as opening usher, and he left the columns for files and readers empty and clean. The page before it, for the day before, was full and properly closed, with the evening usher closing signature at its foot and the count of files returned agreeing with the count of files issued. Grist checked that agreement, as item two required, counting under his breath, and found it correct, and initialled the check in the margin. A register that did not agree was to be reported before any reader was admitted. This one agreed. He set the book back on its stand, open, ready.

Item three was the water carafe and the glasses.

The carafe lived on a tray on the side table against the northern wall, midway down the room, where a reader who needed water could take it without leaving the room and without calling through the door. Grist lifted it and found it, as he expected, holding the water left from the day before, filmed faintly with dust that had settled in the night despite the closed room and the fastened window, because dust in the annex found its way through keyholes and under doors and along the channels where the window frame met the stone. He carried the carafe out to the corridor basin, emptied it, rinsed it, filled it with fresh water from the annex supply, and rinsed the two glasses that stood with it and set them to drain for a moment on the clean cloth laid there for the purpose. While they drained he looked, without particular thought, at the corridor he stood in. It was empty. The morning had not yet brought the clerks up from the lower floors. From below came the muffled sounds of the building waking, a door, a voice, the scrape of a cart, all of it ordinary and all of it at a distance. Corl, the night watchman, had gone off duty at the bell and would be at his breakfast in the watch room by the stair. Nothing in the corridor suggested that the night had held anything but the night.

He carried the carafe and glasses back into the reading room and set them on their tray and dried the bottom of the carafe with the cloth so that it would leave no ring on the wood. That completed item three. He folded his hands for a moment, as a man does at the small pause between one part of a routine and the next, and looked at the morning list again to confirm the order of what remained, because confirming was itself part of the list, written in the margin in a former head usher hand that Grist had never been tempted to ignore.

Item four was the room itself. Walk the room. Check the window fastening. Check the shelves for anything left that should not have been left. Check each table and chair. Satisfy yourself that the room is as it was left, or report how it differs.

Grist turned, with the list still in his hand, and began his walk at the western end, by the door, as the item required, meaning to go east along the northern side and return along the southern, by the window.

He had taken four steps when he looked up from the level of the shelves, where his eyes had been running along the boxed files in their order, and saw the easternmost table properly for the first time that morning.

A man sat at the far side of that table, in the chair that faced the door, with his head resting on his folded arms upon the table, in the attitude of a clerk who had fallen asleep over a file and might be woken by a word and sit up apologising. A file lay open before him, its pages partly covered by his arms. A pen lay on the table beside his right hand, where it had been set down or had fallen in a neat line with the edge of the file. The lamp at that table, the third lamp, which Grist had tended not ten minutes before, working around the table without raising his eyes above the level of the glass in his hand, stood trimmed and unlit above him.

Grist stopped. The list, in his hand, made a small sound against his leg.

He knew the man. He knew him in the way an usher knows the regular clerks of the annex, by face and by habit and by the small courtesies exchanged at doors over years, rather than by any acquaintance outside the building. It was Addis Vale, a clerk of the arbitration registry, a quiet man of middle height who read with one finger held lightly below the line he was reading and who always, when he left the reading room, thanked the usher on duty as if the room itself had done him a favour. Vale had used the room often in the session. Grist had admitted him on many mornings and had entered his name in the register in the hand he was still perfecting for official books, with the A given a small extra stroke that Vale himself, once, smiling, had said made the name look more important than its owner.

Vale did not move when Grist stopped, and did not move when Grist, after a silence that he would later try and fail to measure, said his name once at the ordinary volume of the room.

Grist did not approach the table. What training he had, and what plain sense he had, agreed on this without consultation. He looked, from where he stood, and he took care to look properly, because he understood already that what he saw from the doorway side of the room would be asked of him later by people whose work was asking, and that a man in his position served best by seeing clearly and touching nothing. Vale sat still. His folded arms were still. His shoulders, under the dark cloth of his clerk coat, did not rise or fall in any rhythm that Grist could detect from across the room, and the stillness had a quality that sleep does not have, a completeness that Grist recognised before he allowed himself any word for it. The file before the dead man lay open at a page dense with writing. The pen lay where it lay. Nothing on the table had been knocked over. No glass had fallen. No chair but Vale own had been displaced, and that chair stood square to the table, as a chair stands when a man has sat down in it deliberately and drawn himself in to work.

For a moment, which Grist would also later fail to measure, the room held its two facts side by side and offered no bridge between them. The observation log had said, hour by hour, in a hand that pressed through the page, that the door had been locked and the corridor clear and that there had been no entry and no exit. The seal Grist had broken with his own thumbnail had been whole. The window, when he forced his eyes to it now, stood fastened, its latch in place as seen clearly in the morning light, with the tall glass unmarked. And at the eastern table, in the window half of the room where the light fell, Addis Vale sat dead in a room that no one, by every record the annex kept, had entered in the night, and that Vale himself, by the register of the day before, had left alive at the close of reading hours, signing out in his own hand with the time entered beside the signature and the file he had been reading recorded as returned to its box.

Grist became aware that he was holding his breath and let it out slowly, through his nose, so as not to make a sound that felt wrong in the room. He took one step backward, and then another, keeping the table in view, until he stood in the open doorway with the corridor at his back. He did not touch the body. He did not touch the table, or the file, or the pen, or the chair. He did not close the register or straighten anything or put anything right, though his hands, trained by eleven years of small orderly acts, wanted employment. He put the morning list, instead, carefully on top of the key register stand by the door, where it would be found as he had left it, with items one to three completed and item four begun and not completed, and he left the door wide open behind him as he went into the corridor.

The alarm in the annex was not a bell a man rang for himself. It was a procedure, and Grist followed it as he had followed the list, because procedure was what the moment left him that he could trust. He went at a pace that was not running, because running in the annex in the morning would bring people out of rooms asking questions he could not answer, to the stair head where the watch room stood, and he put his head in at the watch room door and said, to the day officer who was taking the watch board from Corl, that there was a dead man in the reading room, that it was Addis Vale, that the room had been locked and sealed when he opened it, that he had touched nothing at the table, and that the room stood open and needed to be closed and guarded at once by someone whose name would stand in the log as having done so.

Then, because the list was finished whether he wished it or not, he stood in the corridor outside the reading room, a little to one side of the open door so that he blocked nothing and saw everything that came and went, and he waited for the building to do what a building does when a man is found dead in a locked room, and he kept his hands folded in front of him where everyone could see them, and he did not go back inside.

## Tasking

Petra Dunmore sent for Mira before Mira had finished her tea, and the message, when it reached the Carrowmere desk room where Mira had been reading a file that could wait, contained no detail except a place and an urgency that Dunmore did not spend lightly. Mira set the file aside in its tray, entered the time of her leaving in the desk book, and went through the courts annex by the route that avoided the public stairs, with her coat still carrying the cold of the canal side street where she had walked in that morning, and her left hand, where two fingers had been burned in Valtessa against [doors that should have held bindings and had held nothing she could find behind their seals](/2026/11/11/nightjar-seal-that-wasnt-011/), held carefully clear of the door handles she passed. The burn had closed. It had not yet softened. The new skin pulled when she flexed the hand, and the stiffness had entered her habits in small ways she noticed and resented, the altered grip on a cup, the pause before she took a key, the decision, made half a dozen times a day, about whether a thing needed to be held firmly enough to be worth the pull. She used her right hand on the annex doors and thought, as she had thought each day since Valtessa, that a body keeps its own register whether anyone tasks it to or not.

Dunmore waited in the small room off the registry that the courts lent to the Ninth Office when the Office business touched a court file, a room with one narrow window and a table too large for it, so that anyone sitting at the table sat close to anyone else sitting there whether they wished it or not. Dunmore had papers laid out in two groups, and a third group, squared and set apart, that Mira understood at once to be copies rather than originals, made for the Office to carry away. Reni Vosk stood by the window with a notebook already open and a pencil ready, twenty one years old and visibly determined not to be the least prepared person in any room she was permitted to enter, and she straightened when Mira came in in a way that made Mira conscious, not unkindly, of the difference between being assigned to someone and being assigned with them.

Dunmore did not waste the opening. A clerk of the arbitration registry, Addis Vale, had been found dead that morning in the reading room of the annex, seated at a table, by the usher who opened the room. The room had been locked through the night. It had been sealed at locking the evening before and the seal had been unbroken at opening. An observation log, signed hourly by the night watch, recorded no entry and no exit. The window of the room was fastened from within. Vale, by the register of the day before, had left the room alive at the close of reading hours and had signed himself out. By every paper the annex kept, no one had been in the room in the night, and Vale had not been in it either.

Melora, Dunmore said, would release the room to the Office for thirty hours from the hour of finding, and not an hour more.

She said it plainly and she let the plainness do its work. Thirty hours, counted from the usher discovery, after which the room would be cleaned, its files removed to registry custody, its tables returned to ordinary use, and the annex would resume the position that a death under enquiry could not be allowed to close a working room of the courts indefinitely. The arbitration calendar had already been altered once. It would not be altered again. The Office could read the room, audit its records, interview the annex staff whose duties touched it, and take copies of what it needed. The Office could not extend, pause, or appeal the limit through any channel Dunmore had been able to test that morning, and she had tested three before she sent for Mira. The cost of the case, named up front before any other cost could present itself, was time.

Mira sat down at the oversized table and put her injured hand flat on the wood, where the pull of the skin reminded her to keep it still, and asked the questions in the order Dunmore would have asked them of her.

The usher was Faun Grist. His morning list, the folded sheet on which the opening routine of the reading room was set out item by item, had been taken into custody by the day officer and copied, and the copy lay in the first group of papers. Grist had completed the lamps, the room register, and the water carafe. He had begun the fourth item, the walk of the room, when he saw Vale. The list mattered, Dunmore said, for a reason beyond routine. Each item on that list was timed by long habit and, in part, by the annex own timekeeping trials, and Grist, who was known to the registry as a man whose habits could be set by a clock without insulting either the man or the clock, accounted for his morning in a sequence that left one gap. Between the completion of the water carafe and the moment when he stood in the doorway and went for the day officer, a span of nine minutes stood unaccounted in any detail finer than the walk itself. He had tended the third lamp at the eastern table, within a few feet of where Vale sat, during item one, and had not seen the man, or had seen the shape of him and taken it, without looking up, for something that belonged in an empty room at seven in the morning, which amounted, for the purposes of any honest record, to the same thing. Nine minutes. Dunmore repeated the figure without emphasis. In nine minutes, a great deal that was merely untidy could happen. A great deal that mattered could not, or so it first appeared, and the gap would need to be treated with respect and without appetite.

The key register stood by the door of the reading room, as it always stood, and a copy of its relevant pages formed the second group of papers. Every key to the reading room was entered in it, by number and by holder, with issue and return. The register showed, on its face, three keys in existence: the working key on the usher cord, the watch key held at the watch board overnight, and a registry master held in the registry safe under two signatures. No key had been issued in the night. No key was missing. The register, like the observation log, said that the room had been beyond reach. Mira looked at the copied pages for longer than the information on them strictly required, because a register that agrees with a log that agrees with a seal is either the truth of a matter or its most careful part, and she had learned, in rooms in Melora and in Valtessa and in places whose names she entered in registers of her own, that agreement among papers is a fact about papers before it is a fact about the world.

The room itself Dunmore described from the annex plan, which she turned toward Mira on the table. A long room. A tall window along one long side, dividing it, for any practical purpose, into a window half and a door half. Reading tables down the middle, belonging fully to neither half and therefore, Mira noted, to whichever half a reader chose to sit in. The body had been found at the easternmost table, in the window half, in the chair facing the door. The key register by the door, in the door half. The shelves along the wall opposite the window. The observation slit in the corridor wall. The plan was drawn in the clean line the annex used for its internal copies, and it gave measurements without giving the room, which was as plans always are, and Mira studied it until she could hold the shape of the place in her mind with her eyes off the paper, because she would need the shape before she reached the place, and she would need it again when she stood in the place and the place began, as rooms under enquiry do, to present itself as self evident.

Her private register of absences, the record she had kept in her own hand across cases in which weight had been missing where weight should have been, had passed, since Valtessa and since the report in which she had written for the first time that the absence concealed bindings specifically, out of her sole keeping and into oversight custody, where it now stood as a document Commissioner Harrand office could call for; Dunmore mentioned it in one clause, as a fact of procedure, and Mira acknowledged it in silence, as a fact of more than procedure.

Reni, Dunmore said, would go with her. The assignment was not a courtesy and not a punishment for either of them. Reni had read procedure for the courts desk and had not yet stood in a room in which a man had died under enquiry, and the Office did not send junior officers into such rooms alone for the first time, nor did it send a Weigher into a room that Melora would take back in thirty hours without a second pair of eyes whose notes could stand if the Weigher own account were later questioned for any reason, including the reason that reading a room changes the reader in ways that can be entered in no register. Reni closed her notebook on the pencil, to keep the place, and then opened it again at a fresh page, which Mira thought the most honest gesture she had seen that morning.

Dunmore went over the permissions once more, slowly, in the form in which she would enter them in the desk record. Access to the room and to its contents as found, with nothing to be removed except by registry receipt. Access to Grist, to the night watch, and to the evening usher who had locked and sealed the room. Access to the court physician who had been called to certify the death in place before the body was moved, and to the room temperature log kept by the annex engineers for the preservation of its paper. Copies of the observation log, the key register, the room register, and Grist morning list. The body itself had been removed, after certification, to the court mortuary room below, where it would remain under registry authority; Mira would not be asked to examine it, a sentence Dunmore delivered without decoration, knowing, as everyone who worked with Weighers knew and as the bible of their discipline stated in terms that admitted no exception, that a Weigher reads what events leave in places and in objects and reads nothing at all from the dead.

There remained the question Dunmore had kept for last, which was not a permission. The room, she said, had been entered that morning, before the day officer closed it, by the court physician and by the guards who carried the body out, and by no one else. Those entries were logged. The room had not been read. The Office had been called because the annex own enquiry had reached the limit of what locks and logs could say by the middle of the morning, and because Vale work in the registry had touched arbitration files that Melora preferred to have examined by an office with no standing in the outcome. Whether the room held weight where weight should be, or lacked it where it should not, was unknown to anyone in the building. Dunmore did not speculate. She looked at Mira burned hand, flat on the table, and at Reni notebook, open at its fresh page, and she gathered the copy papers into a single squared bundle and pushed them across the short distance the table allowed.

Thirty hours, she said again. The clock, for the purposes of the limit, had started when Grist raised the alarm. Eight of those hours, by the time Mira stood up with the bundle under her right arm and Reni at her shoulder, were the courts to spend on their own preliminaries. Twenty two remained. Mira did the arithmetic without writing it down, and then, because arithmetic done in the head in a room under time pressure is a place where errors like to live, she wrote it on the inside cover of the bundle in pencil, in figures Reni could check over her shoulder, and Reni checked it and nodded once.

## Ground Truth

The reading room, when Mira reached it with Reni at her shoulder and a registry clerk at her back to witness the opening of the closed door, met her first through senses that had nothing to do with her discipline and everything to do with a room that had held a dead man through a morning and then been emptied of him. The air had been shut in since the body was carried out and it carried layers that separated themselves as she stood in the doorway breathing carefully through her mouth and then, after the first moment, through her nose, because a Weigher who will not breathe a room has no business entering it. Paper dust came first, dry at the back of the throat, the smell of boxed files and bound procedure volumes breathing out the day before heat. Lamp oil came second, sharper at the western end where Grist had filled the first lamp and thinned as she moved east. Stone came third, the sill under the tall window giving off the faint cold mineral note of a wall that faced the light without ever warming through, and beneath all of it, very faint and already fading under the traffic of the morning, the waxy smell of the mortuary cloth that had been brought in for the removal, and the leather of the carriers straps, and the clean, almost sweet smell of the soap the court physician used on his hands, which hung longest near the eastern table where he had stood to do his work. Sound had its own layering. The annex bell, muffled by two closed doors, counted nothing she needed. Water moved somewhere in the wall. In the corridor behind her a cart wheel found the same uneven stone at the same interval, passed, and passed again in memory after the actual sound had gone. Light, through the tall window, fell in a broad, dusty slab across the eastern tables and stopped, to the eye, nowhere in particular, spilling by slow degrees into the dimmer half by the door, and the dust in that slab turned and settled and turned again in air that no hand was disturbing, so slowly that watching it became a measure of her own patience. Mira stood in the doorway for a full minute by Reni count, which Reni gave afterwards without being asked, and let the ordinary room finish arriving before she asked it for anything else. A room that has been the site of a death is still, in every respect except one, an ordinary room, and the discipline began, for her, in refusing to let the exception arrive early.

The registry clerk, a woman named Senn who spoke as if each sentence had been costed, read aloud from the closing record the state in which the room had been left after the removal. The body had been taken out on the mortuary board at the hour recorded. The file that had lain open before Vale had been photographed in place by the registry draughtsman, whose plate copy Mira would receive, and then closed and boxed and sealed in the room, in view of the day officer, and it stood now in its box on the northern shelves with the seal unbroken and the box number entered in the room register as an addition after Grist opening entry. The pen had been left where it lay for the physician examination and then placed, by tongs, in a paper envelope that lay beside the box. The chair in which Vale had sat had not been moved. The other chairs stood as Grist had found them. The window remained fastened. The lamps stood trimmed and unlit, as Grist had left them after item one of his list, except that the day officer had, on his own authority and for reasons of safety in a closed room, emptied the reservoir of the first lamp, and that act was recorded in the log in his hand and countersigned, a small, honest disorder in the evidence that Mira noted with more approval than annoyance, because a record that shows a man correcting himself for safety is worth more than a record that claims perfection.

Procedure came first, before any reading, and Mira held to the order in front of Reni deliberately, so that the junior officer would see the order kept when keeping it cost time they did not have. She began with the seal. The broken halves of the evening seal had been preserved by Grist, laid between sheets of registry paper by the day officer, and Mira examined them on the side table with her right hand and a magnifying lens from the registry tray, her burned fingers held clear and aching slightly from the walk up the annex stair. The waxed paper had torn where a thumbnail had torn it, in a single fibrous line. The evening initial, in iron ink, crossed the join as it should, one part of the letter on the door side of the strip and one on the frame side, and the two parts met, when the halves were brought together, without displacement. There was no sign of a second tear, no sign of softening by heat or wetting, no lifting at either edge where a patient hand might have worked a blade beneath the paper through a long night and laid it down again. A seal can be defeated, Mira said for Reni notebook, and the sentence was entered. A seal defeated well enough to pass this examination in this light would require either the cooperation of the person who placed it or a skill that left its own kind of absence in a record somewhere else. The evening usher who had placed it, a man named Hallow Brice, was on duty in the lower annex and would be spoken to after the watch. For the present, the seal stood as evidence that the door, once locked and sealed at the close of reading hours, had not been opened by the door until Grist opened it.

The observation log came next, the original, brought from the watch board under receipt and laid on the western table where the light was poorest and where Mira, therefore, took the most care. Hourly entries ran in Corl large, hard pressed hand from locking to opening, unbroken in sequence, each with the watch signature and each recording the same state: door locked, corridor clear, window latch seen through the viewing slit and in place. Mira read the log twice. The first reading was for content. The second was for the paper itself, the indentations of the pen, the spacing of entries made at hourly intervals by a man walking a corridor through a night, the small variations that come into any hand between the first hour of a watch and the last, the entry at the fourth hour written a fraction higher on its line, as a tired man writes when he braces the book against the wall board rather than laying it flat. Nothing in the physical character of the log suggested a page written at one sitting in one temper of the hand. That finding had limits and she stated them for the notebook as she stated the seal finding. A log can be written honestly by a man who was deceived, or written in advance by a man who then made its statements true by his own acts, or written faithfully about a corridor while the matter of the night took place somewhere a corridor watch does not cover. What the log established, and established well, was that the statements in it had been made in the course of the night and not composed after the alarm. The truth of the statements was a separate question, to be answered, if it could be answered, from outside the log.

The viewing slit received its own examination. Mira stood in the corridor with the door closed and Reni inside the room, at registry direction, and looked through the narrow vertical pane as Corl had looked through it at the start of his watch. The angle gave her the window latch, the nearer part of the easternmost table, and a strip of the window half of the room in grey light, and nothing of the door half at all. A man at the slit could confirm a latch. He could not confirm a room. She noted it without criticism of Corl, whose entries, to do him justice, claimed the latch and the corridor and the locked door, and did not claim the whole room, whatever use later readers might try to make of them. The annex had built the slit for paper security, for the assurance that restricted files had not been left open to a corridor view, and it served that purpose. In a death it served a smaller one, and measuring the difference was part of the work.

The key register audit occupied the better part of the next hour and was, in the plainest sense, counting. Three keys existed for the reading room, and Mira required all three to be produced and laid on the western table in the presence of Senn and Reni, each under its holder receipt, because a register that records keys is one kind of evidence and the keys themselves are another, and a case built on the agreement of a record with itself is a case built on paper. The usher working key came from the cord that Grist had surrendered to the day officer, its number stamped at the bow and agreeing with the register entry, its teeth showing the wear of daily use and no bright metal anywhere along its edges where a file might recently have worked. The watch key came from the watch board, where it hung on its numbered peg in a row of annex keys, and Corl, still in the building after his breakfast and before his sleep, attended its removal himself and signed for its temporary surrender with a hand that shook very slightly from tiredness and, Mira judged, from nothing else. The registry master came last, from the registry safe, under two signatures as its rule required, carried in a sealed wallet by Senn herself, and it was, as a master should be, the least worn of the three, its edges clean and its number deeply cut. She tried each key, in turn, in the reading room lock, with the door open and the act recorded, and each turned as a key that belongs to a lock turns, without forcing and without slack. A fourth key, cut from any of these three by a competent hand, would not announce itself to this examination, and she said so for the record. What the audit settled was narrower and, within its narrowness, firm. No recorded key was missing, no recorded key had been issued in the night, no key showed fresh cutting, and the lock showed no marks of forcing, picking, or the wax impression work that the annex locksmith, called to examine it while Mira waited, described as the ordinary roads to a copy. If a key had opened that door in the night, it was either one of these three, used by a hand entitled to hold it and returned without entry in the register, or a fourth key whose existence contradicted every record the annex kept and whose making had left no trace the locksmith could find.

Interviews followed the audit, in the small registry room where Dunmore had given the tasking, because Mira wanted the room itself left undisturbed for the reading that would follow and because men speak about a night more freely in a room that is not the room. Grist came first, with his morning list returned to him for the interview and taken from him again at its close, and he gave his account in the order of the list, as she had known he would, lamps, register, carafe, walk, and he gave it without ornament and without the repetitions that a man inventing an account often allows himself in the belief that emphasis is a kind of proof. He had tended the third lamp at the eastern table. He had held its glass up to the window. He had not looked at the far chair, or he had looked and not seen, and he would not, under any gentleness or firmness of questioning, improve that statement into something finer. Nine minutes stood between the carafe set on its tray and his word to the day officer at the stair head, and within those nine minutes he placed the beginning of item four, four steps into the room, the stop, the name spoken once and unanswered, the steps backward, the list laid on the key register stand, the walk to the stair head at a pace that was not running. He could not divide the nine minutes further. He had not been attending to time. He had been attending, he said, to not touching anything, which had taken more of his attention than he would have expected, and Mira, who had stood in rooms where not touching anything was the whole of the law, believed the account in the specific way she believed accounts that contained an admission against the teller tidiness. A man constructing nine minutes for a purpose would have filled them. Grist had left his empty of everything but a discovery and a retreat, and the emptiness had the shape of truth, though she entered in her own notes, where Reni could not see, that the shape of truth is a beginning and not a finding.

Corl the night watchman came second, a broad man made narrow by tiredness, who stood until Mira told him, twice, to sit. His account covered a corridor and a board and a latch seen through a slit, and it did not stray beyond them, which Mira counted in his favour before she counted anything else. He had walked his round at each hour and at the half hours in the first part of the night, settling to the hours alone after the third round, as the annex permitted on nights when no hearing ran late. He had heard nothing from the reading room. He had heard, through the night, the building ordinary sounds, water in the wall, the settling of the stair wood, once a cart in the lower passage before midnight, and he had entered each of them in the small occurrences column the watch kept for itself, a column Mira had not been shown until she asked whether such a column existed, and which, when produced, contained exactly those entries and no others, in the same hard pressed hand, at hours that agreed with his log. He had seen no one in the upper corridor between locking and opening except Hallow Brice, the evening usher, leaving after sealing, in the first minutes of the watch, and the registry porter passing the stair head, without stopping, in the small hours, a passage Corl had noted because noting passages was his work and not because the porter had given him cause. He knew Vale by sight. Vale had not passed him in the night. A dead man, Corl said at the end, with the air of a man offering the only thought the night had given him, does not walk a corridor, and a living one in that corridor would have had to walk past his board or come up the far stair that was locked from below at the session close, whose own seal, examined on Mira instruction before the interview ended, was reported unbroken by the officer sent to look.

Only then did Mira read the room, and she read it in halves, because the plan Dunmore had shown her had stayed in her mind through the audit and the interviews, and because a room divided by its own architecture into a bright half and a dim half, a window half and a door half, asks to be approached as it stands before it is approached as a whole. Reni took the reading record, seated just outside the open door with the book on her knees, where she could hear everything Mira said and see the clock on the corridor wall, and the registry clerk Senn stood at the corridor side of the doorway as witness that nothing was moved. Mira entered with her coat removed and her sleeves drawn back from her wrists, her burned fingers stiff and held slightly curled, and she began, as her discipline required, at the threshold, with her right hand resting flat on the door frame at the height of the lock.

The door half gave her nothing.

She said the words aloud for the record, after a silence long enough for Reni to look up from the book, and then she worked the half properly, because a first nothing is an impression and a worked nothing is a finding. She moved along the door half from the western end to the middle of the room, touching, with the back of her right hand and then with her palm, the key register stand, the door frame at two further heights, the side table where the carafe had stood, the northern shelves at the level of a clerk hand, the backs of the chairs on the door side of the western and middle tables, and the floor boards themselves at the point where a person entering must set a foot. Stone and wood and metal all agreed. Where weight should have been, in a working room locked at the end of a working day, the small accumulated residue of a day of handling and entering and leaving, of files carried and tempers kept and errands run, there was instead a cleanness that had no gradation at its onset within the half, a reading flat and even and empty, as a page is empty that has been washed rather than one that was never written on. She had met absence before. In Melora own courts annex, in an earlier case, [she had walked a line on a floor where weight stopped and started](/2026/11/01/nightjar-edge-of-nothing-002/), an absence with an edge, and the memory of that line came back to her feet as she moved east through the reading room, so that she slowed before she reached the middle of the room, not from knowledge but from the discipline of a body that had learned a shape before the mind had finished naming it. In a collection storeroom in this same city, [a whole volume had read as nothing across its whole extent](/2026/11/07/nightjar-false-provenance-007/), and that total absence, terrible as it had been to stand inside, had at least possessed the honesty of completeness. This room did not. Somewhere across its width, the nothing ended.

The window half gave her an ordinary morning.

She crossed into it at the level of the middle table, and the change came under her right hand where it rested, for balance and for reading together, on the table edge, with a suddenness that made her stop and breathe and begin the half again from its boundary, so that the record would show the boundary found twice. On the window side of a line that ran, as near as hand and foot could judge it, down the length of the room parallel to the window and a little to the door side of the tables central row, weight lay as weight should lie in a reading room of the courts on a working morning. She read the eastern table first, where Vale had been found, and the reading there was dense and ordinary and layered in the way of a place much used by clerks whose work was paper and whose days were ruled by other people urgency. She felt, in the wood and in the brass of the lamp and in the stone of the sill above all, the settled residue of many mornings, clerks arriving with files under their arms and the small apprehension that belongs to any man carrying a file he has not yet read into a room where he will be judged on his reading of it, advocates under supervision working with the particular intentness of men who know their time with a document is counted, the low, steady application of registry staff checking entries against entries, a boredom so regular it had become a kind of calm, and over it, fresher and nearer the surface of the reading, the morning of the day before, the last working day of Addis Vale, arriving alive at the eastern table with a file, settling in the chair that faced the door, reading with the unhurried intent of a man who expected to finish what he had begun, pausing, turning pages, making a note, pausing again in the manner of a clerk who has found a figure that does not agree and is deciding whether the disagreement is his error or the record. Nothing in the window half spoke of violence. Nothing in it spoke of fear beyond the ordinary fears of working men. Nothing in it, Mira said aloud, slowly, for Reni book, spoke of a death at all, and she stood for a moment with her eyes closed and her hand flat on the table where a dead man had rested his head on his folded arms, and she felt the morning of his working day go on around the place where his own presence should have deepened it, because weight does not read a body, and a dead man is the quietest object in any building, and the table gave her the room he had worked in and gave her nothing of the man himself except the shape of his habits in the habits of the room.

She worked the whole window half after the eastern table, the sill, the window frame at the latch, where metal held an older, duller residue of many hands and many fastenings, the southern sides of all three tables, the chairs that faced the door, the floor where the light fell. Everywhere the reading held. Everywhere it was ordinary. Clerks, paper, Vale arriving alive and working, ordinary intent laid over ordinary intent in the strata of a room that had been used as it was meant to be used, and the ordinariness, when she stepped back at last into the doorway and felt the cold of the reading come off her in the slow tide that left her hands unsteady and her mouth dry, was the most disturbing finding of the day so far, because the ordinariness stopped at a line. East and south of the line, a working room. West and north of it, along the door and the shelves and the key register stand, a washed cleanness, extending, as her worked reading had established, over the whole door half, from the door itself to the middle of the room, from the floor to the height of a man head and beyond it as far as her hand could reach on the shelving. The absence was partial. It had an edge. The edge, at discovery, ran straight enough to be followed by a hand that doubted it, and Mira followed it three times, at the eastern table, at the middle table, and at the western table where the lamps and the key register stood, and each time her palm passed from reading to nothing within the width of two fingers, at points that aligned when Reni, at her direction, marked them in chalk on the floor boards at the edge of the tables and stepped back so that Mira could see the marks in a row.

She did not leave the finding at the level of the hand. A hand can be deceived by a draught, by weariness, by the wish of the person whose hand it is to find a pattern the room has not offered, and Mira had known Weighers, good ones, who had carried a line in their minds into a room and had then found the line wherever they chose to stop walking. So the measurement was made a second way, with the hand withdrawn, by points and by record. Reni, at the doorway, read aloud the list of points as Mira named them, and Senn marked the floor with chalk at each point where the reading changed, and after the chalk came the tape, laid from the window wall toward the door in three straight runs, at the eastern table, at the middle table, and at the western table, with the distance at each run entered in figures and spoken back before it was written. At the eastern run the change came at the distance Senn recorded first and Mira confirmed from the marks. At the middle run it came at the same distance, within the width of the chalk itself. At the western run it came at the same distance again, and the agreement of the three runs was the point at which Mira permitted the word line to enter the record in place of the word boundary, because three points that agree in a straight run down the length of a room are a line for any honest purpose an enquiry can name, and because the alternative, that her hand had stopped at the same place three times by private decision, was a possibility she stated aloud to Reni and to Senn and had entered beneath the measurements as a caution, together with the only answer she had to it, which was that the runs had been made in different orders and at different points in the reading, with the tape work done by other hands than hers. The shelves received the same treatment at the height of a man shoulder, where the boxes stood, and there too the reading failed on the door side and held, thin but present, in the shallow depth the window light reached along the northern wall at the eastern end, a detail she entered without interpreting, because a shelf is a place where files rest and where hands pass briefly, and its weight is always thinner than the weight of a table where men sit for hours, and thinness is not absence, and confusing the two has ruined more readings than any deliberate deception she had ever encountered in a room. By the time the measurement stood complete, the cold that a reading leaves in her had settled past her wrists and into the bones of her forearms, and she had begun, without yet admitting it to the record, to count the hours in which a second measurement would have to be made if the first were ever to mean more than a curiosity entered in a registry book and forgotten.

The following table settles the state of the two halves of the room and the position of the edge between them at the measurements recorded in this beat and at the further measurements provided for in the annex return, and it is to be read as the finding of the reading and of no other act.

| Time of measurement | Window half | Door half | Edge position |
|---|---|---|---|
| At discovery, first reading | Ordinary weight throughout, layered and current | No weight found at any tested point | Straight line down the room, level with the door side of the central tables |
| At six hours from discovery | Ordinary weight throughout, upper layer thinning | No weight found at any tested point | Line retreated a finger width at the eastern table |
| At eighteen hours from discovery | Ordinary weight throughout, upper layer thinning further | No weight found at any tested point | Line retreated again at the eastern and middle tables |
| At thirty hours from discovery, before release | Ordinary weight throughout, as at discovery | No weight found at any tested point | Final position a hand breadth from the first chalk marks, the retreat confirmed at all three tables |

With the reading entered and the chalk marks recorded by measurement from the window wall and from the door, Mira set out, for the desk record and in Reni hearing, the questions the beat had opened, and she numbered them in speech so that no later account could soften them into impressions. First, if Vale left the room alive at the close of reading hours, by what route and in whose company did his body return to a locked room in the night? Second, who held, or could have held, any key to the reading room in the night hours beyond the three holders the register accounts for? Third, where exactly does the weight in this room stop, and does that line follow the division of window half and door half or a line of its own that only resembles it? Fourth, what was the file that lay open before Vale, and was it the same file he had returned to its box when he signed out on the day before? Fifth, if no person entered the room in the night, what within the room changed between the evening locking and the morning opening? She let the five stand in the record without attempting any of them, because the next part of the work was not answering but arranging, in full view, the possibilities that the evidence so far had left alive, and she owed the record the arrangement before she owed it any preference of her own.

Three possibilities stood, and Mira stated them in the registry room, with Dunmore words from the tasking repeated back at the head of the statement so that the limits of time governed the speculation as they governed everything else. The first possibility was that Vale had been dead before the room was locked, that the register signature and the evening account were wrong or had been made wrong, and that the locked room was a stage dressed after the event it pretended to precede; the nine minutes in Grist morning bore directly on a narrower version of this possibility, and the version would stand or fall by evidence about time and the body. The second possibility was that something in the room had killed him, some property of the place or of an object in it, acting in a locked and empty room upon a man alone in it; the partial absence bore on this possibility in a way Mira declined to specify until the edge had been measured again, and the ordinariness of the window half bore against it, since a room that kills might be expected to hold, in the half where the dying happened, something other than a clerk ordinary morning. The third possibility was that Vale had killed himself and had arranged the circumstances badly, or had arranged them well and been misread by the annex records; against this possibility stood the signed out register, the returned file, and the absence in the door half, none of which a man dying by his own act at the eastern table obviously required, and for it stood only the locked room itself, which was the fact every possibility had to carry and which therefore, on its own, carried none of them. Dunmore, when the statement reached her by Reni hand that evening, returned it with a single marginal direction in pencil, which was to proceed to the body evidence and the time evidence before any second reading was discussed, and Mira, whose hands had not yet stopped shaking from the first, obeyed it with a gratitude she did not enter in any record.

## The Collapse

The court physician was a woman named Doctor Sella Aren, who had certified the death in the reading room in the morning before the body was moved and who received Mira and Reni, in the mortuary room below the annex, with the courtesy of a person whose work has been interrupted twice by the same enquiry and who intends that there should not be a third interruption without new business to justify it. Mira had no business with the body itself, and she said so at the threshold, before the door was fully open, because the limit of her discipline in the presence of the dead was not a preference to be negotiated by the urgency of a case. A Weigher reads what events leave in places and in objects. A body is neither, for those purposes, and Addis Vale, under the clean cloth in the cold room beyond the inner door, was the quietest object in the building, a phrase Mira did not use aloud in that place, out of respect for the woman whose work the dead properly were. What Mira needed from Doctor Aren was not the body but the examination, the method by which the court had established what it claimed to know about the time and manner of the death, and the room temperature log that the annex engineers kept for the preservation of paper and that Aren had, with a foresight Mira privately applauded, demanded from the engineers before she began her own work in the morning.

Aren set out the examination on the mortuary room table, in the form of the notes she had made at the eastern table in the reading room, with the times of each observation entered against the annex bell as she had heard it through the closed door. She had first observed the position of the body without touching it, and had recorded the position and the stillness and nothing more, in accordance with the court rule for a death in place. She had then made the examination that the court required for its certification, and it was the method of that examination, rather than any single finding, that Mira asked her to describe in full, for the record Reni was keeping and for the deduction Mira already suspected the record would have to carry. Aren described it without impatience, as a person describes a procedure she has performed often enough to distrust any performance of it that felt inspired. The temperature of the body was taken by the court method, at the places the method prescribed, and taken twice, at an interval, so that a rate and not merely a reading could be entered. The temperature of the room was taken at the eastern table, at the middle table, and by the door, and those readings were checked against the engineers log for the reading room, which recorded the heat given out by the annex system through the night at intervals of the hour, from a sensor set in the northern wall above the shelves. The state of the body was assessed by the progression of its stiffness, joint by joint, in the order the method prescribed, and by the settling that a body undergoes in the posture in which death leaves it, assessed at the folded arms and at the head where it had rested, and each observation was set, in her notes, against the two temperatures and against the known course of such changes in a room held at the temperatures the log recorded, a course the court physicians kept in tables compiled from their own certified cases and revised, Aren said, whenever a case with a witnessed hour of death gave them cause to revise it. It was not, she said, a method that produced an hour and a minute. It produced an interval, and an honest physician reported the interval and resisted, on the record, every invitation to narrow it for the convenience of an enquiry.

The interval, for Addis Vale, was this. Between the moment of his death and the moment of Doctor Aren examination in the reading room, a period of not less than ninety minutes must have elapsed, at the temperatures the room had held through the night and the early morning. The finding rested on the conjunction of the cooling rate she had measured and the progression of the stiffness she had recorded, each of which, alone, would have supported a longer and looser interval, and which together supported ninety minutes as a floor below which the court tables, in a room at that temperature, with a body of Vale build seated and clothed as he had been found, did not permit her to go. The ceiling of the interval she placed, with less confidence, at some hours beyond the floor, and she declined to fix it more closely until the enquiry could give her a last witnessed sighting of Vale alive against which to set her tables, at which point, she said, the ceiling might be made to do honest work.

Mira took the interval, the room log, and Grist morning list, and she laid them side by side on the mortuary room table, with Reni reading the figures back to her from the copies so that the deduction, when it came, would rest on figures heard aloud by three people and not on any figure carried in her own head from a morning of reading a washed room. The deduction followed within the space of that laying out, and it killed the first possibility where it stood. The only interval in which the dead-before-locking account, in the form in which the annex had begun to comfort itself with it, could do its work was the nine minutes of Grist list, the nine minutes in which the usher had tended a lamp at the eastern table and had not looked up, and in which, on that account, a Vale already dead might somehow have come to be seated at the table without the sealed and logged and locked night having to be contradicted at all. Nine minutes was the whole estate of that account. Against it stood ninety minutes, the floor of the court physician interval, the least time the body condition required between death and the state in which the physician had examined it, and no arrangement, carriage, seating, or settling that began and ended inside Grist nine minutes could produce, at the hour of examination, a body whose cooling and stiffness declared that it had been dead and seated for ninety minutes and more. The account did not merely lack evidence. It lacked time, by a factor that no adjustment of Grist habits or of the annex bell could supply. Vale had not died, and had not been placed, in the nine minutes. If he had been dead before the room was locked on the evening before, then the evening register, the evening usher, the signature in Vale own hand at sign out, the returned file, and Doctor Aren ceiling, when the last sighting evidence reached her, would all have to be dismantled on other evidence, and the enquiry possessed no such evidence and could no longer use the morning gap as a shelter for the idea. Mira entered the collapse in the record in those terms, as a collapse of the lead in the only form in which it had ever been workable, and Reni, writing, underlined the two figures where Mira told her to underline them, nine and ninety, so that a later reader of the notes would find the hinge of the case without having to be told where it was.

The collapse rearranged the other two possibilities, and Mira, back in the registry room with the light going out of the narrow window and the thirty hour limit reduced, by the annex own clock, to a figure she wrote on the inside cover of the bundle and showed to Reni without comment, set out the rearrangement in full before she allowed herself to consider what act it required of her. The second possibility, that something in the room had killed him, weakened on two grounds and reshaped on a third. It weakened, first, on the physician evidence, since Doctor Aren examination had found no indication she was willing to enter of any property of a place or an object acting upon the body in a manner her method could recognise, and her method, Mira noted, recognised a considerable and melancholy range. It weakened, second, on the reading, since the window half of the room, the half in which Vale had sat and in which he had, on any account that survived, spent the last working hours the room remembered, held an ordinary morning and nothing else, no fear beyond working fear, no violence at all, in strata that reached the surface of the day before without disturbance. It reshaped, on the third ground, into a narrower and less comfortable form, in which whatever had acted on Vale had acted in a manner that left the window half ordinary, and had left the door half washed clean of the residue that any ordinary act in a working room deposits, and a possibility that requires a partial absence as part of its mechanism is no longer a possibility about a property of a room. It is a possibility about a practice, and about a hand directing it, and Mira let that sentence stand in the record without enlarging it, because enlargement, at that stage, would have been invention wearing the coat of reasoning.

The third possibility, that Vale had killed himself and had arranged the circumstances badly, weakened further and did not reshape so much as subside. A man who dies by his own act at a reading table, in a room he has entered by whatever route the locked room problem required, leaves, in the ordinary course of the world, some congruence between his last acts and the state of the table before him. The file before Vale had lain open at a working page, its sequence undisturbed, as the registry draughtsman plate confirmed. The pen had lain in a neat line with the edge of the file, where a working clerk sets a pen down between notes. The chair had stood square. The physician interval placed the death at a remove of ninety minutes and more from the examination, and the evening evidence placed Vale, alive and signing his name in a clerk steady hand, at the close of reading hours, returning the very file that the draughtsman plate and the registry box count now identified, beyond dispute, as the file that had lain open before him when he was found. That identification closed the fourth question of the reading record, the question of the file, in full: it was the same file, returned, boxed by the registry in the ordinary course of the evening, and present on the eastern table in the morning by a route that the evening boxing made harder and not easier to explain. The settling evidence closed the fifth question in its original form as well, since the engineers log and the watch occurrences column, laid together, showed a room and a corridor in which nothing measurable had changed between locking and opening except the slow fall of the night temperature and its morning recovery, so that whatever had changed in the reading room had changed in the register that only a Weigher reading could reach, and had changed, precisely, in one half of the room and not in the other. The first question, of the route by which the body had returned, stood open and had grown heavier with everything that had closed around it. The second question, of the key, stood open and narrower, since the audit had left only an entitled hand using a recorded key without entry, or a key that should not exist. The third question, of the line where the weight stopped, stood open most consequentially of all, because it was the only one of the five that further evidence, rather than further argument, could still move.

Mira set out the new questions as she had set out the first five, numbered in speech, with Reni writing and Senn, who had returned with the engineers log, standing witness at the door of the registry room in the manner of a woman who has decided that witnessing is the most useful act available to her. First, at what point in the evening after Vale signed out, within the interval the physician tables allow, did Vale die? Second, by what route did the file that the registry had boxed in the evening come to lie open on the eastern table in the morning? Third, whose entitled hand, if the key audit holds, used a recorded key in the night without making the entry the register exists to hold? Fourth, what practice, directed by a hand, removes the weight from one half of a room and leaves the other half in its ordinary state? Fifth, will the edge of the absence stand where the first reading found it when the room is read again, or will the line have moved, and by how much, and in which direction? She stopped at five. There were more questions in the room than five, as there always are when a lead collapses honestly, but a record that opens every question it can name opens none of them properly, and the five she had named shared a property the earlier five had not all shared: each of them could be answered, or narrowed toward an answer, by evidence that still existed somewhere inside the thirty hours, if the enquiry moved while the evidence remained where the night had left it.

Four of the five required work that other hands could do, and she assigned that work before she spoke of the fifth, because the fifth required her own hands and she wanted the record to show that every alternative had been given its hour first. Reni would take the last sighting evidence to Doctor Aren, the sign out, the porter passage in the watch occurrences column, the registry staff account of Vale leaving the annex by the lower door in the company of no one, and would bring back the narrowed ceiling of the physician interval, or would bring back the refusal to narrow it, which would itself be evidence of a kind. Senn would produce, under receipt, the registry boxing record for the file, with the names of the two staff who had boxed it and the hour at which the box had gone to the shelf, and the shelf record for the night, if a shelf record existed in any form finer than the room register itself. The key question would go, in writing, to Dunmore, for Dunmore to put to the registry at the level at which an entitled hand could be asked about without the asking itself becoming an accusation entered in no book; Mira would not put that question in the annex herself, and she gave her reason plainly, which was that a Weigher asking a registrar about keys in a building where a clerk had died would be remembered as an accusation whatever words she chose, and the case had accusations enough in prospect without manufacturing one from a tone of voice. The fourth question, of the practice, she reserved. No one in the annex was to be asked about practices at all. That question would be answered, if it was answered in this case, from the room and from the records of the room, and from her own register of absences as it now stood in oversight custody, a copy of the relevant entries in which she had already requested through Dunmore by the proper channel, naming the Melora floor line and the storeroom of the collection as the entries she required, and no others.

The fifth question required the room to be read again.

Mira stated the requirement first as a fact of method, in the flat tone she used for facts of method, so that Reni notebook would hold it in that tone before it held anything else. The edge of the absence had been measured once, at discovery, by chalk and by hand, at three points down the length of the room. A single measurement of an edge states where the edge was. It cannot state what the edge is doing. If the absence in the door half was a fixed condition of the room, laid down at whatever hour and by whatever means, and thereafter stable, then a second reading would find the line where the first had found it, and the case would proceed on the footing that the partial absence was a completed act with a geography that could be mapped against the geography of the room, the tables, the door, the shelves, and the place where the body had sat. If the absence was not stable, if its edge stood in a different place at a second reading, then the case was of another kind altogether, and every hour spent proceeding on the first footing was an hour spent building on a measurement that the room itself had already withdrawn. There was no third reading posture available within the Melora limit that could substitute for the second reading, no interview and no register and no table of the court physicians that could tell her whether a line in a room was holding or moving. The room would tell her, or no one would, and the room would only tell her in the act of being read.

The standing medical advice of the Office, for Weighers, was not ambiguous, and Mira did not pretend, in the registry room or in the record, that it was. A Weigher who had read a room of any density was not to read the same room again within a full day and a night, and was preferably not to read any room of comparable density within that span, because the cost of a reading is taken in the body and in the sleep that follows it, and a second reading taken before the first has cleared compounds the cost in a manner that the Office physicians, whose tables on this subject Mira respected as she respected Doctor Aren tables on hers, described as multiplicative rather than additional. Mira had read the reading room that day, in halves, thoroughly, over the better part of an hour of contact, with her right hand and with the cold still in her bones at the hour when she sat in the registry room considering the second reading, and with her burned fingers stiffened further by the work, so that she had twice failed, in Reni sight, to make her left hand close properly around a cup. Her state entering the case had been full, rested, fed, and unburdened by any reading in the days before it, which was precisely the state in which the Office permitted a Weigher to spend herself once. At the point the case had now reached, she was spent in the ordinary, accounted way, and the advice existed for exactly the decision she was contemplating, and she knew it, and Reni knew that she knew it, and the notebook, at that moment, recorded a silence that Reni afterwards described, in her own private recollection of the day and in no official place, as the longest of the case.

Dunmore objection arrived, as objections from Dunmore generally arrived, in writing, by the hand of a registry runner, on a single sheet folded once, in response to the message in which Mira, following the rule that a Weigher contemplating a reading against advice notifies the tasking officer before and not after, had stated the requirement and the interval she proposed. The objection was complete and it was fair. Dunmore relayed the Office medical standing instruction in its own words, which Mira read without disputing any of them. She relayed the alternative, which was to hold the room under guard and to seek, through Commissioner Harrand office, a Melora extension on medical grounds, and she gave the honest assessment, which Mira also did not dispute, that Melora had refused one extension already in spirit by the terms of the original release, and that a medical application would consume, in drafting and in answer, a large part of the time it sought to gain. She relayed, finally, the point that weighed most with her as the tasking officer, which was that a junior officer would be present at the reading, that Reni would see whatever the reading did to the Weigher she was assigned to, and that the Office did not lightly place its junior officers as witnesses to the cost of a decision they had no standing to oppose. The sheet ended with an instruction that was not an order, because Dunmore, who understood the governance of Weighers better than most officers who had never read a room, knew that the decision to read, in the end, could be taken only by the body that would pay for it, and that an order forbidding a reading that a case required was an order that transferred the cost of the unmade reading to the case itself, where it would be paid by other people, in other coin, later and less visibly. She required, instead, that if Mira decided to read, the decision, the advice, and the objection should all three stand in the record in full, and that Reni should be given, by Mira personally and in words, the standing to call a halt to the reading if what she witnessed passed the limits set out in the Office instruction for witnesses, a copy of which was folded inside the sheet for that purpose.

Mira read the sheet twice, as she had read the observation log twice, once for its content and once for its character, and she gave Reni the witness instruction to read in her own time before any reading took place, and she entered in the record, in her own hand, beneath Reni typed account of the five new questions, the three statements Dunmore had required: that the standing medical advice was against a second reading of the same room within a full day and a night; that the tasking officer objection had been received, read, and understood, and was relayed to the record in full on the sheet that accompanied it; and that the interval within which the Melora release would still permit the reading to bear on the case, and within which a movement of the edge, if movement there was, would still be measurable against the chalk marks and measurements of the first reading, was eighteen hours from the first reading, and not a wider span, because beyond eighteen hours the release itself had less than half its life remaining and a finding that arrived with no time to act on it was a finding the case could not use. Eighteen hours, she wrote, and underlined the figure once, as the nine and the ninety had been underlined, so that the record would hold the three figures of the case together in the same hand.

Then, in the registry room, with the annex bell sounding the evening hour through two closed doors and Reni standing with the notebook open at the page where the decision would be entered, Mira made the decision against the standing medical advice, over the objection Dunmore had relayed, in full knowledge of the cost her discipline kept its accounts in, and she stated it for the record in a single sentence that she did not qualify afterwards. She would read the room a second time within eighteen hours of the first reading.

## Pressure

Mira stood in the corridor outside the reading room with her back to the annex wall and made the decision in full view of the person who would have to live with its consequences beside her. Reni Vosk stood two paces away with the decay table folded in her hand and the key register copy beneath it, and she had already said, once and without emphasis, that the standing medical advice was against a second read within eighteen hours. She did not say it again. She waited the way a junior officer waits when she has put the relevant fact on the record and understands that the record will not make the decision for anyone. Mira looked at the reading room door, at the seal the courts had placed and broken and placed again under Melora procedure, at the narrow strip of corridor floor between the door and the turn where the porters came through with their trolleys, and she said that she would read the room a second time, and that this time she would include the corridor. Reni asked whether the corridor was a change of scope or a correction of scope, and Mira said it was a correction. The first pass had treated the room as the place and the corridor as the approach to the place, and that division had been hers and it had been wrong in the particular way that matters in a Weigher's work, because weight does not respect the plan of a building. It settles where events settle. It thins where something takes it away. If the door half of the room read as nothing while the window half read as an ordinary morning, then the line between those halves was not a wall and not a piece of furniture. It was the edge of whatever had happened, and an edge has two sides, and she had read only one of them. Reni wrote that down, or the shape of it, in the small notebook she kept in her coat, and then she asked the question that mattered more. She asked whether Mira wanted the corridor read first or last. Mira said last, and then, after a pause in which she tested the honesty of the answer against what her hands were already telling her, she corrected herself again and said first. If the absence in the door half had a counterpart outside the door, she needed to meet it while she still had the full use of herself. If she read the room first she would spend what the room cost and arrive at the corridor already diminished, and the thing she most needed from this pass was an undiminished reading of the ground where she now believed the case had actually turned. Reni nodded and recorded the order, and recorded the time, and recorded Mira's statement that the second read was undertaken against standing medical advice and by her own choice, not under instruction from Dunmore and not under pressure from the courts, who had given them thirty hours before the room was released and wanted, as courts always want, an answer that arrived dressed as certainty. Mira was not offering certainty. She was offering to pay for information in the only currency her practice accepted, and she wanted the record to show that the price had been named before it was paid.

Her fingers were the first part of the negotiation. The burns from the previous case had healed to the point where the skin held, but holding was not the same as working, and when she flexed her right hand against the corridor rail the last two fingers answered late, a small delay that she had learned to read the way other people read a clock. She could grip. She could release. What she could not do quickly was grip and release in sequence without thinking about it, and a read is nothing but sequence, anchor to anchor, the hand learning what the mind is not permitted to invent. She told Reni this as well, because Reni was the witness and a witness who has not been told the state of the instrument is only half a witness. She said the fingers would be slow on the door frame and slower on the rail, and that if she asked for a surface to be named aloud Reni should name it and not touch her to guide her to it. Reni said she understood. She said it in the flat tone she used for procedure, and Mira was grateful for the flatness in a way she did not say aloud, because the corridor was already doing the other thing, the preliminary thing, the faint pressure at the edge of attention that comes before a Weigher commits and which is not yet information and must not be treated as information. The window half of the room beyond the door had been heavy on the first pass, heavy with the ordinary accumulation of a working morning, Vale arriving alive and settling to his files, the small frictions of a clerk's day laid down in the wood of the table and the arms of the chair. The door half had been nothing. Standing here, with the door closed between her and that divided room, Mira could feel the corridor under her shoes doing something she had walked across twice without attending to, and she understood, with the particular shame that belongs to a missed obvious thing, that she had treated her own transit as neutral because transit is supposed to be neutral. People cross corridors. Porters cross corridors. Ushers cross corridors. A corridor is where a building keeps its intentions to go elsewhere. Nobody had asked what this corridor had kept. She put her left hand, the better hand, flat against the annex wall at shoulder height and breathed once, and told Reni to begin the timing.

The wall gave her the building first, as walls always do when they are asked properly, which is to say it gave her almost nothing and gave it slowly. Annex stone and old plaster held the residue of decades of unremarkable passage, clerks and counsel and witnesses moving between rooms with their minds on the next hour, a low background of impatience and performance and private worry that no single event had marked deeply enough to stand out from the rest. She moved her hand along it toward the reading room door in increments she chose and announced under her breath, not for Reni's benefit but for her own discipline, because a read that is not paced becomes a fall. The weight thickened as she approached the door, and the thickening had a quality that she registered before she had words for it, a gathering, the way still air gathers before weather that has already been decided somewhere else. She noted that she was reaching for weather and set the comparison aside. Comparisons are a form of invention. What the wall held, as her palm crossed the last stretch before the door frame, was intent, held tight and carried low, intent of a kind that did not belong to the ordinary traffic of the annex because ordinary traffic does not brace itself. People on their way to a hearing brace themselves, but they brace upward, toward the face they mean to present. This weight sat lower in the wall and lower still as she let her hand descend to the frame itself, and her slow fingers found the wood of the frame and closed on it with their small delay, and the frame gave her the door half of the room all over again, gave her the nothing, clean and bounded, and she held herself at the edge of that nothing the way she had held herself on the first pass, respectful of it, reading its perimeter rather than its contents because it had no contents to read. That was the discipline Dunmore had approved and the physicians would have approved, if physicians were ever asked to approve the second half of a mistake. She had intended to hold the perimeter for a count of sixty and then step back into the corridor and begin again on the floor. She counted. Reni counted with her, silently, in the notebook, marking the seconds as they were spent. At forty the nothing changed, or rather her understanding of the nothing changed, because the perimeter she was holding began to behave not like a boundary but like a held breath.

She should have stepped back at forty. She has thought about that interval since, in the hours when thought was all she could afford, and she knows the shape of the error precisely because it was not a failure of knowledge. She knew the standing advice. She knew her hands. She knew that a second read within eighteen hours takes from a smaller reserve than the first, and that the reserve is not restored by wanting the answer more. What she did at forty was choose, again and in miniature, the same choice she had made standing against the wall, which was to include the part she had left out. The corridor floor was behind her. The door frame was under her slow fingers. The nothing in the door half was in front of her, and it occurred to her, with a clarity that felt like the read itself offering her a kindness, that the absence might not be a wall with the room on one side and the corridor on the other, but a single withholding stretched across both, and that if she turned her attention from the frame to the floor without breaking contact, without the small reset that stepping back would have given her, she would learn whether the withholding was continuous. It was the correct question. It was also the question that cost what it cost, and she does not pretend, to Reni or to the record, that she did not know it might. She slid her hand down the frame to its base, where the wood met the stone of the threshold, and she turned, still holding, and set her palm flat on the corridor floor at the point where the threshold's shadow would fall if the annex had admitted honest light, and the corridor gave her everything at once.

Ninety seconds. That is what Reni's timings say, and Mira believes the timings over her own sense of it, because her own sense of it has no floor and no ceiling and cannot be entered in a register. Ninety seconds of violence, laid down in stone and held there and then released into the one person who had come asking, at full force, all of it arriving in the order it had been suffered and none of it softened by distribution. Later, when she could order it, she understood why. The absence in the door half had not only taken weight away. It had been holding the room's share back, dammed against its edge, the ordinary morning on the window side and the event itself pressed against the boundary of the nothing like water against a gate that was never built to be one. The corridor had kept its own share separately, out here where she had not thought to ask, and the two shares were connected in the way that a held thing and the hand that holds it are connected. When she crossed from frame to floor without breaking, she did not open a door between two readings. She removed the last obstacle between herself and a single event that had been divided by an act of erasure, and the division ended, and the event came through her in one piece. Fear first. Vale's fear, though weight never names a person and she did not receive a name, only the affect in its purity, the fear of a man who understood in a corridor, between one ordinary step and the next, that the person with him had not accompanied him by accident. It came at her low and fast and it filled the corridor for her so completely that the annex wall at her back ceased to be available as a reference, and that was the perceptual narrowing beginning, the field of the world contracting to the field of the event. Then violence, which in weight is not an image and not a sound but a direction and a commitment, force applied with intent and continued past the point where intent might have reconsidered. The violence had a direction. She received the direction the way the body receives a slope, without argument. It ran along the corridor, not across it, from the turn where the porters came through toward the reading room door, and it ran close to the wall, her wall, the wall her hand had travelled, and it was brief and it was total and it did not move the way a struggle moves. A struggle spreads. This did not spread. This arrived, and accomplished, and then changed its character in her hands while she still knelt at the threshold with her palm losing its argument with the stone. Grief was not in it. Rage was not in it. What followed the violence was a dreadful practicality, affect flattened into purpose, the weight of a decision being carried out by someone whose fear was of discovery and not of what he had done, and the floor took that purpose and gave it to her step by step, because the next thing the corridor held was carriage. A burden moved along the same line the violence had run, close to the wall, toward the door, moving more slowly than a person walks when his hands are free, with pauses that were not rest. The door opened in the weight, not as a sight but as a change in resistance, the threshold crossed under load, and the burden passing into the room and the door closing behind it, and beyond that point the corridor's account ended in the nothing she already knew, because inside the room the door half had been emptied and there was nothing left to carry her further. She had what she had come for. She had it at a price that was already being collected.

At the peak the world was very small. Stone under palm. Direction along wall. Force. Then carriage. Then door. Her sentences, later, when Reni repeated what she had said aloud during the read, turned out to have shortened in step with the contraction, and she had not known she was speaking. Floor. Wall side. Coming this way. Not a fight. He knew. After that, carrying. Slow. He is carrying. Door. She heard about the shape of those words from a chair, hours afterward, and she recognised the discipline buried in them even as the discipline failed to protect her, because she had gone on naming anchors and directions while the event was taking the rest of her in payment, and the naming is the only part of a Weigher that the weight cannot use. The cold arrived the way it always arrives with her, except that this time it arrived as a tide rather than a draught, rising from the hand on the stone through an arm that had stopped reporting its position accurately, into a chest that had begun to treat breathing as a task requiring supervision. Cost, not damage. There was no wound to describe and she would not have described one if there had been, because what the read took was function, spent in the order she could least afford to lose it. Her right hand, set down to brace her as the corridor tilted in her perception, answered later than her left and with less conviction. Her knees, which had held her at the threshold in the posture of her practice, began a negotiation she was not party to. The corridor's ordinary sounds, Reni's pen, the distant traffic of the annex going about the business of a Session day, receded behind the last of the carriage weight as it crossed the threshold in her memory of the stone, and then her body made the final decision of the read without consulting her, which is the body's right when the reserve reaches the bottom she had been warned about in writing. She went down.

She went down sideways, Reni's timings and Reni's account agree, not forward onto the threshold and not into the room, whose seal she did not break with her fall, but sideways onto the corridor floor with her left hand still extended toward the base of the door frame as if the read had not yet released her from its sequence. Reni's response, entered in the notebook in the same hand and at the same pace as everything that preceded it, is the part of the beat Mira has since read with the closest attention, because it is the part she cannot supply herself. Reni did not call out. Reni stepped to her, set one hand under her shoulder and one at her elbow, and moved her, in two stages and with a pause between them to check the corridor, away from the threshold and clear of the door's swing, so that neither of them obstructed the seal and neither of them entered the room. She placed Mira with her back against the annex wall, in the stretch of corridor where the weight, as Mira had announced it before the read, belonged to the ordinary traffic of the building, and she arranged her coat under Mira's head without being asked and without describing what she was doing as care. She checked the reading room door. She confirmed the court's seal intact and the door closed, and she wrote that confirmation down before she wrote anything about Mira, which is the correct order and which Mira has not forgotten. She recorded the time the read began, the time Mira's speech shortened, the time she went down, and the time she was moved, four times in a column, and below them she recorded that the subject was breathing, that she was cold to the touch at the hands and face, that she did not respond to her name on the first asking or the second, and that she did respond, on the third, with the word corridor. She sent the annex porter who appeared at the turn not for a physician, not yet, but to stand at the far end of the corridor and keep it empty, and she sent him with instructions that named no emergency, so that the corridor stayed a corridor and did not become a scene. Only when the corridor was held and the room was sealed and the times were written did she go for the physician herself, and she went at a walk that she afterward described, when Mira pressed her, as deliberate. When the physician arrived Mira was where Reni had placed her, down against the wall with the coat under her head and her slow fingers curled against the stone as if still holding an anchor, and in her memory, intact beneath the cold and beneath the body's refusal to resume its ordinary services on demand, the corridor's evidence sat complete and ordered and hers. The violence had run along the wall toward the reading room door. Addis Vale had been killed in the corridor. His body had been carried the short distance, close to the wall, across the threshold, and into the room she had been reading in halves. She lay on the floor of the place where it had happened and held the direction of it like a compass point while everything else in her went quiet and spent, by her own choice, to the last of the reserve she had decided to spend.


## The Silence

She did not speak the solution when speech first came back to her, and the not speaking was not confusion. The physician had come and gone with his instructions, and Reni had carried them out and written them down, and Mira sat against the annex wall in the quiet stretch of the corridor with the coat still under her head and her hands in her lap, where she could watch them decide, slowly, to be hands again. Reni asked once whether she should send word to Dunmore. Mira shook her head, a movement so small that Reni had to ask whether it had been a movement at all, and Mira said, not yet. The pieces were in the room with her, or in the corridor, or in the folded papers in Reni's hand, and for the first time since Faun Grist had opened a locked door onto a dead clerk, the pieces were all of them present at once, and she wanted the silence in which they could turn into place without the pressure of an audience arranging them into a report before they had finished arranging themselves. The absence first. Half a room emptied and half a room left heavy, the edge of the emptiness crossing the reading room at a line no furniture explained. The nine minutes second, the gap in the usher's list that had tempted them toward a dead man locked in with his own death, until the condition of the body had closed that road with its ninety minutes and its patience. Ninety minutes at the least required, and nine minutes of a list available, and between those two numbers a man could not be made to fit, however convenient the fit would have been. The window half third, heavy with Vale alive and working, the files opening under his hands, an ordinary working day laid down in wood and cloth, heavy precisely because nothing had been taken from it. The corridor fourth, running along the wall, violence with a direction and carriage behind it, a burden crossing a threshold. And the papers last, the watch occurrences column in Reni's hand, with the registry porter passing the stair head in the small hours, the key audit and its one hard residue, a recorded key in an entitled hand leaving no entry behind it, and the registry roster Senn had produced under receipt, naming the porter on duty through the night, papers that had been there since the first hours of the case asking nothing of anyone. She turned them over in the order she had received them and then in the order they had happened, and the two orders met, and the shape they made was whole and unforced and left nothing standing outside it except the name the roster supplied and which the weight, as always, had refused to supply for itself. Reni watched her and said nothing, and then, in the minimal exchange the silence allowed, she said the word roster, as a question. Mira nodded. Reni said, the door half is the carrying half. Mira nodded again, and felt the cold sit steady in her rather than deepen, which was how she knew the turn was complete. She held what she knew a while longer, not from doubt and not from theatre, but because a solution spoken becomes other people's property and she wanted, once, to keep it in the quiet where she had paid for it. Then she asked Reni to help her into the chair the porter had brought, and to send word to Dunmore, and she said nothing of the solution itself until both of those things had been done.


## Close

She gave the reveal from a chair, six hours after she went down in the corridor, in a small room off the annex that the courts had lent them and that held, in its weight, nothing more recent than other people's waiting. The chair had arms, which she had asked for without explaining that arms were what made sitting a thing she could promise to continue doing. A cup stood on the table beside her and had been standing there long enough to go from warm to honest, because holding a cup is a sequence, grip and lift and steadiness, and her hands were rationing sequence. She would lift it eventually. The delay was the cost made visible in a small unit, and she let it sit there in view rather than hide it, because Dunmore was watching from across the table with the expression of an analyst who measures what a report leaves out, and Reni was at her right with the notebook and the register copy and the decay table, and a reveal that hides its price invites its hearers to spend the currency again. She was still cold. Six hours had not settled that account. The cold lived in her hands and in the long muscles of her back, and it had opinions about the chair and about the length of the sentences she could sustain, and she shaped the reveal accordingly, plainly and in order, the way she had assembled it in the silence. She began with what the case was not. Addis Vale had not died in the reading room. The usher's list, with its nine minute gap, was a clerical wound in an honest document and nothing more. It had tempted them to place the death in the morning, inside nine minutes, and the condition of the body forbade it: ninety minutes at the least stood between the death and the finding, and the physician's narrowed interval, set against the evening sign out, placed the death in the evening before, and ninety minutes is the fact against which every other arrangement in this case must be tested. Faun Grist had found exactly what he reported finding, a dead man at a reading table in a locked and observed room, and the lock and the observation were both genuine, and both set in place over a room already arranged, by hands that did not know what they were guarding.

She laid out the room in halves, as she had read it on the first pass and confirmed on the second. The window half held an ordinary working day at full weight, Vale alive at the eastern table, Vale working, the small honest accumulation of a clerk among his files on the last day the room was in ordinary use. That half was never emptied and never needed to be, because nothing in it contradicted the story the room was made to tell, the story of a man who had come to work and died at his table. The door half held nothing. Not the faintness of a place little used, not the thinning that time works on any room, but an absence with an edge, measured and recorded, the half of the room between the door and the table stripped of the weight that events lay down there. She asked them to hold that geography and to set the corridor beside it. In the corridor, read on the second pass, the violence had a direction and the direction ran along the wall toward the reading room door, brief and accomplished, followed by carriage at a burden's pace along the same line, a burden crossing the threshold and a door closing behind it. Vale was killed in the corridor. He was carried the short distance, close to the wall, through the door, and set at the reading table where Grist later found him, arranged into the posture of a man who had died among his files. The carrying happened through the door half of the room. The feet that carried him crossed the door half. The burden was set down from the door half. And the absence was applied to the door half, to that half and not the other, because that half was where the carrying had happened and where the weight of the carrying would otherwise have spoken. Whoever emptied the room emptied the incriminating half and left the innocent half to testify, and the testimony of the window half, heavy and ordinary and true, was what had made the nothing beside it so easy to walk past. Half a room is a more persuasive lie than a whole one. She said that sentence carefully, because it was the centre of the case, and then she gave them the name, and she gave it from the evidence and from nowhere else.

The weight gave her no name. She said that first, and she watched Dunmore receive it, because the limits of the practice are part of the evidence and a reveal that omits them corrupts the custody of everything else in it. Weight does not record who. It never has. What the corridor read supplied was direction and character, violence along the wall and carriage to the door, and what the room read supplied was the geography of the erasure. The name comes from the papers. Reni, at her gesture, laid the registry roster and the boxing record open on the table and set her finger, not Mira's, beneath the duty line, because her finger could be relied on to arrive where it aimed. The roster Senn had produced under receipt, the production the collapse of the first reading had set in train, named the staff of the registry floor for the night in question, and across the whole of the interval the physician allowed, the evening after Vale signed out and the small hours that followed, it named one registry porter on the upper floor, a man whose duties gave him the corridor as his workplace and the carrying of burdens along it as his description. Hallis Brume, a records porter of these courts. The watch occurrences column, in Corl's hard pressed hand, places Brume at the stair head in the small hours, passing without stopping, in a building where no second porter was on duty to pass there. The corridor read places the violence and the carriage on the porters' line along the wall, the line his work walks daily, which is why its passage excited no remark in a building trained to step around a porter with a load. The halves complete it. A porter carries a burden into a room and sets it down, and the half of the room he works in is emptied afterward, and the arranging was finished before the evening usher locked and sealed the room in ignorance of what it held, which is why the register of keys is silent about the night and why its silence is true: no key was issued in the night because none was needed, the room having been entered while it still stood in staff use, by a hand the building entitled to be there. Each strand is a thing established in this case and in this Session's work, the reading of a room in halves, the reading of the corridor, the roster and the watch column, the ninety minutes of the body, and she claimed nothing beyond the reach of those four. On that evidence, and on the direction of the courts of Melora in whose building and custody the matter lay, Hallis Brume was taken into Melora court custody before the reveal had finished travelling the short distance from her chair to the record, and the case question, who killed Addis Vale and how was a dead man found locked in a room where he did not die, was answered in full.

Dunmore tested it then, as an analyst should, and Mira welcomed the testing because a reveal that cannot survive an analyst in a quiet room will not survive the courts in a loud one. Dunmore asked why the porter could not have been any porter, and Mira answered from the roster alone, that the roster and the boxing record named the registry staff of that floor for that night, that one porter stood on the upper floor across the whole of the interval the physician allowed, and that the watch column, kept for another purpose by a man with no interest in the answer, placed that porter at the stair head in the small hours with no second porter anywhere in the building to stand in his place. She asked whether another porter might have walked the corridor line, and Mira answered from the corridor read alone, that the question was fair and that the read could not answer it, because weight gives direction and character and never a face, and the corridor had given her violence along the wall and carriage to the door and nothing with a name on it, which was precisely why the papers mattered and precisely why she would not let the read be made to say more in the record than it had said in the stone. Dunmore asked why the window half had been left heavy, if a man thorough enough to empty half a room had stood in that room with time to work, and Mira said that thoroughness was the error in the question, because emptying the window half would have emptied Vale's morning as well, the arrival and the working and the ordinary accumulation that made the room's story plausible, and a wholly empty room announces itself as a tampered room to the first Weigher who kneels in it, while a half empty room argues, persuasively and in Vale's own working day, that nothing here needs explaining. Dunmore asked about the door, the locked and observed door that had governed the first hours of the case, and Mira returned to the ninety minutes, patiently, because patience with that number was the discipline the case had taught all of them. The door was locked from the time the room was set, and observed thereafter, and Grist's evidence on both points stood. It also stood later than the death. The body's condition required ninety minutes at the least, and the physician's interval placed the killing in the evening, after Vale signed out, in the hours when the corridor was still a working corridor and the reading room stood open to the registry staff whose duties took them there, and the locking that so impressed the first inquiry was a locking performed on a room already arranged, a seal set on a story already composed, by the ordinary procedures of the courts acting in ignorance of what they were sealing. The nine minutes in the usher's list belonged to that later, innocent stretch, a gap in a list and not in a life. Reni, without being asked, turned the notebook to the four times she had recorded in the corridor and laid it beside the roster copy, so that the reveal had its documents in a row, the roster, the table, the times, each one made by a hand other than the hand that had done the reading, and Mira felt the arrangement steady her in a way the chair's arms could not. This is how a case should close in the Ninth Office, she thought, not on the strength of a gift but on the agreement of papers that do not know one another. The roster did not know the corridor. The corridor did not know the body. The body did not know the halves of the room. Four witnesses who had never met, telling one night in four languages, and the night they told was the same night. She said some of that aloud, in shorter sentences than she would have used before the corridor, and Dunmore wrote it down in her own hand rather than trusting it to Reni's notebook, which Mira understood as the analyst's way of taking custody of a conclusion. When Dunmore asked, finally, whether anything in the reveal depended on a capability exercised for the first time in this case, Mira said no, and enumerated them as the self check would require, the reading of a room in halves, the reading of a corridor as an anchor in its own right, the roster and the watch column consulted as documents, the condition of a body taken as a measure of time. Nothing new had been invented to close the case. The case had closed because the old tools were at last pointed at all of the ground, including the ground she had first walked across without asking it a question.

What remained was the cost, and she paid the last of it openly because leaving it unspoken would have made Reni carry it alone. She told Dunmore that the second read had been her choice against standing medical advice, that the corridor inclusion had been her correction of her own first error, and that the price had come due in the corridor in front of a junior officer who had then performed, in correct order, the securing of the room, the recording of the times and the removal of her supervising officer from the floor. She said that this too belonged in the accounting of the case. A junior officer had watched her go down. That fact would live in Reni's service the way such facts live, retold or not retold, and it would shape what Reni understood a Weigher's work to demand long after the physicians had closed their file on the cold and the rationed hands. She named it as part of what the case had cost her, not as an apology and not as a warning, but as a debt entered honestly in the same ledger as the rest. Reni, beside her, kept her eyes on the notebook and wrote that down too, after a hesitation so brief that only someone watching her hands, as Mira now watched all hands, would have caught it. Dunmore closed the folder she had not opened. She said the courts would have their copy within the hour, and that Mira would not be reading anything, for anyone, until the physicians and she had finished disagreeing about the date. Mira lifted the cup at last, with both hands and with a delay between the decision and the arrival of the lift that she declined to disguise, and she drank, and the reveal was done.


## The Notch

The case closed, and the room went on keeping its own record, which is the part of the work that no custody order can hurry. Melora had given them thirty hours before the reading room was released back to the ordinary business of the courts, and across those thirty hours Reni, on her own initiative and without being tasked by Dunmore or encouraged by Mira, went back to the edge of the absence and measured it. She used the method of the decay table that the earlier work on the case had established, the measured position of the line where the door half's nothing gave way to the window half's ordinary weight, taken against fixed points in the room that did not depend on anyone's memory, the threshold, the table leg, the line of the shelving. The table already held the absence as it was measured at discovery and twice since, three positions for the edge, each with its hour. What Reni's returning added was not a new kind of fact but the patience to let an existing fact finish speaking. The edge had moved. It had moved measurably since the death, a small distance in the scale of a room and an unmistakable one in the scale of the table, the nothing yielding ground to the ordinary as the hours accumulated, the line retreating toward the door through which the carrying had come. It moved far more slowly than weight fades. Weight, left alone, thins the way a scent thins, generous at first and then reluctant, and a Weigher learns its pace the way sailors learn a coast. This was slower, slower by an order that the table made plain without anyone needing to argue for it, a retreat so gradual that across any single hour it could be mistaken for permanence, and across thirty hours it could not. Mira, still moving through the end of the case at the speed her hands allowed, set the decay observation in the record against the register material and the other papers of the case, and wrote the entry herself, because some entries should be made by the officer who will be asked about them in later Sessions. The absence decays. Whatever the absence is, and she claimed no knowledge beyond the measurements, it is not permanent. That was the installment the case left open behind it as it closed, concrete and small and sharp as a notch cut in a door frame to mark a height, a single carried question with a measurement for its handle. Something that empties a room can itself run out. Something that was done to the door half of the reading room is, slowly and measurably, coming undone, and what it was holding back may not stay held forever, and may not need to, because the corridor has already spoken. She filed that thought where such thoughts belong, in the record and not in the reveal, and let the closed case stay closed around it.

The legend arrived before the thirty hours did, as legends do, travelling faster than the fact it feeds on and arriving better dressed. It came by way of a court official of the annex, a man of the Session's administration who had business with their borrowed room and who, business done, lingered in the doorway to offer what the building was already saying about her, in the tone of a person conferring an honour. They said in Melora that the Ninth Office's Weigher had read the dead clerk's last thought, that she had laid her hand on the room where Addis Vale died and taken his final thinking out of it like a letter out of a drawer, and that the name of his killer had come from the dead man's own mind. He repeated it with satisfaction. He looked at her hands as he said it, as if the hands were the instruments of the miracle, and she understood that the story had everything a story needs, a locked room, a dead clerk, a woman on the floor of a corridor, and an arrest, and that the truth, which was a corridor read along a wall and a window half read honestly and a register read by daylight, could not compete with it on any ground the annex cared about. She read a corridor and a window half. She cannot read the dead at all, and never could, and the dead clerk had kept his last thought, whatever it was, entirely to himself, in the privacy the practice guarantees even to the murdered. She did not correct the official. She thanked him for the room and watched him go, carrying the better story out into the building at a pace the measurements could only envy, and Reni, when the door had closed, looked at her with the question plain in her face and received no answer she could use, because a correction issued once, in a borrowed room, to one satisfied man, would have travelled nowhere except into the story as a denial, and denials are seasoning to a legend. So the legend went uncorrected into the Session's record of talk, further from what the reader of the room had actually done than any earlier telling, and Mira let it go the way she let the cup sit, as a cost counted and accepted. Late, when the borrowed room was to be surrendered and the papers of the case had gone to their custodies one by one, Reni found herself holding the three measurements of the edge with nowhere clean left to put them, the table itself already filed and sealed into the record, and no paper on her person that was hers to write on. She took the pen from her coat. She wrote the three measurements on her own wrist in pen, small figures in a careful hand along the inner skin where a pulse runs, because there was no paper, and Mira saw it, and said nothing.

## Case File

### Q: How does Melora administer its reading rooms for arbitration business?

Melora administers its reading rooms as controlled working space rather than as public accommodation. Each room is assigned by the registrar for a stated matter, a stated period and a named set of users, and no other business may be introduced during that assignment. Rooms are opened by an usher, inspected before occupation and inspected again on vacation. Furniture, seals and document trays are counted against a room list. Parties read in the room only material lodged for their matter, and material may not be removed without a custody note. At the close of business the registrar reconciles assignments, keys and room lists, and any discrepancy is entered in the day book before the annex is secured for the night.

### Q: What medical limits govern a Weigher assigned to field duties?

A Weigher assigned to field duties works under medical limits set by the Ninth Office and reviewed after any heavy read. The limits govern the number of reads in a period, the depth of read permitted, the interval required between reads and the conditions under which a read must be refused. A Weigher who reports pain, disorientation, loss of discrimination between layers of weight or failure to recover within the expected interval is withdrawn from reading until a medical officer clears a return. Limits are recorded on the assignment sheet. A commanding officer may narrow a limit for a particular case but may not widen a limit set on medical advice.

### Q: What access may Accord officers claim inside a member court building?

Accord officers may claim access inside a member court building only under the standing terms agreed between the Meridian Accord and the member court. Access covers inspection of premises connected with an Accord matter, attendance at a hearing by invitation or by right of oversight, and entry to rooms where Accord records or Accord evidence are held. Access does not extend to private chambers, to matters in which the Accord has no standing, or to sealed material belonging to another party. Officers announce themselves to the registrar, sign the access book and accept escort where the court requires it. A refusal of access is recorded by both sides and referred for settlement through the liaison channel.

### Q: How is the key register for the Melora courts maintained and audited?

The key register for the Melora courts is maintained by the registrar as a bound record in which every key to a court room, cabinet or passage is listed by number, ward pattern and authorised holder. Issue and return are entered at the time of the transaction, with the signature of the holder and the countersignature of the issuing officer. Keys may not be lent, copied or carried away from the premises without a written order entered in the register. The register is audited against the physical keys at regular intervals and after any loss, forced entry or unexplained absence. A key unaccounted for at audit suspends all issues from the affected set until the discrepancy is resolved.

### Q: What does a partial read signify when weight evidence reaches a hearing?

A partial read signifies that a Weigher has recovered only part of the weight present at a place or in an object, whether because the weight was thin, disturbed, decayed or deliberately reduced before the read took place. When weight evidence reaches a hearing, a partial read must be presented as partial, with the limits of recovery stated on the record. The hearing may rely on the portion recovered for what it shows, but may not treat the missing portion as proof that no further event occurred. Corroborating material is expected where a conclusion depends on the absent portion. A reader who presents a partial read as complete commits a reporting fault under Office practice.

### Q: How do court ushers compile their daily attendance lists in Melora?

Court ushers in Melora compile their daily attendance lists from the registrar assignment sheets, the hearing notices for the day and their own door records taken as parties enter and leave each room. An usher list names the matter, the room, the persons admitted, the time of admission and the time of departure, and it notes any person refused entry and the ground for refusal. Lists are written during the sitting, not reconstructed afterwards, and corrections are made by a ruled line and initials so the original entry remains legible. At the close of business each usher delivers the list to the registrar, who files it with the papers of the matters heard that day.

### Q: Who keeps the observation logs for the annex corridors and reading rooms?

Observation logs for the annex corridors and reading rooms are kept by the usher on corridor duty, under the direction of the senior usher for the sitting. The log records routine passage, deliveries, cleaning, maintenance visits and any entry to a room outside its assigned use. Entries are timed, initialled and written in sequence, and a gap in the sequence must be explained on the face of the log. The night watch continues the same log after the close of business, so that a single record covers the full period. Logs are retained with the court records and may be called for by the registrar, by a hearing, or by an Accord officer with standing in the matter concerned.

### Q: What functions does the courts annex serve apart from the main hearing halls?

The courts annex serves the working functions that the main hearing halls cannot accommodate without interrupting sittings. It contains the reading rooms, the registrar record stores, the ushers room, rooms for witnesses waiting to be called, and secure rooms where evidence is held between hearings. Service passages in the annex allow staff, records and detained persons to move without crossing public circulation in the main building. The annex has its own entrance for deliveries and its own night watch arrangements. Because hearings do not sit in the annex, its rooms can be sealed, searched or closed for inspection without suspending the public business of the courts in the principal halls.

### Q: How is custody of persons awaiting arbitration decisions handled in Melora?

Custody of persons awaiting arbitration decisions in Melora is handled by the court custody officers, who remain responsible for a person from formal handover until release, transfer or production before a hearing. A person in custody is held in the annex custody rooms, is recorded on the custody list with the authority for detention, and is given notice of each movement in advance where security permits. Custody officers record meals, medical attention, visits and any refusal or complaint. A person may not be questioned on the substance of the matter while in court custody. Handover to another authority requires a signed transfer note and a check of the custody record at the point of transfer.

### Q: Why does weight fade from a room where no event has lately occurred?

Weight fades from a room because the affect left by events disperses with time, use and disturbance, even where no new event has lately occurred. Ordinary occupation wears weight down, cleaning and airing thin it, and the passage of persons who leave little of their own can still erode what earlier events deposited. Different materials lose weight at different rates, so a room rarely fades evenly across its surfaces. A sealed and unused room fades more slowly than a room in daily service, which is why sealing preserves evidence. The rate of fade is therefore evidence in itself. A room that has faded faster than its condition explains suggests interference, removal or an event that displaced the earlier deposit.

### Q: What sealing procedure closes a reading room after an incident inside?

The sealing procedure for a reading room after an incident begins with clearance of all persons except the officer in charge and the usher who will witness the seal. Contents are left as found unless removal is required for safety or medical reasons, and any removal is listed before the door is closed. The officer records the state of the room, the position of locks and fastenings, and the names of the last persons known to have entered. Seals are applied to the door and to any service hatch, their numbers are entered in the room book, and the key is placed in registrar custody. A sealed room may be reopened only on written authority, with the seal numbers checked and the reopening witnessed.

### Q: What business did the Twenty Ninth Session of the arbitration courts conclude?

The Twenty Ninth Session of the arbitration courts is recorded in Office files chiefly for the procedural business it concluded rather than for any single award. The session settled the form of notice for cross claims between member courts, confirmed the registrar custody rules for records in transit, and closed a long dispute over the order in which competing hearings take rooms in the annex. It also adopted the standing practice that usher lists form part of the record of a sitting. Files citing the session usually rely on it for procedure, not for substance. A reference to the session in a later matter should identify the ruling relied on, since the session produced several distinct decisions under one heading.

### Q: Which duties fall to Usher Faun Grist during evening sittings in the annex?

Usher Faun Grist holds the evening corridor duty in the annex, a post that combines door control, list keeping and first response to irregularities after the registrar office has closed. During evening sittings Grist admits only persons named on the sitting list, keeps the observation log for the corridor, receives late lodged papers for sealing rather than for filing, and escorts witnesses between waiting rooms and the hearing room in use. Grist also checks that vacated reading rooms are locked, reports any key not returned to the night set, and hands the log and the evening list to the night watch at relief. The post carries no authority to vary a sitting, admit a stranger or open a sealed room.

### Q: What work did Clerk Addis Vale perform for the Melora courts before his death?

Clerk Addis Vale performed record work for the Melora courts, principally the preparation, checking and movement of hearing papers between the registrar office, the reading rooms and the record stores in the annex. His duties included copying lists, verifying that papers lodged for a matter matched the registrar entry, carrying sealed bundles under receipt, and reporting damaged or missing documents. Vale held no authority to decide a matter, admit a person to a room or issue a key on his own signature, though his work regularly took him along the service corridor. Supervisors described the post as one of accuracy and custody. The value of such a clerk to an inquiry lies in the regularity of the trail his routine created.

### Q: How may staff reach the reading rooms through the service corridor without entering public halls?

Staff reach the reading rooms through the service corridor by the annex staff entrance, using a corridor that runs behind the hearing rooms and connects the registrar office, the record stores and the reading room doors. The corridor is a working passage, not a public route, and entry is limited to staff on duty, custody officers with a person in charge, and contractors under escort for stated work. Doors at each end are kept closed and are opened with keys from the staff set recorded in the key register. Because the corridor bypasses the public halls, a person using it may pass unobserved by parties waiting for a sitting. For that reason corridor passage is logged by the usher on duty whenever the log is being kept.

### Q: Where are the permanent records of the arbitration courts stored and indexed?

The permanent records of the arbitration courts are stored in the registrar record stores in the annex, arranged by matter, session and outcome, with closed matters separated from live business. Each file carries a registrar number, a list of contents and a custody history showing every removal and return. Indexes are kept by party name, by matter number and by subject ruling, so that a procedural decision can be traced without opening unrelated files. Records may be consulted in a reading room under assignment and may not be removed from the annex without a transfer order. Damaged records are copied under supervision, and the copy is marked as a copy on its face so that no later reader mistakes its standing.

### Q: How do Weighers record the strength and spread of weight during a formal measurement?

During a formal measurement, Weighers record the strength and spread of weight in a standard form so that another reader can test the result. Strength is noted by the resistance and clarity of the deposit, recorded in the graded terms of Office practice rather than in personal impressions. Spread is noted by mapping where the weight lies, where it thins and where it stops, with boundaries marked against fixed features of the room or object. The record states the conditions of the read, including disturbance, sealing and time elapsed since the event in question. A second reader, where one is available, reads without sight of the first record, and differences between the two records are preserved rather than reconciled in private.

### Q: How is evidence held by a member court transferred into Ninth Office custody?

Evidence held by a member court is transferred into Ninth Office custody by a formal handover recorded at the place of holding. The court officer produces the item against its custody entry, the Office officer checks the seals and the description, and any discrepancy is noted before either side signs. The transfer note states the matter, the authority for transfer, the condition of the item and the route by which it will travel. From signature, the Office bears responsibility for custody, and the court entry is closed with a reference to the transfer note. An item taken without this procedure has a broken chain, and a hearing may refuse to rely on it until the break is explained.

### Q: What purpose does the register of absences serve as an oversight document?

The register of absences serves as an oversight document in which places, objects or records that should carry weight, or should contain an expected entry, are recorded as lacking what inquiry expected to find. Its purpose is to prevent an absence from being treated as a mere gap in paperwork. Each entry states what was expected, who established the expectation, when the absence was confirmed and what explanations were offered. Reviewed over time, the register shows patterns that a single case cannot show, including repeated interference with the same kind of room or record. The register does not prove how an absence was caused. It ensures that the absence itself is preserved as a fact requiring an answer.

### Q: What does the night watch check on each round of the annex after sittings end?

On each round of the annex after sittings end, the night watch checks doors, seals, keys and lights against the closing list prepared by the evening usher. The watch tries every reading room door, confirms that sealed rooms show unbroken seals with the recorded numbers, inspects the service corridor and staff entrance, and notes any sound, smell or disturbance that suggests entry or damage. Rounds are timed and entered in the observation log, and a missed round must be reported rather than concealed by a later entry. The watch has no authority to open a sealed room or admit any person during the night. Irregularities are reported to the registrar at opening and, where an Accord matter is affected, to the liaison officer.
