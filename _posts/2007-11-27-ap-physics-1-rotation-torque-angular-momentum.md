Rotation is the unit where AP Physics 1 students discover whether they learned physics or learned formulas. A spinning wheel, a rolling ball, a door pushed near its hinge, a skater pulling in her arms: none of these scenarios surrenders to the linear equations in their raw form, yet every one of them yields to a method the student already owns, translated into angular language. That translation is the whole subject. Position becomes angle, velocity becomes angular velocity, force becomes torque, mass becomes moment of inertia, and momentum becomes angular momentum, with each new quantity obeying a law whose shape the reader has seen before. Students who build the translation table for themselves find the unit short and almost friendly. Students who memorize a second, disconnected formula sheet find it long, arbitrary, and punishing under time pressure.

![AP Physics 1 rotation study with torque, moment of inertia, and angular momentum worked examples - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-111.webp)

Performance data from graded practice tells a consistent story about where this unit bites. Errors cluster in three places: choosing the wrong moment of inertia for the object or the axis named in the prompt, dropping or inventing signs in torque sums, and treating rolling as pure translation with a decorative spin attached. Each error is a translation failure rather than a knowledge failure, which is encouraging news, because translation can be drilled. This article works the cluster at full depth: rotational kinematics, torque and the rotational form of Newton's second law, moment of inertia, rotational energy, angular momentum and its conservation, and rolling without slipping, with every worked number checked twice and the linear mechanics underneath it routed to the companion deep dive where it belongs.

## Where rotation sits inside the AP Physics 1 exam

The course framework gives torque and rotational motion an approximate published weight in the low-to-mid teens of the multiple-choice section, placing the unit behind energy and dynamics in raw reach but well ahead of gravitation and simple harmonic motion. Both sections draw on it. Multiple-choice items favor single-concept probes: the torque produced by an angled force, the angular momentum of a clay wad striking a disk, the ranking of objects racing down a ramp. Free-response questions use rotation as a second act, attaching a spinning pulley to a familiar Atwood machine, or asking for an experimental design that measures an unknown moment of inertia, or staging a collision in which a rotating platform absorbs a moving object and the solver must decide which conservation law survives the impact.

Scoring weight understates the unit's strategic value for two reasons. First, rotational questions discriminate sharply between students who reason from principles and students who pattern-match, so they separate scores near the top of the distribution where a 5 is decided. Second, rotation rarely appears alone. A rolling object is an energy problem wearing angular clothes. A massive pulley is a dynamics problem with one extra equation. A turntable collision is a momentum problem in which the conserved quantity happens to be angular. The candidate who owns the translation collects points across all of those hybrids, while the candidate who quarantined the unit loses points in three topics at once. The score-a-5 campaign laid out in [the AP Physics 1 score playbook](/2008/01/20/how-to-get-a-5-on-ap-physics-1/) treats rotation exactly this way, as connective tissue rather than as an isolated chapter, and the ordering of this article follows the same logic.

## The InsightCrunch rotational analogy table

One artifact carries the article's central claim, and students should copy it onto the first page of their own notes. Every row pairs a linear quantity with its rotational counterpart and names the bridge relation that converts between the two worlds at a point on the rim. Read down the table and the unit's thesis becomes visible: the rotational course is the linear course, re-applied with disciplined substitutions.

| Linear quantity | Rotational counterpart | Bridge or law |
|---|---|---|
| Position x | Angular position theta | Arc length s = r times theta |
| Velocity v | Angular velocity omega | v = r times omega at the rim |
| Acceleration a | Angular acceleration alpha | Tangential acceleration = r times alpha |
| Mass m | Moment of inertia I | I sums m times r squared over the mass |
| Force F | Torque tau | Tau = r times F times sin of the angle between them |
| Newton's second law, F = ma | Rotational second law | Net torque = I times alpha |
| Momentum p = mv | Angular momentum L | L = I times omega for a rigid rotator |
| Impulse, F times time | Angular impulse | Net torque times time = change in L |
| Kinetic energy, one half mv squared | Rotational kinetic energy | One half I times omega squared |
| Work, F times distance | Rotational work | Tau times angular displacement |
| Power, F times v | Rotational power | Tau times omega |

Fluency with this table changes what studying means. Instead of asking what formula governs a spinning object, the student asks what governed the analogous sliding object, then translates. A torque problem becomes a force problem with lever arms attached. A rotational collision becomes a momentum problem whose bookkeeping runs on I and omega. Even the kinematic equations survive the crossing intact, since the constant-angular-acceleration equations are the linear ones with each symbol swapped for its twin. The sections that follow take the rows one at a time and put each to work.

### Is rotation a brand-new subject, or the linear course wearing new symbols?

Rotation is the linear course wearing new symbols, and treating it as a translation exercise rather than a new subject cuts the learning load roughly in half. Angle replaces position, angular velocity replaces velocity, torque replaces force, moment of inertia replaces mass, and each governing law keeps the algebraic shape it had in the linear unit it mirrors.

Evidence for the claim sits in the exam's own habits. Prompts about spinning objects reuse the scenario furniture of the linear units: ramps, pulleys, collisions, Atwood machines, objects launched and objects dropped. What changes is the bookkeeping variable. A student who asks, of any rotation problem, which linear problem it resembles, inherits a solution strategy for free. A student who files rotation as unrelated material must build every strategy twice, once for sliding and once for spinning, and under exam pressure the second set is always the one that blurs. The remedy is deliberate analogy practice: for each rotational law, state its linear parent aloud, name what changed, and name what stayed fixed. That habit, repeated across the unit, converts the analogy table from a poster into a reflex.

## Angular position, angular velocity, and the radian

Describing rotation begins with a reference line fixed in the rotating object and the angle that line makes with a fixed direction. That angle, theta, is measured in radians, a choice the exam treats as mandatory rather than decorative. One radian is the angle for which the arc length traced on a circle equals the radius, so a full turn measures 2 pi radians and the conversion between revolutions and radians is a simple multiplication by 2 pi. Radians earn their keep through the bridge relation s = r theta, which holds only in radian measure, and through its descendants v = r omega and a = r alpha, which inherit the condition. A solver who feeds degrees into those bridges gets numbers wrong by a factor near 57 without any algebra error to blame.

Angular velocity, omega, is the rate of change of angular position, measured in radians per second, with counterclockwise conventionally positive when the rotation is drawn in the plane of the page. Angular acceleration, alpha, is the rate of change of omega, measured in radians per second squared. Both are properties of the whole rigid object: every point of a rigid rotor shares the same theta, omega, and alpha at every instant, a fact that simplifies rigid-body work enormously and that students underuse. Points on the object differ only in their linear quantities, which scale with distance from the axis. The rim moves fastest, the hub crawls, and the axis itself sits still, all while the angular description stays uniform across the body.

Frequency and period translate without friction into this language. An object completing f revolutions per second carries angular velocity omega = 2 pi f, and its period is T = 2 pi over omega. Exam items occasionally disguise a rotation rate as a tachometer reading in revolutions per minute, and the two conversions, minutes to seconds and revolutions to radians, are both required before any bridge relation applies. Writing the conversion chain explicitly, as a line of unit cancellations, prevents the most mechanical error in the unit.

## The kinematic equations, translated

Constant angular acceleration produces a family of equations that mirrors the linear kinematic family symbol for symbol. Angular velocity grows as omega = omega_0 + alpha t. Angular displacement accumulates as theta = omega_0 t + one half alpha t squared. The time-free relation reads omega squared = omega_0 squared + 2 alpha theta, and the average-velocity form, theta = one half (omega_0 + omega) t, rounds out the set. Each equation carries the same condition as its linear parent: alpha must be constant across the interval, and the moment a prompt describes a torque that changes as the object turns, the family no longer applies directly and energy or angular impulse methods take over.

A worked pass fixes the pattern. A grinding wheel starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 2.0 radians per second squared for 5.0 seconds. Its final angular velocity is omega = alpha t = 10 radians per second. The angle swept is theta = one half alpha t squared = 25 radians, which converts to just under four full revolutions. At a radius of 0.30 meters, the rim's tangential speed at the end of the interval is v = omega r = 3.0 meters per second. Notice what the solution never needed: the wheel's mass. Kinematics describes motion without asking what caused it, in rotation exactly as in translation, and recognizing a pure kinematics prompt saves the solver from hunting for forces the question never supplied.

Reading graphs in this language follows the same translation. On an omega-versus-time graph, the slope at any instant is alpha and the signed area beneath the curve is the angular displacement. On a theta-versus-time graph, the slope is omega. Torque-versus-time graphs appear later in this article, where their area becomes angular impulse. Students fluent in the linear graph grammar from [the forces, kinematics, and energy deep dive](/2008/03/23/ap-physics-1-forces-kinematics-energy/) already own this skill; the rotational version asks only that they swap the axis labels and proceed with confidence.

## Tangential and centripetal acceleration at one point

A point on a spinning object can carry two accelerations at once, pointing in perpendicular directions, and combining them correctly is a recurring exam task. The tangential acceleration, a_t = r alpha, points along the tangent and exists only while the angular speed is changing; it is the rotational descendant of ordinary linear acceleration. The centripetal acceleration, a_c = omega squared times r, equivalently v squared over r, points toward the axis and exists whenever the point is moving in a circle at all, even at perfectly constant angular speed. Because the two components are perpendicular, the total acceleration is their Pythagorean sum, and its direction sits at an angle between the tangent and the inward radial line.

Numbers show the balance. Take a point 0.30 meters from the axis of a rotor spinning at omega = 5.0 radians per second while accelerating at alpha = 4.0 radians per second squared. The tangential component is 4.0 times 0.30 = 1.2 meters per second squared. The centripetal component is 25 times 0.30 = 7.5 meters per second squared. The total magnitude is the square root of 1.44 + 56.25, about 7.6 meters per second squared, aimed mostly inward and tilted only about nine degrees toward the tangent. Two lessons hide in that arithmetic. First, at any respectable spin rate the centripetal term dominates, because omega enters it squared. Second, the moment alpha falls to zero the acceleration becomes purely centripetal, never zero, a point exam items probe by asking about a point on a steadily spinning disk and offering zero as the trap answer.

## Torque: force with leverage

Torque measures how effectively a force twists an object around a chosen axis, and its definition carries the whole idea in one line: tau = r F sin theta, where r is the distance from the axis to the point where the force acts and theta is the angle between the direction of r and the direction of the force. The sine factor is where the intuition lives. A force applied along the line through the axis, aimed straight at it or straight away from it, produces theta of zero or 180 degrees, a sine of zero, and no twist at all. A force applied perpendicular to r produces a sine of one and the maximum twist the same force can deliver. Between those extremes, only the perpendicular component of the force works, and the product r times F_perpendicular is often the fastest route to the value.

The equivalent lever-arm picture reads the same product from the other side. Extend the line of the force backward or forward, drop a perpendicular from the axis to that line, and the length of that perpendicular is the lever arm; torque is force times lever arm. Long lever arms explain the unit's everyday cast of characters: door handles sit far from hinges, wrench handles lengthen to free stuck bolts, and a force that feels modest twists hard when it acts at the end of a long handle. Exam diagrams test both pictures, sometimes hiding the angle inside a drawing of a force pulling a rod at a slant, so fluency in switching between the r F sin theta form and the lever-arm form is worth drilling until the switch is automatic.

Units deserve one sentence of care. Torque is measured in newton meters, dimensionally identical to the joule, yet torque is never quoted in joules, because torque is not energy; the resemblance is a notation trap that multiple-choice options exploit, and the defense is to keep the physical picture, twisting effectiveness, attached to the number.

### Why does a force aimed straight through the axle fail to spin the wheel?

A force aimed straight through the axle fails because torque depends on the perpendicular component of the force, and a force directed along the line from the rim to the center has no perpendicular component at all. Its angle in the formula is zero, the sine vanishes, and the whole push loads the axle instead of turning the wheel.

Door arithmetic makes the point concrete and shows the sine at work. A push of 15 newtons applied 0.60 meters from the hinges, squarely perpendicular to the door's face, delivers tau = 0.60 times 15 = 9.0 newton meters. Swing the push around so it meets the door at 30 degrees from the door's surface, and the angle between the radius and the force becomes 30 degrees; the torque falls to 0.60 times 15 times sin 30 degrees = 4.5 newton meters, exactly half, although the same hand pushed with the same strength. Students who skip the sine factor predict no change and miss the item; students who internalize it can rank angled pushes at a glance. Directions matter as well as magnitudes. By convention a torque that would rotate the object counterclockwise scores positive, clockwise negative, and the convention must be declared once and held for the entire problem, because the sign errors this unit is famous for are born when a solver flips convention mid-sum.

## Choosing the pivot and summing torques

Net torque is a sum over every force acting on the object, each contributing its own signed r F sin theta about the chosen axis. Choosing that axis is a strategic act, and it is the single most transferable skill in the unit. Forces acting at the axis contribute zero torque about it, since their r is zero, so selecting the axis at the point where an unknown force acts erases that force from the torque equation entirely. Unknown hinge forces, unknown normal forces at a contact, unknown tensions anchored at a point: all vanish from the sum when the pivot is placed under them. Skilled solvers choose the pivot to delete the force they least want to compute, write the torque sum, and only afterward, if the problem asks, return with a force balance to recover the deleted unknown.

A seesaw shows the arithmetic at its cleanest. A child of mass 30 kilograms sits 1.5 meters to the left of the pivot, exerting a torque of magnitude 30 times 10 times 1.5 = 450 newton meters under g = 10 meters per second squared, tending to rotate the left side downward. An adult of mass 45 kilograms balances the board by sitting where 45 times 10 times x equals 450, which gives x = 1.0 meter on the other side. The heavier rider sits closer, in inverse proportion to mass, and the pivot's own normal force never entered the calculation because its lever arm about itself is zero. That erasure is not a trick confined to playgrounds. The same move simplifies ladder problems, beam problems, and the lever-and-pivot items the brief for this unit flags as a named question family.

When the axis itself accelerates, care is needed: the rotational second law in its simple form, net torque = I alpha, applies about the center of mass or about a fixed axis. About an arbitrary moving point it acquires extra terms the course does not ask students to handle. Free-response prompts respect that boundary, almost always naming the hinge, the axle, or the center of mass as the natural axis, and the solver's job is to notice which one the prompt quietly selected.

## Static equilibrium: the balance of torques

An object at rest and not rotating satisfies two conditions at once: the vector sum of forces is zero, and the sum of torques about any axis is zero. The second condition is the workhorse, because it converts diagrams of beams, signs, ladders, and booms into single equations. A classic arrangement rewards a full walk-through. A uniform beam 4.0 meters long and 10 kilograms in mass, weighing 100 newtons acting at its midpoint, is hinged to a wall at its left end and held level by a cable running from its far end up to the wall, the cable meeting the beam at 30 degrees. A sign of mass 20 kilograms, weighing 200 newtons, hangs 3.0 meters from the hinge. Taking torques about the hinge deletes the unknown hinge force. The beam's weight twists clockwise with 100 times 2.0 = 200 newton meters. The sign twists clockwise with 200 times 3.0 = 600 newton meters. The cable tension T twists counterclockwise with lever arm 4.0 times sin 30 degrees = 2.0 meters, contributing 2.0 T. Balance gives 2.0 T = 800, so T = 400 newtons. A force balance afterward, if asked, would return the hinge force components; the torque equation alone delivered the headline answer.

Two habits separate clean equilibrium work from messy work. First, draw every force at its true point of application, because torque lives at that point, not at the object's center by default. Weight is the exception that proves the rule: for a uniform object, gravity's torque equals the torque of the full weight applied at the center of mass, a theorem students may use freely and should be able to justify. Second, expect the pivot choice to be graded implicitly. Choosing the hinge rather than the cable end is what turns a two-unknown tangle into one equation, and graders reward the economy even when they never say so.

## Moment of inertia: mass, relocated

Moment of inertia is the rotational measure of how stubbornly an object resists angular acceleration, and its definition explains every behavior the exam tests: I equals the sum over all pieces of the object of each piece's mass times the square of its distance from the axis. Two facts follow immediately, and both are examination favorites. First, distribution matters more than quantity. A kilogram parked at the rim contributes far more inertia than a kilogram hugging the axle, because distance enters squared. Second, the axis is part of the definition. The same object owns a different I for every axis one might choose, so the phrase "the moment of inertia of the rod" is incomplete until the axis is named, and prompts exploit that incompleteness ruthlessly.

The standard shapes recur often enough to memorize cold, always with the axis specified. A hoop or thin ring about its center carries I = MR squared, every gram of it at full radius. A solid disk or cylinder about its center carries I = one half MR squared, its mass spread between center and rim averaging out to the halfway factor. A solid sphere about a diameter carries I = two fifths MR squared. A thin rod about its center carries I = one twelfth ML squared, and about its end, one third ML squared. A point mass on a massless arm carries I = mr squared exactly, the definition itself with a single term. Coefficients in front of MR squared function as shape fingerprints, and this article uses the letter beta for that coefficient wherever rolling races are scored, since the entire outcome of a race is encoded in it.

The parallel axis theorem extends the table to any axis parallel to one through the center of mass: I = I_cm + Md squared, where d is the separation between the axes. A rod of mass 3.0 kilograms and length 1.2 meters illustrates the bookkeeping. About its center, I = one twelfth times 3.0 times 1.44 = 0.36 kilogram meters squared. About its end, the theorem adds M times (L over 2) squared = 3.0 times 0.36 = 1.08, giving 1.44 kilogram meters squared, which matches the tabulated one third ML squared exactly. The agreement is a useful self-check and a reminder that the tabulated values are not independent facts to memorize but consequences of one definition plus one theorem.

Wrong-inertia errors, flagged in the brief for this article as the unit's signature mistake, usually take one of three forms. A solver uses the diameter where the formula wants the radius, quadrupling or quartering the result. A solver reaches for the hoop coefficient when the object is a disk, because both are round, although the disk's interior mass sits closer to the axis. Or a solver carries the center-axis value into a problem whose axis is the rim, the end, or the contact point with the ground. Each error is prevented by the same ritual: before writing I, say aloud what the object is, what shape family it belongs to, and where the axis runs.

## The rotational second law: net torque equals I alpha

With torque and inertia in hand, the dynamical law of the unit arrives as a direct translation: the net torque about an axis equals the moment of inertia about that axis times the angular acceleration. Every condition from the linear law has its counterpart. The torque must be net, summed with signs. The inertia and the acceleration must refer to the same axis as the torques. And the law describes the instant, so a changing torque produces a changing alpha, trackable moment by moment by the same equation.

A compact example calibrates the magnitudes. A solid disk of mass 4.0 kilograms and radius 0.20 meters has I = one half times 4.0 times 0.040 = 0.080 kilogram meters squared about its axle. A cord wrapped at the rim is pulled with 10 newtons, delivering torque 10 times 0.20 = 2.0 newton meters. The angular acceleration is alpha = 2.0 over 0.080 = 25 radians per second squared. Starting from rest, the disk reaches omega = 50 radians per second after 2.0 seconds, at which moment its rim moves at v = omega r = 10 meters per second. Compare the mental picture with the linear parent: a modest force, a smallish inertia, a brisk spin-up. The numbers behave exactly as their linear cousins do, which is the analogy table keeping its promise.

When friction acts at the axle, the net torque shrinks by the friction torque, and alpha shrinks with it; when the driving force is applied at a slant, the sine factor shrinks it again. Exam items stack those two discounts, testing whether the solver applies both before dividing by I. Writing the torque sum symbolically first, then substituting, keeps the discounts visible and prevents the common error of dividing a gross torque by the inertia and calling the quotient the acceleration.

## The massive pulley: where the unit stops being optional

The Atwood machine with a real pulley is the archetype that drags rotation into the dynamics unit, and it appears in free response with metronomic regularity. Give the pulley mass and two gifts of the massless idealization disappear: the tension is no longer the same on both sides, and the pulley's inertia joins the system's resistance to acceleration. Three equations govern the arrangement, one force equation for each hanging mass and one rotational equation for the pulley, tied together by the no-slip condition a = alpha R at the rim.

Set the numbers concretely. Masses of 5.0 and 3.0 kilograms hang from a solid disk pulley of mass 2.0 kilograms and radius 0.10 meters, whose inertia is one half times 2.0 times 0.010 = 0.010 kilogram meters squared. Let a be the acceleration of the heavier mass downward. The heavy side obeys 50 minus T_1 = 5a. The light side obeys T_2 minus 30 = 3a. The pulley obeys (T_1 minus T_2) times R = I times a over R, which simplifies to T_1 minus T_2 = I a over R squared = 1.0 times a. Adding the three statements eliminates both tensions and yields 20 = (5 + 3 + 1) a, so a = 20 over 9, about 2.2 meters per second squared, against 2.5 for the idealized massless pulley. The tensions follow: T_1 = 5 times (10 minus 2.22) = 38.9 newtons, and T_2 = 3 times (10 + 2.22) = 36.7 newtons, differing by 2.2 newtons, exactly the Ia over R squared the pulley equation demanded. Every number cross-checks, which is the signature of a correct solution and a verification habit worth copying onto the exam page.

The structural lesson generalizes. The pulley behaves like an extra mass of I over R squared added to the system, here 1.0 kilogram, and an experimenter who times the fall can work backward to measure an unknown I, a design the experimental free-response family exploits directly. The full force and energy treatment of the underlying machine lives in [the mechanics deep dive on forces and energy](/2008/03/23/ap-physics-1-forces-kinematics-energy/), which handles the massless case at length; this unit adds the rotor and the second tension.

## The falling disk: rotation and translation in one body

A disk hung from a string wrapped around its axle, released to unwind as it falls, fuses translation and rotation in a single object and tests whether the solver can keep two equation sets talking to each other. The center of mass obeys Newton's second law with gravity and tension. The rotation about the center obeys the torque law, with tension at the rim supplying the only torque, since gravity acts at the center and twists nothing. The string's no-slip condition welds the two: a_cm = alpha R.

For a solid disk of mass 2.0 kilograms, the weld gives a clean result. The torque equation reads T times R = one half M R squared times a over R, collapsing to T = one half M a. The force equation reads M g minus T = M a. Substituting, M g = M a plus one half M a, so a = g over 1.5 = 6.7 meters per second squared under g = 10. Tension follows as T = one half times 2.0 times 6.7 = 6.7 newtons, well under the 20-newton weight, which is why the fall is brisk but not free. Swapping the disk for a hoop, whose beta is 1, slows the descent to a = g over 2 = 5.0 meters per second squared. The pattern a = g over (1 + beta) is worth memorizing as a shape, because it recurs in the rolling races below with sin theta attached, and recognizing it on sight converts a three-equation problem into a one-line answer with two lines of justification.

## Rotational kinetic energy and the energy ledger

A spinning object carries kinetic energy in its rotation, computed as one half I omega squared, the exact translation of one half m v squared through the analogy table. Shape decides the haul. Spin a hoop and a disk of identical mass 2.0 kilograms and radius 0.50 meters at the same omega = 4.0 radians per second, and the hoop, with I = 2.0 times 0.25 = 0.50 kilogram meters squared, stores one half times 0.50 times 16 = 4.0 joules. The disk, with I = 0.25, stores 2.0 joules. Same mass, same size, same spin rate, double the energy, because the hoop parks all of its mass at the radius where it counts twice over. That comparison is a favorite multiple-choice seed, and it also previews why rolling objects divide their energy unevenly between moving and spinning.

Rolling merges the two ledgers. A rolling object's total kinetic energy is the translational energy of its center of mass plus the rotational energy about the center: one half M v_cm squared plus one half I_cm omega squared. Under the rolling condition the two terms fuse into a single expression in v_cm, and the split between them is fixed by beta alone. The hoop sends half its energy into spin. The disk sends a third. The solid sphere sends two sevenths. Frictionless sliding sends none. Those fractions, more than any single formula, are what the rolling race items are truly testing.

An elegant cross-check exists through the instantaneous axis. At any moment, a rolling object rotates purely about its contact point with the ground, so its energy can also be written as one half I_contact omega squared, with the parallel axis theorem supplying I_contact = I_cm + MR squared. For the hoop, I_contact = 2MR squared, and one half times 2MR squared times omega squared = MR squared omega squared, matching the two-ledger sum of one half MR squared omega squared for translation plus one half MR squared omega squared for spin. Agreement between methods is the same verification signature met in the pulley problem, and solvers should cultivate the reflex of computing a contested quantity two ways whenever the exam clock allows.

## Rolling without slipping: the condition that runs the unit

Rolling without slipping imposes a strict weld between the translational and rotational motion: v_cm = omega R, with the companions a_cm = alpha R and s_cm = theta R trailing behind it. The condition says the contact point is instantaneously at rest relative to the surface, which is why the friction involved is static rather than kinetic, and why that friction, acting at a point with no displacement relative to the ground, does no work in the ideal model. Mechanical energy survives a rolling descent intact, a fact the energy method exploits and students routinely doubt on first meeting.

Violation of the weld has its own vocabulary, useful because prompts describe it. If v_cm exceeds omega R, the object skids forward, spinning too slowly for its speed, as a car does in the first instant of a hard launch on ice. If omega R exceeds v_cm, the object spins in place excessively, burning rubber without travel. Kinetic friction then acts to restore the weld, opposing the slip direction at the contact patch. Bowling balls released with forward speed and little spin live both phases in sequence, sliding first while friction builds their rotation, then settling into pure rolling once the weld is met, and a well-constructed exam narrative can ask about either phase separately.

### Why does the solid sphere always beat the hoop down the same ramp?

The solid sphere wins because less of its mass rides at large radius, giving it the smallest moment of inertia coefficient of the racers, so gravity's energy buys mostly translation instead of spin. Its center reaches the bottom fastest, the disk follows, and the hoop, which invests half of every joule in rotation, finishes last regardless of mass or radius.

Energy bookkeeping proves the ranking and prices each finisher. Dropping height h, a roller obeys M g h = one half M v squared times (1 + beta), so v = the square root of 2 g h over (1 + beta). Mass and radius cancel entirely, which is why the race is fair across sizes and why any answer that depends on them is a trap. From h = 1.8 meters with g = 10, a frictionless slider reaches the square root of 36 = 6.0 meters per second. The solid sphere, beta = 0.4, reaches the square root of 36 over 1.4, about 5.1 meters per second. The disk, beta = 0.5, reaches the square root of 24, about 4.9. The hoop, beta = 1, reaches the square root of 18, about 4.2. The ordering never inverts, on any ramp, at any size, and stating it with the beta logic attached earns the justification points that bare rankings leave behind.

Dynamics confirms the same order through acceleration. On an incline of angle theta, a roller's center accelerates at a = g sin theta over (1 + beta). At theta = 30 degrees, where g sin theta = 5.0, the sphere accelerates at 3.6 meters per second squared, the disk at 3.3, the hoop at 2.5, and a frictionless block at 5.0. Two methods, one ranking: the cross-check pattern holds again, and an exam candidate who derives the result by energy and sanity-checks it by dynamics has effectively graded the answer before the reader sees it.

## Friction: the quiet enabler of rolling

Rolling down an incline happens only because static friction supplies the torque. Gravity acts at the center and twists nothing; the normal force acts at the contact along a line through the center and twists nothing; friction alone, tangent at the rim, spins the object up as it descends. Its direction surprises students every season. For a ball rolling down a ramp, friction points up the ramp, opposing the slip that would occur at the contact patch if the surface were icy, since the patch tends to slide down the slope as the center accelerates ahead of the spin. The same friction that retards the center's acceleration enables the rotation, and its magnitude is fixed by the torque it must provide, not by the maximum available: f = beta over (1 + beta) times M g sin theta. For the disk at 30 degrees, that is one third of M g sin theta; a 2.0-kilogram disk feels 3.3 newtons of friction, far below any plausible ceiling, which is why ordinary surfaces roll objects without drama.

Invert the scenario and the direction flips with it. A driven wheel on a car accelerates its contact patch backward against the road, so static friction points forward and becomes the force that propels the car. A ball rolling up a ramp keeps its friction pointing up the ramp as well, because the spin still needs decelerating torque in the rotational sense the geometry demands, a subtlety best handled by the torque requirement rather than by slogans about opposing motion. The durable rule is to ask what torque the rotation needs and let friction's direction answer that question. Static friction in rolling is a servant of the weld, taking whatever size and direction the no-slip condition requires, up to the limit mu_s times the normal force. For the disk on the 30-degree ramp, that limit translates to a minimum coefficient near 0.19, a modest demand that explains why rolling problems rarely hinge on whether the surface can supply the grip, although an exam item can ask exactly that.

## Angular momentum: the conserved currency of spin

Angular momentum completes the analogy table's momentum row. For a rigid object spinning about a fixed axis it is L = I omega, measured in kilogram meters squared per second. For a single particle it is L = r p sin theta, the momentum's lever-arm product about the chosen point, which reduces to m v r when the velocity runs perpendicular to the radius, as it does for a particle in circular motion or a projectile of clay arriving tangentially at a disk's rim. The impulse connection translates as well: the net external torque acting over an interval equals the change in angular momentum, tau_net times delta t = delta L, so the area under a torque-versus-time graph is an angular impulse with the same bookkeeping privileges the area under a force-time graph enjoys in the linear unit.

A small calibration fixes the scale. A torque of 12 newton meters sustained for 0.50 seconds delivers an angular impulse of 6.0 kilogram meters squared per second. Handed to a rotor with I = 2.0 kilogram meters squared initially at rest, that impulse buys delta omega = 3.0 radians per second. Notice that the path, whether the torque arrived steadily or in a brief shove, is irrelevant to the outcome; only the accumulated area matters. That indifference to path is what makes impulse methods the fastest route through any problem that specifies a torque acting for a time rather than a torque acting through an angle, where work methods using tau times theta take the lead instead.

## Conservation of angular momentum

When the net external torque about an axis is zero, the angular momentum about that axis does not change, whatever rearrangements the system performs internally. The condition is subtly weaker than the absence of external forces. Gravity and a normal force both act on a skater throughout her spin, yet about her vertical axis they exert no torque, so her L stands fixed while her shape, her I, and therefore her omega all change. The conservation law is about the chosen axis and the torques around it, never about forces in the abstract, and stating that condition precisely is what separates a credited justification from a hopeful one.

### What makes a skater spin faster when she pulls her arms inward?

Pulling her arms inward shrinks her moment of inertia, and with no external torque about her spin axis her angular momentum cannot change, so her angular velocity must rise in inverse proportion to the inertia she shed. Halving her moment of inertia doubles her spin rate, while the extra energy is paid for by muscular work pulling mass inward.

The arithmetic of that trade deserves display. Give the skater I = 6.0 kilogram meters squared with arms extended, spinning at omega = 2.0 radians per second, for L = 12 kilogram meters squared per second. Pulling in to I = 3.0 doubles omega to 4.0 radians per second. Her rotational kinetic energy, however, climbs from one half times 6.0 times 4 = 12 joules to one half times 3.0 times 16 = 24 joules. Momentum conserved, energy doubled, and the difference is no paradox: her muscles did work against the effective outward pull of the moving masses, and that work is exactly the energy gained. Exam items adore the apparent contradiction, offering energy conservation as a distractor in a situation where only angular momentum survives. The same structure returns in collapsing stars, in divers tucking mid-flight, and in a student on a rotating stool hauling dumbbells toward the chest, the laboratory version of the skater that classroom demonstrations favor.

A satellite or a planet supplies the quiet version of the same law. With gravity acting centrally, its torque about the sun vanishes, orbital angular momentum is conserved, and the planet sweeps equal areas in equal times, moving fastest at closest approach. The course treats gravitation at overview depth elsewhere, but the angular momentum reading of Kepler's second law is pure Unit 7 logic and a rewarding place to spend it.

## Rotational collisions: when spin absorbs impact

Collisions involving rotation sort themselves by the same question as linear collisions, sharpened: which quantity survives the instant of impact? The answer, whenever the impact torques from outside are negligible during the brief collision, is angular momentum about the impact axis. Kinetic energy generally does not survive, because sticking, denting, and friction at the contact spend it. The collision family therefore rewards the solver who reaches for L first and audits energy afterward, reporting the loss rather than assuming it away.

The clay-and-disk archetype prices the method. A wad of clay of mass 0.20 kilograms travels at 3.0 meters per second and strikes the rim of a stationary solid disk of mass 2.0 kilograms and radius 0.30 meters, tangentially, and sticks. Before impact the system's angular momentum about the axle is the clay's alone: L = m v R = 0.20 times 3.0 times 0.30 = 0.18 kilogram meters squared per second. After impact the combined inertia is the disk's one half times 2.0 times 0.09 = 0.090 plus the clay's 0.20 times 0.09 = 0.018, totaling 0.108 kilogram meters squared. Conservation gives omega = 0.18 over 0.108, about 1.7 radians per second. Energy tells the epilogue: the clay arrived carrying one half times 0.20 times 9 = 0.90 joules, while the spinning pair afterward stores one half times 0.108 times (1.67) squared, about 0.15 joules. Five sixths of the energy left as heat, sound, and deformation, and the correct solution says so rather than apologizing for it.

A second archetype drops a rotor onto a twin. A disk with I = 0.40 kilogram meters squared spins at 6.0 radians per second; an identical disk, initially at rest, is lowered onto it, and friction between the faces brings the pair to a common spin. Angular momentum conservation across the doubled inertia halves the rate to 3.0 radians per second, and the kinetic energy falls from 7.2 joules to 3.6 joules, half spent to the rubbing faces. The symmetry with the perfectly inelastic linear collision is total, down to the factor of one half in the energy loss when equal inertias merge, and students who narrate that parallel in a justification demonstrate precisely the transfer the unit exists to build. The linear collision machinery underneath these problems is treated at length in the mechanics deep dive already cited, the calculus-based extension of the same conservation law appears in [the AP Physics C Mechanics guide](/2008/03/05/ap-physics-c-mechanics-guide/), and the FRQ-specific scoring of collision justifications is developed in [the physics free-response strategy article](/2008/02/21/ap-physics-frq-strategy-all-exams/).

## Combined translation and rotation: the full problem family

The richest problems in the unit set an object both moving and spinning, then ask questions that require both ledgers at once. A ball rolling off the edge of a table is the cleanest specimen. It leaves the surface rolling without slipping, so its center carries speed v = omega R horizontally while its spin continues at the same omega through the fall, since gravity acts at the center and the air exerts no torque worth modeling. The translational half of the flight is an ordinary projectile problem, inherited whole from the kinematics unit. The rotational half is a single sentence: omega stays constant during the fall, so the angle turned during a flight of duration t is simply theta = omega t. Students who try to accelerate the spin mid-flight, reasoning that the falling ball must spin faster, invent a torque that the free-body diagram does not contain, and the item is engineered to catch exactly that invention.

A second specimen runs the fusion in reverse. A rolling object climbs a ramp and the question asks for the height reached. Energy settles it in one line, provided the ledger includes both terms: the initial one half M v squared times (1 + beta) converts entirely into M g h, giving h = v squared (1 + beta) over 2 g. A hoop launched at 6.0 meters per second climbs h = 36 times 2 over 20 = 3.6 meters, against 1.8 meters for a sliding block at the same launch speed, because the hoop arrives carrying twice the total energy and the ramp collects all of it. Forgetting the rotational half underprices the climb by exactly the spin fraction, an error with a distinctive fingerprint that graders recognize instantly.

The general method for the family is bookkeeping discipline. Maintain two ledgers, one linear and one angular, each with its own conservation or accumulation law, and connect them only through the weld conditions the scenario supplies: v = omega R for rolling contact, a = alpha R at a pulley rim, a shared time interval for a fall. Problems become hard when solvers smuggle quantities across ledgers without a weld. Problems become routine when every crossing is licensed by a named condition written on the page.

## The question types that test this unit

Multiple-choice items in this territory fall into recognizable families, and naming them speeds recognition under time pressure. Torque-ranking items show several forces applied to a rod or a wheel at assorted points and angles and ask for the greatest, least, or zero torque, rewarding the r F sin theta reflex and punishing magnitude-only comparisons. Inertia-ranking items display objects of varied shape and ask which is hardest to spin, or present one object with several candidate axes. Race items line up rolling objects and ask for the finishing order, the speed ratio, or the height climbed. Graph items present omega-time or torque-time plots and ask for displacement, acceleration, or accumulated angular impulse. Collision items stage a moving object meeting a rotor and ask for the final rate, with energy claims trailing as distractors.

Free-response questions build longer arcs from the same bricks. The massive-pulley arc derives an acceleration, then asks how the result changes if the pulley's mass doubles, then invites a graph or a proportionality argument. The experimental arc hands the student a spool, a string, hanging masses, and a timer, and asks for a procedure to measure an unknown moment of inertia, including which quantities to plot so that the slope carries the answer; for the falling-disk arrangement, plotting a against the driving torque or against 1 over (1 + I over MR squared) style rearrangements linearizes the relationship, and the strongest responses state the graph before touching the apparatus. The justification arc presents a claim, often a peer's wrong claim about a race or a collision, and asks the student to agree or disagree with reasoning, the format in which the skater's energy paradox and the hoop's energy split earn their keep. Fluency across these arcs, rather than in any single one, is what the section rewards, and [the complete AP Physics 1 guide](/2008/03/28/ap-physics-1-complete-guide/) maps how this unit's arcs interleave with the rest of the course across a full season of preparation.

## Designing the measurement of an unknown inertia

The experimental design deserves a full pass here, because it fuses every skill in the unit into one procedure. Apparatus: the object mounted on a low-friction axle, a string wrapped at known radius r, the string passing to a hanging mass m whose fall the student times or whose acceleration a sensor records. The physics is the falling disk run backward. The hanging mass obeys mg minus T = ma. The rotor obeys T times r = I times a over r. Eliminating the tension gives a = g over (1 + I over m r squared), and rearranged, I = m r squared times (g over a minus 1). A single careful trial yields a value; a defensible experiment repeats the measurement across several hanging masses, plots g over a minus 1 against m, and extracts I from the slope times r squared, spreading random error across the line and exposing systematic error as a nonzero intercept.

Credible designs name their error sources in the language of the equations. Friction at the axle steals torque, inflating the apparent I, so the design either measures the friction torque separately or chooses masses large enough to dwarf it. The string's own mass and stretch enter at precision levels the course accepts as negligible, but the strong response says so explicitly rather than leaving the assumption silent. Repeating trials and averaging the acceleration addresses reaction-time error when stopwatches replace sensors. Each of these sentences is a scoring point in the design rubric, and each is available to any student who has understood the derivation rather than memorized the apparatus.

## Traps that cost points in this unit

The unit's error catalogue is short, stable, and worth memorizing as a checklist. Wrong shape coefficient heads it: hoop values installed in disk problems, disk values in sphere problems, usually because the solver read the silhouette and skipped the noun. Wrong axis runs second: center-of-mass inertia used for a rim axis, a contact-point axis, or a rod's end, when the parallel axis theorem stood ready. Dropped sine factors run third, angled forces treated as perpendicular because the diagram looked close enough. Sign drift runs fourth, a torque sum that begins counterclockwise-positive and quietly flips when a clockwise term feels large.

Rolling contributes its own wing to the catalogue. Static friction gets pointed downhill by reflex, because friction opposes motion and the center moves downhill, although the torque argument shows it points uphill for a descending roller. Rolling gets modeled with kinetic friction, importing an energy leak the scenario never authorized, and energy solutions then bleed points they had already earned. The weld v = omega R gets applied to objects that are sliding, or withheld from objects that are rolling, each a one-line misread with a full problem's worth of consequences. Finally, energy conservation gets asserted across rotational collisions where only angular momentum survives, the skater paradox returning in collision dress. Every trap in this catalogue is an instance of a single failure: skipping the sentence that states which principle governs and why. Writing that sentence first, before any algebra, disarms the entire list, which is why the practice plan below grades the sentence, not just the answer.

## How this unit connects to the rest of the course

Rotation is deliberately placed late in the course, and the placement is a clue to its nature: it is a capstone that spends the currency of every earlier unit. Kinematics supplies the motion language. Dynamics supplies the force discipline and the free-body diagram. Energy supplies the ledger that prices rolling. Momentum supplies the conservation instinct that angular momentum inherits. The waves and oscillation unit then borrows rotation back, describing simple harmonic motion as the projection of uniform circular motion, so the angular velocity fluency built here becomes the omega that runs the oscillator equations there. Nothing in the unit is terminal; every tool is spent again.

That interconnectivity is the answer to the student deciding how much revision time the unit deserves. Time spent here is not fenced inside Unit 7's exam weight. It raises scores on the pulley items hiding in dynamics papers, the rolling items hiding in energy papers, and the collision items hiding in momentum papers. ## Work and power in rotation

Work translates as torque times angular displacement, W = tau times theta, valid when the torque holds constant across the turn and generalizable by accumulation when it varies. A motor exerting a steady 8.0 newton meters through 50 radians delivers 400 joules to its flywheel, and the energy lands in the ledger as one half I omega squared, closing the loop with the work-energy theorem exactly as F times d closed it for sliding objects. Power translates as P = tau times omega, the rotational twin of F times v. The same motor, spinning its load at 20 radians per second, delivers 8.0 times 20 = 160 watts at that instant. Engine and motor specifications trade on this relation: a quoted power and a quoted torque curve imply each other through the operating omega, and an exam item can ask the student to walk the implication in either direction.

Work also exposes a subtlety with static friction in rolling that deserves its own paragraph. A force whose point of application does not move relative to the ground does no work, so static friction in pure rolling transfers no energy, even while it exerts force and provides torque. The energy accounts balance without it: gravity's work on a descending roller becomes translation plus rotation, split by beta, with friction acting as the broker that enforces the split and charges nothing for the service. Friction in the axle bearings, by contrast, acts at a moving surface in the rotational sense and does negative work, which is why real wheels slow down and exam wheels, idealized, do not. Reading a problem for which friction it means, broker or drain, is a discrimination the question types exercise deliberately.

## The falling rod: torque that changes as it turns

A uniform rod pivoted at one end and released from horizontal is the cleanest demonstration that rotational dynamics, not kinematics, owns this unit. At the instant of release, gravity acts at the rod's center, a distance L over 2 from the pivot, fully perpendicular to the rod, so the torque is M g L over 2. The inertia about the end is one third M L squared. The initial angular acceleration is therefore (M g L over 2) divided by (one third M L squared) = 3 g over 2 L. For a rod 1.2 meters long, alpha = 30 over 2.4 = 12.5 radians per second squared, and the free tip's tangential acceleration is alpha L = 15 meters per second squared, half again greater than g itself, a result that startles students who assumed nothing in free fall's neighborhood could out-accelerate gravity. The tip does not violate anything; it is driven, not dropped, pushed around its arc by the rod's internal stresses, and only the center of mass is bound to the force law in its familiar form, accelerating tangentially at alpha L over 2 = 7.5 meters per second squared, consistent with the net force the pivot and gravity supply.

As the rod falls, the torque wanes, because gravity's lever arm shrinks with the cosine of the angle from horizontal; at vertical, the line of gravity passes through the pivot and the torque vanishes. Alpha is therefore not constant, the kinematic family of equations is disqualified, and the correct tools are the torque law applied instant by instant and the work-energy theorem applied across the swing: the center of mass drops L over 2, gravity pays M g L over 2 in work, and the rod arrives at vertical carrying one half times (one third M L squared) times omega squared, giving omega = the square root of 3 g over L, for this rod the square root of 25 = 5.0 radians per second. Recognizing when constant-acceleration formulas are disqualified is a scoring skill across the whole course, and the falling rod is its rotational showcase, because the disqualification is visible in the geometry rather than hidden in fine print.

## Graphs in the rotational dialect

Three graph types dominate the unit's data questions, and each translates a linear original. The omega-versus-time graph carries alpha in its slope and angular displacement in its accumulated area, so a trapezoidal region under a plotted spin-up can be priced for total angle turned without any kinematic formula. The alpha-versus-time graph carries changes in omega in its area, letting a solver read the spin gained across a surge of acceleration directly off the grid. The torque-versus-time graph carries angular impulse in its area, the change in L, and a triangular pulse makes the arithmetic friendly: a torque rising linearly to a peak of 20 newton meters at 2.0 seconds and falling back to zero at 4.0 seconds encloses one half times 4.0 times 20 = 40 kilogram meters squared per second of angular impulse. Applied to a rotor of I = 5.0 kilogram meters squared starting from rest, that pulse leaves the rotor at omega = 40 over 5.0 = 8.0 radians per second, a complete answer extracted from geometry alone.

Experimental questions reverse the direction, handing the student plotted data and asking what the slope means. Plotting measured torque against measured alpha for a fixed rotor yields a line whose slope is I, a direct-reading instrument for the quantity the experimental design family wants measured. Plotting angular momentum against omega does the same. Plotting omega against time during a friction-dominated spin-down yields alpha from the slope and the friction torque from I alpha, chaining two readings into one conclusion. The graph grammar is uniform: slope is a rate ratio, area is an accumulation, and the units of the axes announce which is which before any physics is consulted.

## A worked approach: the bullet and the pivoted rod

The collision family reaches its full difficulty when the projectile meets an extended object rather than a disk, and a complete walk-through shows the method holding under load. A uniform rod of mass 2.0 kilograms and length 1.0 meter lies on a frictionless horizontal table, pivoted at one end. A bullet of mass 0.050 kilograms, traveling at 40 meters per second perpendicular to the rod, strikes the free end and embeds itself. The pivot can exert force during the impact, so linear momentum is not conserved; the pivot's force, however, acts at the axis and exerts no torque about it, so angular momentum about the pivot is conserved, and that choice of bookkeeping point is the entire battle.

Before impact, the angular momentum about the pivot is the bullet's alone: L = m v L_rod = 0.050 times 40 times 1.0 = 2.0 kilogram meters squared per second. After impact, rod and bullet rotate together with combined inertia one third M L squared plus m L squared = 0.667 plus 0.050 = 0.717 kilogram meters squared. Conservation returns omega = 2.0 over 0.717, about 2.8 radians per second. The energy audit reports the usual collision story in extreme form: the bullet arrived with 40 joules and the rotating wreck stores about 2.8 joules, the remainder spent deforming the rod's end and heating the bullet. A solver who attempted energy conservation here would overshoot omega by a factor near four; a solver who attempted linear momentum conservation would land somewhere else entirely; only the angular ledger about the pivot survives, and the problem is a machine for testing whether the solver can find it. The transferable habit is to interrogate each conservation law about its conditions, naming the external impulse or torque that each candidate law must survive, before adopting any of them.

## A worked approach: balancing the meter stick

The lever family deserves one full treatment at classroom scale, since the brief names lever and pivot problems as a tested family and they reward the pivot-choice strategy directly. A uniform meter stick of mass 0.20 kilograms rests pivoted at its 30-centimeter mark. A weight of 3.0 newtons hangs at the 10-centimeter mark. The task: place a weight of 5.0 newtons so the stick balances. The stick's own weight, 2.0 newtons, acts at its center, the 50-centimeter mark, which lies 0.20 meters to the right of the pivot, producing a torque of 2.0 times 0.20 = 0.40 newton meters tending to drop the right side. The hanging weight sits 0.20 meters to the left of the pivot, producing 3.0 times 0.20 = 0.60 newton meters tending to drop the left. The left side leads by 0.20 newton meters, so the new weight must act on the right with 5.0 times d = 0.20, giving d = 0.040 meters, four centimeters right of the pivot, at the 34-centimeter mark. Check the full sum: left torques 0.60; right torques 0.40 plus 5.0 times 0.04 = 0.20, totaling 0.60. Balance confirmed, and the pivot's force never appeared, because the pivot was chosen at itself.

Variations on this scaffold generate the whole family. Move the pivot and the stick's weight changes sides, a subtlety that catches solvers who assume the center always helps the same direction. Add a second hanging weight and the algebra stays linear. Ask for the pivot force afterward and the force balance, run once at the end with all weights known, returns it immediately. The scaffold's lesson is procedural: torques first, about the pivot, with every force at its true point of application, and forces second if at all.

## Signs, directions, and the right-hand rule

Angular quantities are vectors aimed along the axis of rotation, assigned by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of rotation and the thumb points along omega, with alpha and L aligned or opposed according to whether the spin is speeding up or slowing down in that sense. Physics 1 works mostly in a plane, where the full vector apparatus collapses into the sign convention already adopted in this article, counterclockwise positive and clockwise negative, but knowing the rule explains two things the convention alone cannot. It explains why angular momentum about an axis can be conserved while the object's orientation changes in complicated ways, and it explains the otherwise mysterious fact that a torque perpendicular to a spin axis changes the spin's direction rather than its rate, the seed of gyroscope behavior that later courses harvest. For exam purposes, the operational content is modest but real: assign signs by the declared convention, let negative torques decelerate positive spins, and never let the magnitude of a term talk the solver into flipping its sign mid-problem.

Sign discipline interacts with the rolling weld in a way worth stating once. On an incline, taking down-the-slope as positive for the center's motion pairs with the sense of rotation that rolls the object down the slope as positive for omega and alpha. Mismatched pairing produces a spurious minus sign in a = alpha R and a family of wrong answers that all look internally tidy. The defense is to draw the rolling direction first, mark the rotation sense on the drawing, and only then write equations, in that order, every time.

## The turntable walk: conservation with moving parts

A person standing on a rotating platform turns conservation of angular momentum into a two-body bookkeeping exercise and supplies the unit's most instructive laboratory scenario. Model the platform as a disk of mass 100 kilograms and radius 2.0 meters, inertia one half times 100 times 4.0 = 200 kilogram meters squared, turning at omega = 0.50 radians per second. A rider of mass 60 kilograms stands at the rim, contributing I = 60 times 4.0 = 240 kilogram meters squared, for a system total of 440 and an angular momentum of L = 440 times 0.50 = 220 kilogram meters squared per second. The bearing is frictionless, no external torque acts about the vertical axis, and the rider now walks to the center. The rider's contribution collapses toward zero, the total inertia falls to 200, and conservation sets the new rate at omega = 220 over 200 = 1.10 radians per second, more than double the starting spin.

Walking outward reverses the trade, spinning the platform down, and stopping halfway, at radius 1.0 meter where the rider contributes 60 kilogram meters squared, lands the system at omega = 220 over 260 = 0.85 radians per second. The kinetic energy ledger again refuses to balance on its own: the walk inward raises the stored energy from 55 joules to 121 joules, paid by the rider's legs, while the walk outward lets the rotation pay energy back into the rider's muscles as they brake the inward pull. A variant beloved of exam writers has the rider catch or drop a heavy object at the rim, converting the problem into a rotational collision with a moving participant. Every variant reduces to the same two steps: inventory the inertia before, inventory it after, divide L by the second. The scenario also quietly tests whether students include the platform's own inertia, the term most often dropped and the one that caps the spin change at a finite value.

## Gravity, the center of mass, and a torque that vanishes

One theorem quietly powers half the shortcuts in the unit: about the center of mass, the torque of gravity is always zero, because gravity's resultant acts at the center of mass itself and a force acting at the reference point has no lever arm. That is why a projectile's spin, once imparted, persists unchanged through its flight, why the falling disk's rotation equation involved tension alone, and why the rolling object's rotation about its center is driven by friction alone on any slope. The theorem also licenses the cleanest decomposition in the subject: any rigid motion separates into translation of the center of mass, governed by the net force, plus rotation about the center of mass, governed by the net torque about that point, with the two equations independently true. Problems that look entangled, a rod struck off-center in mid-air, a rolling ball colliding with a curb, decompose on contact with that sentence.

A companion result governs equilibrium: when forces balance to zero, the net torque is the same about every axis, so in a statics problem the pivot truly is the solver's free choice, with no hidden penalty for choosing boldly. In dynamics the freedom narrows, since net torque equals I alpha holds about the center of mass or a fixed axis, and about other points it must be corrected. Marking which regime a problem occupies, statics or dynamics, before choosing the pivot is the final form of the pivot skill, and it is the distinction the falling rod and the beam problem were placed side by side to teach.

Sequencing matters more than volume in this unit, because the skills stack. Move next to pure torque and inertia drills: angled forces on rods, torques about eccentric axes, inertia computations for composite objects built from the standard shapes, and the parallel axis shifts that recur in every problem family. Only then take up the hybrid machines, the massive pulley and the falling disk, solving each twice until the three-equation structure becomes a single gesture.

Collisions close the sequence, with the two-step inventory method drilled until the before-and-after inertia accounting is automatic. Across all blocks, the highest-yield habit is the governing-principle sentence: one line at the top of each solution naming the law in play and the condition that licenses it, graded as strictly as the final number. ## Reading torque from a diagram: three forces on one rod

Diagram fluency deserves a dedicated rehearsal, because most torque errors are committed in the reading, not the arithmetic. Picture a horizontal rod of length 1.0 meter, pivoted at its left end, with three forces drawn on it. Force A, 10 newtons, pulls straight up at the free end. Force B, 20 newtons, acts at the midpoint, angled 30 degrees above the rod's direction, pulling away from the pivot. Force C, 6 newtons, pulls straight down at a point 0.75 meters from the pivot. Scoring each force separately, with counterclockwise positive: A contributes 1.0 times 10 times sin 90 degrees = 10 newton meters. B contributes only through its perpendicular component, 0.50 times 20 times sin 30 degrees = 5.0 newton meters; the other half of B runs along the rod and merely strains the pivot. C contributes minus 0.75 times 6.0 = minus 4.5 newton meters. The sum is 10 plus 5.0 minus 4.5 = 10.5 newton meters counterclockwise, and against a rod whose inertia about the end is 2.0 kilogram meters squared, the rod angularly accelerates at 5.25 radians per second squared.

The reading habits embedded in that pass generalize to every torque diagram on the exam. Decompose each force at its point of application, never at the center of the object. Score forces one at a time in a fixed order, pivot outward, writing each sign as it is earned. Treat any force whose line passes through the pivot as a zero to be noted, not skipped, because noting it proves the omission was deliberate. And when two forces oppose, resist the urge to net them early; the torque sum wants them separately, since their lever arms differ even when their magnitudes match.

## Building inertia from pieces: composites and holes

Real objects on the exam are assembled, and their inertias are computed by addition and subtraction of standard pieces. The rules are bookkeeping: the inertia of a composite about an axis is the sum of the inertias of its parts about that same axis, with the parallel axis theorem relocating any piece whose tabulated axis sits elsewhere. A point mass bolted to a disk adds its mr squared at its actual distance. A rod welded across a hoop adds its own shifted value. Each piece is priced separately and the total is their sum, which is precisely the sum in the definition of I, evaluated in chunks instead of point by point.

Subtraction handles holes, through the negative-mass device. Consider a uniform plate, radius 0.40 meters and mass 8.0 kilograms, with a circular hole of radius 0.20 meters cut so the hole's center sits 0.20 meters from the plate's center. The full plate's inertia about its center is one half times 8.0 times 0.16 = 0.64 kilogram meters squared. The missing disk carries a quarter of the area and so 2.0 kilograms of the mass; about its own center its inertia is one half times 2.0 times 0.040 = 0.040, and shifted to the plate's center by the parallel axis theorem it becomes 0.040 plus 2.0 times 0.040 = 0.12. The pierced plate's inertia is therefore 0.64 minus 0.12 = 0.52 kilogram meters squared. The method is general, and its exam value is that it converts an intimidating shape into two tabulated ones, at the price of one subtraction performed with the axes aligned. Misaligning the axes before subtracting is the standard way this problem type is lost, so the shift of the hole's value to the common axis should be written as its own visible step.

## Angular momentum without a circle: the straight-line particle

Angular momentum belongs to any moving mass, not only to things going in circles, and grasping that widens the conservation law's reach considerably. A puck of mass 0.50 kilograms slides in a straight line at 4.0 meters per second across frictionless ice, passing a fixed post with a closest approach of 0.30 meters. About that post, the puck's angular momentum is m v times the perpendicular distance from the post to the line of motion: 0.50 times 4.0 times 0.30 = 0.60 kilogram meters squared per second. The value does not change as the puck travels, because r sin theta, the perpendicular distance from the post to the velocity's line, is the same at every point of the path, even though r and theta individually vary continuously. The impact parameter, as that perpendicular distance is called, is the quantity the formula truly uses, and the circular case m v r is merely the special case where the impact parameter equals the radius everywhere.

This is the machinery that makes collision analysis work before contact occurs. A projectile approaching a pivoted rod carries angular momentum about the pivot from the moment the problem begins, priced by its impact parameter, and the collision transfers that stock into the rod's rotation. It also explains, without any new law, why a planet speeds up near the sun and why a comet's angular momentum about the sun stays fixed along the whole ellipse: the relevant lever arm is the perpendicular distance, and gravity, acting along the line to the sun, never twists it. Students who meet the impact-parameter idea here stop reserving angular momentum for wheels, which is the single largest conceptual upgrade the unit offers.

## The ladder: equilibrium with friction at one end

The ladder against a wall is the statics family's final examination, combining torque balance, force balance, and the friction ceiling in one diagram. Set a ladder 5.0 meters long, mass 12 kilograms, against a smooth wall, its base 3.0 meters from the wall so the geometry forms a 3-4-5 triangle: the top touches at height 4.0 meters, sine 0.80, cosine 0.60. A climber of mass 60 kilograms stands 4.0 meters up the ladder. The smooth wall can only push horizontally, with force N_w at the top. The floor supplies an upward normal force and a static friction force at the base. Force balance fixes the normal at once: it carries both weights, 120 plus 600 = 720 newtons. Torque balance about the base, chosen to erase both floor forces, prices the wall's push. The ladder's weight, 120 newtons, acts at its center, 2.5 meters along its length, at horizontal distance 2.5 times 0.60 = 1.5 meters from the base, contributing 180 newton meters of clockwise torque. The climber's 600 newtons act at horizontal distance 4.0 times 0.60 = 2.4 meters, contributing 1,440. The wall's push acts at height 4.0 meters, counterclockwise, so N_w times 4.0 = 1,620, and N_w = 405 newtons. Horizontal force balance then demands 405 newtons of static friction at the base, requiring a coefficient of at least 405 over 720, about 0.56, or the ladder slips.

Every element of the unit appears in that one computation: a pivot chosen to delete unknowns, weights acting at their true points, lever arms read off a triangle, a friction force whose required size is computed before its ceiling is consulted. The climactic comparison, required friction against available friction, is the form's signature final step, and prompts that ask whether the ladder holds are asking for the ratio, not for either number alone.

## One race by a third route: torque about the contact point

The rolling acceleration derived twice already, by energy-flavored reasoning and by the force-plus-torque pair, falls out of a third method that many strong students prefer for its economy: take torques about the instantaneous contact point. About that point, friction and the normal force act at the reference itself and vanish from the sum, and gravity, acting at the center of mass, is the only force with leverage. Its lever arm about the contact, for a slope of angle theta, is R sin theta measured perpendicular to the weight's line, so the torque is M g R sin theta. The inertia about the contact comes from the parallel axis theorem, I_contact = I_cm + M R squared = M R squared times (1 + beta). Dividing gives alpha = g sin theta over (R times (1 + beta)), and the weld a_cm = alpha R returns a_cm = g sin theta over (1 + beta), the established result, purchased with one equation instead of two.

Numbers anchor the route. A disk of mass 2.0 kilograms and radius 0.30 meters on a 30-degree slope feels a gravitational torque about its contact patch of 2.0 times 10 times 0.30 times 0.50 = 3.0 newton meters. Its contact-point inertia is 0.090 plus 0.18 = 0.27 kilogram meters squared. Alpha is 3.0 over 0.27 = 11.1 radians per second squared, and a_cm = 11.1 times 0.30 = 3.3 meters per second squared, matching the earlier dynamics computation to the digit. Three routes, one answer, each route exposing a different assumption: energy hides friction entirely, the two-equation method displays friction's size, and the contact-point method displays why friction's size never needed computing. Exam problems occasionally force a route by what they give and what they ask, and owning all three is the difference between choosing a method and being chosen by it.

## Coupled rotors: belts, gears, and shared rims

Machines join rotors at their rims, and the joint imposes a weld as strict as rolling contact: the rim speeds match. Two pulleys joined by a belt, radii 0.10 and 0.30 meters, share the belt's speed at both rims, so when the small pulley turns at 12 radians per second its rim moves at 1.2 meters per second and the large pulley turns at 1.2 over 0.30 = 4.0 radians per second. Angular velocities trade in inverse proportion to radii, exactly as the seesaw's distances traded against masses, and a solver who sees the family resemblance files both under one idea: at a shared rim, v is common property and omega is private. Gears enforce the same weld with teeth, adding the possibility of reversed direction at each mesh, and gear teeth counts substitute for radii in the proportion.

Torque transmission across the joint follows from power conservation in the ideal machine: tau times omega entering equals tau times omega leaving, so the pulley that slows the rotation multiplies the torque by the same factor, here threefold. That exchange, speed for torque, is the rotational restatement of the lever's bargain and the reason bicycle gearing and automobile transmissions exist. The exam stays at the level of the weld and the ratio, asking for the second rotor's rate, the belt's speed, or the period of a marked point on the driven wheel, all of which collapse to the single rim-speed equality applied with care about which radius belongs to which wheel.

## The spool puzzle: pulling a spool toward you, or away

A thread wound around the inner hub of a spool, pulled outward at an angle, produces the unit's most instructive parlor problem, because the spool can roll toward the puller or away from the puller depending on the angle, and the deciding analysis is pure torque about the contact point. Draw the line of the pulling force and extend it; the torque about the contact patch depends on which side of the patch the line passes. When the string leaves the bottom of the hub and the pull angles steeply upward, the line of force passes on the side that twists the spool toward the puller, and the spool rolls in, winding the string further. When the pull runs shallow, nearly horizontal, the line passes on the other side, the twist reverses, and the spool rolls away, unwinding. Between the two regimes sits a critical angle at which the line of force passes exactly through the contact point, the torque vanishes, and the spool, if it moves at all, slides without rolling.

The puzzle's value is diagnostic rather than recreational. It cannot be solved by force intuition, since the horizontal force points the same way in both regimes, and it cannot be solved by memorizing an outcome, since the outcome flips with geometry. It can only be solved by the lever-arm discipline this article has drilled: locate the contact point, draw the force's line, measure the perpendicular distance, and let the sign of the torque announce the direction. A student who can narrate the spool has understood torque; a student who can only compute tau = r F sin theta has memorized it, and the exam's diagram items are built to tell those two students apart.

## Three error autopsies

Dissecting flawed solutions is the fastest calibration a review can buy, and the unit's three most instructive corpses repay examination. The first autopsy concerns a disk problem solved with the hoop's inertia. The student's energy split came out half and half, the race ranking tied the disk with the hoop, and the answer contradicted the printed shapes. Cause of death: the silhouette was read and the noun skipped; the object was called a disk in the stem and a ring in the solution. The preventive habit is the spoken identification ritual, object, shape family, axis, before any coefficient is written.

The second autopsy concerns a clay collision solved with energy conservation. The student set the clay's incoming kinetic energy equal to the final rotational energy, computed an omega near four times the true value, and never noticed the surplus. Cause of death: a conservation law adopted without its conditions; the impact was a sticking collision, the canonical energy spender, and angular momentum about the axle was the only ledger the impact respected. The preventive habit is the conditions interrogation: for each candidate law, name the external agent that could break it, and check whether the scenario contains that agent.

The third autopsy concerns a rolling solution in which static friction was drawn down the slope and kinetic friction's energy loss was subtracted at the end. The student reasoned that friction opposes motion and that moving surfaces rub, both true in other provinces and both false at a rolling contact patch, which is instantaneously at rest and served by static friction pointing where the torque ledger requires. Cause of death: slogans substituted for the torque requirement. The preventive habit is to derive friction's direction from the rotation's needs, asking what torque the spin requires and which way friction must point to supply it, a question with a unique answer in every rolling scenario the exam presents.

## Anatomy of a long question: the table, the pulley, and the hanging block

One archetype gathers the whole unit into a single stem, and walking it end to end shows how the parts of a long free-response question talk to each other. A block of mass 2.0 kilograms rests on a frictionless horizontal table, tied by a string that passes over a pulley at the table's edge to a hanging block of mass 3.0 kilograms. The pulley is a solid disk of mass 4.0 kilograms and radius 0.20 meters, free to spin on a frictionless axle. The system is released from rest.

The first part asks for the acceleration, and the solution assembles three equations. The table block obeys T_1 = 2a, since tension is its only horizontal force. The hanging block obeys 30 minus T_2 = 3a. The pulley obeys (T_2 minus T_1) times R = I alpha, and with I = one half times 4.0 times 0.040 = 0.080 kilogram meters squared and alpha = a over R, the pulley's statement becomes T_2 minus T_1 = I a over R squared = 2.0 a. Adding the three eliminates both tensions: 30 = (2 + 3 + 2) a, so a = 30 over 7, about 4.3 meters per second squared. The tensions follow as T_1 = 8.6 newtons and T_2 = 17.1 newtons, and their difference, 8.6 newtons, matches 2.0 a exactly, the pulley's toll for spinning up. Had the pulley been massless, the acceleration would have been 30 over 5 = 6.0 meters per second squared with a single tension of 12 newtons throughout; the massive pulley slows the system and splits the tension, and stating that comparison is the kind of physical commentary the second part of such questions usually requests.

A later part doubles the pulley's mass and asks for the new acceleration. Nothing in the method changes: the pulley's effective contribution I over R squared doubles from 2.0 to 4.0 kilograms, the denominator becomes 2 + 3 + 4 = 9, and a = 30 over 9 = 3.3 meters per second squared. The pattern, total driving weight divided by total effective mass including the pulley's I over R squared, is the general machine behind every problem in this family, and deriving it once in symbols, a = m_hang g over (m_1 + m_2 + I over R squared), converts the whole family into arithmetic. The final part asks for an energy audit after the hanging block has fallen 1.0 meter. Kinematics gives v squared = 2 a d = 8.57, so v is about 2.93 meters per second and omega = v over R is about 14.6 radians per second. Translational energy totals one half times 5.0 times 8.57 = 21.4 joules. Rotational energy is one half times 0.080 times omega squared = 8.6 joules. The sum, 30.0 joules, equals the gravitational payment m g h = 3.0 times 10 times 1.0 exactly. When a multi-part answer closes its own books like that, the reader is persuaded; when it does not, the discrepancy is the student's signal to recheck before the reader has to.

## A decision procedure: which rotational tool, when

Method selection in this unit reduces to matching the problem's given and requested quantities against four tools, and the matching can be rehearsed as a procedure. When a problem hands over forces and lever arms and asks about an instant, the torque law net tau = I alpha governs, possibly with force balances riding alongside; the massive pulley and the falling rod at release live here. When a problem describes a change across an angle or a height, work and energy govern, because tau times theta and the energy split by beta accumulate exactly what instantaneous analysis cannot reach; the races, the climbs, and the rod's arrival speed live here. When a problem describes a change across a time interval, angular impulse governs, reading delta L off the torque's accumulated area. When a problem contains an impact, a catch, a release of stored configuration, or any sudden rearrangement with negligible external torque about some axis, angular momentum conservation governs, and the solver's first task is to find the axis about which external torque vanishes, as the pivoted rod rewarded and the skater assumed.

The procedure's value is diagnostic speed. A prompt that mentions how long a torque acts is confessing its tool. A prompt that gives a height is confessing its tool. A prompt that stages a collision is confessing its tool and simultaneously warning against the energy ledger. Writing the confession at the top of the solution, in the governing-principle sentence the practice plan demands, commits the solver before algebra begins and prevents the most expensive failure mode in the unit, which is not weak technique but strong technique applied by the wrong tool for four minutes.

## The bicycle wheel demonstration: conservation you can sit on

The classroom's signature demonstration places a student on a freely rotating stool, holding a bicycle wheel spinning about a vertical axle, and its analysis is pure conservation with a sign flip. Give the wheel I = 0.20 kilogram meters squared spun at omega = 10 radians per second, for an angular momentum of 2.0 units directed upward. Student, stool, and wheel-as-luggage together carry I = 5.0 kilogram meters squared about the vertical axis, initially at rest rotationally apart from the wheel's internal spin, so the system's total vertical angular momentum is the wheel's 2.0. The student now flips the wheel over. The wheel's angular momentum becomes minus 2.0, and conservation therefore assigns the student and stool plus 4.0 units, setting them rotating at omega = 4.0 over 5.0 = 0.80 radians per second in the wheel's original sense. No motor, no push against the floor, and yet rotation appears, conjured entirely from the flip's internal rearrangement.

The demonstration repays the same interrogation as the skater. Energy is not conserved; the student's arms worked to flip a gyroscopically stubborn wheel. Torques from the student's hands on the wheel are internal to the system, matched by opposite torques of the wheel on the student, which is precisely how the stool's share changed while the total held. Prompts built on this scenario ask for the direction of the resulting spin as often as its size, and the direction falls out of the sign bookkeeping alone: the person must end up carrying twice the wheel's original stock in the original direction, because the wheel ends carrying one stock in the opposite direction. Narrating that sentence, rather than reporting the number baldly, is what a complete justification looks like in this family.

## Ranking at speed: the pattern reflexes

Multiple-choice rotation items are usually ranking items in disguise, and ranking items reward reflexes that bypass full computation. Three reflexes cover most of the territory. The beta reflex settles anything about rolling: read each object's shape coefficient, and the finishing order, the energy split, the acceleration, and the climb height all rank by beta alone, smaller winning speed and larger winning height, with mass and radius dismissed as stage dressing. The lever reflex settles torque comparisons: before comparing any two torques, convert each to its perpendicular component times its distance, and remember that a modest force at a long arm beats a heroic force at a short one; most wrong options in torque rankings pair the largest force with a poor angle or a short arm. The inventory reflex settles collisions: before computing anything, list the system's inertia in the initial and final configurations, and the ratio of final to initial omega is the inverse ratio of the inventories, so the direction and rough size of the change are known before a single angular momentum is evaluated.

Each reflex is a compressed derivation, not a guess, and the strongest students can decompress any of them on demand when a free-response part asks for the reasoning. That two-level command, reflex for the multiple-choice clock and derivation for the free-response page, is the practical meaning of mastery in this unit, and it is why the review plan sequences drills before mixed sets: reflexes built on derivations survive pressure, while reflexes memorized as slogans collapse the first time a problem varies the furniture, swapping a shell for a sphere or moving the axis from center to rim.

One more pattern deserves naming because it inverts a common instinct. In rotational problems, doubling a radius usually matters fourfold, because radius enters inertia squared and rim speed linearly and torque linearly all at once, and the effects compound along different paths in different problems. When an item asks how a quantity responds to a doubled radius, the safe procedure is to write the governing relation symbolically and count the radius factors, rather than trusting a sense of proportion trained on linear problems, where doubling anything rarely squares anything.

## The translation circuit: a self-test that covers the table

A compact self-test circuit, run weekly, keeps the analogy table honest, and each station takes minutes. Station one, kinematics: state an omega from a graph's area and an alpha from its slope, then convert a tachometer reading in revolutions per minute through radians per second to a rim speed. Station two, torque: take one diagram and score every force's torque about two different pivots, confirming that the net torque agrees in direction and that the statics, if the object is balanced, vanish about both. Station three, inertia: price one composite object by addition and one pierced object by subtraction, stating object, shape, and axis aloud before each coefficient. Station four, machines: solve the massive pulley symbolically, then read off how the acceleration moves when the pulley's mass, the hanging mass, and the radius each double in turn.

Station five, rolling: run the race ranking from beta, then compute one object's descent time by energy and its friction magnitude by dynamics, and state friction's direction with its one-sentence torque justification. Station six, collisions: take one impact and produce three numbers, the final omega from angular momentum, the final kinetic energy, and the energy lost, followed by the sentence naming why energy was not the conserved ledger. A student who can run all six stations without notes owns the unit in the only sense the exam recognizes, and a student who stalls at a station has located the next hour of study with unusual precision. Circuits like this, applied unit by unit across the course, are the working core of the preparation rhythm described in [the full AP Physics 1 guide](/2008/03/28/ap-physics-1-complete-guide/), where this unit's circuit joins those of the other six.

## The swinging sign: torque as a restoring force

Hang a uniform rod or a shop sign from a pivot at its top edge and displace it slightly, and rotation turns into oscillation, the unit shaking hands with the oscillations material that follows it in the course. Displaced by a small angle theta from hanging vertical, the object's weight acts at its center, offset horizontally from the pivot by (L over 2) sin theta, and the resulting torque, M g (L over 2) sin theta, points back toward vertical: a restoring torque, proportional to the displacement for small angles where sin theta and theta nearly agree. Proportional restoring effects produce simple harmonic motion in any dialect, linear or angular, so the sign swings with the steady period the proportionality fixes. For a rod of length 1.2 meters pivoted at its end, the torque law gives alpha = (3 g over 2 L) sin theta with the restoring sign, an oscillation whose angular frequency squared is 3 g over 2 L = 12.5, whose frequency is about 3.5 radians per second, and whose period is about 1.8 seconds.

The derivation matters more than the period. It rehearses the full torque method on a moving axis problem: choose the pivot, locate the weight at the center, read the lever arm off the displaced geometry, divide by the inertia about that same pivot, and interpret the resulting differential statement. It also explains why the small-angle condition follows the student from the pendulum unit into this one, since the proportionality, and with it the clean oscillation, survives only while sin theta can be replaced by theta. A shop sign in a strong gust, displaced far, swings with a period that drifts with amplitude, and the reason is sitting in this paragraph: the restoring torque grew more slowly than the displacement, because sine saturates where a straight proportion would keep climbing.

## Rotation in mid-air: the center of mass minds its own business

The decomposition theorem earns a final display with objects in flight. A dumbbell, two heavy balls joined by a light rod, slides across frictionless ice while spinning, struck earlier by an off-center blow. After the blow, no horizontal force acts, and gravity and the ice's normal force cancel vertically, so the center of mass travels in a straight line at constant velocity, indifferent to the spin. About the center of mass, no force exerts torque either: gravity acts at the center, the normal force balances it along the same line, and so the spin rate holds constant through the whole glide. Translation and rotation proceed as strangers sharing a vehicle, each obeying its own conservation law, and the trajectory of either ball, a cycloid-like weave around the center's straight path, is the superposition of the two simple motions.

Toss the same dumbbell into the air and the separation survives intact: the center of mass draws the parabola the projectile unit predicts, while the dumbbell rotates about that moving center at the constant rate the toss imparted, because gravity, acting at the center of mass, twists nothing. Astronauts exploit the fact when a tossed tool tumbles at a stately, unchanging rate beside a spacecraft whose center follows its orbit. Exam scenarios use humbler furniture, a baton, a wrench, a two-mass rotor on a cart, but the analysis is identical, and the governing sentence is always the decomposition: net force steers the center, net torque about the center steers the spin, and neither equation borrows trouble from the other. Students who write that sentence early in a solution rarely tangle the two motions, and students who skip it often try to make the whole object's rotation respond to forces that only the center can feel.

## The spin-down: friction at the axle, timed and priced

Not every rotor in the unit is driven; some are abandoned to their bearings, and the spin-down problem exercises the torque law and the work-energy theorem on the same object in succession. A flywheel with I = 2.0 kilogram meters squared spins at 30 radians per second when its motor cuts out, and bearing friction exerts a steady opposing torque of 0.40 newton meters. The torque law gives the deceleration directly: alpha = 0.40 over 2.0 = 0.20 radians per second squared, constant because the friction torque is modeled as constant, so the kinematic family is licensed and the stopping time is omega over alpha = 150 seconds. The angle turned while stopping is the average omega, 15 radians per second, times 150 seconds = 2,250 radians, several hundred revolutions of coasting.

Now the audit that makes the problem instructive. The wheel's initial rotational energy is one half times 2.0 times 900 = 900 joules. Friction's work across the spin-down is tau times theta = 0.40 times 2,250 = 900 joules, negative, consuming the store exactly. Two unrelated methods, one built on rates and one on accumulation, agree to the joule, and a solver who runs both has verified the answer with the strongest check the unit offers. The contrast with rolling's static friction should be stated plainly, because exam items probe it: bearing friction acts at surfaces that slide rotationally against each other, so it does work and drains energy, while rolling contact friction acts at an instantaneously stationary point and does neither. Same word, opposite bookkeeping, and the diagram, not the vocabulary, decides which ledger applies.

## Energy shares inside machines

The massive-pulley archetypes close with a question students rarely think to ask: of the energy gravity pays into such a machine, what fraction does the pulley keep? The answer is a clean ratio. In any of these systems the pulley's share of the final kinetic energy equals its effective mass, I over R squared, divided by the total effective mass of everything moving. In the table-and-hanging-block archetype the shares were 2.0 of 7.0 for translation of the blocks... precisely, the blocks' translation took 5.0 sevenths and the pulley's rotation took 2.0 sevenths, about 29 percent, matching the joule audit of 8.6 out of 30.0. Let the pulley grow heavier and its share climbs toward dominance; let it shrink toward the idealized massless wheel and its share vanishes, returning the textbook machine whose string carries one tension and whose energy is purely translational.

The ratio is worth internalizing because it converts design questions into arithmetic. An engineer choosing a flywheel, or an exam asking which modification most slows a descent, is asking how the shares move. Adding mass to the rim of a pulley raises I over R squared kilogram for kilogram at full value, while adding the same mass near the hub buys almost nothing, the distribution principle operating inside the machine family exactly as it operated inside the inertia section. Conversely, a yo-yo or a falling-disk device designed to descend slowly wants a large beta, mass parked at the rim, while a device designed to fall freely wants the rotor's share minimized. Reading any machine in this family as an energy-sharing arrangement, with each component's share set by its effective mass, unifies the pulley, the falling disk, and the rolling race into a single picture: gravity or a motor pays one sum, and the geometry of mass distribution writes the split.

## The last checks: limiting cases and the fifteen-second audit

A finished solution in this unit can be audited in seconds, because rotational formulas confess their errors at their extremes. Every rolling result must return the sliding result when beta is set to zero: v = the square root of 2 g h over (1 + beta) collapses to the square root of 2 g h, and a = g sin theta over (1 + beta) collapses to g sin theta, and a formula that fails either collapse is wrong before its numbers are even examined. Every massive-pulley result must return the massless machine as I falls to zero: the acceleration computed earlier, 20 over 9 for the 5-and-3-kilogram Atwood pair, climbs back to 20 over 8 = 2.5 meters per second squared, and the two tensions merge into one, so a solution whose tensions stay split in that limit has misassigned the torque. Every conservation result must hold still when nothing changes: a skater whose inertia is unchanged keeps her omega, and a walker who returns to the rim returns the turntable to its original rate, so any drift in those checks exposes an inventory error.

The dimensional audit runs beside the limiting cases. Torque divided by inertia must yield inverse seconds squared, and it does: newton meters are kilogram meters squared per second squared, and dividing by kilogram meters squared leaves the radians per second squared that alpha wears. Angular momentum divided by inertia must yield inverse seconds, and it does. An answer whose units fail these divisions is not approximately wrong but structurally wrong, usually because a radius was dropped from a bridge relation or a diameter was promoted into one. The audit also catches the quietest error in the unit, a torque quoted in joules by a solver who multiplied a force by a distance and stopped thinking; the number may be right while the concept is wrong, and the concept is what the next part of the question will spend.

A final sign audit closes the routine. The spin's sense, the torque's sense, and the friction's direction must tell one story on the page: a ball rolling down a ramp spins faster in the rolling sense, its alpha carries that sign, the friction torque that produces the alpha points up the ramp, and the center's acceleration, retarded by that same friction, comes out smaller than g sin theta by exactly the beta factor. When any element of the story contradicts the others, the contradiction localizes the error faster than recomputation does. These three audits, limits, dimensions, and signs, cost under a minute combined and rescue more points across a season than any single formula in the unit, which is why they are placed last in this article's order of business rather than first: they are checks on understanding, and they only bite for a solver who has built something worth checking.

## The forearm lever: torque in the body

The lever family reaches its most personal form in the human arm, which is a torque problem the reader is using this instant. The elbow is the pivot. The biceps attaches to the forearm about 4 centimeters from that pivot, while a load held in the hand sits about 32 centimeters out. Holding a 5-kilogram mass, a 50-newton load, motionless with the forearm horizontal requires the biceps to supply the balancing torque: F times 0.04 meters must equal 50 times 0.32 meters, so F = 400 newtons, eight times the load, before the forearm's own weight is even counted. Muscles work at a severe mechanical disadvantage in this arrangement, and the exam's lever items test exactly this ratio thinking with bars and cables in place of tendons.

The disadvantage purchases something worth the price, and naming the trade completes the analysis. Because the hand rides eight times farther from the pivot than the muscle's attachment, the hand moves eight times as far and eight times as fast as the muscle shortens. The body trades force for speed and range at the elbow, gaining a hand that can sweep a wide arc quickly at the cost of a muscle that must pull enormously to hold a modest load. The seesaw traded in the opposite direction, distance surrendered for force. Both are the same conservation of work wearing different geometry, torque times angle on one side matching torque times angle on the other, and recognizing the trade in a diagram, which side holds the long arm and which holds the short one, tells the solver before any arithmetic whether the unknown force will come out larger or smaller than the known one. That directional expectation is a free error check, and lever problems are where it pays most reliably.


## The verdict on rotation, torque, and angular momentum

Rotation is the unit where the course's wager pays off. Physics 1 bets that a small set of principles, deeply owned, outperforms a large set of formulas shallowly held, and no unit tests that bet more honestly, because every rotational law arrives as a principle the student already owns wearing a new symbol. The candidate who builds the analogy table, respects the axis, chooses pivots with intent, and audits energy after conserving angular momentum will find the unit's questions among the most predictable on the exam. The candidate who memorizes a second formula sheet will find them among the least forgiving. The difference is visible in a single sentence at the top of a solution, and it is trainable in a week of deliberate practice. Students revising under time pressure should spend that week on the hybrids rather than on isolated drills, because the exam itself refuses to keep the unit isolated, and every hour spent translating between the linear and angular ledgers pays interest in three other units at once.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How is rotational motion tested on AP Physics 1?

Rotational motion is tested in both sections of the exam. Multiple-choice items probe single ideas: the torque of an angled force, the ranking of rolling objects, the inertia of a shape about a named axis, the result of a rotational collision. Free-response questions build longer structures, attaching a massive pulley to an Atwood machine, asking for an experimental design that measures an unknown moment of inertia, or staging an impact between a moving object and a rotor. The unit also appears inside hybrid problems nominally owned by other units, since rolling is an energy problem and a turntable collision is a momentum problem. Expect the framework's mid-teens share of multiple-choice weight, with influence well beyond that share through the hybrids.

### Q: What is torque on the AP Physics 1 exam?

Torque is the rotational effect of a force, defined as tau = r F sin theta, where r is the distance from the axis to the force's point of application and theta is the angle between the radius direction and the force. Only the component of force perpendicular to the radius contributes, which is why a force aimed through the axis twists nothing. Torque carries a sign set by the sense of rotation it would produce, counterclockwise positive by the usual convention, and net torque about an axis determines angular acceleration through net tau = I alpha. On the exam, torque is tested through angled forces on rods and wheels, lever and balance problems, friction's torque on rolling objects, and the differing tensions that spin a massive pulley.

### Q: How is angular momentum tested on AP Physics 1?

Angular momentum is tested as the rotational form of momentum, L = I omega for a rigid rotor and L = m v r for a particle moving perpendicular to its radius. Items ask for the angular momentum of spinning objects and of projectiles approaching pivoted targets, then lean on its conservation whenever net external torque about the chosen axis is zero. The skater pulling in her arms, the rider walking inward on a turntable, the clay wad striking a disk, and the disk dropped onto a spinning twin are the recurring scenarios. Energy is the standing distractor in this family: angular momentum survives sticking collisions and internal rearrangements while kinetic energy generally does not, and recognizing which ledger survives is the skill being measured.

### Q: How do you apply rotational kinematics on AP Physics 1?

Rotational kinematics applies when angular acceleration is constant, using the translated family: omega = omega_0 + alpha t, theta = omega_0 t + one half alpha t squared, and omega squared = omega_0 squared + 2 alpha theta. The method is to identify which three of the five quantities, theta, omega_0, omega, alpha, and t, the problem supplies, then select the equation containing those and the target. Two supporting skills are tested alongside: reading omega-time graphs, where slope is alpha and area is angular displacement, and converting among revolutions, radians, and rim quantities through s = r theta and v = r omega. When torque varies as the object turns, as with a falling rod, the family is disqualified and energy or impulse methods replace it.

### Q: What is the moment of inertia on AP Physics 1?

The moment of inertia is an object's resistance to angular acceleration about a specified axis, defined as the sum of each mass element times the square of its distance from that axis. Because distance enters squared, mass far from the axis counts far more than mass near it, and the same object owns a different value about every axis. The exam expects the standard shapes with their axes named: hoop MR squared, solid disk or cylinder one half MR squared, solid sphere two fifths MR squared, rod one twelfth ML squared about its center and one third ML squared about its end. The parallel axis theorem, I = I_cm + M d squared, extends values to shifted axes, and computing composites by adding pieces and holes by subtracting them is a tested skill.

### Q: How is angular momentum conservation tested on AP Physics 1?

Conservation is tested through scenarios in which a system's configuration changes while external torque about the axis stays zero, so the product I omega holds fixed. The standard forms are a spinner who changes shape, a person walking on a rotating platform, an object caught or dropped by a rotor, and a collision in which something sticks to a spinning body. The solution method is an inventory: compute the total inertia and angular momentum before the change, compute the total inertia after, and divide. Justification points attach to naming the condition, the absence of net external torque about the axis, rather than merely asserting that conservation applies. Prompts frequently pair the computation with an energy question, and the correct treatment reports kinetic energy gained or lost instead of assuming it is conserved.

### Q: How do you relate torque and angular acceleration on AP Physics 1?

The relation is the rotational form of Newton's second law: net torque about an axis equals the moment of inertia about that axis times the angular acceleration. Applying it well requires three disciplines. First, sum the torques with signs from every force, each computed with its own lever arm and sine factor, including friction and axle effects when present. Second, use the inertia for the same axis the torques were taken about, shifting with the parallel axis theorem when the axis is not the center of mass. Third, remember the law is instantaneous, so a torque that changes with position produces an alpha that changes too. The relation is tested on spun-up disks, massive pulleys with two tensions, falling disks on strings, and any rotor whose spin changes under a stated force arrangement.

### Q: How is rotational energy tested on AP Physics 1?

Rotational energy is tested as the term one half I omega squared inside work-energy accounting. Rolling objects are the main stage: their total kinetic energy combines translation of the center, one half M v squared, with rotation about the center, and the split between the terms is fixed by the shape coefficient beta. Items ask for speeds at the bottom of ramps, heights climbed, and comparisons among hoop, disk, and sphere, all settled by v = the square root of 2 g h over (1 + beta) or its rearrangements. Work in rotation, tau times theta, and power, tau times omega, also appear, and spin-down problems with bearing friction test the work-energy theorem against the torque law as mutual checks. Energy conservation across rotational collisions is the standard trap and is never valid unless the collision is stated to be elastic.

### Q: How do you analyze a rotating system on AP Physics 1?

Analysis begins by declaring the axis and writing the governing-principle sentence: which law, torque balance, energy, angular impulse, or angular momentum conservation, the scenario licenses, and why. The solver then inventories the system's inertia about that axis, using standard shapes, the parallel axis theorem, and addition or subtraction for composites. Forces are placed at their true points of application and their torques scored with lever arms and signs. If parts of the system translate as well as rotate, separate ledgers are kept for the center of mass and for the rotation about it, connected only by weld conditions such as v = omega R or a = alpha R. The strongest analyses close with a check, a limiting case, a second method, or an energy audit, and exam rubrics reward exactly those closing moves.

### Q: What is the rotational form of Newton's second law on AP Physics 1?

The rotational form states that the net torque about an axis equals the moment of inertia about that axis multiplied by the angular acceleration, net tau = I alpha. It is the direct translation of F = ma through the analogy that runs the whole unit: torque plays the role of force, moment of inertia the role of mass, and angular acceleration the role of acceleration. It holds about the center of mass or about a fixed axis, conditions the exam respects in its prompts. Its uses span the unit, from finding the spin-up rate of a disk under a rim pull, to the differing tensions of a massive pulley, to the instantaneous angular acceleration of a falling rod. Every torque must be computed about the same axis as the inertia, which is the detail most often dropped.

### Q: How do rolling objects appear on AP Physics 1?

Rolling objects appear as races down ramps, climbs to a height, launches off tables, and components inside larger energy and dynamics problems. The governing facts are the rolling condition v_cm = omega R with a_cm = alpha R, the total kinetic energy one half M v squared times (1 + beta), and the acceleration g sin theta over (1 + beta) on an incline. Items ask for finishing orders among hoop, disk, and sphere, for speeds and heights computed from the beta split, for the direction and size of the static friction that makes rolling possible, and for judgments about whether a stated surface can supply enough grip. Mass and radius cancel out of every race result, so options that depend on them are traps. Rolling also anchors justification questions, because the energy split by shape is a claim students must defend from the inertia definition rather than recite.

### Q: How do you connect linear and angular quantities on AP Physics 1?

The connections run through the radius of the point in question. Arc length is s = r theta, tangential speed is v = r omega, and tangential acceleration is a_t = r alpha, all requiring angles in radians. These bridge relations convert an object's single angular description, shared by every point of a rigid body, into the different linear motions of its different points, the rim racing while the hub crawls. The same bridging logic connects the laws: torque is force with the radius folded in, angular momentum is momentum with the radius folded in, and moment of inertia is mass with the squared radius folded in. Exam errors in this territory are usually conversion errors, degrees fed into radian formulas, diameter used where radius belongs, or a rim relation applied at a point that is not the rim.

### Q: What is angular velocity on the AP Physics 1 exam?

Angular velocity, omega, is the rate at which a rotating object's angular position changes, measured in radians per second, with the sign fixed by the declared convention, counterclockwise positive when drawn on the page. Every point of a rigid rotating object shares the same omega at every instant, a fact that simplifies rigid-body analysis and distinguishes angular from linear velocity, which does vary from point to point. Omega connects to period through T = 2 pi over omega and to frequency through omega = 2 pi f, and it is read from an omega-time graph directly, while the area under that graph gives angular displacement. On the exam it appears in kinematics computations, as the variable in L = I omega and rotational energy one half I omega squared, and as the quantity conservation laws hold or change across collisions and rearrangements.

### Q: How is a lever or pivot problem tested on AP Physics 1?

Lever and pivot problems are tested as torque-balance exercises, usually in static equilibrium. A beam, board, sign, or ladder is held by some combination of weights, cables, hinges, and applied forces, and the task is to find a tension, a distance, or the pivot force that keeps the system balanced. The scored method is to take torques about the pivot, which erases the unknown pivot force from the equation, place every weight at its true point of application, the center of mass for a uniform object, and set the signed torque sum to zero. Variations move the pivot so an object's own weight changes sides, add hanging masses, or ask for the pivot force afterward through a force balance. Items are lost through lever arms measured to the wrong point and through assuming the pivot force contributes a torque about itself.

### Q: How do you solve a combined translation and rotation problem on AP Physics 1?

The reliable method keeps two ledgers and connects them only through stated weld conditions. The translational ledger follows the center of mass under the net force, F_net = M a_cm. The rotational ledger follows the spin about the center of mass or a fixed axis under the net torque, tau_net = I alpha. The welds are the rolling condition v_cm = omega R and a_cm = alpha R for rolling contact, the rim condition a = alpha R for a string over a pulley, or a shared time or height linking the two halves of a motion. A rolling ball flying off a table, a disk unwinding from a string, and a spool pulled by a thread are the standard stages. Errors come from smuggling quantities between ledgers without a weld, or from applying rolling conditions to objects that are sliding.

### Q: How do you choose a pivot point to simplify a torque problem on AP Physics 1?

Choose the pivot where the most troublesome unknown force acts, because a force acting at the reference point exerts no torque about it and disappears from the torque equation. Unknown hinge forces, contact normal forces, and anchored tensions are the usual targets for deletion. In static equilibrium the choice is entirely free, since net torque is zero about every point when the force balance also holds, so the solver may pick the point that deletes two unknowns at once when forces share a location. In dynamics the safe pivots are the center of mass or a fixed axis, where net tau = I alpha holds without correction. The habit is to survey the diagram, mark every unknown force's location, and place the pivot on the location whose force the problem least asks about, recovering it later by force balance only if requested.

### Q: What is the rolling without slipping condition on AP Physics 1?

The condition is v_cm = omega R: the center's speed equals the rim speed of rotation, so the contact point is instantaneously at rest relative to the surface. Its companions are a_cm = alpha R and s_cm = theta R, and its consequences organize the whole topic. Because the contact point does not slide, the friction present is static, sized by the torque the rotation requires rather than by a friction law maximum, and in the ideal model it does no work, leaving mechanical energy conserved during rolling. When the condition fails, the object skids or spins, kinetic friction takes over, and energy drains until the weld is restored. Exam items test the condition directly, ask which scenarios satisfy it, and punish its application to sliding objects or its omission for rolling ones.

### Q: How do tangential and centripetal acceleration combine on AP Physics 1?

A point on a rotating object experiences tangential acceleration a_t = r alpha along the tangent, present only while angular speed changes, and centripetal acceleration a_c = omega squared r directed toward the axis, present whenever the point moves in a circle at all. Because the two components are perpendicular, the total acceleration is the Pythagorean sum, with magnitude the square root of a_t squared plus a_c squared, directed between the tangent and the inward radial direction. The centripetal term usually dominates at working spin rates because omega enters it squared: in this article's worked case the components were 1.2 and 7.5 meters per second squared, combining to about 7.6. A common item format asks for the acceleration of a rim point on a steadily spinning disk, where alpha is zero and the answer is purely centripetal, with zero offered as the trap.

### Q: How does a pulley with mass change an Atwood machine on AP Physics 1?

A massive pulley removes the two gifts of the idealized machine. The tension is no longer the same on both sides, because a net tension difference is needed to supply the torque that angularly accelerates the pulley, and the system's effective inertia grows by the pulley's I over R squared, which behaves exactly like extra mass in the denominator of the acceleration. Solving requires three equations, a force equation for each hanging mass and the torque equation (T_2 minus T_1) R = I alpha with alpha = a over R. The acceleration always falls below the massless-pulley value; measured against the ideal machine's single tension, the heavier side's tension rises toward its weight while the lighter side's tension falls, and the difference between the two tensions is precisely the pulley's toll, I a over R squared. This is among the most frequently tested free-response structures in the unit.

### Q: How is angular momentum handled in a rotational collision on AP Physics 1?

In a rotational collision, angular momentum about the impact axis is the quantity conserved, provided external torques about that axis are negligible during the brief impact, as they are when the collision forces dwarf gravity and bearing effects. The method is a before-and-after inventory: total the incoming angular momentum, including a projectile's m v times its impact parameter about the pivot, then total the combined inertia after the objects join or interact, and divide to find the final omega. Linear momentum may fail to be conserved when a pivot exerts an external impulse, and kinetic energy is generally spent on deformation, heat, and sound, so it is audited afterward rather than assumed. Sticking collisions between a clay wad and a disk, a bullet and a pivoted rod, and two rotors dropped together are the standard forms.
