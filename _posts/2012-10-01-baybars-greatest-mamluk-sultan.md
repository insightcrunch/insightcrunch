Six weeks after the Mamluk army stopped the Mongol advance in Galilee, the sultan who had commanded it was killed on a hunting expedition by the officer who had led his vanguard. That officer took the sultanate, held it for seventeen years, and turned a regime that had murdered its way through five rulers in a decade into a state that lasted two and a half centuries.

The claim this article defends is the real founder thesis: that although the Mamluk sultanate is conventionally dated from 1250, the ruler who converted a military coup into a functioning state was Baybars, and the institutions, the frontiers and the administrative machinery of the whole period are substantially his.

![Sultan Baybars explained, showing his rise after Ayn Jalut, his campaigns against Mongols and crusaders and the institutions he built - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-46.webp)

The reduction this corrects is treating him as the hero of one battle. The article on [the Battle of Ayn Jalut](/2012/09/01/battle-of-ayn-jalut-mongols/) owns that engagement, and this article's argument is that what he did in the seventeen years afterward matters considerably more than what he did on one afternoon in 1260.

The complication that runs through the whole subject is that his reign produced a legend, and a cycle of popular romance grew around him over the following centuries that has to be separated from the documented record at every point.

The regime he consolidated belongs to the article on [the Mamluk sultanate](/2012/07/01/mamluk-sultanate-of-egypt/), the system that produced him to the article on [who the Mamluks were](/2012/08/01/who-were-the-mamluks/), and the army he rebuilt to the article on [the Mamluk war machine](/2013/02/01/mamluk-war-machine-explained/).

## Where He Came From

The origin of the man is the standard Mamluk story and its details are less secure than most accounts imply.

The birth was on the steppe north of the Black Sea, among the Kipchak Turks, which the article on who the Mamluks were identifies as the principal recruitment region of the earlier sultanate.

### Where did Baybars come from before Egypt?

From the Kipchak steppe north of the Black Sea, captured as a boy during the upheavals accompanying the Mongol advance and sold into the slave trade that supplied Egypt. He passed through more than one owner, was reportedly rejected by one buyer because of a physical defect, and was eventually purchased into the household of the Ayyubid sultan in Egypt.

The capture is generally attributed to the disruption the Mongol expansion caused across the steppe, which the article on who the Mamluks were notes as a grim irony: the Mongol destruction of the steppe world supplied the markets from which Egypt bought the soldiers who stopped the Mongols.

The rejection story, in which a buyer refused him because of a blemish in one eye, is widely repeated and belongs to the category of biographical detail that a successful career attracts afterward.

The purchase into the Ayyubid sultan's own corps placed him in the household quartered on an island in the river, which the article on crusader invasions of Egypt describes as the unit that won the campaign of 1250 and then took the state.

The training followed the standard course the article on who the Mamluks were describes, and he emerged as a cavalryman of the household that would shortly become the ruling caste.

The physical description in the sources is consistent enough to be worth noting, giving a tall man of powerful build with a blemish in one eye, which is the sort of detail chroniclers record when the person is famous enough to be looked at.

The general observation is that his origin was entirely ordinary within the system, and everything remarkable about his career happened after the point at which thousands of other purchased boys had reached the same position.

## The Institutions Listed

A consolidated statement of what the reign built is worth giving in one place, since the article has treated the items separately and the cumulative effect is the argument.

The caliphate in Cairo, established 1261, which invested every Mamluk sultan for two and a half centuries and gave a regime of former slaves a formal legitimacy it could not otherwise have claimed.

The postal relay network, with staging posts, fresh horses and fixed routes linking Cairo to Damascus and the Syrian frontier, carrying messages in days and giving a distant government current knowledge of its border.

The pigeon post, operating alongside it as the fastest channel available anywhere in the period.

The four chief judgeships, one for each Sunni legal school, which distributed the state's endorsement across the entire scholarly establishment rather than concentrating it in one tradition.

The fiscal survey, restoring the register on which the revenue assignment system depended, which is what makes an army payable and what the article on taxation identifies as the foundation of every Egyptian regime.

The fortress network across Syria, rebuilt and garrisoned where a place could be held and demolished where it could not.

The fleet, addressing the structural weakness the article on Saladin and the crusades identifies and the article on the Ayyubid economy explains.

The expanded corps, purchased continuously through a supply line protected by the diplomacy the article's section on it describes.

The general observation is that this list is the reign, and a reader who takes nothing else from this article should take the fact that seven of these eight items were still operating when the Ottomans arrived two hundred and forty years later.

## The Two Biographers

The men who wrote the reign deserve individual attention, since the article's section on the sources treats them collectively and their differences matter.

Both were chancery officials, which means both had documentary access, both could date events precisely and both owed their positions to the man they were describing.

The first was a senior secretary whose account is the fuller and more official, drawing on correspondence and administrative records, and its value is exactly the documentary detail an outsider could not supply.

The second wrote a work with a different emphasis, and the two together supply the bulk of what is known about the reign in narrative form.

The later compilations drew on both extensively, and the fifteenth century Egyptian tradition this series uses throughout preserves material from them that is otherwise lost.

The tests the article on Saladin as sultan sets out apply directly, since that article describes a procedure for reading a favourable tradition that mirrors the procedure the article on al-Hakim applies to a hostile one.

The most valuable material by those tests is the circumstantial administrative detail, since a secretary describing the postal system or the judicial appointments has no motive to invent and every reason to be accurate.

The least valuable is the characterization, since a chancery official describing his sultan's piety and justice is performing a duty rather than reporting.

The general observation is that an official biography is simultaneously the best and the worst kind of source, unmatched on procedure and worthless on character, and the discipline is knowing which parts of it to use for what.

## The Reputation Among Historians

How the reign has been assessed by scholarship deserves a short section, since the article's thesis is not original and its standing is worth stating.

The medieval Muslim assessment was high, and the chronicle tradition treats him as the restorer of Muslim fortunes after 1258, with the Ayn Jalut victory and the crusader campaigns as the evidence.

The popular assessment took the form the article's section on the romance cycle describes, which is enthusiastic and useless as evidence.

The modern scholarly assessment has consistently rated the reign as the most consequential of the sultanate, and the phrase effective founder or second founder appears widely in the specialist literature.

The grounds given are the ones this article has assembled: the institutions, the frontiers, the caliphate and the duration in a system that had produced nothing durable in the preceding decade.

The dissenting emphasis notes that the succession system emerged from the whole period rather than from any design of his, and the article's section on the founding claim states that objection.

The comparison with later sultans is the other qualification, and the article on Mamluk architecture in Cairo describes a fourteenth century reign whose building programme was far larger.

The European scholarly attention has been thinner than the Saladin material has received, which the article on the legacy of Saladin explains: the chivalric reputation drew attention that institutional achievement never does.

The general observation is that this article's thesis is a mainstream scholarly position stated at length rather than a novel claim, and its interest lies in the reasoning rather than in the conclusion.

## The Southern Frontier

The Nubian intervention deserves fuller treatment than the article's earlier section gave it, since it ended an arrangement six centuries old.

The Christian kingdoms of Nubia had faced Egypt since the seventh century, and the article on the Arab conquest of Egypt describes a negotiated agreement reached shortly after the conquest that had held remarkably well.

The terms of that agreement had been an exchange of goods rather than a tribute in the ordinary sense, and its durability across six centuries is among the more striking facts in Egyptian frontier history.

The intervention of this reign broke it, imposing a client ruler, exacting payment on different terms and establishing a pattern of interference that continued afterward.

The motives available include the security of the southern approaches, the gold and slave routes running through the region, and the ordinary logic of a state removing independent actors from territory it claims.

The commercial dimension connects to the article on the Ayyubid economy, which describes the desert roads and the Red Sea corridor as the expensive legs of the transit trade.

The religious dimension is real, since a Muslim state imposing a client on a Christian kingdom during a war against Christian powers is making a statement as well as a calculation.

The long consequence was the decline of Christian Nubia, which the article on the lasting legacy of Coptic Egypt touches, since the kingdoms that had preserved a Christian order on the upper Nile did not survive the following two centuries.

The general observation is that the peripheral campaigns of a reign frequently have longer consequences than the central ones, and an intervention treated as a footnote in accounts of Mongol and crusader wars ended a six century arrangement permanently.

## What a Contemporary Would Have Noticed

An account built from institutions risks describing a reign nobody experienced, so it is worth asking what changed for the people living through it.

The security improved measurably, and a population that had watched the Mongols destroy Baghdad in 1258 and had heard the ultimatum reach Cairo in 1260 understood what the alternative was.

The taxes were heavy and continuous, which the article's section on the cost describes, and the demands of seventeen years of campaigning fell on the cultivators the article on taxation describes.

The parades were visible, and the article's section on the ceremonial describes prisoners and trophies displayed in Cairo after each campaign.

The new buildings appeared, with the great mosque, the citadel works and the endowed foundations changing the fabric of the capital.

The post riders were a visible novelty on the roads, and a relay network with staging posts is infrastructure ordinary travellers would have encountered.

The judicial change affected anyone with a case, since the article's section on the four chief judges describes a reorganization of the courts.

The non Muslim communities experienced harder enforcement, which the article's section on them states plainly.

The general observation is that a founding reign feels to those living through it like heavy taxation, frequent parades and a great deal of construction, and the institutions that make it historically important are mostly invisible to the people paying for them.

## The Frontier Fortresses

The Syrian building programme deserves its own treatment, since it was the largest sustained expenditure of the reign and it is invisible in most accounts.

The requirement followed from the frontier, and the article's section on the Mongol contest describes a line on the Euphrates that could not be held by field armies alone.

The inheritance was a landscape of crusader and Ayyubid castles, some captured, some ruined and some still in enemy hands.

The decisions taken were threefold: rebuild, garrison or demolish, and the choice depended on whether a place could be relieved and whether an enemy could use it.

The inland castles were generally rebuilt and garrisoned, since they served the Mongol frontier and no fleet could reach them.

The coastal places were generally demolished, and the article's section on that policy explains why.

The engineering was substantial, since a fortress rebuilt for a garrison in the age of stone throwing engines and mining required serious work, and the article on the Citadel of Cairo describes the same techniques in use at the capital.

The garrisons required continuous pay and supply, which is the recurring cost the article's section on the economy describes.

The general observation is that the visible legacy of the reign in Egypt is a mosque and in Syria is a chain of rebuilt fortifications, and the second cost far more than the first and appears in almost no account of the man.

## Two Rulers Compared by Position

A final analytical comparison is worth making, since the article's earlier section set this reign against Saladin's by character and position is more informative.

The position Saladin held is the one the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes: a family confederation of relatives governing autonomously, held together by personal standing rather than by office, which trades permanence for speed.

The position this reign held was a military caste with no families, no inheritance and no autonomous territories, in which every amir owed his standing to a patron and every patron competed with every other.

The consequence for what each could attempt is direct. A confederation can expand quickly and fragments on the founder's death; a caste cannot expand as fast and survives its founders indefinitely.

The evidence is what happened to each. The article on Ayyubid Egypt describes succession conflicts breaking the confederation within months of 1193; this article's section on the succession describes sons removed within three years and a state that continued without interruption.

The comparison the article on Saladin and the crusades draws between two commanders by position rather than ability applies here as well, since a ruler's options are set by the structure he operates in more than by his own qualities.

The judgment available is that this reign inherited a worse structure and left a better one, while the Ayyubid founder inherited a good position and left a fragmenting one.

The qualification is that neither man chose his structure, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate argues that the succession solution emerged from the practice of the whole period rather than from any one reign's design.

The general observation is that comparing rulers by achievement without comparing the positions they held produces a ranking of luck rather than of ability, which the article on Saladin and the crusades makes in a military context and which applies just as directly to a political one.

## The Word Greatest

A note on the article's own vocabulary is worth including, since greatest is a word this series generally avoids.

The problem is that greatness is not a property, and the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry makes exactly this point when comparing two states: the word smuggles in a criterion without stating it.

The criteria available here are several and they give different answers. Longest reigning gives a fourteenth century sultan; greatest builder gives the same man; most territory gives this reign or its immediate successor; most decisive battle gives Ayn Jalut and therefore his victim.

The criterion this article uses is founding, meaning the creation of institutions that outlast the founder, and the article's section on the founding claim defends it explicitly.

The alternative criteria are legitimate and produce different rankings, which is why this article states its own rather than asserting a general superlative.

The scholarly consensus, such as it is, does rate this reign as the most consequential of the sultanate, and the assessment as effective founder is a settled judgment rather than a novel one.

The popular usage is looser and rests substantially on the romance cycle the article's section on the legend describes.

The honest formulation is that he was the most consequential Mamluk sultan by the criterion of institutional foundation, and that other criteria exist and other sultans win under them.

The general observation is that superlatives require a stated criterion, and an article using one without naming it has expressed a preference rather than made an argument.

## Why This Reign Is Worth Studying

A closing argument for the attention this article has given to a thirteenth century sultanate.

The first reason is the founding question itself, since distinguishing a regime's start date from the point at which it began to function is a distinction that applies almost everywhere and is almost never made.

The second is the legitimacy problem, and the article's sections on the caliph and on the judiciary describe a ruler manufacturing authority from nothing with instruments that lasted two and a half centuries.

The third is the information system, since the postal relay and the pigeon towers are an unusually clear case of a state solving a strategic problem with communications rather than with force.

The fourth is the contrast with reputation, and the article's sections on the legend and on the comparison with Saladin show a case where institutional achievement and historical fame diverge sharply.

The fifth is the demolition policy, which the article's section on it describes as a coherent answer to a structural constraint and which shaped a coastline permanently.

The sixth is the source problem, and the article's section on it presents a reign documented abundantly by its own officials, which is a methodological situation worth practising on.

The seventh is that the whole Mamluk period, which the article on the Mamluk sultanate covers, becomes intelligible once these seventeen years are understood, since everything afterward runs on what was built in them.

The general recommendation is that founding reigns repay more study than glorious ones, because what a ruler builds determines what his successors can do and what a ruler wins determines only what he did.

## Intelligence and Surveillance

The information system of the reign deserves separate treatment, since it is the least discussed and among the most modern seeming of its features.

The postal relay is the backbone and the article's section on the reforms describes staging posts, fresh horses and messages moving in days rather than weeks.

The pigeon towers were the faster channel, carrying short messages over the same routes at speeds nothing else in the period could match.

The purpose was military first, since the article's section on the Mongol frontier explains that a Cairo government defending the Euphrates needs information travelling faster than an enemy army.

The second purpose was internal, since the same network carried reports about his own commanders, and the article's section on holding power notes that the amirs who had made him were the men most capable of unmaking him.

The informants are reported in the sources, with a network of agents supplying information from Syria, from the crusader states and from the Mongol territories.

The reported use of assassination as an instrument appears in the accounts and belongs, like much else in this reign, to the category of claims that are plausible and unverifiable.

The frontier intelligence was systematic, and knowing when a Mongol force was assembling is the difference between meeting it and being surprised by it.

The general observation is that a state defending a distant frontier with a professional army is fundamentally an information problem, and this reign's most durable institution is the one built to solve it rather than any of the fortresses or the fleets.

## The Ceremonial of the Reign

How the sultanate presented itself deserves a section, since a regime built by a former slave had particular reasons to be visible.

The investiture by the caliph was the founding ceremony, and the article's section on him describes an act that converted a usurper into a recognized sultan.

The processions through Cairo were substantial, and the article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo describes an earlier tradition of ceremonial display in the same streets for very different reasons.

The parade of prisoners and trophies after campaigns is recorded repeatedly, which converts a distant victory into a visible event for a population that had not seen it.

The citadel provided the setting, and the article on the Citadel of Cairo describes a complex whose Mamluk phase turned a fortress into a palace with halls built for exactly this purpose.

The pilgrimage caravan was the other great annual display, and its protection was the most legible service a Muslim ruler could render.

The audience for all of it was double, since the population needed reassurance and the amirs needed reminding, and the article on who the Mamluks were describes a political system in which the second audience mattered more.

The contrast with the Ayyubid practice is instructive, and the article on the Citadel of Cairo notes a preceding dynasty that dispensed with ceremonial almost entirely because a diploma from Baghdad required no demonstration.

The general observation is that ceremonial tracks the basis of authority, and a sultan whose legitimacy rested on a manufactured caliph and on continuous military success needed to display both constantly.

## The Fall of Antioch

The single largest event of the crusader campaign deserves its own treatment, since it ended a state that had existed since the First Crusade.

The principality had been founded in 1098 and had survived for a hundred and seventy years, longer than the kingdom of Jerusalem in its original form.

The strategic position had become untenable, since its Armenian ally was a Mongol client and its Byzantine connections could not deliver relief against an Egyptian army operating from Syria.

The siege was short, and the city fell in 1268 after the walls were breached, which is what happens to a large circuit defended by an inadequate garrison.

The sack that followed is described severely in both traditions, with the population killed or enslaved in numbers the sources report as very large.

The letter reportedly sent to the absent prince, describing in detail what had been done to his city, is among the more chilling documents of the period and it is preserved in the chronicle tradition.

The contrast with 1187 is the comparison the article on Saladin and the crusades invites, since that article describes a surrender on terms and this describes a storm.

The strategic consequence was to remove the northern crusader presence entirely, which simplified the Syrian position and freed forces for the Mongol frontier.

The reputational consequence in Europe was considerable and it contributed to the last crusading expeditions, which achieved nothing.

The general observation is that this article's subject was harder than his celebrated predecessor and accomplished more, and holding both facts together is what the article on Saladin as hero or conqueror would call the honest position.

## Why He Demolished What He Took

The systematic destruction of captured coastal places is the reign's most distinctive military policy and it deserves explanation.

The problem it solved is the one the article on Saladin and the crusades identifies as structural: crusader positions were supplied by sea, Egypt could not contest the sea reliably, and a captured port could therefore be retaken by any fleet that arrived.

The article on the Ayyubid economy explains the underlying constraint, since Egypt has almost no shipbuilding timber and every fleet the country built depended on imports.

The solution was to remove the asset rather than to hold it, dismantling walls, harbours and fortifications so that a landing force would find nothing usable.

The precedent existed in the Egyptian response to the crusader invasions, and the article on crusader invasions of Egypt describes Damietta itself demolished after 1250 for exactly this reason.

The cost was commercial, since a ruined port serves no trade either, and the article on Mamluk Egypt and world trade treats the consequences for the Syrian coast.

The judgment implied is that security was worth more than commerce on that coastline, which is a defensible calculation for a state whose main commercial artery ran through Egypt rather than Syria.

The permanence is the striking result, since the Syrian coastal cities did not recover their medieval position and the region's commercial centre of gravity shifted decisively toward Egyptian ports.

The general observation is that a state which cannot hold a position and cannot allow an enemy to hold it will destroy the position, and this reign applied that logic along an entire coastline with consequences lasting centuries.

## What the Reign Cost Egypt

An article in a series about Egypt should ask what the country paid for all of this, since the achievements are usually listed without the bill.

The fiscal burden was continuous, and the article on taxation describes a system extracting from cultivators whose yield the flood determined, with the demands set by what the state needed rather than by what the land produced.

The military expansion was the largest single item, and the article on who the Mamluks were sets out why a caste renewed by purchase requires permanent expenditure.

The fortress programme in Syria was the second, since garrisoned strongpoints on a contested frontier cost money continuously rather than once.

The campaigns themselves consumed resources for seventeen years without interruption, on three fronts.

The direct exposure of Egypt to fighting was minimal, since the wars were fought in Syria, Anatolia and Nubia, which means most Egyptians paid for wars they never saw.

The compensating benefits were real. Security from Mongol invasion is worth a great deal to a population that had watched Baghdad destroyed, and the article on the Battle of Ayn Jalut describes what the alternative looked like.

The infrastructure spending returned value directly, since canal and harbour works serve agriculture and trade rather than only the state.

The general observation is that a founding reign is expensive and the people paying for it are rarely the people commemorated by it, which the article on the legacy of Saladin makes in a different form about a different ruler.

## The Successors

What happened to the state after 1277 completes the argument, since the founding claim depends on what continued.

The immediate succession failed, and the article's section on it describes his sons removed within three years.

The commander who took the throne in 1279 completed the crusader campaign, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate records the final positions falling in the 1290s under his line.

The institutions continued without interruption, which is the article's central evidence. The postal system, the four judgeships, the caliphate in Cairo, the fiscal survey and the fortress network were all still operating.

The frontier held, and the article on the Battle of Ayn Jalut describes a Mongol contest continuing for decades after his death and ending in a negotiated peace in 1323.

The pattern of succession settled into the form the article on the Mamluk sultanate describes, with sons as placeholders and senior commanders taking real power, which is exactly what happened to his own family.

The great building century followed, and the article on Mamluk architecture in Cairo describes a programme that dwarfed anything of his reign.

The comparison worth drawing is that later sultans built more and founded less, which is the distinction the article's section on the founding claim defends.

The general observation is that the test of a founder is whether the successors inherit a state or a problem, and the seventeen years described in this article are what made the difference between the decade before them and the two centuries after.

## The Chronology of the Reign

Setting the sequence out in one place is worth doing, since the article treats themes and a reader needs the order.

In 1250 the Ayyubid heir was killed by the corps quartered on the river island, and the article on crusader invasions of Egypt describes the campaign that produced the killing.

Across the 1250s the sultanate changed hands repeatedly, and he spent part of the decade in Syria having backed a losing faction.

In 1258 the Mongols destroyed Baghdad and the Abbasid caliphate, which the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry treats as the end of an institution five centuries old.

In 1260 he returned to Egyptian service, commanded the vanguard at Ayn Jalut in September, and murdered the victorious sultan roughly six weeks later on the march back to Cairo.

In 1261 he installed a claimed Abbasid survivor as caliph in Cairo and received investiture from him.

Across the 1260s he built the postal system, reorganized the judiciary, expanded the corps and began the sequential reduction of the crusader positions.

In 1263 he took a Galilean city, and in 1268 Antioch fell, ending a principality established during the First Crusade.

In 1271 the Templar fortress fell, and by the early 1270s the Syrian Ismaili strongholds had been taken.

Across the same years he intervened in Nubia, imposed a client ruler and maintained the alliance with the northern Mongols.

In 1277 he campaigned into Anatolia, defeated a Mongol force, withdrew, and died at Damascus within months.

Between 1277 and 1279 his eldest son reigned and was deposed, a younger son was installed briefly, and a senior commander took the throne outright.

The shape the sequence reveals is that the campaigns and the institution building ran in parallel throughout rather than in sequence, which is the article's central observation stated chronologically.

## Reading a Founding Reign

The procedure this article has used generalizes, and stating it makes the approach usable elsewhere.

The first question is what the regime looked like before, and the article's section on the decade before the throne describes five rulers in ten years and no institutions.

The second is what survived afterward, since the test of a founding reign is what continues without the founder, and the article's section on the succession supplies the answer.

The third is to separate the campaigns from the machinery, because campaigns fill chronicles and machinery runs states, and this article has argued the second matters more.

The fourth is to identify the legitimacy problem, since every regime has one and the solution reveals what the regime thought it lacked.

The fifth is to ask who wrote the account, and the article's section on the sources notes that two chancery officials produced most of it.

The sixth is to check the legend against the record, and the article's section on the romance cycle shows a popular tradition with almost no overlap with the documented reign.

The seventh is to test the founding claim against rivals, and the article's section on it states the case for 1250 and for later sultans before answering both.

Applied to any reign these seven distinguish a successful ruler from a founding one, which is a distinction most political history blurs and which this case makes unusually clear.

## The Man Himself

What can be said about the person rather than the reign deserves a section, since the sources record a good deal and most of it needs qualifying.

The physical description is consistent across accounts, giving a tall man of powerful build with a blemish in one eye, and the consistency suggests observation rather than convention.

The energy is the quality every source emphasizes, describing a ruler who moved constantly between Egypt and Syria, appeared unexpectedly at frontier posts and covered enormous distances at speed.

The suspicion is recorded alongside it, and a man who reached the throne by assassination and knew exactly how the system worked had rational grounds for watching his commanders closely.

The piety appears in the endowments and in the pilgrimage protection, and the article's section on the judiciary describes a legitimacy programme in which sincerity and calculation are not separable.

The cruelty is recorded too, and the sack of Antioch and the treatment of certain garrisons are in the record alongside the achievements.

The interest in intelligence is the most distinctive personal trait the sources describe, and the postal system, the pigeon towers and the reported use of informants all point the same way.

The literacy question is unresolved, since a Kipchak boy trained in the barracks would have received the Arabic instruction the article on who the Mamluks were describes, and the level attained is not documented.

The general observation is that the personal portrait is drawn by his own officials, which the article's section on the sources treats, so the energy and the piety are as reliable as the omissions are systematic.

## What Would Change This Picture

Naming the evidence that would revise the account is the fairest test of a reign documented by its own chancery.

An independent account of Ayn Jalut, written outside his patronage, would settle the attribution question the article on that battle treats as unresolvable.

A hostile Muslim account of the reign would be transformative, and the article on Saladin as hero or conqueror makes the same point about a different figure: the absence of a critical contemporary voice is the largest gap in any favourable tradition.

Fiscal records of the reign would establish the scale of the reforms, which are described rather than measured, and would settle what the postal system and the fortress programme actually cost.

Chancery documents beyond what the biographers quote would show the diplomacy in its own words rather than in their summaries.

Archaeological work on the postal stations would establish the network's extent, which is presently known from narrative description.

Evidence about the treatment of the Coptic community across the reign, beyond the incidents the chronicles record, would firm up the article's section on the communities.

None of these is likely, and the boundary is therefore clear. The chronology, the campaigns, the caliph, the reforms and the succession are documented. The scale of the reforms, the assessment of his conduct and his role at Ayn Jalut rest on sources with an interest.

The general observation is that a well documented reign can be harder to assess than a poorly documented one, since abundant favourable evidence is more persuasive than sparse evidence and no less partial.

## The Economy Under the Reign

The financial basis of everything described in this article deserves attention, since seventeen years of campaigning and building is expensive.

The revenue base was the combination the article on the Ayyubid economy describes: flood dependent agriculture producing a surplus, plus the transit trade between three seas producing cash.

The fiscal survey is the reign's most important economic act, and the article's section on the reforms notes the restoration of the register underlying the revenue assignments, which is what makes an army payable.

The trade policy protected the corridor, and the article on Mamluk Egypt and world trade treats the subject, with commercial treaties and secured routes as the standing requirements.

The Red Sea attention followed from that, since the article on the Ayyubid economy identifies the southern corridor as the route eastern goods took to reach Europe.

The plunder from the crusader campaigns supplied a supplementary income, and the article on Saladin and the crusades describes the ransom economy that accompanied such warfare.

The infrastructure spending was substantial, with canal and harbour works recorded, which the article on taxation identifies as the standing requirement of a flood based system.

The cost of the corps was the largest continuing expense, and the article on who the Mamluks were sets out why a caste renewed by purchase requires permanent outlay in a way a hereditary nobility does not.

The general observation is that the reign was affordable because Egypt was rich, and the article on the Ayyubid economy's argument about monetizing geography explains where the money for the postal relays, the fortresses, the fleet and the purchased boys came from.

## Comparing Him With Saladin

Setting the two great medieval rulers of Egypt against each other clarifies both, and the comparison is instructive rather than competitive.

The origins differ in kind. The article on Saladin as sultan describes a Kurdish officer from a family already in senior service; this article describes a boy bought in a market.

The rise differs correspondingly, since one was advanced by an uncle and a patron and the other by murdering his sovereign.

The military records are comparable in scale and different in completeness. The article on Saladin and the crusades describes a settlement leaving the crusaders the coast; this reign reduced them to a remnant and his successors finished it.

The conduct differs sharply, and the article on Saladin as hero or conqueror describes a reputation for clemency built by enemies, while no comparable reputation attaches here.

The institutional records are the reverse of what the reputations suggest. The article on Ayyubid Egypt describes a confederation that fragmented on its founder's death; this reign left machinery that survived the removal of its founder's sons within three years.

The legacies diverge accordingly, and the article on the legacy of Saladin describes a modern symbol of enormous proportions while this ruler is remembered chiefly by specialists and by a popular romance cycle.

The judgment available is that one was the better man by the standards of his own century and the other the better founder, which is a distinction worth holding rather than resolving.

The general observation is that reputational fame and institutional achievement are largely independent variables, and this pair of Egyptian rulers demonstrates the point about as clearly as any two figures could.

## The Judiciary and the Scholars

The relationship with the religious establishment deserves its own section, since it is where a foreign military caste bought the standing it could not inherit.

The problem is the one the article on Ayyubid Egypt identifies for a previous dynasty and which applies with greater force here. A ruler born a slave abroad, holding power by murder, has no claim the population would recognize without help.

The four chief judges arrangement is the reign's structural answer, appointing a chief judge for each of the four Sunni legal schools rather than one, which the article on religion and tolerance in Ayyubid Egypt describes as distributing the state's endorsement across the whole establishment.

The effect was to give every school a stake in the regime, which is the same calculation the Ayyubids had made in their college patronage and which this reign extended into the judiciary itself.

The endowment programme accompanied it, and the article on religion and tolerance in Ayyubid Egypt explains why endowment is the instrument: property assigned irrevocably creates institutions and obligations that outlast any ruler.

The pilgrimage protection is the other half of the legitimacy purchase, since securing the routes and the holy cities is the single most visible service a Muslim ruler can render.

The caliph in Cairo completes the package, and the article's section on him describes an investiture that converted a usurper into a recognized sultan.

The scholars' reciprocal service was endorsement, and a regime that appoints, funds and houses the religious establishment receives its approval as a matter of course.

The general observation is that legitimacy in this world was bought rather than inherited when inheritance was unavailable, and this reign spent heavily and systematically on exactly that.

## Religion and the Communities

The treatment of non Muslims under this reign deserves a direct statement, since it was harder than under the preceding dynasty and the difference is real.

The framework was the inherited protected status the article on Copts under early Islamic rule describes, guaranteeing worship and property alongside a poll tax and a subordinate position.

The application under this reign was stricter, with enforcement of dress restrictions, dismissals from office and pressure on the Coptic administrative class recorded more frequently than under the Ayyubids.

The context supplies part of the explanation, since a state at war with Christian powers on two fronts and with a Mongol enemy allied to Christian Armenia had reasons to view its own Christian population with suspicion.

The article on religion and tolerance in Ayyubid Egypt describes the preceding arrangement and notes that rhetoric hardened while policy did not, which makes this reign the point where the policy hardened too.

The fiscal contradiction still operated, and the article on conquest and conversion explains why: the poll tax fell only on non Muslims, so pressure toward conversion cost the treasury revenue.

The administrative dependence also persisted, and the article on Copts under early Islamic rule describes dismissal decrees reliably followed by reinstatement because the expertise was irreplaceable.

The Nubian campaign belongs here as well as in the article's section on the peripheral wars, since imposing a client on a Christian kingdom is a religious act as well as a strategic one.

The general observation is that this article does not smooth the record, and a reign that deserves credit for institutions also presided over harder treatment of the country's Christian population than its predecessors had.

## The Army He Rebuilt

The military instrument of the reign deserves description, since the campaigns and the reforms both depended on it.

The inheritance was the corps described in the article on who the Mamluks were, purchased, trained in barracks, manumitted and bound to patrons, which had won at Ayn Jalut and had killed two sultans in a decade.

The expansion was substantial, since a sultan's standing depended on the size of his own household and the article on who the Mamluks were describes purchases beginning immediately on accession.

The purchases required the supply line the article's section on diplomacy describes, which is why the northern alliance mattered so much.

The organizational reform distributed the corps between the sultan's own household, the units of the amirs and the regiments serving in the Syrian garrisons.

The training establishment was maintained and enlarged, and the article on who the Mamluks were describes a decade long process producing the standard the article on the Mamluk war machine treats.

The naval arm was built almost from nothing, addressing the weakness the article on Saladin and the crusades identifies as structural, though the article on the Ayyubid economy explains why an Egyptian fleet was always expensive.

The auxiliary forces included Bedouin contingents, Syrian troops and the free cavalry serving alongside the Mamluks proper, which the article on the Mamluk war machine describes.

The result was the force that reduced the crusader states and held the Euphrates, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate describes it as the finest army of its period in the region.

The general observation is that rebuilding an army is an administrative achievement rather than a military one, and the purchases, the training establishment, the pay assignments and the garrison rotations are what a reign like this actually consisted of.

## Holding Power for Seventeen Years

The survival of the reign in a system that had removed five rulers in a decade deserves examination, since it is the achievement everything else rests on.

The first instrument was his own household, purchased continuously and large enough to outweigh any rival's, which the article on who the Mamluks were identifies as the currency of Mamluk politics.

The second was the removal of rivals, conducted steadily and without much publicity, since the amirs who had made him were the amirs most capable of unmaking him.

The third was continuous campaigning, which occupies an army, supplies plunder, distributes rewards and leaves commanders no leisure for conspiracy.

The fourth was legitimacy, and the article's section on the caliph describes the manufacture of an authority that made deposing him a different kind of act from deposing a predecessor.

The fifth was the information system, since the article's section on the postal relay describes a network that carried intelligence about his own commanders as readily as about the enemy.

The sixth was distribution, since revenue assignments granted to loyal amirs and endowments funding religious institutions purchase support in the two places it matters.

The seventh was competence, since a sultan who wins is harder to remove than one who does not, and seventeen years of successful campaigning is its own protection.

The general observation is that survival in this system was an active project requiring constant attention, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate notes that most sultans failed at it, which is what makes this reign the exception that founded the state.

## The Mongol Frontier

The northern contest ran for the whole reign and its character deserves fuller description, since it was the strategic problem everything else served.

The situation after 1260 was that the Persian Mongol power held Iraq, Iran and much of Anatolia, and the article on the Battle of Ayn Jalut describes a single defeat that broke a presumption without removing a threat.

The frontier settled on the Euphrates, which is a line rather than a barrier, and holding it required garrisons, fortresses, intelligence and the ability to concentrate forces quickly.

The postal system exists because of this frontier, and the article's section on the reforms explains the logic: a Cairo government defending a line six hundred kilometres away needs information moving faster than an enemy army.

The fortress programme in Syria serves the same purpose, since strongpoints slow an advance and give a defender time to assemble.

The raiding across the line was continuous in both directions and it is the ordinary texture of the frontier, punctuated by larger campaigns.

The Armenian kingdom in Cilicia was a Mongol client and therefore a target, and campaigns against it recur through the reign, which the article on Saladin and the crusades notes as a longstanding alignment on the crusader side of the region's politics.

The diplomatic dimension is the article's section on the alliances, since a threat in the Mongol rear was worth more than any number of frontier garrisons.

The outcome across the reign was that the frontier held without either side achieving a decision, and the article on the Battle of Ayn Jalut records that the contest continued for sixty three years after the battle and produced Mongol victories as well as Mamluk ones.

The general observation is that the reign's most expensive and least dramatic commitment was a defensive frontier, and the postal relays, the fortresses and the diplomacy were all built to serve it.

## The Diplomacy

The foreign relations of the reign deserve their own treatment, since a slave state defending a distant frontier had to be unusually good at them.

The northern Mongol alliance was the centrepiece and the article's section on it explains the double purpose, military and commercial.

The Byzantine relationship followed from the recruitment requirement, since the article on who the Mamluks were describes a supply line from the Black Sea that had to pass the straits, and no amount of military success protects a state whose replacement soldiers cannot reach it.

The Italian maritime cities were the carriers, and the article on who the Mamluks were notes the paradox that the shipping supplying the Egyptian army belonged to states whose coreligionists it was fighting.

The commercial treaties with those cities served the wider trade the article on the Ayyubid economy describes, since the transit commerce funding the state required foreign merchants in Egyptian ports.

The European contacts extended further, and diplomatic exchanges with several western rulers are recorded, conducted while the same ruler was reducing crusader fortresses.

The Nubian and Arabian dimensions completed the picture, with a client ruler imposed in the south and the protection of the pilgrimage routes maintained as a standing obligation.

The instrument throughout was the chancery, and the article's section on the sources notes that two of its officials wrote the reign's history, which means the diplomatic record survives unusually well.

The general observation is that the popular image of a warrior sultan omits an extensive diplomatic system operating simultaneously with the campaigns, and the alliance protecting a slave supply route is the clearest evidence that this ruler understood exactly what his state rested on.

## The Decade Before the Throne

The years between 1250 and 1260 are usually skipped and they explain why the throne was available to be taken.

The killing of the Ayyubid heir in 1250 involved him directly, and the article on crusader invasions of Egypt describes the corps turning on the man summoned to rule them.

The instability that followed was extreme, with the sultanate changing hands repeatedly and the article on the Mamluk sultanate describing a decade in which no arrangement held.

His own position moved with the factions, and he spent part of the period in Syria having fallen out with the regime in Cairo, which was the ordinary consequence of backing a losing side.

The return came under the sultan who would command at Ayn Jalut, who needed experienced commanders as the Mongol threat approached and welcomed back men who had been exiled.

The Mongol advance is the context for that reconciliation, and the article on the Battle of Ayn Jalut describes the destruction of Baghdad in 1258 and the ultimatum that reached Cairo in 1260.

The vanguard command at the battle was his, which is the achievement the popular account concentrates on and which the article on Ayn Jalut treats.

The general observation is that a decade of factional survival was his real apprenticeship, and a man who had backed losing sides, been exiled and returned understood the system he would shortly be running better than any battlefield could teach him.

## Taking the Throne

The murder of the victorious sultan in 1260 is the ugliest episode in the article and it deserves plain treatment.

### How did Baybars come to power?

By assassinating the sultan who had just won at Ayn Jalut. On the return march to Cairo, roughly six weeks after the battle, a group of amirs including Baybars killed Qutuz during a hunting expedition. Baybars was acclaimed sultan by the commanders present and reached Cairo as the new ruler within days.

### Why did Baybars kill Qutuz?

The motive most sources give is that he had asked for the governorship of Aleppo and been refused, taking the refusal as a sign he would be sidelined. Older grievances are also cited. The deeper explanation is structural: a sultan owed his place to the amirs, and any amir strong enough to replace him had reason to try.

The mechanism is the one the article on who the Mamluks were describes at length. Personal loyalty attached to the patron who had purchased and freed a man, did not transfer to that patron's successor, and released a dead man's entire household into open competition with every other, which makes assassination the ordinary instrument of succession in this regime rather than an aberration requiring special explanation.

The acclamation by the commanders present is the constitutional element and it matters, since a Mamluk sultan held power by the agreement of the amirs and Baybars secured that agreement on the spot.

The absence of any consequence is what confirms the reading. Nobody avenged the murdered sultan, no faction anywhere took up his cause, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate describes a political system in which this outcome was entirely normal.

The historiographical problem this creates is severe and the article's later section on the sources treats it, since the surviving account of Ayn Jalut was written under the man who murdered its victor.

The comparison with the previous decade is what makes his reign remarkable rather than his accession. Five rulers in ten years had come and gone by very much the same route, and this one lasted seventeen years.

The general observation is that taking a throne in this system was easy and holding one was not, and the article's remaining sections are about the second problem rather than the first.

## The Caliph in Cairo

The most inventive political act of the reign came in its first year and it is the clearest evidence of the state builder rather than the soldier.

### Why did Baybars install an Abbasid caliph in Cairo?

To supply the legitimacy his regime otherwise lacked. The Mongols had destroyed the Abbasid caliphate in 1258, and a former slave who had murdered his predecessor needed authority beyond force. Installing a survivor of the Abbasid house in Cairo in 1261 gave him a caliph who could invest him formally.

The situation after 1258 was that the Sunni world had no caliph for the first time in five centuries, which the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry describes as the end of an institution that had held recognition without power for three hundred years.

The survivor produced in Cairo claimed Abbasid descent, and contemporaries questioned the claim, which the sources record without much embarrassment since the political usefulness did not depend on the genealogy.

The transaction was explicit. The caliph invested the sultan with authority over the Muslim lands, and the sultan housed, funded and controlled the caliph completely.

The irony the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry supplies is worth stating. The city built by the Fatimids as the seat of a caliphate opposed to Baghdad became the home of the Abbasid line instead, three centuries later and as a puppet.

The durability is the striking part, since the institution persisted for two and a half centuries and every Mamluk sultan was invested by it, which means an improvisation of 1261 became a permanent constitutional fixture.

The value delivered is what the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry calls a free endorsement, since it cost the sultan almost nothing and conferred a standing no amount of military success could.

The general observation is that a regime lacking hereditary or religious legitimacy will manufacture some, and installing a caliph one controls is among the more economical solutions any government has found to that problem.

## The Wars

The military record of the reign is substantial and it was conducted on several fronts simultaneously.

### How did Baybars defeat his enemies?

By fighting separate fronts with a professional standing army, never letting a coalition form against him, and combining campaigns with diplomacy. Against the Mongols he held a frontier and raided; against the crusaders he reduced strongholds one at a time; against the Ismailis he applied pressure until they submitted.

The Mongol front was defensive in character and it consumed the most attention, since the Persian Mongol power remained the principal threat throughout the reign.

### What alliances did Baybars make against the Mongols?

The most important was with the Mongol power of the northern steppe, which had converted to Islam and was hostile to its Persian cousins, and which also controlled the region supplying Egypt with Mamluk recruits. He also maintained relations with Italian maritime cities and with the Byzantine emperor, whose control of the straits was necessary for the recruitment trade.

The strategic logic of that alliance is worth stating plainly. The northern Mongols occupied the Persian Mongols' rear and controlled the slave supply, which makes the alliance simultaneously a military arrangement and a guarantee of the recruitment pipeline the article on who the Mamluks were describes.

The Byzantine relationship followed from the same requirement, since ships carrying purchased boys from the Black Sea had to pass the straits.

The crusader campaigns are treated in the article's next section, and the Anatolian and Nubian expeditions in a later one.

The general observation is that this was a coherent grand strategy rather than a series of campaigns, and a ruler simultaneously fighting Mongols, reducing crusader fortresses and negotiating with Constantinople to protect a slave supply route is operating at a level the popular image of a warrior sultan does not suggest.

## The Crusader Fortresses

The campaign against the remaining Latin states occupied fifteen years and its method is more interesting than any individual siege.

### Which crusader fortresses did Baybars capture?

A long series across the 1260s and 1270s, including inland castles of the military orders and coastal cities. Among the most significant were the great hospitaller fortress in the Syrian interior, the northern city of Antioch in 1268 and the Templar fortress taken in 1271. The reduction was methodical rather than a single campaign.

The method was sequential and it followed from the analysis the article on Saladin and the crusades supplies. Crusader strength rested on fortification and sea supply, so an attacker reduces the inland positions first, where relief by sea is impossible, and then closes on the coast.

The demolition policy is the distinctive element and the article on the Mamluk sultanate identifies it as the answer to the naval problem that had defeated Saladin. Captured coastal places were dismantled rather than garrisoned, since a ruined port cannot receive a fleet.

The fall of Antioch in 1268 was the largest single blow, ending a principality that had existed since the First Crusade, and the accounts of the sack are severe.

The treatment of garrisons varied and the article on Saladin and the crusades describes the general conventions, with surrender on terms in some cases and storm and massacre in others.

The contrast with Saladin is the one the article on Saladin as hero or conqueror weighs. Baybars was harder, took more, kept less and completed considerably more of the task, and no comparable reputation for clemency attaches to him.

The completion came after his death, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate records the final crusader positions falling in the 1290s, which means the campaign he began was finished by his successors.

The general observation is that the elimination of the crusader states was a fifteen year programme of siege work rather than a war of battles, and Baybars conducted it with a consistency that the article on crusader invasions of Egypt notes his predecessors never managed.

## The Assassins

The reduction of the Ismaili strongholds in Syria deserves its own section, since it removed a political factor that had operated for two centuries.

### How did Baybars treat the Assassins of Syria?

He reduced them to submission and then to dependence. Across the 1260s he pressured their mountain strongholds, extracted tribute, installed his own appointees over their affairs and by the early 1270s had taken the fortresses directly. The organization survived as a religious community and ceased entirely to be a political or military actor.

The background belongs to the article on Shia Islam and the Fatimid caliphate, which describes the schism of 1094 and the branch that rejected the Cairo line and established itself in Persia and Syria.

The method was pressure rather than a single campaign, applied over years, combining military threat with the manipulation of internal divisions.

The tribute reversal is the neat detail. A group that had extracted payments from neighbouring rulers for generations found itself paying them instead.

The reported employment of their methods by the sultan afterward appears in the sources and belongs to the category of claims that are plausible and unverifiable.

The wider significance is that a small organization with disproportionate political leverage was removed from the region's politics permanently, which changed the calculations of every ruler who had previously had to allow for it.

The Mongol parallel is worth noting, since the Persian branch had been destroyed by the Mongols a decade earlier, meaning the two great powers of the period eliminated the two halves of the same movement within fifteen years of each other.

The general observation is that this was a policing operation rather than a war, and its inclusion in the reign's record shows a ruler systematically removing every independent actor from the territory he claimed.

## The Reforms

The administrative work of the reign is the article's central claim and it is the part the popular image omits entirely.

### What reforms did Baybars make?

A comprehensive programme: reorganization and expansion of the army, a fast postal relay system linking Cairo to the Syrian frontier, systematic rebuilding of fortresses and city walls, a new fleet, repairs to canals and harbours, a reformed judiciary with chief judges of all four Sunni schools, and the reconstruction of the fiscal survey underlying the revenue assignments.

### How did Baybars strengthen the Mamluk state?

By building institutions that outlasted him. The postal system, the judicial arrangement, the fortress network, the fleet and the administrative reorganization were all still functioning generations later, which is the difference between a successful reign and a founding one. He also expanded the corps substantially through purchase.

### What was the Mamluk postal relay system?

A network of staging posts with fresh horses at intervals along fixed routes, connecting Cairo with Damascus and the Syrian frontier, carrying messages in days rather than weeks. It gave a sultan in Egypt current knowledge of a frontier hundreds of kilometres away, and it is the reign's least celebrated achievement.

The strategic value of the relay system is what makes it worth emphasizing. A state defending a distant frontier against a mobile enemy needs information faster than the enemy moves, and this is the instrument that made a Cairo based government able to respond to events on the Euphrates.

The pigeon post operated alongside it for the fastest messages, with towers and trained birds forming a second and quicker channel.

The four chief judges arrangement is the judicial reform and the article on religion and tolerance in Ayyubid Egypt explains why it mattered, since appointing chief judges of all four Sunni schools rather than one distributes the state's endorsement across the whole scholarly establishment.

The fiscal survey rebuilt the basis of the revenue assignments the article on the Mamluk sultanate describes, which is unglamorous and is what makes an army payable.

The fleet was built to address exactly the weakness the article on Saladin and the crusades identifies as structural, and the article on the Ayyubid economy explains the timber constraint that made it expensive.

The general observation is that this list is what the article's title claims. A man remembered for a battle spent seventeen years building a postal system, a judiciary, a fleet, a fortress network and a tax register, and those are what the sultanate ran on for the following two centuries.

## What He Built

The construction programme deserves separate treatment, since it is the physical record of the reign.

### What did Baybars build in Cairo and Damascus?

A large congregational mosque in Cairo bearing his name, a college attached to a shrine, extensive works at the citadel, and in Damascus a palace complex and other foundations. He also rebuilt fortifications across Syria at the places he had taken, repaired canals and harbours in Egypt, and endowed institutions throughout both countries.

The Cairo mosque is the principal surviving Egyptian monument of the reign, a large congregational building whose scale announces the standing of its founder.

The citadel works continued the process the article on the Citadel of Cairo describes, in which every regime holding Egypt rebuilt part of the complex.

The Syrian fortification programme was the largest expenditure, since holding a frontier against a Mongol power requires garrisoned strongpoints and the places captured from the crusaders had to be either rebuilt or dismantled.

The infrastructure works in Egypt included canal repair and harbour improvement, which the article on taxation describes as the standing requirement of a flood based agricultural system.

The endowment programme followed the pattern the article on who the Mamluks were explains, since a caste forbidden to found families founded institutions instead.

The Damascus foundations reflect where the reign's attention lay, since a sultan fighting on a northern frontier spent a great deal of time in Syria.

The general observation is that a building programme is a budget made visible, and the distribution of this one between Egyptian religious foundations and Syrian fortifications shows exactly where the reign's priorities sat.

## The Peripheral Campaigns

The expeditions beyond the main fronts are usually omitted and they show the reach the state had acquired.

### What were Baybars campaigns in Nubia and Anatolia?

Southward he intervened in Nubia, the Christian kingdom on the upper Nile, imposing tribute and installing a client ruler, which began a process of Egyptian domination that continued after his death. Northward he campaigned into Anatolia in 1277, defeating a Mongol force and briefly occupying territory before withdrawing, which was the deepest offensive of the reign.

The Nubian intervention is the less discussed of the two and it altered the southern frontier permanently, since the Christian kingdom that had faced Egypt since the seventh century began the decline that ended it.

The article on the Arab conquest of Egypt describes the earlier arrangement with Nubia, a negotiated agreement that had held for centuries and that this reign effectively ended.

The Red Sea and Arabian dimension accompanied it, and the article on the Ayyubid economy explains why the southern approaches mattered commercially.

The Anatolian campaign of 1277 was the reign's last, penetrating into territory the Persian Mongols dominated and defeating a force there.

The withdrawal that followed is the informative part, since holding Anatolian territory against the main Mongol power was not sustainable and he knew it, which makes the campaign a raid rather than a conquest.

The strategic purpose was demonstration rather than annexation, showing that the Mongol frontier could be crossed in both directions.

The death followed within months of the return, which the article's next section treats.

The general observation is that a state fighting Mongols in the north, crusaders on the coast and Nubians in the south simultaneously was operating at a scale no Egyptian regime had managed since the Ayyubids, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt notes that even they had not attempted all three at once.

## Death and What Followed

The end of the reign and its aftermath are the test of the founding claim, and the results are mixed.

### How did Baybars die?

At Damascus in 1277, shortly after returning from the Anatolian campaign, of an illness the sources describe variously. The most repeated account has him poisoned by a cup intended for someone else, which is a story of the kind that attaches to famous deaths. Other accounts give simple illness. He was in his fifties and had reigned for seventeen years.

The poisoning story is widely repeated and unverifiable, and the article on al-Hakim makes the general point about retrospective diagnosis at this distance.

The concealment of the death for some days follows the pattern the article on crusader invasions of Egypt describes for 1250, since a ruler's death announced at the wrong moment is worth an army to an enemy.

### Did Baybars secure the succession for his sons?

He tried and failed. His eldest son was proclaimed and reigned about two years before the amirs deposed him, and a younger son was installed briefly before a senior commander took the throne in 1279. The system made the attempt futile, since sons were outside the caste and the amirs owed them nothing.

The pattern is exactly the one the article on the Mamluk sultanate identifies as structural, in which a sultan's son serves as a transitional figure while the commanders settle the real succession among themselves.

The irony is complete, since the man who had murdered his own predecessor could not prevent the same logic operating against his children.

The successor who emerged in 1279 consolidated the position further and completed the crusader campaign, which means the state Baybars built survived the failure of his dynastic ambition.

The general observation is that the founding claim rests on the institutions rather than on the family, and the fact that the state functioned smoothly through the removal of his sons is evidence for the claim rather than against it.

## Record Against Legend

The separation of the documented career from the romance that grew around it is a recurring requirement in this subject.

### How is Baybars remembered in popular tradition?

Through a large cycle of popular romance that developed over later centuries and was performed across the Arabic speaking world. In it he becomes a folk hero, with adventures, companions and marvels that have almost nothing to do with the historical reign. The cycle is not evidence about the thirteenth century.

The mechanism is the same one the article on the legacy of Saladin describes at length, in which a reputation acquires a life independent of the person and is reshaped by the needs of each period that transmits it.

The difference is the register. The Saladin material developed in courtly and then in European literary traditions, while this cycle is popular, performed and vernacular.

The reasons he attracted it are recoverable. A man who rose from slavery, defeated Mongols and Christians, killed a sultan and ruled for seventeen years has the biography a folk hero requires, and his social origin makes him accessible in a way a hereditary prince is not.

The evidentiary status of the cycle is that it tells you about the centuries that produced it rather than about the reign, which is exactly what the article on the legacy of Saladin argues about a different body of material.

The contrast with the documented record is stark and it is this article's point. The romance concentrates on adventures; the record concentrates on postal relays, judicial appointments, fiscal surveys and siege programmes.

The reputation among historians has been consistently high, and the assessment as the effective founder of the sultanate is a scholarly judgment rather than a popular one.

The general observation is that a reign productive enough to justify a legend is usually productive in ways the legend ignores, and this is among the clearest instances available.

## The Sources

The evidence for the reign is abundant and its character requires careful statement.

### How reliable are the sources on Baybars?

Rich and systematically favourable. Two officials of his own chancery wrote accounts of the reign and the later Egyptian tradition drew heavily on them. The material is detailed, well informed and produced by men whose careers depended on him, so the procedure for reading a favourable tradition applies directly.

The two contemporary biographers were chancery officials, which means they had documentary access and an obvious interest.

The value of that access is high, since a chancery official can quote correspondence, describe appointments and date events precisely in a way an outside chronicler cannot.

The bias operates by selection and emphasis rather than by invention, which is the same finding the article on Saladin as sultan reaches about a different favourable tradition.

The Ayn Jalut problem is the sharpest instance and the article on that battle states it: the surviving account of the engagement was shaped under the man who murdered its victor, which means his role in it cannot be assessed independently.

The later compilations are extensive, and the fifteenth century Egyptian tradition this series uses throughout preserves a great deal drawn from the contemporary works.

The crusader and Armenian sources supply an independent perspective on the campaigns, which is the corroboration the article on Saladin and the crusades treats as the most valuable evidence available.

The Mongol and Persian material adds a third viewpoint on the northern frontier.

## The Founding Claim Examined

The article's thesis deserves testing rather than assertion, since 1250 is the conventional date and this article proposes a different one.

### Why was Baybars the greatest Mamluk sultan?

Because he converted a coup into a state. The sultanate had existed a decade and produced five rulers, chronic instability and no institutions. He held power seventeen years, installed the Cairo caliphate, built the postal system, reorganized army and judiciary, and left machinery that ran for two centuries.

### What did Baybars achieve?

Stopped the Mongol advance becoming a conquest, reduced the crusader states to a remnant, removed the Ismaili strongholds as a political factor, installed a caliph who legitimized every later sultan, built a communications network across two countries, rebuilt the army and fleet, and reformed the judiciary.

The case against the claim is worth stating, since 1250 is when the regime began and the article on the Mamluk sultanate dates it there.

The strongest version of that objection is that the succession system, which the article on the Mamluk sultanate identifies as the regime's defining solution, emerged from the practice of the whole period rather than from any one reign.

The second objection is that the great building sultan of the following century and the commander who completed the crusader campaign both have claims of their own.

The reply this article offers is that founding is different from building. The institutions a state runs on have to exist before anyone can improve them, and the postal system, the caliphate, the judicial arrangement, the fiscal survey and the reorganized army all date from these seventeen years.

The second reply is the test of survival. The removal of his sons within three years, described in the article's section on the succession, demonstrated that the machinery worked independently of the man, which is the definition of an institution.

The general observation is that the conventional periodization dates regimes from their first ruler and the useful question is when they became functional, and for the Mamluk sultanate those are ten years apart.

## Myths Worth Correcting

Six claims about Baybars circulate and each misleads.

The first is that he founded the Mamluk sultanate. The regime dates from 1250 and he came to power a decade later, which is why this article uses the phrase effective founder rather than founder.

The second is that he won Ayn Jalut. He commanded the vanguard and the sultan he later murdered commanded the army, and the article on that battle treats the whole question of attribution.

The third is that he expelled the crusaders. He reduced them to a coastal remnant across fifteen years and the final positions fell in the 1290s under his successors.

The fourth is that the Cairo caliph had authority. He had none whatever, and the article on the Fatimid and Abbasid rivalry describes exactly what a recognition without power is worth to the man who holds it and to the man who controls him.

The fifth is that he was primarily a warrior. The article's section on the reforms lists a postal network, a judiciary, a fleet, a fiscal survey and a fortress programme, all built in the same seventeen years.

The sixth is that the popular romance preserves anything about him. The cycle developed over subsequent centuries and is evidence about its own period rather than about the thirteenth century.

A seventh belief is a framing error rather than a myth, which is treating the murder of his predecessor as a stain requiring explanation. The article's section on his accession argues it was the ordinary mechanism of succession in a system where personal loyalty did not transfer, and five previous rulers had arrived and departed by the same route.

## The Verdict

The argument this article has defended throughout is that the state builder matters considerably more than the warrior, and that the seventeen years after 1260 are what the Mamluk sultanate actually rested on for the following two and a half centuries.

The accession was squalid and this article has not softened it anywhere. A man murdered the sultan who had just saved Egypt from the Mongols, six weeks after the battle that did it, on a hunting expedition during the march home, and was acclaimed sultan on the spot by the officers who had helped him do it. Nobody avenged the dead man and no faction took up his cause.

The system explains it rather than excusing it. The article on who the Mamluks were describes a bond of personal clientage that attached a freedman to the man who bought and freed him and that did not transfer to that man's successor, which leaves a regime with no mechanism for transferring loyalty and makes assassination the ordinary instrument of succession rather than an aberration requiring explanation.

The reign that followed is the entire case for the claim. Seventeen years of rule in a regime that had managed five rulers in the previous decade, and every one of those seventeen years spent building something that outlasted him.

The institutions are the evidence and the article's own section lists them: a caliph installed in 1261 who legitimized every subsequent sultan until 1517, a postal relay system giving a Cairo government current knowledge of a frontier six hundred kilometres distant, a pigeon post faster than anything else in the period, chief judges of all four Sunni schools, a rebuilt fleet, a fortress network across Syria, a restored fiscal survey and a corps expanded through continuous purchase.

The wars were conducted alongside all of it rather than instead of it, on three fronts simultaneously, with a diplomatic system reaching from the northern steppe to Constantinople to the Italian ports, protecting the slave supply route on which the entire arrangement depended.

The failure was dynastic and it was structurally inevitable. The article's section on the succession describes his eldest son deposed after two years and a younger son installed briefly as a placeholder, removed by exactly the same logic that had put their father on the throne, since a Mamluk's sons were born free and outside the caste and the amirs owed them nothing at all.

The proof of the founding claim is precisely that failure. A state that functions smoothly through the removal of its founder's entire family within three years, without disruption to its frontiers, its revenue, its courts or its communications, is a state running on institutions rather than a household running on a leader, and that distinction is the whole of what this article has been arguing.

The qualifications are those stated throughout and they are real. The sources were written by two officials of his own chancery, which makes the administrative detail excellent and the characterization worthless. His role at Ayn Jalut cannot be assessed independently, because the surviving account of the battle was shaped under the man who murdered its victor. The popular romance cycle is evidence about later centuries rather than about this reign. And the crusader campaign he conducted for fifteen years was completed by his successors in the 1290s rather than by him.

What remains is the observation this article opened with. The man who murdered the victor of Ayn Jalut, six weeks after the battle and on a hunting expedition, spent the next seventeen years building a postal relay system, appointing judges of all four legal schools, surveying land, rebuilding a fleet, fortifying a frontier and installing a caliph in Cairo. Every Mamluk sultan for the following two and a half centuries governed with the machinery he left behind. The regime is conventionally dated from 1250 because that is when the killing started, and it began working in 1260 because that is when somebody started building.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Sultan Baybars?

A Kipchak Turk born on the steppe north of the Black Sea, captured as a boy during the upheavals of the Mongol advance, sold through the slave trade and purchased into the household of the Ayyubid sultan of Egypt. He was among the corps that killed the last Ayyubid heir in 1250, commanded the vanguard at Ayn Jalut in 1260, murdered the victorious sultan six weeks later and took the throne himself. He reigned until 1277 and is generally regarded as the ruler who converted the Mamluk regime into a functioning state.

### Q: Why was Baybars the greatest Mamluk sultan?

Because he converted a military coup into a state. The sultanate had existed for a decade before him and had produced five rulers, chronic instability and almost no institutions. He held power for seventeen years, installed a caliph in Cairo who legitimized every subsequent sultan, built a postal relay network, reorganized the army and the judiciary, rebuilt the fleet and the fortress system, secured both frontiers and left administrative machinery that ran for two centuries afterward. Later sultans reigned longer or built more; none founded as much.

### Q: How did Baybars come to power?

By assassinating the sultan who had just won at Ayn Jalut. On the return march to Cairo, roughly six weeks after the battle, a group of amirs including Baybars killed Qutuz during a hunting expedition, and Baybars was acclaimed sultan on the spot by the commanders present. He reached Cairo as the new ruler within days. Nobody avenged the murdered sultan and no faction took up his cause, which is the clearest possible evidence that this was the system operating normally rather than an aberration.

### Q: What did Baybars achieve?

He stopped the Mongol advance becoming a Mongol conquest, reduced the crusader states to a coastal remnant, eliminated the Syrian Ismaili strongholds as a political factor, installed a caliph in Cairo who legitimized every Mamluk sultan until 1517, created a fast communications network spanning Egypt and Syria, rebuilt the army and the fleet, reformed the judiciary by appointing chief judges of all four Sunni schools, restored the fiscal survey underlying the revenue assignments, and gave an unstable military regime the institutional form it kept for two and a half centuries.

### Q: How did Baybars defeat his enemies?

By fighting on separate fronts with a professional standing army, never permitting a coalition against him, and combining campaigns with diplomacy. Against the Mongols he held a frontier, raided across it and allied with their northern rivals. Against the crusader states he reduced strongholds methodically over fifteen years, taking inland castles first where relief by sea was impossible and demolishing captured ports rather than garrisoning them. Against the Ismaili strongholds of Syria he applied steady pressure until they submitted and then took the fortresses.

### Q: How did Baybars die?

At Damascus in 1277, shortly after returning from his Anatolian campaign, of an illness the sources describe with conflicting detail. The most repeated account has him drinking poison from a cup intended for somebody else, which is exactly the kind of story that attaches to the deaths of famous men and cannot be verified. Other accounts give straightforward illness. He was in his fifties and had reigned seventeen years. The death was concealed for some days, following the same logic that had governed a comparable concealment in 1250.

### Q: What reforms did Baybars make?

A comprehensive programme carried out alongside continuous campaigning. He reorganized and expanded the army through large scale purchase of new Mamluks. He built a postal relay system with staging posts and fresh horses linking Cairo to the Syrian frontier. He rebuilt fortresses and city walls across Syria and Egypt. He constructed a new fleet. He repaired canals and harbours. He appointed chief judges of all four Sunni legal schools rather than one. And he restored the fiscal survey on which the whole revenue assignment system depended.

### Q: How did Baybars strengthen the Mamluk state?

By building institutions that outlasted him, which is the difference between a successful reign and a founding one. The postal system, the judicial arrangement, the fortress network, the fleet, the fiscal survey and the Cairo caliphate were all still functioning generations after his death. The clearest proof is what happened when he died: his sons were removed within three years by the amirs, and the state continued to function smoothly throughout, which is the definition of machinery working independently of the man who built it.

### Q: Why did Baybars install an Abbasid caliph in Cairo?

To manufacture the legitimacy his regime otherwise lacked entirely. The Mongols had destroyed the Abbasid caliphate at Baghdad in 1258, leaving the Sunni world without a caliph for the first time in five centuries, and a former slave who had murdered his predecessor needed authority beyond force. Installing a claimed survivor of the Abbasid house in Cairo in 1261 gave him a caliph who could invest him formally. Contemporaries questioned the man's descent, and the political usefulness never depended on the genealogy being sound.

### Q: What was the Mamluk postal relay system?

A network of staging posts with fresh horses at fixed intervals along established routes, connecting Cairo with Damascus and the Syrian frontier, capable of carrying a message in a few days rather than several weeks. A pigeon post with towers and trained birds operated alongside it for the most urgent traffic. Its purpose was strategic: a state defending a distant frontier against a highly mobile enemy needs information travelling faster than the enemy does, and this is what made a Cairo based government able to react to events on the Euphrates.

### Q: Which crusader fortresses did Baybars capture?

A long series across the 1260s and 1270s. Among the most significant were the Galilean city taken in 1263, the great hospitaller fortress in the Syrian interior, the northern city of Antioch in 1268, which ended a principality established during the First Crusade, and the Templar fortress taken in 1271. The reduction was methodical rather than a single campaign, working inland first where sea relief was impossible, and captured coastal places were systematically dismantled so that no fleet could ever use them again.

### Q: How did Baybars treat the Assassins of Syria?

He reduced them to submission and then to dependence. Across the 1260s he pressured their mountain strongholds, extracted tribute from a group that had spent generations extracting it from others, installed his own appointees over their affairs, and by the early 1270s had taken the fortresses outright. The organization survived as a religious community and ceased entirely to be a political or military actor. The Persian branch had been destroyed by the Mongols a decade earlier, so the two halves of the same movement fell within fifteen years of each other.

### Q: What alliances did Baybars make against the Mongols?

The most consequential was with the Mongol power of the northern steppe, which had converted to Islam and was hostile to its Persian cousins. That alliance served two purposes at once: it placed a threat in the Persian Mongols' rear, and it secured relations with the power controlling the region that supplied Egypt with Mamluk recruits. He also maintained relations with the Italian maritime cities that carried the trade and with the Byzantine emperor, whose control of the straits was necessary for any shipment from the Black Sea.

### Q: Where did Baybars come from before Egypt?

From the Kipchak steppe north of the Black Sea, captured as a boy during the disruption accompanying the Mongol advance and sold into the trade that supplied Egypt with military slaves. He passed through more than one owner and was reportedly rejected by one buyer because of a blemish in one eye, a detail widely repeated and belonging to the category of biography a successful career attracts. He was eventually purchased into the Ayyubid sultan's own corps, quartered on an island in the river.

### Q: Why did Baybars kill Qutuz?

The motive most sources give is that he had asked for the governorship of Aleppo and been refused, taking the refusal as evidence that the sultan meant to sideline him, and older grievances involving a previous sultan are also cited. The deeper explanation is structural rather than personal. A Mamluk sultan held his position by the agreement of the amirs who had made him, personal loyalty did not transfer between patrons, and any amir strong enough to replace him therefore had every incentive to try.

### Q: What did Baybars build in Cairo and Damascus?

In Cairo a large congregational mosque bearing his name, which is the principal surviving Egyptian monument of the reign, along with a college attached to a shrine and extensive works at the citadel. In Damascus a palace complex and other foundations, reflecting how much time a sultan fighting a northern frontier spent in Syria. Beyond the capitals he rebuilt fortifications across Syria at the places he had taken, repaired canals and harbours in Egypt, and endowed institutions throughout both countries.

### Q: How is Baybars remembered in popular tradition?

Through a large cycle of popular romance that developed over the following centuries and was performed and recited across the Arabic speaking world. In it he becomes a folk hero of enormous proportions, with adventures, loyal companions, disguises and marvels that have almost no connection to the historical reign. His biography suited the form perfectly: a man who rose from slavery, defeated Mongols and Christians, killed a sultan and ruled for seventeen years. The cycle is evidence about the centuries that produced it rather than about the thirteenth.

### Q: Did Baybars secure the succession for his sons?

He tried and failed, exactly as the system made inevitable. His eldest son was proclaimed and reigned about two years before the amirs deposed him, and a younger son was installed briefly as a placeholder before a senior commander took the throne outright in 1279. A Mamluk's sons were born free and Muslim and therefore outside the caste entirely, so the amirs owed them nothing. The irony is complete: the man who murdered his own predecessor could not prevent the same logic operating against his children.

### Q: What were Baybars campaigns in Nubia and Anatolia?

Southward he intervened in Nubia, the Christian kingdom on the upper Nile, imposing tribute and installing a client ruler, which began a process of Egyptian domination that continued after his death and ended an arrangement between Egypt and Nubia that had held since the seventh century. Northward he campaigned into Anatolia in 1277, defeating a Mongol force and briefly occupying territory before withdrawing. The withdrawal is the informative part: it was a demonstration that the frontier could be crossed rather than an attempt at conquest.

### Q: How reliable are the sources on Baybars?

Rich and systematically favourable. Two officials of his own chancery wrote accounts of the reign, which gives them documentary access, precise dating and an obvious interest in the subject, and the later Egyptian chronicle tradition drew heavily on them. The bias operates through selection and emphasis rather than invention. The sharpest instance is Ayn Jalut, since the surviving account of that battle was shaped under the man who murdered its victor, which means his role in it cannot be assessed independently of a source with a motive.
