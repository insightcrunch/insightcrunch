The Protection of Civil Rights Act is the statute that Article 17 of the Constitution ordered into existence, and it is close to a dead letter. The article abolishes untouchability, forbids its practice in any form, and declares that the enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law. The law it contemplated is this one, enacted in 1955 as the Untouchability (Offences) Act and renamed in 1976. It remains in force. Its offences describe, with uncomfortable precision, the ordinary transactions of Indian life: drawing water, entering a temple, buying goods, hiring a room, sitting in a classroom, taking a hospital bed. Its punishments for those offences are imprisonment measured in months and fines measured in hundreds of rupees, on a scale whose ceilings were set in 1955 and never raised. And in the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment's annual report on the working of the Act, the whole of India registered thirteen cases under it in 2022.

That combination, a fundamental right with a statute of its own, a live text, trivial sanctions and near-total disuse, is the subject of this article. The claim it defends is simple enough to be quoted: the law for the fundamental right is the one nobody uses. The statute has not been repealed, replaced or struck down. It has been bypassed, first by the passage of time, which left its fines behind, and then by the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act of 1989, which covers much of the same conduct with heavier punishment, compensation machinery and procedural protection, but does not cover all of it. The conduct that falls between the two statutes, exclusion without violence, refusal without an audience, discrimination that meets none of the later Act's conditions, is covered by the Constitution, covered by this Act, and in practice prosecuted under neither.

Three readers need this statute stated exactly, and the article is built to serve each of them. The person refused service at a shop, turned away from a hostel, denied a house or obstructed at a well needs to know which law covers the refusal, because the answer depends on conditions that are not obvious and the wrong statute is a lost case. The advocate needs the operative text, the presumption that assists proof of the ground, the forum the offences are tried in, and the points on which the case law has settled the Act's meaning. The researcher needs the disuse documented rather than asserted, with the figures tied to the official series that publishes them, and the coverage gap between this Act and the Act of 1989 drawn category by category. The article takes the three in that order of urgency: the statute first, the courts second, the record of use third, and the argument about what to do with a law nobody uses last.

## The constitutional command that this statute answers

Article 17 is short enough to quote entire. It provides that untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden, and that the enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law. Three features of that text drive everything in the statute that followed. The first is that the article does not define its central term. The word untouchability appears in quotation marks in the constitutional text, a signal the Constituent Assembly understood: the subject is not the literal act of touching or refusing to touch, but the practice as it had developed in India, the hereditary imposition of disabilities on communities placed at the bottom of the caste order. Neither the Constitution nor the Act of 1955 has ever supplied a definition, and the consequences of that silence run through the case law examined later in this article.

The second feature is the structure of the promise. The first sentence abolishes and forbids, without exception and without any qualifying words in the text. The second sentence does not itself punish anything: it declares that enforcement of a disability shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law, which is a direction to a legislature to make the law. Article 17 therefore contains its own incompleteness. Until Parliament legislated, the abolition was a constitutional fact without a criminal sanction behind it, and the disabilities it named could be enforced by private persons with no offence committed under any statute, however plain the constitutional prohibition.

The third feature is who may legislate. Article 35 of the Constitution reserves to Parliament, and withholds from the state legislatures, the power to make laws prescribing punishment for acts declared to be offences under Part III. The statement of objects and reasons appended to the Bill of 1955, signed by the Home Minister, Kailas Nath Katju, put the point plainly: no central law existed on the subject, and a law had to be enacted by Parliament as required by Article 35. The abolition of untouchability is thus one of the small number of fundamental rights whose penal enforcement the Constitution assigns to the Union alone, and the Act of 1955 is the direct product of that assignment.

The exclusivity of Article 35 has a consequence that is easy to miss. Because only Parliament may prescribe punishment for the enforcement of a disability arising out of untouchability, no state legislature can build a rival penal code on this ground, and the provincial statutes that existed when the Constitution commenced survived only until the central Act displaced them. The states retain the field of administration, welfare and prevention, and the Act of 1955 itself assigns them the implementation duties in Section 15A, but the criminal law of untouchability is a single national code by constitutional design. A refusal in any state in India is therefore governed by the same sections, the same penalties and the same presumption, a uniformity that the subject's history made necessary and that few other fields of Indian criminal law possess in the same degree.

Article 17 also sits in instructive company. It stands at the end of the equality code, Articles 14 to 18, and immediately before the freedoms of Article 19, and it shares its enforcement structure with the right against exploitation that follows it: Article 23, prohibiting human trafficking and forced labour, and Article 24, prohibiting child labour in hazardous work, are the other provisions the Supreme Court has treated as running against private persons. The three articles together form the Constitution's small horizontal family, guarantees aimed not at the state's own discrimination but at the private organisation of unfreedom, by caste, by trafficker and by employer. The difference within the family is instructive for this statute. Articles 23 and 24 acquired their working law piecemeal, in the bonded labour and child labour statutes of later decades, while Article 17 received its dedicated penal code within five years of the Constitution, from the first Parliament, as a matter of stated constitutional obligation. No fundamental right in Part III was implemented faster. None has been allowed to decay further from the purpose its text announces.

The Assembly had adopted the article, as draft Article 11, on 29 November 1948, and the adoption is remembered for the cries of "Mahatma Gandhi ki Jai" that greeted it, the only occasion on which the Assembly marked a clause in that way. The debate itself was brief and unusually consensual. A suggestion that the word unapproachability be added alongside untouchability, to catch the cognate practice of enforcing distance rather than contact, was made and not accepted. What the members understood themselves to be doing was not describing a ritual technicality. They were writing into the fundamental rights chapter a prohibition aimed at a social system, and they left the punishment, deliberately, to a law that took another six and a half years to arrive.

Those six and a half years matter to the shape of the Act. When the Constitution commenced in January 1950, the field was not empty: it was occupied by a patchwork of provincial statutes of uneven scope, most of them confined to particular disabilities such as temple entry or the use of wells, and many of them confined in terms to Hindus. The Act of 1955 had to displace that patchwork with a single code for the whole of India, and its Schedule, which repeals twenty-one provincial and princely-state enactments to the extent of their repugnancy, is the record of what it displaced. The statute is therefore best read not as an innovation of 1955 but as the national consolidation of a legislative effort that had been running, province by province, since the nineteen thirties.

## The road to 1955: provincial laws, a gap in the Constitution, and the Katju Bill

The provincial statutes that preceded the central Act were the work of the limited legislatures of the late colonial period and the first years of independence. Madras led with the Removal of Civil Disabilities Act of 1938, which attacked the exclusion of the depressed classes from public places and services within the province. Bombay followed with the Harijan (Removal of Social Disabilities) Act of 1946 and a separate Harijan Temple Entry Act of 1947. The United Provinces passed a Removal of Social Disabilities Act in 1947, Orissa a Removal of Civil Disabilities Act in 1946 and a Temple Entry Authorisation Act in 1948, Bihar a Harijan (Removal of Civil Disabilities) Act in 1949, and Mysore, the Central Provinces and Berar, East Punjab, West Bengal, Hyderabad, Madhya Bharat, Saurashtra, Travancore-Cochin and Coorg each enacted measures of the same family. The Schedule to the Act of 1955 lists them all, and the list itself is an education in the federal untidiness the Constitution's Article 35 was designed to end: twenty-one enactments, in force in different territories, with different catalogues of disability and different penalties, governing a practice the Constitution had just abolished for the whole country at a stroke.

Behind the provincial statutes stood a generation of agitation that the drafters of 1955 had lived through. The Vaikom Satyagraha of 1924 and 1925, fought over the right of avarna Hindus to use the public roads around the temple at Vaikom in Travancore, fixed the template: the contested ground was not the sanctum but the road, the well and the approach, the shared spaces of ordinary life. The Travancore Temple Entry Proclamation of 1936, by which the Maharaja threw open the state's temples to all Hindus, showed that executive action could abolish in a day what custom had maintained for centuries, and the provincial legislatures of the following decade converted that demonstration into statute, one disability at a time. The central Act of 1955 inherited both the template and the lesson. Its catalogue is a catalogue of approaches and shared spaces, roads, wells, conveyances, shops, schools, because that is where the practice had been fought, and its confidence that law could abolish the practice came from the provinces where law, backed by movement, already had.

The provincial laws had three limitations that the central Bill set out to cure, and the statement of objects and reasons names them with a drafter's economy. The Bill was not confined to Hindus, as most of the provincial measures had been: untouchability was to be an offence whoever practised it and, subject to the statute's terms, whoever suffered it. A person did not cease to count as a victim of the practice merely because he resided outside the locality in relation to which he had been specified in the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order of 1950, a trap in some of the older drafting that tied protection to a single district's notification. And the Bill criminalised not only the enforcement of disabilities but participation in the excommunication of, or the imposition of social disability on, a person who refused to practise untouchability or who acted in furtherance of the new law, closing the retaliation route by which communities policed their own members into enforcing the system. A fourth feature, noted in the same statement, added a deterrent with teeth of a practical kind: on conviction for an offence committed in the course of a profession, trade, calling or employment, the court could cancel or suspend the offender's licence.

The interval between the Constitution and the Act deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the interval the article's structure created. From January 1950 to May 1955, the abolition of untouchability was the law of the land and its enforcement was no offence under any central statute. Prosecutions for the enforcement of disabilities continued where provincial Acts supplied them, on the provincial catalogues and the provincial penalties, and the constitutional prohibition itself could be, and was, invoked in writ proceedings against public authorities. But a private person who closed a well or a temple door in a territory whose provincial law did not reach the act committed no offence known to a national code, and the anomaly was the sharper because Article 35 had, from the first day, forbidden the state legislatures to repair the gap with penal laws of their own. The five years are the measure of the new Parliament's other preoccupations, land reform, the Hindu code, the first general election, and they set the pattern the subject has followed since: constitutional priority, legislative delay.

Parliament enacted the Bill in the sixth year of the Republic. The Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955, Act 22 of 1955, received assent and was published on 8 May 1955, and it commenced on 1 June 1955, the date appointed by notification. Its preamble stated its business in the compressed style of the period: an Act to prescribe punishment for the practice of untouchability, for the enforcement of any disability arising therefrom, and for matters connected therewith. The twenty-one provincial enactments in the Schedule stood repealed to the extent to which they corresponded to, or were repugnant to, the new Act. What replaced them was a short statute of seventeen sections, organised not around the victim's identity but around the acts of exclusion themselves, and it is that organisation which still gives the Act its distinctive character when it is set beside the legislation of 1989.

One feature of the original settlement deserves emphasis before the provisions are examined, because later commentary persistently misstates it. The Act of 1955 was not a Scheduled Castes statute in its operative design. Its offences are framed around the ground of the act, untouchability, and most of them protect any person against whom a disability is enforced on that ground. The Scheduled Castes enter the text at specific points, in the insult offence, in the presumption, and in the definition inserted in 1976, but the architecture of Sections 3 to 6 is general. A refusal of entry to a place of public worship is an offence because of the ground on which the refusal rests, and the prosecution's burden is to prove that ground, a burden the statute assists with a presumption in defined circumstances but does not remove. The contrast with the Act of 1989, whose offences are built around the caste identity of victim and offender, is the single most consequential structural difference between the two statutes, and it returns throughout this article.

## How the Protection of Civil Rights Act is built

The statute is best understood as four concentric parts. At the centre are the offences, Sections 3 to 7A, which catalogue the disabilities whose enforcement is criminal. Around them sits a ring of deterrent and restorative devices: cancellation of licences, resumption of government grants, punishment for abetment including the deemed abetment of a neglectful public servant, an enhanced ladder for repeat offenders, and the power to impose a collective fine on a locality. Around that sits the procedural machinery: a presumption that assists proof of the ground, offences made cognizable and mostly triable summarily, a bar on civil courts recognising any custom that imposes a disability, and an overriding effect against inconsistent laws and decrees. And on the outside sits the one part of the Act that is not penal at all, Section 15A, which obliges state governments to take positive measures, legal aid, supervising officers, special courts, committees, surveys and the identification of areas where disabilities persist, so that the rights the offences protect are actually availed of. Most accounts of the Act read only the centre. The disuse examined later in this article is a failure of all four rings at once.

The definitions in Section 2 do more work than definitions usually do. Civil rights, a term the amendment of 1976 wrote into the Act when it also supplied the new title, means any right accruing to a person by reason of the abolition of untouchability by Article 17. The definition is circular in form and substantive in effect: it ties every right the Act protects back to the constitutional abolition, so that the statute's reach is exactly the reach of Article 17 and neither wider nor narrower. A place of public worship is defined by use rather than by ownership or denomination, and the definition expressly includes a privately owned place of worship which is in fact allowed by its owner to be used as a place of public worship, together with its appurtenant lands and subsidiary shrines. A shop is defined to include any place from which goods are sold by a hawker, a vendor or a mobile van, a laundry and a hair-cutting saloon, and any other place where services are rendered to customers, which is why the barber and the washerman, the two figures who recur in every account of village untouchability, are inside the Act from its second section onward.

The remaining definitions and the closing provisions complete the scheme. A place includes a house, building and other structure and premises, and also a tent, a vehicle and a vessel, so that the Act follows its subject into the temporary and the mobile: the travelling theatre, the ferry, the bus. Offences committed by companies are dealt with on the standard pattern, every person in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the business being deemed guilty unless he proves the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it, with directors, managers and officers who consented to the offence liable in their own right. The Central Government makes the rules, and made them in 1977. And Section 17, with the Schedule, performs the Act's act of succession, repealing the twenty-one provincial enactments to the extent of their correspondence or repugnancy, so that the statute book carries one law of untouchability where it had carried a shelf of them.

### What counts as a shop under the Act?

A shop means any premises where goods are sold wholesale or by retail, and the definition is then widened to include a place from which goods are sold by a hawker or vendor or from a mobile van or cart, a laundry, a hair-cutting saloon, and any other place where services are rendered to customers.

The widening is deliberate and it is the part of the definition that matters in practice. The refusal that untouchability most often produced in a village was not a refusal at a department store counter; it was the barber who would not cut hair, the washerman who would not take clothes, the tea-stall keeper who kept a separate set of cups. A definition confined to premises selling goods would have missed the service economy in which the practice actually lived, and the drafters of 1955, following the provincial Acts, wrote the definition to catch the transaction rather than the building. The same instinct appears in the definition of a hotel, which includes a refreshment room, a boarding house, a lodging house, a coffee house and a cafe, and in Section 4, which protects the use of utensils and other articles kept in a public restaurant, hotel, dharamshala, sarai or musafirkhana for the use of the general public. The separate cup and the separate plate were not incidental humiliations; they were the daily machinery of the system, and the statute names them.

### Does a temple have to be publicly owned for the Act to protect entry to it?

No. A place of public worship is defined by the use to which it is in fact put, not by ownership. The definition covers any place used as a place of public religious worship, and it expressly includes a privately owned place that its owner in fact allows to be used as a place of public worship.

The point decides real cases, because the exclusion that matters is usually enforced at a temple that is nobody's public property in the conveyancing sense: a village shrine maintained by a family or a caste body, a math with hereditary trustees, a festival pandal on private land. If the owner holds the place out for public worship, Section 3 treats entry to it as a public right and prevention of entry on the ground of untouchability as an offence, whatever the title deeds say. The 1976 amendment reinforced the point by writing the privately owned temple, with its lands and subsidiary shrines, into the definition itself. Ownership remains relevant elsewhere in the Act, in the provision for resumption of government grants to institutions whose managers are convicted, but it is not a condition of the entry offence.

Two administrative facts complete the architecture. The Act extends to the whole of India, and it is implemented, as the Ministry's own chapter on the statute records, by the state governments and the Union territory administrations: the police who register its offences, the prosecutors who conduct its trials and the departments that run its Section 15A measures are state instrumentalities working a central code. That division, central text and state machinery, is the standard Indian pattern for criminal law, but it has a particular consequence here. The quality of the Act's enforcement is the sum of twenty-eight and more separate administrative decisions about priority, staffing and attention, and the annual report's state-by-state returns are the record of those decisions. A national right is thus executed at district level, and it is at district level, in the police station and the magistrate's court, that its fortunes are decided.

## The offences of exclusion: Sections 3 and 4

Section 3 punishes the enforcement of religious disabilities, and its two limbs correspond to the two things a person does in a place of worship: go in, and worship. Whoever, on the ground of untouchability, prevents any person from entering any place of public worship which is open to other persons professing the same religion or any section of it commits the first limb. Whoever prevents a person from worshipping, offering prayers or performing any religious service in such a place, or from bathing in, or using the waters of, any sacred tank, well, spring or watercourse, river or lake, or bathing at any ghat of such a tank, watercourse, river or lake, in the same manner and to the same extent as is permissible to others of the same religion, commits the second. An explanation then does quiet but important work: persons professing the Buddhist, Sikh or Jaina religion, and persons professing the Hindu religion in any of its forms or developments, including Virashaivas, Lingayats, Adivasis, the followers of Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samaj and the Swaminarayan Sampraday, are deemed to be Hindus for the purposes of Sections 3 and 4. The deeming prevents the defence that a temple open to Hindus generally was not open to the complainant because his tradition was somehow a different religion from the one the temple served.

The comparative structure disposes, without further argument, of the defence of separate equality. The practice the Act was written against rarely announced itself as exclusion; it announced itself as separation, the separate tap beside the common tap, the separate cup in the tea stall, the doorway at the side of the temple, the hour fixed for the use of the well after others had finished. Section 3's formula, in the same manner and to the same extent as is permissible to other persons, and Section 4's parallel drafting, deny the defence its premise: a facility held out to the public is used on the public's terms or it is not used lawfully at all, and a separate facility assigned on the ground of untouchability is the enforcement of a disability however clean the water in it. The same logic reaches the timed exclusion, the procession routed away from the main street, the ceremony permitted only after dark. Manner and extent are the operative words, and they convert what would otherwise be a litigation about facilities into a litigation about treatment, which is the litigation the Act intends.

The measure of the offence in Section 3 is comparative. The Act does not create a free-standing right to enter every sacred place; it forbids a differential. If others professing the same religion may enter, worship and use the waters, the complainant may do so in the same manner and to the same extent, and prevention on the ground of untouchability is the crime. That comparative structure runs through the whole statute and explains both its strength and its limit. The strength is that the prosecution need not litigate theology: it need only show the practice open to others and closed to the complainant on the prohibited ground. The limit is that the Act polices exclusion from what exists; it does not command the creation of access where a facility is genuinely closed to everyone, and it does not reach a purely private devotion that is never held out to any public at all.

Section 4 is the statute's longest offence and its social core, punishing whoever, on the ground of untouchability, enforces against any person any disability across eleven heads. Read them in groups and the design becomes clear. The first group is commerce and hospitality: access to any shop, public restaurant, hotel or place of public entertainment, and the use of any utensils and other articles kept in such places for the use of the general public. The second group is livelihood: the practice of any profession, or the carrying on of any occupation, trade or business, or employment in any job, to which the 1976 amendment added the employment words expressly. The third group is the commons: the use of or access to any river, stream, spring, well, tank, cistern, water-tap or other watering place, any bathing ghat, burial or cremation ground, any sanitary convenience, any road or passage, or any other place of public resort which other members of the public have a right to use. The fourth group is collective provision: places used for a charitable or public purpose maintained wholly or partly out of state funds or dedicated to the use of the general public, and the enjoyment of any benefit under a charitable trust created for the general public. The fifth group is movement and settlement: the use of any public conveyance, and the construction, acquisition or occupation of any residential premises in any locality whatsoever, a head whose importance has grown with every decade as exclusion migrated from the well to the housing market. The sixth group is shelter and ceremony: the use of any dharamshala, sarai or musafirkhana open to the general public, the observance of any social or religious custom, usage or ceremony, or taking part in, or taking out, any religious, social or cultural procession. And the last head is the smallest and the most telling: the use of jewellery and finery.

The jewellery head repays a pause, because it shows what kind of document Section 4 is. A legislature drafting in abstractions would have written a general clause against discrimination and stopped. The drafters of 1955 had before them the actual inventory of humiliation, gathered from the provincial Acts and from the record of the practice, and they wrote the inventory down: the bridegroom who could not wear ornaments or ride in procession, the family served in a separate cup, the man refused a seat in the conveyance, the mourner turned away from the cremation ground. An explanation added in 1976 then generalises what the inventory implies, providing that enforcement of a disability includes any discrimination on the ground of untouchability. The section is therefore both a list and a principle, and a court asked to apply it to a form of exclusion the list does not name has the explanation to work with, a resource the courts have used more sparingly than the text invites.

Worked through ordinary situations, the two sections show their reach. A family draws water at the village well in the morning and is driven off by men of the dominant community who say the well is not for them: the prevention is Section 3 if the well is a sacred source, Section 4 in any case, and the prosecution is assisted by the presumption if the family belongs to a Scheduled Caste. A bridegroom of the same community is stopped on the road because his procession is wearing the finery and following the route that custom reserves: Section 4, in its procession and jewellery heads, is the charge. A tenant is told, after paying a deposit, that the flat has gone to someone else, in a building where flats are let every month: if the refusal rests on the ground of untouchability, the occupation head of Section 4 and, where the refusal forms part of an organised shunning, the boycott offence in Section 7 are the provisions, and the evidentiary contest will be over the ground, which is why the drafters built the presumption. A student is admitted to the school and then made to sit apart, eat apart and draw water from a separate vessel: Section 5's second limb, discrimination after admission, is written for her. None of these situations requires violence, a weapon, an injury or an audience, and that is precisely the territory the Act of 1989 does not occupy.

## Institutions and traders: Sections 5 and 6

Section 5 addresses the institutions through which the state and charitable society deliver their services. Whoever, on the ground of untouchability, refuses admission to any person to any hospital, dispensary, educational institution or any hostel established or maintained for the benefit of the general public or any section of it, commits an offence; and so does whoever, having admitted the person, does any act which discriminates against that person after admission. The second limb is the subtler and in some settings the more important. The ward in which a patient is placed, the bench on which a student is made to sit, the food a hostel serves and the order in which it serves it, are the places where exclusion survives formal admission, and the drafters saw it. The section's condition is that the institution serve the general public or a section of it; a genuinely private establishment maintained for a closed circle stands outside this section, though a refusal animated by untouchability may still be reached by Sections 4 or 7 in an appropriate case.

Section 6 is the trader's offence, and its drafting is a small lesson in how discrimination actually operates. It punishes whoever, on the ground of untouchability, refuses to sell any goods or refuses to render any service to any person at the same time and place and on the same terms and conditions at or on which such goods are sold or services are rendered to other persons in the ordinary course of business. The qualifier does the work. A trader rarely announces a refusal; he announces a price, a delay, a condition, a sudden want of stock. Section 6 fixes the comparison that exposes the device: the goods were sold to others, at that time, in that place, on those terms, in the ordinary course of business. If they were, the differential treatment of the complainant is the offence, and the trader's stock explanation becomes a question of fact rather than a formula of escape. Section 8 then attaches a consequence to this offence that no fine could match in deterrent effect, and it is examined with the penalty architecture below: the court that convicts a licence-holder under Section 6 for an offence committed in relation to his licensed profession, trade, calling or employment may direct that the licence stand cancelled or be suspended, and the direction has effect as if the licensing authority itself had passed it.

The settings of Sections 5 and 6 explain why their offences are so rarely the subject of reported litigation even where the conduct is common. A hospital that refuses a bed, a school that refuses a child, a trader who refuses a sale, each acts inside an institution with records, registers and alternative explanations, and the complainant is usually a person with the least capacity to sustain a prosecution against the institution he must continue to use. The sections also interact with the general law of the subject in a way worth noticing: a refusal of admission to a state institution may also be challenged as a violation of the equality articles by writ, and the writ route, with its declaration and its publicity, is often the sharper instrument against a public body. The criminal sections retain their force against the private institution and the private trader, against whom no writ lies, and it is there, in the unaided school, the private nursing home and the shop, that Sections 5 and 6 do the work no other provision does.

## The offences of enforcement and retaliation: Section 7

Sections 3 to 6 punish the person who closes a door. Section 7 punishes the wider repertoire by which the system defended itself: obstruction, molestation, boycott, propaganda and revenge. Its first limb is a residuary offence of obstruction. Whoever prevents any person from exercising any right accruing to him by reason of the abolition of untouchability under Article 17 commits an offence, whether or not the particular door he closed appears in the earlier sections. The second limb reaches conduct aimed at the exercise itself: whoever molests, injures, annoys, obstructs or causes or attempts to cause obstruction to any person in the exercise of any such right, or molests, injures, annoys or boycotts any person by reason of his having exercised any such right. The verbs are deliberately ordinary. Molestation and annoyance are not terms of art, and their breadth is the point: the section is aimed at the campaign of pinpricks, the blocked path, the stones on the roof, the abuse at the well, by which a formal right is made unusable without any single act grand enough to found another charge.

Boycott receives a statutory definition, because boycott was the system's enforcement arm. A person is deemed to boycott another when he refuses to let a house or land to that person, refuses to permit him to use or occupy any house or land, refuses to deal with him, work for hire for him, or do business with him, refuses to render to him or receive from him any customary service, or refuses to do any of those things on the terms on which they would commonly be done in the ordinary course of business; and also when he abstains from such social, professional or business relations as he would ordinarily maintain with that person. Two consequences follow for the coverage map drawn later in this article. A landlord's refusal to let a house, where it is part of a boycott of a person for exercising his rights or is itself the enforcement of a disability under Section 4, is inside this Act and inside no successor statute. And the economic siege, the refusal of the whole village to trade, hire or serve, is an offence in itself under the Act of 1955, committed by each participant.

The economics of the boycott explain why the drafters treated it as a principal offence rather than an aggravating circumstance. In the village economy the Act addressed, services ran through hereditary and customary channels: the barber, the washerman, the carpenter, the labourer and the creditor were bound to households by arrangements older than any contract, and withdrawal of those services was a sentence of economic death that required no violence and left no wound to exhibit in court. A boycott organised by a dominant community could starve a family out of a village in a season, and it could be enforced, in its turn, by the threat of the same treatment against any villager who broke ranks and dealt with the boycotted. Section 7 answers that system at each of its joints: the refusal to deal is an offence, the molestation of the person who exercises his rights is an offence, and the excommunication of the insider who refuses to join the enforcement is an offence. The architecture is complete. Its disuse is not a drafting failure.

The third limb of Section 7 is a speech offence of a carefully specified kind. Whoever, by words spoken or written, or by signs or visible representations or otherwise, incites or encourages any person, or class of persons, or the public generally to practise untouchability in any form whatsoever commits an offence. A second explanation, added in 1976, specifies what incitement includes: a person is deemed to incite or encourage the practice of untouchability if he directly or indirectly preaches untouchability or its practice in any form, or if he justifies, whether on historical, philosophical or religious grounds, or on the ground of any tradition of the caste system, or on any other ground, the practice of untouchability in any form. The provision is the Act's answer to the sermon and the pamphlet, and its justification limb is drafted to foreclose the standard defence in advance: that the speaker was expounding scripture, history or custom rather than advocating a practice. Expounding that justifies the practice is the offence. The fourth limb, also a product of the Act's development, punishes whoever insults or attempts to insult, on the ground of untouchability, a member of a Scheduled Caste. It is the one offence in Sections 3 to 7 framed around the victim's membership of a Scheduled Caste rather than around the ground alone, and it is the direct ancestor of the insult offences that the Act of 1989 later rebuilt with a public-view condition and far heavier punishment.

Two further provisions complete Section 7, and both are aimed at retaliation. Sub-section (1A), inserted in 1976, provides that whoever commits any offence against the person or property of an individual as a reprisal or revenge for his having exercised a right accruing from the abolition of untouchability shall, where the offence is punishable with imprisonment for a term exceeding two years, be punishable with imprisonment for a term of not less than two years and also with fine. The drafting is parasitic and intelligent: it takes the grave offences of the general penal code, arson, grievous hurt and their companions, and where such an offence is committed as revenge for the exercise of an Article 17 right, it prescribes a floor of two years beneath the sentence. The reprisal provision's practical career illustrates a recurring fate of parasitic offences. The grave offences it builds on, the arson that burns the hut of the man who asserted his rights, the assault that answers his assertion, are prosecuted under the general penal code in their own right and, where the victim is a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe, under the Act of 1989 as well. The Section 7(1A) charge adds a floor to a sentence the court is already empowered to set well above it, and a prosecutor framing the graver charges gains nothing by adding the parasitic one, whose distinct element, that the offence was revenge for the exercise of an Article 17 right, is one more fact to prove for no additional consequence. The provision therefore sleeps, not because reprisals are rare but because its drafting made it strictly dominated by the charges it accompanies. It stands in the Act as a lesson in penalty design that the rest of the statute repeats: a provision whose consequence is everywhere exceeded by a neighbouring provision will be used nowhere.

Sub-section (2) turns inward, to the community that polices itself. Whoever denies to a person belonging to his own community or any section of it any right or privilege to which that person would be entitled as a member of the community, or takes part in the excommunication of such a person, on the ground that he has refused to practise untouchability or has done any act in furtherance of the objects of the Act, commits an offence. The caste council that expelled the member who would not enforce the code was, in 1955, a familiar institution; the Act made its characteristic act a crime, and the provision remains the only one in Indian statute law addressed to excommunication as an instrument of caste discipline.

## The deemed offence: compelled labour under Section 7A

Section 7A, inserted by the amendment of 1976, is the provision most often omitted from summaries of the Act, and its omission distorts the map of the sanitation cluster. It provides that whoever compels any person, on the ground of untouchability, to do any scavenging or sweeping, or to remove any carcass, or to flay any animal, or to remove the umbilical cord, or to do any other job of a similar nature, shall be deemed to have enforced a disability arising out of untouchability. The deeming is the operative move. Compelling a person to carry night soil or to drag a dead animal from a village street is not, in the ordinary grammar of Sections 3 and 4, the enforcement of a disability against that person; it is the imposition of a burden. The 1976 legislature, acting on the Elayaperumal Committee's account of how the practice survived, declared the burden to be a disability by statute, and so brought the hereditary assignment of degrading work inside the Act's central concept. An explanation widens the compulsion element in the direction the evidence required: compulsion includes a threat of social or economic boycott. No hand need be laid on the person compelled; the threat that his family will be cut off from work, credit and custom is compulsion enough.

The five tasks the section names are a map of the hereditary degrading labour the practice assigned. Scavenging and sweeping are the sanitation tasks, the carrying of night soil and the cleaning of streets and latrines, imposed on the communities at the bottom of the order as a birthright in reverse. Removal of a carcass and flaying an animal are the dead-animal economy of the village, the disposal of fallen cattle and the recovery of hides, work treated as polluting and reserved to the same hands, on which the leather trade rested. Removal of the umbilical cord is the work of the traditional birth attendant, the dai, whose attendance at childbirth across the village was compulsory in custom and untouchable in status. The closing words, or any other job of a similar nature, gather the rest. What unites the list is that none of it is wage labour freely chosen: each task descended by birth, and refusal was met by the sanctions of boycott and force that the explanation writes into the section itself.

The penalty marks the provision's seriousness within the Act's modest scale: imprisonment for a term of not less than three months and not more than six months, with fine, the highest minimum the Act prescribes for a first offence. The provision also has a lineage forward. The hereditary assignment it criminalises was later addressed as an employment practice by the statute of 1993 and then by the Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act of 2013, whose scheme of identification, prohibition and rehabilitation is treated in the [manual scavenging prohibition statute](/2017/04/15/manual-scavenging-act-2013-explained/), and as an atrocity by the Act of 1989, which makes it an offence to make a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe do manual scavenging or to employ or permit the employment of such a member for that purpose. The Supreme Court's decision in Safai Karamchari Andolan v. Union of India, which directed compensation of ten lakh rupees to the families of persons who had died in sewer lines since 1993, belongs to that later line of authority. Section 7A is where the line begins, and it begins with a fiction, the deemed disability, that allowed a statute drafted around exclusion to reach exploitation.

## The penalty scale: the central fact of the Act

Every account of this statute eventually arrives at its punishments, and the punishments explain more of its fate than any other single feature. As enacted in 1955, the offences in Sections 3 to 7 were punishable with imprisonment which could extend to six months, or with fine which could extend to five hundred rupees, or with both. There was no minimum. A court could impose a fine alone, and a small one, and for the first two decades of the Act's life the reported pattern of sentencing sat at the bottom of even that modest range. The maximum fine of five hundred rupees was fixed in the money of 1955, and the maximum term of six months matched the statute's conception of its offences as petty, a conception the provincial Acts had shared.

The amendment of 1976 rebuilt the penalty clauses without touching their ceilings. In place of the old alternative formulation, each offence section prescribes imprisonment for a term of not less than one month and not more than six months, and also with fine which shall be not less than one hundred rupees and not more than five hundred rupees. The changes are three, and each was aimed at a documented failure. A floor of one month's imprisonment ended the practice of treating conviction as a formality discharged by a nominal fine. The conjunction, and also with fine, made imprisonment and fine cumulative rather than alternatives, so that a court may not choose the fine alone. And a floor of one hundred rupees on the fine ended the derisory nominal penalties that the Elayaperumal Committee's review of the Act's working had recorded. Section 7A, inserted by the same amendment, carries a higher floor of three months. What the amendment did not do is as important: six months and five hundred rupees remained the maxima, and they remain the maxima, unrevised by any later Parliament. The accurate statement of the position, which the series record in the social justice guide states and which this article repeats, is that the monetary and custodial scale of the Act in its operative form dates from the amending Act 106 of 1976, built inside ceilings inherited from 1955, and that no figure in it has moved since.

A word about the fine's real weight explains how a scale can decay without any legislator touching it. Five hundred rupees in 1955 was a sum of consequence for the persons this Act prosecutes, a village trader, a temple functionary, a small landholder: it represented months of income, and a court imposing the maximum was imposing a serious penalty by the standards of the economy around it. The figure was then left in the text while the economy moved. Decade by decade the same words came to denote less, until the maximum fine for enforcing a disability abolished by the Constitution stood below the price of the meal service whose refusal might occasion it. No Parliament voted for that reduction; it accumulated by silence, which is why the disuse of the Act cannot be laid at the door of any single decision. The custodial ceiling decayed more slowly, six months being six months in any decade, but the comparative decay was as severe: the offences created around it, in the Act of 1989 and elsewhere, rose to years, and a maximum that had once marked the offence as serious came to mark it as petty by comparison with its neighbours.

For repeat offenders, Section 11 prescribes an ascending ladder. A person already convicted of an offence under the Act, or of abetting one, who is again convicted shall be punishable for the second offence with imprisonment for a term of not less than six months and not more than one year, and also with fine of not less than two hundred rupees and not more than five hundred rupees; and for the third offence, or any offence subsequent to the third, with imprisonment for a term of not less than one year and not more than two years, and also with fine of not less than five hundred rupees and not more than one thousand rupees. The ladder is the Act's only route to a sentence measured in years, and it requires two prior convictions under a statute under which, as the disuse figures below show, convictions of any kind have become rare enough to count individually in the annual report.

Around the fines and terms sit two sanctions of a different character, which the drafters plainly expected to do the deterrent work in commercial settings. Section 8, already noticed, empowers the convicting court, where the offender is convicted under Section 6 and holds a licence for the profession, trade, calling or employment in relation to which the offence was committed, to direct cancellation or suspension of the licence, permit or permission, with the same effect as an order of the licensing authority. For a hotel keeper, a transport operator or a licensed trader, the licence is the business, and the threat of losing it speaks a language that a five hundred rupee fine does not. Section 9 addresses institutions: where the manager or trustee of a place of public worship, an educational institution or a hostel in receipt of a grant of land or money from the Government is convicted of an offence under the Act, and the conviction is not reversed or quashed on appeal or revision, the Government may direct the suspension or resumption of the whole or any part of the grant. Two further provisions harden the scheme at its edges. Abetment of any offence is punishable with the punishment provided for the offence itself, and Section 16A, inserted in 1976, excludes the Probation of Offenders Act, 1958 for any person above the age of fourteen years found guilty under this Act, so that a convicted adult may not be released on probation in lieu of the sentence the Act prescribes.

Set against the Act of 1989, whose principal offences carry imprisonment of not less than six months extending to five years with fine, and which adds compensation to the victim as a statutory incident of conviction, the scale of 1955 and 1976 explains the migration of practice examined below without any need to suppose bad faith in any complainant or officer. A statute whose maximum for a first offence equals the other statute's minimum has priced its own offences as trivial, whatever the constitutional rank of the right behind them.

The sentencing pattern under the Act completes the picture of its deterrent economy. Because the 1976 amendment fixed a floor of one month's imprisonment with a fine, and because the offences sit at the petty end of the criminal calendar, the sentences that courts actually impose cluster at the statutory floor: the conviction in Appa Balu Ingale itself, for preventing a community from drawing water at the point of a threatened gun, was one month's simple imprisonment and a fine of one hundred rupees each, and the Supreme Court restored that sentence without enlarging it, the Act giving it no room to do more for a first offence. A deterrent structure whose typical output for armed exclusion is a month in prison and a hundred rupees is not merely lenient; it signals to every participant in the system, complainant, police officer, prosecutor and accused, the official valuation of the wrong, and the signal is received. The valuation, and not any defect in the offence definitions, is what the reform debate at the end of this article has to answer.

## Proof, presumption and the conduct of the trial

The element every prosecution under this Act must establish, and the element most difficult to prove, is the ground: that the disability was enforced on the ground of untouchability. Motive of that kind is rarely announced in terms, and the statute meets the difficulty with a presumption. Section 12 provides that where any act constituting an offence under this Act is committed in relation to a member of a Scheduled Caste, the court shall presume, unless the contrary is proved, that such act was committed on the ground of untouchability. The presumption has stood in the Act, in substance, from 1955; the amendment of 1976 tidied its text by removing an internal cross-reference to the constitutional definition clause. Its operation is precise and limited. It does not presume the act, which the prosecution must prove in the ordinary way: the refusal, the prevention, the insult must be established by evidence. What it presumes, once the act is proved and proved in relation to a member of a Scheduled Caste, is the ground, and it places on the accused the burden of proving the contrary. In a village setting, where the refusal is real but its reason is contested between caste and some asserted commercial or personal ground, Section 12 is frequently the whole case, and the drafting choice to include it in 1955 shows that the first legislature understood the evidentiary problem as clearly as the committees that later reported on it.

The presumption also defines, by its limits, a gap in the Act's protection. It is engaged only where the act is committed in relation to a member of a Scheduled Caste. The offences themselves are not so confined, since disabilities may be enforced on the ground of untouchability against persons who are not members of a Scheduled Caste within the constitutional definition, converts and members of communities outside the Schedule among them, but a complainant outside the Schedule must prove the ground without the presumption's help. The limit deserves its own analysis, because it interacts with the religious condition of the Scheduled Castes Order. Membership of a Scheduled Caste under the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order, 1950 is confined, by the Order's own terms as amended over the years, to persons professing the Hindu, Sikh or Buddhist religion. A person who is treated as untouchable after conversion to another faith may therefore stand outside the Schedule, outside the presumption, and outside the Act of 1989 altogether, while remaining squarely inside the offences of the Act of 1955, which punish the enforcement of the disability on its ground and do not ask who the victim is. For that complainant, the older statute is not the weaker alternative; it is the only statute, and its ground-based design, so often treated as an archaism beside the identity-based design of 1989, is the feature that keeps the door open. The rebuttal side of the presumption has its own discipline: the contrary must be proved, on the civil standard, by the accused, and a bare assertion of a commercial reason will not discharge it where the pattern of the refusal tracks the pattern of the practice.

Section 13 completes the proof architecture from the civil side. No civil court may entertain or continue any suit or proceeding, or pass or execute any decree or order, if the claim, the decree or the execution would in any way be contrary to the provisions of the Act; and no court shall, in adjudicating any matter or executing any decree, recognise any custom or usage imposing any disability on any person on the ground of untouchability. The second limb is a small constitutional revolution confined to one section: custom, elsewhere a source of law the courts administer, is denied all recognition where it imposes the disability the Constitution abolished, and a decree founded on such a custom cannot be executed. Section 16 gives the Act overriding effect against inconsistent laws, customs, usages, instruments and decrees to the same end.

The procedure is built for speed, and its design tells its own story about what went wrong. Section 15, substituted in 1976, provides that every offence punishable under the Act shall be cognizable, and that every such offence, except where it is punishable with a minimum term of imprisonment exceeding three months, may be tried summarily by a Judicial Magistrate of the first class or, in a metropolitan area, by a Metropolitan Magistrate. Cognizability means the police may arrest without warrant and must register and investigate the complaint as a matter of course, a status the Act has carried since 1955 and one of the features its statement of objects advertised. Summary trial means the case is meant to be short: the magistrate follows the summary procedure of the criminal procedure code, and the expectation written into the design is a quick disposal close to the event, while witnesses remember and before pressure can be organised. The exception is equally deliberate. The reprisal offence under Section 7(1A), with its two-year floor, and repeat offences under Section 11, with floors of six months and one year, fall outside summary trial and take the ordinary course, reserving the fast track for the first-instance petty offences the Act most commonly prosecutes.

### Is a public servant who neglects the investigation of a complaint himself liable under the Act?

Yes. The explanation to Section 10 deems a public servant who wilfully neglects the investigation of an offence under the Act to have abetted it, and abetment carries the punishment provided for the offence itself. Where he acted in the discharge of official duty, cognizance requires the previous sanction of the Central or the State Government, according to his employment.

The provision is the statute's answer to the failure its own committees kept recording: the complaint that was never written down, the investigation that was never made. By deeming wilful neglect to be abetment, the 1976 amendment converted a disciplinary failing into a criminal one, and by requiring sanction for the prosecution of a servant acting in the discharge of his duty, it gave the government of the day a gate through which such a prosecution must pass. The account in this article states the general position under the statute and is not advice on any individual case; a person pursuing a complaint or facing one should take advice on the facts of it, because forum and sanction questions of this kind are unforgiving of error. What can be said generally is that the deeming provision is very rarely the subject of a reported prosecution, a fact that belongs to the disuse analysis below rather than to any defect in its drafting.

## Who the Act binds, and the shape of its protection

A word on the personal scope of the offences collects several points that recur in the questions put to this article. The offender under Sections 3 to 7A is any person: the Act has no requirement, of the kind the Act of 1989 imposes, that the offender stand outside the victim's community, and no requirement that he belong to any community at all. The victim of the access offences is likewise any person against whom a disability is enforced on the ground of untouchability, the statute's one recurring phrase, and the prosecution's case is built on the ground and the act. Two defined categories then do specific work inside that general frame. The insult offence in Section 7 and the presumption in Section 12 are framed around members of the Scheduled Castes, because the legislature took the paradigm case of the practice to be the case the Constitution's Schedule describes. And the institutions of Sections 5 and 9, the hospitals, schools, hostels and grant-aided temples, are bound as institutions, their managers and trustees carrying the personal liability the sections impose. The result is a statute whose general offences are universal in form and whose aggravating and evidentiary provisions are particular, a design that fits the constitutional text it implements, since Article 17 forbids the practice in any form and against any person, while the history that produced it was the history of particular communities.

## A complaint under the Act, worked end to end

The statute's design is best tested against the sequence it prescribes for a single case. Take the characteristic complaint: a family of a Scheduled Caste is refused the village barber's services and the use of the common tap, after the family's son has taken a job outside the hereditary round of labour. The refusal is announced by the panchayat's word rather than by any individual's, which is the usual form. The legal sequence begins at the police station. The offences are cognizable, so the complaint is recorded as a first information report without any prior sanction or permission, and the investigation is the ordinary investigation of a cognizable case, with the supervising structure of Section 15A behind it in any state that has appointed the officers the section contemplates: officers for initiating or exercising supervision over prosecutions, whose existence is the difference between a complaint that travels and a complaint that waits. If the investigating officer wilfully neglects the investigation, the explanation to Section 10 deems him an abettor, though a prosecution of him for that deemed abetment, committed in the discharge of his duty, requires the previous sanction of his government under Section 15(2).

The charge sheet that follows would frame the facts in the sections they fit: Section 4 for the tap and for the barber, the barber being inside the Act through the definition of a shop as a place where services are rendered, and Section 7 for the organised character of the refusal, molestation and boycott being the charges that capture the panchayat's collective act. At the trial, which for these offences may be summary, the prosecution proves the acts: the refusal, the announcement, the family's exclusion from the tap. It does not then have to prove the reason in the jury-box sense, because the acts were committed in relation to members of a Scheduled Caste and Section 12 presumes the ground. The defence case is therefore the real trial: evidence that the refusal rested on some ground other than untouchability, a debt, a quarrel, a rota, which the court weighs against the pattern of the facts. If the ground is proved or presumed unrebutted, conviction follows, and the sentence sits within the band the Act prescribes, imprisonment of one to six months and fine of one hundred to five hundred rupees, with the floor doing the work the ceiling cannot. If the barber holds a licence or permit for his trade, Section 8 lets the court suspend or cancel it in relation to the Section 6 charge where one is framed for refusal to render the service. If the village as a whole is found to have abetted the exclusion or suppressed the evidence of it, Section 10A is available to the State Government on a separate track, by notification and inquiry, not by the magistrate's sentence. And if the son is attacked for having taken the outside job, or the family's crop is damaged in reprisal, Section 7(1A) attaches its two-year floor to any underlying offence grave enough to carry more than two years.

Set beside the same facts run under the Act of 1989, the comparison the overlap section draws becomes concrete. The tap is a common property resource, so the later Act is available if the offender is not himself a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe, and its prosecution would run before a special court with the victim entitled to relief under the rules, quite apart from the sentence. The barber's refusal and the panchayat's boycott, standing alone, fit the later Act awkwardly or not at all, since a refusal of service is not among its named offences and there is no public insult to carry the public-view clauses. The worked case therefore ends where the article's argument ends: part of the family's complaint has a modern statute, and part of it has only the statute of 1955, and the part that has only the statute of 1955 is the part that defines the family's daily life.

## The collective fine and the duty to implement

Section 10A, inserted in 1976, gives the State Government a power of a kind found nowhere else in the modern Indian penal landscape outside the older police legislation: the power to impose a collective fine on the inhabitants of an area. If, after an inquiry in the prescribed manner, the State Government is satisfied that the inhabitants of an area are concerned in or abetting the commission of an offence under the Act, or are harbouring persons concerned in such an offence, or are failing to render all the assistance in their power to discover or apprehend the offender, or are suppressing material evidence of the offence, it may, by notification in the Official Gazette, impose a collective fine on those inhabitants and apportion it among them according to its judgment of their respective means, assigning a portion to a Hindu undivided family where appropriate. The notification is to be proclaimed in the area by beat of drum or in such other manner as the Government thinks best to bring it to the inhabitants' notice, a phrase that has survived from the drafting world of 1976 into the statute book unamended. The individual inhabitant is protected by a petition procedure: any person aggrieved by the imposition or the apportionment may petition, within the prescribed period and without fee, for exemption or modification, and must be heard; the fine apportioned to a petitioner is not realised until his petition is disposed of; and the State Government may exempt outright the victims of the offence and any person who does not fall within the categories the section specifies. What is recovered is recovered as if it were a fine imposed by a Magistrate under the criminal procedure code.

The theory of the collective fine is the theory of the boycott read in reverse. The characteristic offence against the Act's rights is collective: not one man's refusal but a village's, enforced by the knowledge that no individual refuser need carry the whole blame. A sanction addressed only to individuals dissolves against that collectivity, because the prosecution must pick its accused from a crowd acting as one. The collective fine answers collectivity with collectivity, pricing the area's complicity and giving every inhabitant a financial interest in dissociating himself from the enforcement of the disability. It is, on paper, one of the most considered devices in the Act. The annual reports on the working of the Act record its use so rarely that its practical history can be told in a sentence, and it stands in the statute as the clearest example of the series' recurring variant: machinery fully enacted, fully in force, and almost never set in motion.

Section 15A is the Act's other piece of neglected machinery, and it is the one the brief for this article directs attention to, because it converts the statute from a purely penal code into a scheme of governance. Its first sub-section obliges every State Government, subject to rules made by the Central Government, to take such measures as may be necessary for ensuring that the rights arising from the abolition of untouchability are made available to, and are availed of by, the persons subjected to any disability arising out of it. The second sub-section particularises without limiting: the provision of adequate facilities, including legal aid, to persons subjected to disability, to enable them to avail themselves of their rights; the appointment of officers for initiating prosecutions or exercising supervision over them; the setting up of special courts for the trial of offences under the Act; the setting up of committees at appropriate levels to assist the State Government in formulating or implementing the measures; provision for a periodic survey of the working of the Act; and the identification of the areas where persons are under disability, with measures to remove the disability from those areas. The third sub-section obliges the Central Government to take steps to coordinate the measures of the states. The fourth completes the design with an accountability device of constitutional neatness: the Central Government shall, every year, place on the Table of each House of Parliament a report on the measures taken by itself and by the State Governments under the section. The Protection of Civil Rights Rules, framed by the Central Government and notified in 1977, supply the procedural detail for this machinery, including the manner of the inquiry that precedes a collective fine. The report obligation is examined for what it has produced in the disuse section below; here it is enough to register that Parliament in 1976 built itself an annual window onto the working of the Act, and that the window exists whether or not anyone looks through it.

The obligation deserves to be taken seriously as a piece of constitutional machinery, because it is one of the very few places in Indian statute law where a legislature required the executive to account to it, every year and in public, for the working of a rights statute in the states. Its design has real strengths. It is annual, not occasional. It covers the Centre's own measures and the states' together, so the two levels of government cannot each blame the other in separate documents. It is laid on the Table of both Houses, which makes it a public document and a peg for questions, debates and committee examination. And it is tied to a section whose duties are specified in detail, legal aid, officers, special courts, committees, surveys, identified areas, so that a report can be measured against the statute's own checklist rather than against rhetoric. A Parliament that wished to know why the Act of 1955 sleeps has never needed an inquiry to find out; it has needed only to read the document its own amendment requires to be placed before it, and to ask the Ministry about the nil returns, the unconstituted committees and the unidentified areas the document records. That the report is little examined is not a defect of its drafting. It is the measure of the subject's place in the legislature's attention, the same place it occupied when the amending Bill waited four years for enactment.

The publicity element of the collective fine is not an antique survival to be smiled at; it is part of the sanction. Exclusion under this Act is a public practice, maintained by common knowledge that the line exists and will be held. A fine proclaimed by beat of drum in the area itself publishes the opposite fact, that the state has priced the practice and charged the locality for it, and it does so before the only audience whose opinion sustains the practice. The same instinct runs through Section 15A's machinery: committees at appropriate levels, surveys of the working of the Act, identification of the areas where disabilities persist. Each device is a device for making the practice visible and attributable, on the theory, sound in itself, that untouchability enforced by a community's consensus is weakened when the consensus is named. The annual reports record how completely the theory has been left untested. States that return case figures in single digits also return, in the same reports, the committee structures and survey activity that exist largely as entries in the return itself.

## The amendment of 1976: from offences to civil rights

The Act that commenced in 1955 worked badly enough, in its first decade, to earn itself a committee. In April 1965 the Government of India appointed the Committee on Untouchability, Educational and Economic Development of the Scheduled Castes, chaired by L. Elayaperumal, to examine the problem of untouchability and the working of the Untouchability (Offences) Act among other matters. The committee reported in January 1969. Its findings, as the statement of objects and reasons of the amending Bill recited them, were that the Act contained lacunae and loopholes which needed to be plugged and that the penal provisions needed to be made more stringent. The derisory fines, the alternative of fine alone, the gaps in the offence catalogue and the absence of any machinery for implementation were the themes. A Bill to give effect to the recommendations was introduced in 1972 as the Untouchability (Offences) Amendment and Miscellaneous Provisions Bill, and after a passage of four years it became the Untouchability (Offences) Amendment and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1976, Act 106 of 1976, brought into force on 19 November 1976.

The amendment's most visible change was the name. The Untouchability (Offences) Act became the Protection of Civil Rights Act, and the preamble was recast to match, prescribing punishment for the preaching and practice of untouchability where the original had spoken only of its practice. The change of title was a change of theory, and the new definition of civil rights, inserted as Section 2(a), stated it: the statute was no longer to be understood as a list of offences that happened to concern untouchability, but as the protective code for a set of rights, the rights accruing from the abolition itself. A person does not merely refrain from committing the offences in Sections 3 to 7A; he holds the civil rights the abolition created, and the offences are the means by which the code protects them. The retitling also answered a presentational defect of the original name, which named the statute after the offence and so after the offender's act, where the persons the statute existed for appeared in it only as the occasion of a crime.

The substantive changes have been noticed as they arose in the provision-by-provision account, and they collect into a coherent programme. The offence catalogue was widened: the definition of a place of public worship was extended to privately owned temples in fact used for public worship; Section 4 gained the employment limb and its general explanation equating enforcement of a disability with discrimination; Section 7 gained the reprisal offence and the statutory definition of incitement, catching the preacher and the justifier; and Section 7A created the deemed offence of compelled scavenging, sweeping, carcass removal, flaying and removal of the umbilical cord. The penalties were rebuilt with minimum terms, cumulative fine and imprisonment, and an enhanced ladder for repeat convictions. The enforcement machinery was created: the collective fine in Section 10A, the deemed abetment of the wilfully neglectful public servant in Section 10, special courts and legal aid and surveys in Section 15A, the annual report to Parliament, the exclusion of probation for adults in Section 16A, and a substituted Section 15 that kept the offences cognizable and mostly summarily triable while reserving the graver repeat and reprisal offences for ordinary trial. The four years the Bill took, between its introduction in 1972 and its enactment in 1976, are themselves a comment on the subject's place in the legislative queue. A Bill that implemented a committee report of 1969, on a subject the Constitution had placed in the fundamental rights chapter, waited through a full Parliament and was completed in the Parliament that followed it, in the same season that produced the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act. The delay was not caused by controversy over its contents; the amending Act's programme, minimum sentences, new offences, implementation machinery, was the programme of a government committee and attracted no organised opposition worth recording. It was caused by the subject's chronic position in the order of legislative business, urgent in rhetoric and postponable in practice. The pattern did not end in 1976. The penalty ceilings the amendment left untouched have since survived more than four decades of Parliaments, including the Parliament that enacted the Act of 1989 and the Parliaments that amended that Act twice, each of which legislated on this subject-matter with the Act of 1955 open before it and left its figures where they stood.

Judged as drafting, the amendment of 1976 is the moment the Act became the instrument its constitutional parent had contemplated. Judged by what followed, it is also the moment after which the Act's practical history flatlines, because within thirteen years Parliament had built a second, stronger instrument beside it, and the enforcement energy of the state went there.

## What the courts have made of the Act

The case law on the Act is not large, which is itself a datum, but the decisions that exist settle three questions of the first importance. The first is what untouchability means. In Devarajiah v. B. Padmanna, the Mysore High Court held that the untouchability of Article 17 is not untouchability in its literal or grammatical sense but the practice as it had developed historically in the country: the imposition of disabilities on persons by reason of their birth in the communities treated as untouchable. A literal reading, the court reasoned, would sweep in the temporary avoidance of persons for reasons unconnected with the caste practice, the sick during an epidemic, the observances surrounding a birth or a death, a boycott arising from an ordinary dispute, and would dissolve the article's target into absurdity. The historical reading has governed since: the Act punishes the enforcement of the hereditary practice, and conduct is within it because of the system it belongs to, not because of any physical idea of touch. The Rajasthan High Court's Full Bench in Jai Singh v. Union of India later followed Devarajiah in upholding the validity of the Act of 1989, carrying the meaning across into the successor statute.

The second question is whom Article 17 binds. The general run of fundamental rights binds the state, and a private person's conduct is ordinarily reached only through legislation. In People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India, the Supreme Court held that Articles 17, 23 and 24 stand apart: they are enforceable against private individuals as well as the state, and where a right under Article 17 is violated by a private person, it is the constitutional obligation of the state to take the necessary steps to ensure that the right is not violated, the aggrieved person's own ability to enforce the right notwithstanding. The holding is the doctrinal foundation of the whole subject. The abolition of untouchability is one of the very few guarantees in the Constitution with direct horizontal effect, operating between citizen and citizen, and the Act of 1955 is the penal instrument of a right that would bind private conduct even in the Act's absence. The hotel keeper and the temple trustee are not merely regulated by a statute in the ordinary way; they are held to a fundamental right that runs against them personally.

The third question is how a court should read the Act when a case reaches it, and the answer was given in State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale, the decision around which the statute's modern understanding is organised. The facts were of the kind Section 4 was written for. Members of the Harijan community of a village were restrained, by show of force and on pain of a gun being used, from drawing water from a newly dug well. The respondents were tried for offences under Sections 4 and 7. The trial court convicted, sentencing the accused to one month's simple imprisonment and a fine of one hundred rupees each, the floor of the Act's original sentencing pattern in its amended form. The appellate court upheld the conviction of three of the accused. The Karnataka High Court, in revision, reappreciated the evidence and acquitted. The Supreme Court, in a bench of Kuldip Singh and K. Ramaswamy JJ., restored the convictions, holding that the High Court had lost sight of the force by which the disability had been enforced and that the provisions of the Act are to be interpreted in the light of the constitutional goals they serve. Justice Ramaswamy's judgment states the statute's place in the constitutional scheme in terms that have been quoted ever since: untouchability is an indirect form of slavery and an extension of the caste system, and the provisions of the Constitution and of the Act serve a threefold purpose, to outlaw the disabilities to which Dalits are subjected, to make their enforcement an offence, and to provide rights enforceable as civil rights. The same judgment records why Parliament had by then gone further, observing that the stringent provisions of the Act of 1989 were enacted when the mandate of Article 17 was being breached with impunity and the commission of atrocities continued unabated.

It is also worth recording what the courts have not done to the Act. No provision of it has been struck down, read down or held unconstitutional in seven decades, a record few penal statutes of its vintage can match. The statute's constitutional position is the reverse of the usual one: ordinarily a court is asked to save a law from its own excesses, while here the law's infirmity, such as it is, lies in its mildness, which no litigant has standing to complain of and no court can cure. The judiciary's contribution to the subject was therefore always going to be interpretive rather than corrective, and the three decisions above mark the limit of what interpretation can achieve for a statute whose cases stopped arriving.

The civil limb of the Act, Section 13, has done quiet work of a different kind. Its command that no court shall recognise any custom or usage imposing a disability on the ground of untouchability withdraws the protection of the legal system from the practice at the point where the practice most often sought it: the suit for a declaration that a community's custom entitled it to exclude, the execution of a decree founded on such a custom, the injunction sought against a person who had crossed the line custom drew. A custom of exclusion, elsewhere in Indian law a material fact to be pleaded and proved, is under this Act a nullity to be disregarded, and the provision converts every civil court into an instrument of the abolition whenever a disability is dressed up as a right. Read with Section 16, which gives the Act effect notwithstanding any custom, usage, instrument or decree to the contrary, Section 13 is the point at which the statute stops punishing the practice and starts refusing it legal existence, and it is the provision a researcher examining the Act's influence should weigh alongside the criminal sections, whose statistics are so much easier to count and so much less representative of the Act's total effect.

Two features of the later constitutional case law complete the picture without enlarging it. The Supreme Court's consideration of Article 17 in the Sabarimala temple entry litigation, in Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala, treated the article as a guarantee against exclusion grounded in notions of purity and pollution, with Justice Chandrachud's opinion reading its prohibition as reaching the forms of untouchability that a changing society produces rather than only the forms history recorded. And the working of the Section 12 presumption in the trial courts follows the pattern the drafting predicts: convictions under the Act turn, in the reported cases, on whether the ground of untouchability is proved or presumed and left unrebutted, and acquittals turn on its failure. The jurisprudence, in short, has done the statute no harm. What the courts were given to work with was a right they read generously and a caseload that thinned, for reasons outside the courtroom, to almost nothing.

## The overlap with the Atrocities Act, and the gap between the two statutes

The Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 was enacted because Parliament judged, and Justice Ramaswamy's judgment in Ingale recorded, that the mandate of Article 17 was being breached with impunity under the existing law. The relationship between the two statutes is the analytical heart of this article, and [the Atrocities Act and the overlap between the two regimes](/2017/02/15/sc-st-atrocities-act-1989-explained/) are treated in full in that statute's own pillar, to which this account defers for the detail of the later Act's offences, its special courts and its relief machinery. What belongs here is the comparison itself, because the comparison is where the conduct that nobody prosecutes becomes visible.

One condition of the later Act deserves to be isolated, because it manufactures a class of cases that fall back to the Act of 1955 by design. The offences in the Act of 1989 are committed against a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe by a person who is not a member of either. Exclusion practised within the hierarchy, by one listed community against another, or by members of a listed community against their own on the ground of untouchability, is therefore outside the later Act's scheme, however exactly it matches its catalogue of acts. It is inside the Act of 1955, whose offences ask only about the ground of the act. The insult limb of Section 7, the excommunication limb and the boycott definition are all, in part, instruments for this internal frontier of the practice, and a consolidation that discarded the ground-based design would leave the internal frontier unpoliced in the name of tidiness.

The two statutes are built on different principles. The Act of 1955 criminalises the enforcement of a disability on the ground of untouchability: the act and its ground are the offence, and in most of its sections the victim's caste is not an element at all. The Act of 1989 criminalises specified acts committed against a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe by a person who is not a member of either, on the ground of that membership as the statute's later interpretation and amendment have framed it, and for its insult offences it adds the condition that the act occur in a place within public view. The later Act then surrounds its offences with apparatus the earlier Act never had: exclusive special courts, a bar on anticipatory bail, duties on public servants with punishment for their neglect, and a schedule of relief and rehabilitation payable to the victim as an incident of the crime rather than as charity after it. Where both statutes cover the same conduct, the difference in consequence is not marginal. The first offence that earns one to six months under the Act of 1955 earns six months to five years under the Act of 1989, with compensation machinery running beside the prosecution.

### Was the Protection of Civil Rights Act replaced by the Atrocities Act of 1989?

No. The Act of 1989 did not repeal, amend or absorb the Act of 1955, and the two statutes are in force together. The older Act continues to govern the enforcement of disabilities on the ground of untouchability, including conduct the later Act does not reach. What the Act of 1989 replaced was the practice, not the statute.

A prosecution under the older Act remains legally available for any of the offences in Sections 3 to 7A, and no election between the two statutes is forced on a complainant by law. The preference for the later Act wherever its conditions can be satisfied is a product of its consequences, and it is examined in the paragraphs that follow.

The preference is rational at every point where it operates. The complainant under the Act of 1989 is met by a special court, protected witnesses, travelling and maintenance allowances and a relief schedule; the complainant under the Act of 1955 is met by a magistrate's summary trial and, at the end of it, a fine of at most five hundred rupees payable to the state. The investigating officer who registers the graver statute registers the graver crime, and where the facts will bear it, the facts are framed to bear it. Much conduct that begins as exclusion is therefore charged, if it is charged at all, as an atrocity with its accompanying offences under the [general penal code, whose replacement by the new codes](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/) the series treats in its own comparison, and the older statute is left for the residue. The residue is defined by the later Act's conditions. Conduct that is not directed at a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe as such; insult and exclusion that occur away from public view; refusals by traders, landlords, institutions and temple authorities that involve no violence, no threat and no public humiliation: these satisfy the Act of 1955 and satisfy nothing else. The table that follows states the position category by category, and the rows in which the older statute stands alone are the article's argument in miniature.

Charging practice follows the same gradient at the level of the individual file. An occurrence at a well or a temple door typically presents a bundle of facts: the exclusion itself, words spoken during it, sometimes a scuffle, sometimes damage. The registering officer frames the bundle in the provisions that carry the occurrence furthest, the insult and obstruction offences of the Act of 1989 where the victim and the setting allow them, and the hurt, assault and mischief provisions of the general penal code beside them. The Act of 1955, whose provisions fit the exclusion at the centre of the bundle more exactly than any of these, adds nothing to the file that the file's author values: no additional years that matter, no relief for the victim, no special forum. It is omitted not from ignorance but from redundancy within the officer's own scale of goods, and the omission is then reproduced in the statistics as the Act's disuse, which is cited in its turn as the reason the Act need not be revived. The circle is complete, and it is drawn by incentives rather than by any judgment on the statute's merits.

Read the table by its last column and the disuse of the Act of 1955 stops looking like a mystery. In the two categories where the later Act competes, water and insult, practice follows the stronger statute and its machinery. In the five categories where the older Act stands alone, temple entry, trade, institutional admission, housing and compelled scavenging in its deemed form, practice is recorded as rare: the only available prosecution is the one nobody brings. The gap between the statutes is not a drafting oversight that a consolidation could casually close. It is the predictable residue of a system in which the enforcers' incentives follow severity and machinery, and the conduct the weaker statute alone covers is the conduct least likely to attract either.

The asymmetry of relief deserves separate statement, because it operates before any question of punishment arises. Under the Act of 1989 and the rules made under it, a victim of an atrocity is entitled to relief in cash and in kind on a schedule, payable at stages of the case, together with travelling and daily allowances for attending the investigation and trial, and the machinery of a special court and a special public prosecutor. Under the Act of 1955 the victim is entitled to nothing by the statute itself: no compensation, no allowance, no prosecutor of his own, only the satisfaction of a conviction whose fine is paid to the state. A complainant is therefore not merely choosing between two punishments for the offender. He is choosing between a system that treats him as a person to be restored and a system that treats him as a witness to be used, and the choice he makes is recorded in the registration figures the next section examines. Section 15A's promise of legal aid was the 1976 legislature's partial answer to this asymmetry, and its non-implementation in most states left the asymmetry intact.

## The disuse, in the official record

The Act requires the Central Government to lay before Parliament, every year, a report on the measures taken under Section 15A by itself and by the state governments. The reports in that series, published by the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, are the official record of the Act's working, and their figures are the ones the brief for this article directs the reader to, stated with their source and their period. For the year 2022, the annual report recorded thirteen cases registered under the Act in the whole of India, five in Jammu and Kashmir, five in Karnataka, two in Maharashtra and one in Himachal Pradesh, every other state and Union territory recording none or returning no information. The figure for 2021 was twenty-four cases and for 2020 twenty-five. No state or Union territory declared any area to be untouchability prone, the identification of such areas being among the measures Section 15A prescribes. Of the cases disposed of by the courts in 2022, thirty-one in number and mostly the residue of earlier years, one ended in conviction. More than ninety-seven per cent of the cases under the Act stood pending at the year's end.

The reports carry a second, quieter set of figures that measure the machinery rather than the crime. Several states and Union territories returned nil information on the Act's implementation for the year, and one large state returned no information at all, yet the reporting obligation in Section 15A is framed as a duty and the returns are the only account Parliament receives. A nil return is ambiguous in a way a low figure is not: it may record the absence of the practice, or the absence of the apparatus that would notice it, and the report itself does not distinguish. What is not ambiguous is the relationship between the returns and the Section 15A duties. A state that has identified no area where persons live under disability, appointed no machinery that reports a single prosecution, and surveyed nothing has not discharged the duty the section imposes, whether or not an offence was committed in its territory in the year.

Set that record beside the corresponding figures for the Act of 1989 and the contrast requires no rhetoric. The annual reporting on the later Act records 51,656 cases registered against members of the Scheduled Castes and 9,735 against members of the Scheduled Tribes in 2022 alone. The older statute's thirteen cases are not a small share of a joint enterprise; they are a rounding error in it, a fraction of one per cent of the caseload of the statute that overlaps it. Two explanations, both grounded in the statutes' texts, account for the whole of the gap. The first is the price signal: a first offence under the Act of 1955 is worth, at its ceiling, what a first offence under the Act of 1989 is worth at its floor, and the fine that a convicted temple trustee or trader pays is capped at a sum fixed before most of the population was born. A complainant deciding whether to set the criminal law in motion, and an officer deciding how to register what the complainant reports, are both choosing between a prosecution that ends in a few months and a few hundred rupees, with nothing for the victim, and a prosecution that carries years, compensation, and the protection of a special court. The second explanation is the gravitational pull of the overlap: because so much exclusion arrives accompanied by insult, threat or force, the facts of most real complaints can be stated, truthfully, in the language of the later Act, and once stated in that language there is no occasion to open the older statute at all.

The trial figures complete the indictment of the machinery rather than of the text. A statute whose offences were designed in 1976 for summary trial, disposal in the shortest procedure the criminal law allows, records a pendency above ninety-seven per cent in the Ministry's own report: the few cases that enter the system do not move through it either. And of the thirty-one disposals the courts managed in 2022, one conviction stands against thirty acquittals or discharges, a ratio that reflects, in part, the age of the cases reaching decision and the decay of their evidence, and in part the structural weakness of prosecutions in which the complainant has no statutory support, no special prosecutor and no relief to sustain him through years of adjournments. Pendency and acquittal at these levels are not a verdict on the offence definitions, which the courts have repeatedly shown they can apply when a case is properly brought. They are a verdict on the distance between the machinery Section 15A authorises and the machinery the states have built.

The residue the two explanations leave behind is the point the figures make with most force. The conduct that the Act of 1955 alone criminalises, the quiet refusal, the closed temple door, the unlet room, the separate utensils, the admission denied, does not appear in these statistics as prosecuted conduct under another name. It appears, in the main, not at all: thirteen registrations in a country where the practices the sections describe are documented, by every survey the Ministry itself commissions and cites, to persist. The statute has not failed at trial. It has failed at the police station door and before it, at the point where a person refused service weighs the cost of complaining against the value of the remedy on offer, and finds the remedy priced in the money of 1955.

There is a constitutional way of stating what the figures mean, and it is the statement this article rests on. Article 17 does not say that untouchability is discouraged, or that the state shall endeavour to end it. It says the practice is forbidden and that the enforcement of any disability arising from it shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law. The law in accordance with which it is punishable is this Act, and the Act's own accounting mechanism records a year in which the offence was registered thirteen times in a country of India's size, in which no area was identified as one where the disability persists, and in which one conviction closed the year's account. Either the practice has ended, in which case the surveys the same Ministry publishes are mistaken, or the Article's command is being executed by a mechanism that registers almost none of what it forbids. The annual report does not resolve that contradiction. It documents it, year after year, in the state's own hand.

One further feature of the first decade belongs to the record, because it explains the committee of 1965. The Act was used, in its early years, more than its later history would suggest: prosecutions were brought, convictions recorded, and the provincial machinery of the old removal-of-disabilities regime passed into the new code. What the official reviews of the nineteen sixties found was not that the Act was a dead letter but that it was a weak one: the alternative of a fine alone produced trivial sentences, the catalogue left the compelled-labour and reprisal offences outside, the presumption was asked to bear weight in courts without any supporting machinery of prosecution or legal aid, and the whole weight of the abolition rested on the willingness of individual victims to prosecute their neighbours in the magistrate's court. The Elayaperumal Committee was appointed, in other words, because the Act was working and visibly failing at the work. The distinction matters to the verdict on the statute: the Act of 1955 was not stillborn. It was overtaken, first by the limits its own drafters had accepted in 1955 and only partly repaired in 1976, and then by a successor that made its weaknesses impossible to ignore.

## The statute in its neighbourhood

The Act of 1955 does not stand alone in the statute book, and its neighbours explain both its design and its eclipse. It is the first member of a family of statutes by which Parliament has legislated against the hereditary organisation of labour and status: the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act of 1976, which extinguished bonded debt and labour by a single statutory stroke in the same parliamentary season that rebuilt this Act; the Employment of Manual Scavengers and Construction of Dry Latrines (Prohibition) Act of 1993 and its successor of 2013, which took the subject of Section 7A and rebuilt it as a scheme of prohibition, survey and rehabilitation; and the Act of 1989, which took the subjects of exclusion and humiliation and rebuilt them as atrocity. The family's history has a pattern the series has met in other clusters: the older, ground-based, generally framed statute remains in force while each of its subjects is successively carved out into a newer instrument with stronger machinery addressed to that subject alone. What is left behind in the parent Act is the connective tissue, the general prohibition and the general catalogue, together with the subjects nobody carved out. The temple door, the trader's counter, the hostel register and the unlet room are still, in law, the exclusive province of the Act of 1955, and the family pattern is the reason: no later Parliament found a constituency for them.

## The reform argument, stated both ways

The obvious reform is to raise the penalties, and its case is straightforward. A fine ceiling of five hundred rupees and a maximum of six months for a first offence cannot deter conduct engaged in openly and with community support, and a statute that gives effect to a fundamental right ought not to carry a lesser sanction than an ordinary offence against property. The Elayaperumal Committee made a version of this case in 1969, and the amendment of 1976 accepted half of it, building floors under the sentences while leaving the ceilings where 1955 had put them; the case for completing the work, by raising the ceilings to something proportionate to the gravity the Constitution assigns the right, has been available ever since and has never been taken up.

The less obvious objection is also grounded in the record, and it is made by those who work on registration and trial rather than on sentencing: that the binding constraint on this statute is not the sentence at its end but the complaint at its beginning. The Ministry's annual reports record the near-disappearance of registration itself, and a registration failure cannot be cured by a sentencing reform, because a case that is never registered is never sentenced. On this view, doubling or trebling the maxima would leave the thirteen cases of 2022 exactly where they stand, and the reforms that answer the actual failure are the unglamorous ones the Act already authorises and the states already neglect: the officers charged with initiating and supervising prosecutions under Section 15A, the committees, the periodic surveys, the identification of areas where disabilities persist, and prosecutions under the deemed abetment provision when investigation is wilfully neglected. The two positions are not reconcilable by drafting, because they diagnose different failures, and a Parliament that raised the penalties without touching registration would have performed the reform rather than achieved it.

Consolidation, the merger of the two statutes into a single code against caste disability and atrocity, is the third proposal, and the argument against it deserves the same care. The gain would be tidiness and a single door for the complainant. The loss would be structural. The Act of 1955 rests on the ground of the act, and its offences reach exclusion practised against persons whom the Act of 1989, with its architecture of listed communities and its public-view conditions, does not cover; its presumption under Section 12 is keyed to the act and the ground and operates without the later Act's apparatus of conditions; and its catalogue of access offences, the temple, the shop, the hostel, the unlet room, would enter any merged code as the junior partner of the atrocity offences and risk being drafted down to them. A consolidation that carried the earlier Act's offences across intact, with penalties rebuilt, would widen protection. A consolidation conducted as a tidying exercise could narrow it, by subjecting quiet exclusion to conditions designed for public violence. The safer course, on the record of both statutes, is the unglamorous one: leave the two instruments standing, rebuild the penalty scale and the registration machinery of the older one, and let the gap in the table above be closed by use rather than by merger.

What revival would require can be stated with the same precision, because the Act itself lists most of it. The penalty ceilings would have to be rebuilt, so that a first offence under the statute that implements a fundamental right is not capped below the floor of the statute beside it, and the fine would have to be re-expressed in sums that a trader or a temple committee would notice. The machinery of Section 15A would have to be constituted in fact: the supervising officers appointed, the special courts the section authorises actually set up in the districts where the practice persists, legal aid reaching the complainant before the complaint is drafted rather than after it fails. The deemed abetment of the neglectful investigator would have to be used, visibly, in the cases the annual reports already document, so that registration stops being the point at which complaints go to die. And the annual report itself would have to be read, in Parliament and outside it, as the accountability instrument it was drafted to be, rather than laid and forgotten. None of this requires a new statute. It requires the existing one to be taken at its word, which is the one thing, in seven decades, that has not been tried consistently.

## Verdict: the law for the fundamental right is the one nobody uses

The Protection of Civil Rights Act is neither a repealed statute nor a failed text. Its offences describe the lived inventory of untouchability with a precision no later statute has matched, its presumption is well designed for the evidence its cases actually present, its collective fine and its implementation duties were serious machinery when they were enacted in 1976, and the courts, from Devarajiah through People's Union for Democratic Rights to Appa Balu Ingale, have read it at the full width of the constitutional promise behind it. What has failed is the economy around it: penalties left at the money of 1955 and the ceilings of 1955, a stronger statute beside it drawing off every complaint its conditions can absorb, and an annual report to Parliament that records the result, thirteen cases in a year, to a readership of almost none. The constitutional abolition of untouchability contemplates a law providing punishment. This is that law. It remains in force, with penalties measured in months and hundreds of rupees, over conduct that its own sections name exactly, and the conduct that falls between it and the Act of 1989 is prohibited by a fundamental right and enforced, in practice, by nobody.

The precision matters because the remedy follows the diagnosis. If the Act were a bad text, the answer would be a new statute, and India has shown, in 1989 and again in 2013, that it can write one when it chooses. The Act is not a bad text. It is an adequate text abandoned by the economy of enforcement around it, and the conduct in the five rows of the table where it stands alone will remain unprosecuted until either its price is rebuilt or a successor is written for it. Neither has happened. That is the whole of the case, stated in its shortest and plainest form, and it is enough.

For the reader placing the statute in its field, the Act is one layer of the social justice cluster, the penal layer beneath the constitutional text and beside the atrocity and welfare statutes, and [the social justice legislation guide](/2017/02/01/social-justice-laws-india-guide/) maps the field and its three layers as a whole. For the aspirant and the teacher, the [study treatment of the social justice cluster](/2017/06/15/social-justice-laws-upsc-mains/) revises this Act with the Act of 1989 and the manual scavenging statute as one subject; the comparison table in this article is the part built to be revised from, and it can be [worked through as a set of slides in the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which suits the table's row-by-row argument exactly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Protection of Civil Rights Act punish?

It punishes the enforcement of any disability on the ground of untouchability. Section 3 covers religious disabilities, preventing entry to a place of public worship or the use of sacred waters. Section 4 covers social disabilities, exclusion from shops, restaurants, hotels, entertainments, professions, water sources, roads, burial grounds, conveyances, residential premises, ceremonies and processions. Section 5 covers refusal of admission to hospitals, dispensaries, educational institutions and hostels, Section 6 refusal to sell goods or render services, Section 7 obstruction, molestation, boycott, incitement and insult, and Section 7A the compelling of a person to scavenge, sweep, remove a carcass, flay an animal or remove the umbilical cord. Abetment of any of these offences is punished as the offence itself.

### Q: Why was the Untouchability Offences Act renamed?

The amendment of 1976, Act 106 of 1976, renamed it the Protection of Civil Rights Act with effect from 19 November 1976, following the report of the Committee on Untouchability, Educational and Economic Development of the Scheduled Castes chaired by L. Elayaperumal. The renaming expressed a change of theory. The original title named the statute after the offender's act and presented it as a list of offences. The new title named it after the rights it protects, and the same amendment inserted a definition of civil rights as any right accruing to a person by reason of the abolition of untouchability by Article 17 of the Constitution. The preamble was recast at the same time to cover the preaching, as well as the practice, of untouchability.

### Q: What penalties does the civil rights statute carry?

For a first offence under Sections 3 to 7, imprisonment for a term of not less than one month and not more than six months, and also a fine of not less than one hundred rupees and not more than five hundred rupees, the two being cumulative. Compelled scavenging under Section 7A carries a higher floor of three months. Section 11 raises the scale for repeat offenders: a second conviction carries six months to one year and a fine of two hundred to five hundred rupees, and a third or subsequent conviction carries one to two years and a fine of five hundred to one thousand rupees. The maxima for a first offence, six months and five hundred rupees, date from 1955 and have never been revised.

### Q: What is the presumption under the civil rights statute?

Section 12 provides that where any act constituting an offence under the Act is committed in relation to a member of a Scheduled Caste, the court shall presume, unless the contrary is proved, that the act was committed on the ground of untouchability. The prosecution must still prove the act itself by evidence: the refusal, the prevention or the insult. What the presumption supplies is the ground, which is the element hardest to prove because a refusal is rarely accompanied by an announcement of its reason. Once the act and the victim's membership of a Scheduled Caste are established, the burden of proving that the act was done on some other ground passes to the accused. The presumption has stood in the statute, in substance, since 1955.

### Q: Can a collective fine be imposed under the civil rights statute?

Yes. Section 10A, inserted in 1976, empowers the State Government, after an inquiry in the prescribed manner, to impose a collective fine on the inhabitants of an area where it is satisfied that they are concerned in or abetting an offence under the Act, harbouring an offender, failing to assist in discovering or apprehending him, or suppressing material evidence. The fine is imposed by notification in the Official Gazette, proclaimed in the area, and apportioned among the inhabitants according to their means. An aggrieved inhabitant may petition without fee for exemption or modification and must be heard, and the State Government may exempt victims of the offence outright. The power is designed for the characteristic case under this Act, in which exclusion is enforced by a whole village rather than by one identifiable person.

### Q: Why is the civil rights statute barely used today?

For two reasons that operate together. The penalties are too small to be worth invoking: a first offence is capped at six months and a fine of five hundred rupees on a scale last rebuilt in 1976 inside ceilings set in 1955, and there is no compensation for the victim. And the Act of 1989 covers much of the same conduct with six months to five years, special courts, protection for witnesses and a schedule of relief, so a complainant and an investigating officer both prefer it wherever its conditions, including a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe victim and, for insults, a place within public view, can be met. The conduct the older Act alone covers, the quiet refusal without violence or an audience, is the conduct least likely to be reported at all. The Ministry's annual report recorded thirteen cases registered in India in 2022.

### Q: What annual report on civil rights goes to Parliament?

Section 15A obliges the Central Government, every year, to place on the Table of each House of Parliament a report on the measures taken by itself and by the State Governments under that section, the section that requires states to ensure that the rights arising from the abolition of untouchability are actually availed of, through legal aid, officers to initiate and supervise prosecutions, special courts, committees, periodic surveys and the identification of areas where disabilities persist. The reports, published by the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, are the official record of the Act's working. It is from that series that the registration figures come: twenty-five cases in 2020, twenty-four in 2021 and thirteen in 2022, with no state declaring any area untouchability prone.

### Q: How does the civil rights statute differ from the Atrocities Act?

The statutes are built on different principles. The Act of 1955 criminalises the enforcement of a disability on the ground of untouchability, and in most of its sections the victim's caste is not an element of the offence at all. The Act of 1989 criminalises specified acts committed against a member of a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe by a person who is not a member of either, and for its insult offences requires a place within public view. The consequences differ as sharply: one to six months and a fine capped at five hundred rupees under the older Act, against six months to five years, exclusive special courts, a bar on anticipatory bail and statutory relief for the victim under the later one. The older Act also covers ground the later Act does not, including temple entry, refusal of trade or admission, and refusal to let premises.

### Q: When did the Protection of Civil Rights Act come into force?

It commenced on 1 June 1955. The Act was enacted by Parliament as the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955, Act 22 of 1955, and it received assent and was published on 8 May 1955. Section 1 left the commencement date to be appointed by the Central Government by notification in the Official Gazette, and the appointed date was 1 June 1955. On commencement, the twenty-one provincial and princely-state enactments listed in its Schedule, the removal of disabilities Acts of Madras, Bombay, the United Provinces, Orissa, Bihar, Mysore and others, stood repealed to the extent to which they corresponded to or were repugnant to the new central Act. The statute was renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act with effect from 19 November 1976.

### Q: What does the term civil rights mean in the Act?

Section 2(a), inserted by the amendment of 1976, defines civil rights as any right accruing to a person by reason of the abolition of untouchability by Article 17 of the Constitution. The definition is the hinge of the renamed statute. It does not list the rights; it derives them. Whatever right a person holds because untouchability stands abolished, the right to enter, to worship, to draw water, to trade, to be admitted, to occupy, to be served, is a civil right within the Act, and the offences in Sections 3 to 7A are the means by which those derived rights are protected. Section 7 uses the definition directly, making it an offence to prevent any person from exercising any right accruing to him by reason of the abolition, whether or not the particular exclusion appears in the catalogues of the earlier sections.

### Q: Does the Act bind private individuals, or only the government?

It binds private individuals, and that is its central design. The offences are committed by whoever enforces a disability: the temple trustee, the hotel keeper, the trader, the landlord, the caste council. The constitutional foundation is Article 17 itself, which the Supreme Court in People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India held to be enforceable against private individuals and not only against the state, one of the small number of fundamental rights with that horizontal operation. The Court added that where a private person violates the right, the state bears the constitutional obligation to act against the violation. The Act is the penal instrument of that obligation, and its machinery in Section 15A, legal aid, supervising officers and state reports to Parliament, exists because the duty to suppress the practice rests on the government even though the offenders are private.

### Q: Is forcing a person to scavenge or sweep an offence under the Act?

Yes, under Section 7A, inserted by the amendment of 1976. Whoever compels any person, on the ground of untouchability, to do any scavenging or sweeping, or to remove any carcass, or to flay any animal, or to remove the umbilical cord, or to do any other job of a similar nature, is deemed to have enforced a disability arising out of untouchability. The deeming matters, because imposing a degrading task is not in form the denial of a facility; the amendment brought the hereditary assignment of such work inside the Act by declaring it a disability. Compulsion is defined to include a threat of social or economic boycott, so no physical force need be shown. The punishment is imprisonment for three to six months with a fine, the highest minimum for a first offence in the Act.

### Q: Which court tries offences under the Protection of Civil Rights Act?

A Judicial Magistrate of the first class, or in a metropolitan area a Metropolitan Magistrate. Section 15 makes every offence under the Act cognizable, so the police may arrest without a warrant and must register and investigate a complaint, and it provides that every offence, except one punishable with a minimum term of imprisonment exceeding three months, may be tried summarily under the summary procedure of the criminal procedure code. The design is for speed: a short trial close to the event. The exceptions are the graver offences the Act contains, the reprisal offence in Section 7(1A) with its minimum of two years, and repeat offences under Section 11 with minima of six months and one year, which are tried in the ordinary way. Section 15A also empowers state governments to set up special courts for offences under the Act, a power little used.

### Q: Can a trader convicted under the Act lose the licence for his trade?

Yes. Section 8 provides that when a person convicted of an offence under Section 6, the refusal to sell goods or render services, holds any licence under any law for the profession, trade, calling or employment in relation to which the offence was committed, the court trying the offence may direct that the licence stand cancelled or be suspended for such period as the court thinks fit. The direction has effect as if it had been passed by the authority competent to cancel or suspend the licence under that law, and licence is defined to include a permit or a permission. The sanction was part of the original Act of 1955 and is aimed at the commercial settings in which the Act's refusals occur: for a hotel keeper or a licensed trader, the licence is the business, and its loss is a deterrent of a different order from a fine capped at five hundred rupees.

### Q: What did the amendment of 1976 change in the Act?

The Untouchability (Offences) Amendment and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1976, Act 106 of 1976, in force from 19 November 1976, rebuilt the statute on the recommendations of the Elayaperumal Committee. It renamed the Act and inserted the definition of civil rights. It widened the offences, extending the definition of a place of public worship to privately owned temples used for public worship, adding the employment limb to Section 4, the reprisal offence and the definition of incitement to Section 7, and the compelled labour offence in Section 7A. It rebuilt the penalties with minimum terms of one month and minimum fines of one hundred rupees, made imprisonment and fine cumulative, and structured the repeat-offence ladder in Section 11. And it created the machinery the original Act lacked: the collective fine, the deemed abetment of a wilfully neglectful public servant, special courts and legal aid under Section 15A, and the annual report to Parliament.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in Appa Balu Ingale?

In State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale, decided in December 1992, the Supreme Court restored convictions under Sections 4 and 7 of the Act which the Karnataka High Court had set aside in revision. Members of the Harijan community of a village had been restrained by show of force from drawing water from a newly dug well; the trial court convicted and the appellate court upheld the conviction of three accused, but the High Court reappreciated the evidence and acquitted. The Supreme Court held that the High Court had lost sight of the force by which the disability was enforced, and that the Act must be interpreted in the light of its constitutional goals. Justice Ramaswamy's judgment described untouchability as an indirect form of slavery and an extension of the caste system, and stated the threefold purpose of the constitutional and statutory scheme: to outlaw the disabilities, to make their enforcement an offence, and to provide rights enforceable as civil rights.

### Q: Does the Act define untouchability?

No. Neither the Constitution nor the Act defines the term, and the omission is deliberate. Article 17 places the word in quotation marks, and the statement of objects and reasons of the Bill of 1955 recorded that it was not possible to define untouchability, so the scheme of the Act is to make express provision for the common forms of the practice rather than to define it. The courts filled the space the drafters left. The Mysore High Court in Devarajiah v. B. Padmanna held that the term means the practice as it developed historically in India, the imposition of disabilities on persons by reason of their birth in the communities treated as untouchable, and not untouchability in a literal or grammatical sense, which would absurdly capture temporary avoidance for reasons of sickness or mourning. That historical reading has governed the statute since.

### Q: What must a State Government do to implement the Act?

Section 15A places the duty in terms: subject to rules made by the Central Government, the State Government shall take such measures as may be necessary for ensuring that the rights arising from the abolition of untouchability are made available to, and are availed of by, the persons subjected to any disability arising out of it. The section then lists what those measures may include: adequate facilities including legal aid for persons subjected to disability; officers appointed to initiate prosecutions or supervise them; special courts for the trial of offences under the Act; committees at appropriate levels to assist in formulating and implementing measures; periodic surveys of the working of the Act; and the identification of areas where persons live under disability, with measures to remove it. The Central Government must coordinate the states' measures and report annually to Parliament on what has been done.

### Q: Can a person be punished for preaching that untouchability is justified by tradition?

Yes. Section 7 makes it an offence, by words spoken or written, or by signs or visible representations or otherwise, to incite or encourage any person, class of persons or the public generally to practise untouchability in any form. An explanation inserted in 1976 provides that a person is deemed to incite or encourage the practice if he directly or indirectly preaches untouchability or its practice in any form, or if he justifies it, whether on historical, philosophical or religious grounds, or on the ground of any tradition of the caste system, or on any other ground. The justification limb is drafted to close the standard defence in advance: an exposition of scripture, history or custom that justifies the practice is itself the offence, punishable with one to six months and a fine on the usual scale.

### Q: What protection does the Act give a person boycotted by his own community for refusing to practise untouchability?

Section 7(2) is addressed to exactly that person. It makes it an offence to deny to any person belonging to one's own community or any section of it any right or privilege to which he would be entitled as a member of that community, or to take any part in his excommunication, on the ground that he has refused to practise untouchability or has done any act in furtherance of the objects of the Act. The provision dates from 1955 and was one of the features the statement of objects and reasons singled out: the Bill was designed to reach the retaliation by which a community disciplined its own members into enforcing the system. Section 7 also treats boycott itself as an offence where it is directed at a person for exercising a right under the Act, and defines boycott to include refusing to let a house or land, refusing to deal, work for hire or do business, and withholding customary services.
