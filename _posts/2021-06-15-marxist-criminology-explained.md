Marxist criminology explains crime and its control by reference to capitalist social relations, treating law making, law enforcement and the definition of crime itself as reflecting class interests, and treating criminal behaviour as produced by inequality, competition and material need. That shared starting point conceals deep division. Within the tradition sit four distinguishable positions that disagree substantially about how directly power shapes law, about whether the state merely serves owners or enjoys a measure of independence, about whether ordinary offending deserves condemnation or explanation, and about whose suffering should count as the central harm in the first place.

Disagreement of that kind is not a weakness to be smoothed away before the argument begins. Each position arose because an earlier version explained something well and something else badly. A theory that traced every statute to the wishes of property owners illuminated the origins of law yet struggled with reforms passed against business opposition. A theory that stressed ideology explained consent yet risked making domination sound automatic. A theory centred on working class victims corrected romantic accounts of offenders yet pulled the tradition back toward the very crime categories it had questioned. Holding the four apart, rather than blending them into a single vague radicalism, allows each claim to be tested against the evidence it handles best and the evidence it handles worst.

![Overflowing court archive shelves beside an empty corporate boardroom, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-54.webp)

One further caution belongs at the door. Explanation is not excuse, and criticism of law is not indifference to harm. A burglary can ruin a household, an assault can shadow a life for decades, and any account that forgets such facts in its enthusiasm for structural analysis forfeits moral seriousness. Equally, sympathy for victims does not settle the prior question of why some injuries fill courtrooms while other injuries, larger in scale and slower in effect, are handled as regulatory mishaps or business costs. Marxist criminology, in all four of its forms, insists that the sorting process itself requires explanation. Who defines, who enforces, who is watched, who is believed and who is compensated are not administrative details added after crime exists. For this tradition, they are part of what crime is.

## Marxist Criminology and the Question of Definition

Definition precedes causation because no inquiry into causes can begin until a boundary has been drawn around the conduct to be explained. Criminologists who ask why people steal, assault or defraud have already accepted a legal classification that gathers some harmful acts together and leaves others outside the frame. Theft appears, while the slow destruction of health through knowingly unsafe work may not. Assault appears, while the deliberate marketing of a dangerous product may be routed toward compensation rather than prosecution. Fraud appears, while sharp dealing that stays within drafted loopholes escapes the label altogether. The Marxist objection is not that the included acts are harmless or unworthy of attention. The objection is that the boundary is treated as natural when it is in fact made, maintained and defended by institutions with unequal resources behind them.

Once the boundary is seen as made, a different set of questions opens. Which interests benefit when a harm is criminalised and which benefit when the same kind of harm is civil, regulatory or entirely lawful? What work does a definition do for those who own workplaces, lend money, control housing or command administrative machinery? How do legislatures, courts and enforcement agencies decide where scarce attention should fall? Such questions shift criminology away from a narrow focus on the offender and toward the whole apparatus that names offences, selects suspects, funds investigations and sets penalties. The offender remains in view, but the offender no longer monopolises explanation.

Class enters here in a precise sense. Class, in Marxist analysis, names a position within relations of production: whether a person owns productive assets and lives from the labour of others, sells labour power in order to live, manages on behalf of owners, or occupies one of the intermediate positions that modern economies multiply. Class is not a synonym for income band, accent, taste or self image, though all of those may follow from it. Because law protects property, regulates contracts, disciplines labour and secures the conditions under which accumulation proceeds, class position shapes both exposure to law and capacity to shape law. Those who draft, lobby, litigate and interpret begin the contest with advantages that those who are merely subject to rules rarely possess.

Ideology supplies the link between material advantage and public acceptance, and the link matters because domination that relied on force alone would be expensive and brittle. Ideology, used carefully, does not mean a set of lies told by cynics to dupes. Beliefs about desert, danger, responsibility and order circulate through schooling, news, entertainment, professional training and everyday talk, and they are often held sincerely by people at every level of society. Their ideological character lies in the pattern of what they make visible and what they obscure. Crime becomes imaginable chiefly as the act of a stranger in a street, while harm inside a boardroom becomes imaginable chiefly as error, misjudgement or unfortunate side effect. Responsibility becomes intensely individual when the accused is poor and curiously diffuse when decisions pass through committees, subsidiaries and supply chains. A Marxist account asks why those patterns recur, whose interests the recurrence serves, and why alternatives feel implausible even to people harmed by the arrangement.

The state, in turn, cannot be reduced to a committee taking dictation, nor treated as a neutral referee standing above social conflict. States raise revenue, keep records, run courts, license activity, inspect workplaces and monopolise legitimate coercion, and each function creates interests and routines of its own. Officials may resist particular demands from business, courts may rule against powerful litigants, and legislatures may impose costs on industries that fought hard against them. None of that refutes a Marxist reading by itself, because the deeper claim concerns the terrain on which such contests occur. A state dependent on private investment for employment and revenue faces pressure to preserve confidence, profitability and order even when no individual capitalist issues an instruction. Structural pressure and direct command are different mechanisms, and confusing them has generated much needless argument inside and outside the tradition.

### Why does the definition of crime matter as much as its causes?

Definition decides which harms enter criminology at all, which suspects attract investigation, and which injuries receive public sympathy. A cause sought only inside legally defined offences will miss harms excluded by lobbying, drafting, enforcement habit, or evidential difficulty. Definition therefore shapes data, theory, punishment, and memory, long before any offender is explained.

Causal inquiry inherits the limits of the categories it accepts. If official records count only conduct that agencies have chosen to pursue, then theories built on those records will describe the pursued population more accurately than the harmful population. Young men in heavily policed neighbourhoods become highly visible, generating data that appear to confirm their dangerousness, which in turn justifies further patrols and further records. Meanwhile a firm that underreports injuries, misclassifies workers or contaminates a water supply may generate paperwork rather than arrests, and paperwork rarely feeds the public image of crime. The circle is not necessarily conspiratorial. Budgets, career incentives, evidential rules and media demand all push in compatible directions, and compatibility is enough to produce a stable distortion.

Consider how seriousness gets assigned. A single act of violence commands immediate moral clarity because a victim, a perpetrator and a moment can be identified. Diffuse harm resists that clarity. When illness follows years of exposure, when savings vanish through layered financial products, or when a community loses its economic base after decisions taken in distant offices, causation stretches across time and responsibility fragments among many hands. Legal systems built around discrete acts and identifiable intent handle the first kind of harm far more comfortably than the second. Marxist criminology treats that comfort as socially patterned rather than merely technical. Doctrines of intent, causation and corporate personality did not fall from the sky. Doctrines developed within societies organised around private property and wage labour, and their blind spots correspond, with uncomfortable regularity, to the characteristic harms of such societies.

Definition also governs the moral status of the people involved. Call an act crime and its author becomes a criminal, an identity that follows a person into employment, housing and family life. Call a comparable act a breach, a violation or a compliance failure and its author remains a professional who made an error. Language performs sorting work before any sentence is passed. The teenager who takes goods from a shop and the executive who authorises the sale of goods known to be unsafe may both act knowingly for gain, yet only one is routinely described through the vocabulary of wickedness. Explaining that asymmetry does not require denying differences between the acts. Differences exist, in immediacy, in intention and in the experience of victims. Explanation requires noticing that the vocabulary available for each case was prepared in advance by institutions that treat property, contract and market order as the background conditions of social life.

A final reason definition matters concerns reform. Campaigns that accept existing categories tend to ask for tougher sentences, faster processing or wider nets, and wider nets usually catch more of the people already most visible to enforcement. Campaigns that interrogate categories ask a prior question: what would a system look like if harm, rather than legal label, determined priority? Answers vary sharply among Marxist positions, from abolishing whole bodies of law to redirecting enforcement upward toward boardrooms, from expanding the concept toward social harm to insisting that working class victims need the protection of a more honest conventional criminology. The quarrel over definition is therefore also a quarrel over political direction, which is why the four positions cannot be collapsed into one another without loss.

### How does class enter the analysis before any offence occurs?

Class shapes the writing of rules, the ownership of property those rules protect, the resources available for lobbying and litigation, and the neighbourhoods subjected to routine surveillance. Long before an alleged offence, class has influenced which conduct was prohibited, which harms were tolerated, and which people enforcement expects to find offending.

Begin with property itself. A legal order that protects existing ownership must decide what counts as taking, damage, trespass and interference, and those decisions presuppose a distribution of assets that is itself the outcome of earlier economic and political struggles. Enclosure, colonisation, inheritance, company law and labour discipline all belong to the long prehistory of ordinary property offences, even though a courtroom hearing a theft charge will treat ownership as a settled fact rather than a question. Marxist analysis refuses that amnesia. When a hungry person takes food and a landowner excludes a community from land once used in common, law sees a crime in the first scene and a right in the second. Pointing out the contrast does not settle what a just law would say. Pointing out the contrast does show that the starting line was drawn by power, and that neutrality at the moment of enforcement can coexist with partiality at the moment of definition.

Labour discipline forms a second early entry point for class. Societies organised around wage labour need workers to appear, comply, accept direction and refrain from combining in ways that threaten production, and law has repeatedly supplied tools for those needs: rules against vagrancy, combination, picketing, absconding and breach of contract have all, at various times, converted economic dependence into legal jeopardy. Even where such tools have softened, subtler versions persist in the treatment of benefit claimants, migrants without secure status, street vendors, rough sleepers and others whose survival strategies unfold in public view. Visibility itself becomes a class condition. Conduct that would pass unnoticed behind the doors of a house, a club or an office becomes actionable when poverty forces it into streets, stations and shopping precincts.

Resources form a third entry point. Shaping law is expensive and slow, favouring actors who can fund research, retain counsel, sustain lobbying across many sessions and absorb the cost of test cases. Winning in court is likewise shaped by the ability to hire expertise, delay proceedings, settle strategically and appeal adverse rulings. None of this requires corruption. A system can be formally open and substantively tilted, much as a race can be formally open while some runners start far ahead with better shoes, coaching and rest. Marxist criminology attends to the tilt, and to the way formal equality before the law can legitimise outcomes produced by substantive inequality outside it.

Exposure completes the picture. Policing concentrates where disorder is expected, schools exclude pupils already marked as troublesome, employers screen out applicants with records, and each intervention raises the probability of the next. A record acquired young narrows lawful options, narrowed options raise the temptation and sometimes the necessity of unlawful ones, and the resulting offences are then cited as proof that the original attention was warranted. Critics outside the Marxist tradition have documented similar feedback loops under other names. What Marxism adds is an account of why the loops align so consistently with class position: because the institutions running them protect an economic order in which some people command assets and others must sell themselves by the hour, the week or the task.

None of this makes individual choice unreal. People in identical circumstances act differently, show courage or cruelty, resist temptation or embrace it, and a sociology that dissolves agency into structure explains too much and too little at once. Class analysis works at the level of probabilities, pressures and menus of choice. Menus differ. For one person the menu includes accountants who can restructure a transaction until its illegality dissolves into ambiguity. For another the menu includes pawnshops, loan sharks, overcrowded housing and streets where every dispute risks police attention. Judging the choices without comparing the menus yields moralism dressed as analysis. Comparing the menus without judging the choices yields determinism dressed as compassion. The stronger Marxist work holds both, uneasily, and the tension between structure and agency runs through all four positions like a fault line.

### What do the four positions agree on despite their quarrels?

All four hold that crime categories reflect power, that enforcement falls unevenly across classes, that capitalism generates pressures toward offending, and that harms committed by the powerful deserve far greater attention. Agreement ends at mechanism and remedy, where state autonomy, ideology, victim experience, and the proper scope of criminology divide them sharply.

Agreement on power and categories separates the tradition from approaches that treat law as a simple expression of shared values. Consensus accounts of law, in which prohibitions mirror a moral unity supposedly present in society, struggle with the historical record of laws imposed on hostile populations and maintained for narrow benefit. Marxist positions, whatever their differences, start from conflict: law emerges from struggle among groups with unequal resources, and its content bears the marks of who won. Even reforms that genuinely protect the vulnerable are read within that frame, as concessions won by organisation, as measures that stabilise a system by softening its edges, or as evidence that the state must sometimes act against individual owners to preserve the order owners collectively need. Interpretation differs, but the refusal of innocent law is constant.

Agreement on uneven enforcement is equally firm. Arrest, prosecution and imprisonment have never fallen randomly across the social structure, and no position in the tradition treats that pattern as accidental. Disagreement concerns explanation and significance. Traditional accounts emphasise deliberate class bias in the design and operation of law. Neo-Marxist accounts add the relative autonomy of legal institutions and the ideological work that secures consent. Accounts centred on hegemony and moral panic examine how episodes of fear recruit public support for expanded control. Left realists insist that uneven enforcement coexists with real victimisation concentrated in poor communities, so that criticising the police cannot become a reason to abandon the policed. Each position can cite the same prison population and tell a different, partly true story about how it came to be filled.

Capitalism as a generator of offending supplies a third shared commitment. Competition rewards acquisition, advertising cultivates dissatisfaction, insecure work corrodes routine and status, and markets in illegal goods arise where legal livelihoods fail or where legal markets have created demand they refuse to supply. Offending, on this view, is not an alien invasion of an otherwise harmonious order. Offending often expresses the order's own values carried into unauthorised channels: get ahead, display success, seize opportunity, treat others as instruments. The young man who steals branded goods and the trader who manipulates a market differ enormously in method and consequence, yet both act inside a culture that measures worth through possession and victory. Traditional writers pressed that parallel hardest, while later positions complicated it with culture, identity, gender and local circumstance, but the underlying thought survived every complication.

Attention to the harms of the powerful forms the fourth bond. Unsafe products, polluted environments, stolen wages, fraudulent finance, negligent care and violence committed or licensed by states appear in every version of the tradition, sometimes as crime improperly unlabelled, sometimes as social harm beyond the reach of criminal law, always as a rebuke to a criminology content to study the powerless. Where the positions split is over remedy. Should enforcement be redirected upward within existing institutions, or are those institutions so structured that redirection will always be absorbed and deflected? Should the concept of crime be stretched to cover boardroom harm, or abandoned in favour of a broader vocabulary of harm that escapes legal categories altogether? Should working class demands for safety be met by reformed policing, or does every concession to policing strengthen an apparatus that will be used downward? Those questions organise the rest of the article, and no honest summary can pretend they have been settled inside the tradition itself.

A preview of the four positions, with a fifth development attached, will fix the map before the detailed treatment begins. The traditional position, associated with Willem Bonger, William Chambliss and Richard Quinney, reads law as an instrument shaped by and for a ruling class, enforcement as class selective, and crime as a product of capitalist competition and deprivation. Its strength is clarity about interests. Its vulnerability is a tendency to understate the independence of courts, the reality of working class victimisation and the occasions when law restrains capital itself. Section two of this article examines that position at length, because every later position defines itself partly against it.

Neo-Marxist work loosened the instrumental picture. Drawing on Antonio Gramsci and on structural accounts of the state, writers in this vein argued that law possesses relative autonomy, that ruling class power works through hegemony rather than constant command, and that legal forms can impose genuine constraints while still securing the long term conditions of accumulation. A landmark collective study of policing and punishment in capitalist societies treated the criminal justice system as a site of struggle rather than a simple tool, and allowed that reforms, rights and due process protections carry real weight even inside an unequal order. Critics from the traditional side heard retreat in such arguments, a softening of class analysis into general talk about complexity. Defenders replied that a theory unable to explain why states sometimes jail executives, break monopolies or expand welfare is not radical but merely crude.

The hegemony and moral panic position grows from that neo-Marxist soil while shifting attention toward culture and meaning. Stuart Hall and colleagues, analysing a panic over mugging, argued that a relatively minor category of street robbery became the focus of intense public fear at a moment of economic and political crisis, and that the fear did political work: it authorised a more coercive state, displaced anxiety about structural decline onto folk devils drawn from marginalised communities, and built consent for order among people who gained little from the order defended. Moral panic analysis has since travelled far beyond its Marxist origins, sometimes losing its class anchor entirely. In its original form the anchor was essential. Panics were not random gusts of public feeling but episodes in which media, police, courts and politicians converged, consciously or not, to manage a crisis of hegemony by dramatising threats from below while threats from above faded from view.

Left realism arrived as the internal correction, a phrase this article will use again when the traditional position is examined, because the correction was aimed first at fellow radicals. Jock Young, John Lea and their collaborators charged that a certain left idealism had romanticised offenders, minimised the suffering of victims in poor neighbourhoods, dismissed public fear as manufactured, and offered no practical programme for communities demanding both safety and justice. Working class people, they insisted, suffer disproportionately from burglary, violence and disorder, and a left that cannot speak to that suffering abandons the field to punitive politics. Left realism retained the critique of capitalism and of selective enforcement while demanding that conventional crime be taken seriously, that victims be surveyed and heard, and that policing be made accountable rather than simply denounced. Traditionalists and neo-Marxists suspected a slide toward the very law and order agenda the tradition existed to oppose. Left realists answered that refusing to govern is also a political choice, and one whose costs fall on the poorest.

Social harm, finally, is less a fifth position with a settled doctrine than a further development pressing against the boundaries the other four still inhabit. Writers associated with the zemiological turn, among them Paddy Hillyard and Steve Tombs, propose that criminology should study harm wherever it occurs: in workplaces, markets, environments, care settings and state action, whether or not law names it crime. The move solves one problem and creates another. Harm escapes the distortions of legal definition, yet harm itself needs definition, and the definitional struggle returns at a higher level, now conducted in the language of human need, rights and ecological limits rather than statute. Whether that escape succeeds, and what is lost when the specificity of crime and punishment drops away, remains an open dispute. The dispute is productive precisely because each side can point to real failures in the other: crime categories that hide corporate killing, and harm categories so broad that almost any policy failure qualifies.

Running beneath the map is a methodological warning. Each position selects evidence that flatters it. Traditional accounts gravitate toward the origins of statutes and the composition of prisons. Neo-Marxist accounts gravitate toward institutional complexity and ideological consent. Panic accounts gravitate toward media episodes. Left realists gravitate toward victim surveys and local case studies. Social harm accounts gravitate toward occupational death, pollution and financial loss. A reader who follows only one literature will inherit its selection along with its conclusions. Serious engagement requires moving among the literatures and asking, for each favoured example, what the rival positions would notice in the same scene. A vagrancy statute, a panic headline, a victim survey and a factory inquest can all describe the same society. Marxist criminology at its best is the argument among those descriptions, conducted with the knowledge that the argument itself reflects the divisions it studies.

Gender, race and migration status complicate the class story without dissolving it, and the stronger work in every position has learned to say so. Women experience harm in domestic and intimate settings that older criminology, Marxist work included, long treated as private and therefore peripheral, while enforcement against women has often policed respectability as much as safety. Racialised communities face surveillance regimes whose histories involve empire, slavery and border control, forces that interact with capitalism without being reducible to a simple reflex of it. Migrant workers occupy a position in production made more coercive by legal insecurity, so that class exploitation and state classification reinforce one another in a single working day. A class analysis that cannot absorb such facts becomes a skeleton without a body. A class analysis that absorbs them well shows how labour markets segment, how some workers are made disposable, how respectability divides the poor against themselves, and how fear of the outsider can be mobilised to defend an order that harms insider and outsider alike, though rarely in identical ways.

Everyday life supplies the texture that grand categories can flatten. Rent arrears, shift patterns, school exclusions, debt collection, benefit sanctions and the price of food do not appear in most theories of crime as named variables, yet they form the weather in which decisions get made. A young person recruited into selling illegal drugs may be chasing status, excitement and belonging as much as income, and a purely economic reading will miss the cultural charge of the work. An employee who falsifies records may fear dismissal more than desire gain. A manager who cuts a safety budget may never meet the people endangered and may sincerely describe the cut as efficiency. Marxist criminology does not require cartoon villains or saints. Capitalist relations operate through ordinary incentives, respectable routines and plausible self descriptions, which is precisely why their effects are so durable. People reproduce the order while pursuing intelligible aims: keeping a job, winning promotion, protecting a family, avoiding shame. Condemnation that ignores those aims preaches. Analysis that excuses everything because aims were intelligible abdicates. Between preaching and abdication lies the difficult ground the four positions occupy in different ways.

Evidence poses a standing challenge for the whole tradition, and candour about that challenge strengthens rather than weakens the case. Showing that a law benefits owners does not by itself prove that benefit caused the law, since many measures benefit groups that did not seek them and fail groups that did. Showing that enforcement falls on the poor does not by itself prove discriminatory intent, since offending patterns, reporting patterns and deployment patterns intertwine. Showing that a panic coincided with a crisis does not by itself prove that the panic was engineered to manage the crisis. Marxist writers at their most careful therefore work comparatively and historically: tracing drafting records, following money and personnel, comparing how similar harms are handled across sectors, and asking whether the pattern survives when rival explanations are given full weight. Where the evidence shows mixed motives, institutional inertia or genuine public demand, the honest Marxist account incorporates those findings and narrows its claim accordingly. Grand theory that survives every possible observation explains nothing. A tradition that can lose arguments in detail is one that can also win them.

Public demand deserves particular care, because dismissing it is both an analytical error and a political gift to opponents. Fear of burglary, assault and disorder is not simply implanted by headlines. Experience, gossip, visible decay and the memory of specific incidents all feed it, and in neighbourhoods where services retreat and informal controls weaken, fear can be a rational reading of the environment. Neo-Marxist and panic centred work sometimes wrote as if amplification explained everything, leaving the original experience unexamined. Left realism built its identity on correcting that error. Yet the correction can overshoot when demand for safety is treated as transparent and self interpreting. What residents ask for, more patrols, longer sentences, removal of particular groups, may reflect the menu of responses made available by politicians and media rather than the full range of measures that would actually reduce harm, from youth provision and addiction treatment to lighting, housing repair and accountable local policing. Respecting experience while questioning the menu is the balanced position, and reaching it took the tradition decades of internal argument.

Punishment, too, looks different once definition has been questioned. If crime is partly a label applied downward, then prison populations reveal as much about the labellers as the labelled. That insight, central to the tradition from its earliest statements, does not entail that everyone imprisoned is harmless or that custody never protects anyone. Particular individuals commit grave violence and may need restraint under any plausible social order. The Marxist point operates at the level of the system: a society that imprisons the poor at vast scale while fining, settling or excusing the harms of institutions has made a distributive choice, and describing that choice as the natural output of neutral law conceals it. Debates about abolition, reduction and reform divide the four positions, sometimes bitterly. Traditional and neo-Marxist writers lean toward suspicion of penal expansion in any form. Left realists accept a role for enforcement and custody while demanding accountability and proportionality. Social harm writers ask whether the energy spent refining punishment might better go toward preventing the conditions, market, workplace and domestic, in which serious injuries accumulate unseen.

One more preliminary belongs in this opening section, concerning Marx himself. Marx wrote no systematic criminology, and the tradition that bears the adjective Marxist was built by later writers applying his analysis of capitalism to questions he touched only in passing: the criminal as a figure in economic satire, the lumpenproletariat in political writing, primitive accumulation and the bloody legislation against the expropriated in his account of capital's origins. That last discussion matters most for criminology, because it presented law as an active maker of the working class, driving people from land, criminalising their resulting destitution and disciplining them into wage labour. Later theorists generalised the insight far beyond its original setting. Fidelity to Marx is therefore a weak test of a Marxist criminology, since the master left fragments rather than a system. Fidelity to his method is the stronger test: begin from material relations, trace how legal forms arise within them, follow the interests and the illusions together, and expect the categories of an age to defend the arrangements of that age while presenting themselves as timeless.

With definition, class, ideology and the state linked in outline, and with the four positions plus the social harm development placed on the map, the detailed examination can proceed in order. The traditional position comes first, not because it is the most adequate, but because it is the source from which the others flow and against which they still measure themselves. Its claims are the boldest, its examples the most quoted, its weaknesses the most instructive. Understanding why vagrancy law became its classic demonstration, why corporate harm became its central exhibit, and why its own allies eventually mounted the internal correction against it will make the later positions intelligible as responses to real problems rather than as fashions in theory. The question of definition, opened here, will recur in every section, because each position is finally a different answer to it: who gets to say what crime is, and in whose interest the saying is done.

Readers approaching the tradition for the first time sometimes ask whether its claims can be summarised as the assertion that everything is class and nothing else matters. The four positions, taken together, answer no. Class organises the terrain, ideology shapes perception of the terrain, institutions act with habits and interests of their own, culture gives offending and victimhood their local meanings, and individuals choose within conditions they did not choose. What Marxist criminology adds to criminologies that start elsewhere is a refusal to treat the terrain itself as scenery. Terrain was built, is maintained, and could be built otherwise, and the building work is done largely through law.

## The Traditional Position: Law Made for Property

The traditional position states its thesis without decoration. Law in a capitalist society is made in the interests of those who own property, enforced selectively against those who do not, and surrounded by a picture of crime that directs attention toward the powerless while the gravest harms proceed under the protection of legality, regulation or polite neglect. Crime itself, in the traditional account, is not a mystery of individual pathology. Crime grows from the soil of capitalism: from competition that teaches people to measure worth by acquisition, from inequality that places coveted goods in view and lawful routes to them out of reach, from insecurity that corrodes obligation, and from a market order that celebrates appetite while punishing the appetite of the poor when it takes unauthorised forms. Boldness is the position's virtue and its danger. A thesis stated so plainly can be tested, quoted and taught, and it can also be caricatured, both by opponents eager to refute a simple version and by supporters tempted to repeat the simple version long after the evidence has demanded qualification.

Property stands at the centre because property, for this tradition, is not one interest among many but the organising interest of the social order. To own productive assets is to command the labour of others, to decide what will be made, where investment will flow and which communities will prosper or decline. Law secures that command at every point: title, contract, inheritance, company personality, limited liability, bankruptcy priority and the enforcement of debts together form the legal skeleton of accumulation. Criminal law appears, in the traditional reading, as the guard at the edge of that skeleton. Offences against property vastly outnumber offences against the person in the business of courts, penalties for taking from owners have historically carried a severity out of proportion to the harm done, and the whole apparatus of policing grew up alongside the protection of trade, warehouses, docks, factories and shops. None of this requires a secret meeting at which owners dictate statutes. Legislators drawn disproportionately from propertied and professional circles, advised by interests able to sustain a permanent presence, and dependent on an economy they dare not destabilise will reliably produce law congenial to property, and will experience that production as common sense rather than as service.

Willem Bonger gave the position one of its earliest systematic statements, arguing that capitalism cultivates egoism by placing individuals in competitive relations where the success of one requires the failure of another. Altruism, in Bonger's reasoning, withers when social organisation rewards self seeking and penalises solidarity, and crime follows as egoism in action, distributed across classes but expressed through the opportunities each class commands. The bourgeois offender defrauds, adulterates, speculates and corrupts. The proletarian offender steals, brawls and pilfers. Moral condemnation falls overwhelmingly on the second list, though the first may injure far more people, because law and opinion together are formed inside the same egoistic order and share its blind spots. Bonger also pressed the connection between economic insecurity and offences born of desperation, insisting that a society which tolerates destitution amid plenty manufactures a portion of the crime it then punishes. Later critics found his psychology broad and his categories loose. Defenders reply that breadth was the point: Bonger refused to treat crime as a special behaviour requiring a special explanation, and instead located offending inside the normal moral economy of capitalism, a move every subsequent Marxist position retained in some form.

Selective enforcement supplies the second pillar. Even where a statute is written in general terms, the traditional argument runs, its application bends toward the bottom of society. Police patrol public space, where the poor live much of their lives, far more intensively than private space, where the comfortable conduct theirs. Prosecutors weigh the cost and difficulty of cases, and cases against the powerful are expensive, slow and professionally risky, while cases against the powerless are quick and career safe. Evidential rules that seem neutral in the abstract operate unequally in practice: the paper trail of a street offence is short, while corporate wrongdoing hides inside delegated decisions, complex accounts and legal advice protected from disclosure. Bail, fines, legal representation and the collateral consequences of conviction all distribute their weight by wealth. A fine that inconveniences an executive can destitute a cleaner. A record that a professional can survive can permanently exclude a manual worker from employment. Formal sameness, substantive inequality: the traditional position returns to that contrast repeatedly because the contrast recurs wherever the system is examined closely.

Defenders of the existing order answer that enforcement follows offending, and that offending is concentrated where poverty, family disruption and neighbourhood disorder concentrate. Traditional writers rarely denied the premise outright. Their reply operated at two levels. First, the distribution of offending is itself partly an artefact of the definitions and deployments already described, so citing enforcement statistics to justify enforcement patterns argues in a circle. Second, even granting real differences in conventional offending, the comparison class has been rigged in advance. Include wage theft by employers, safety violations that maim, frauds that empty pension funds, pollution that shortens lives across whole districts, and the picture of who harms whom changes beyond recognition. The traditional position does not need to claim that the poor never offend. The position needs the narrower and stronger claim that the official portrait of crime is systematically unrepresentative of harm, and that the unrepresentativeness serves identifiable interests by legitimating intensive control of the poor and light touch control of the rich.

### Why do vagrancy laws matter to the traditional position?

Vagrancy laws show law being remade as labour needs changed, punishing the masterless and mobile poor until they accepted wage work on terms set by landowners. The example links definition, class interest, and enforcement in one documented sequence, giving the traditional claim a historical demonstration rather than a slogan.

William Chambliss supplied that demonstration in a study of vagrancy legislation published in 1964, and its influence on the tradition is difficult to overstate. Chambliss traced how laws against vagrancy changed in character as the economic order changed around them. In their earlier form, the laws addressed a problem of labour supply. After plague had thinned the working population and surviving labourers could bargain, wander or refuse work on the old terms, legislation criminalised the condition of being without a master, without visible means of support, without settled employment. The able bodied person who declined available work at prevailing wages became an offender not because of any act of violence or taking, but because mobility and independence threatened the supply of cheap labour to landowners. Punishment aimed to coerce people back into service and to hold wages down by destroying the option of refusal. Law, in short, was drafted to solve a problem for employers, and its target was a status created by economic change.

Later centuries gave the same legal family a different job, and Chambliss followed the transformation with care. As commerce expanded and towns grew, vagrancy law shifted toward the protection of trade and travel, targeting those thought to threaten merchants, carriers and property in transit. Robbers, cheats and disorderly wanderers replaced the labour refuser at the centre of concern, not because the poor had changed their nature, but because the dominant economic interests had changed their needs. Still later, the laws settled into a general instrument of public order, used to move on, harass or detain people whose presence unsettled commercial and residential comfort. Across each phase, Chambliss argued, the constant was not the behaviour of the vagrant, which varied widely and often amounted to little more than poverty in public. The constant was the interest served. When landowners needed labour, vagrancy law disciplined labour. When merchants needed safe roads and markets, vagrancy law policed movement. When propertied townspeople needed reassurance, vagrancy law removed the visible poor from view.

Several features make the study a classic demonstration rather than merely an interesting history. The evidence comes from the laws themselves, their timing and their stated purposes, so the argument does not depend on speculation about hidden motives. The changes in legal content track changes in economic structure closely enough to embarrass any account in which law simply expresses timeless morality, since the morality supposedly expressed condemned idleness in one century, mobility in another and visible destitution in a third. The victims of the laws were, throughout, people whose real offence was a weak position in the labour market. And the enforcement pattern matched the drafting pattern, falling on those without protectors, property or a plausible story. A single legal family thus exhibits, in sequence, every element of the traditional thesis: definition shaped by ruling interests, enforcement aimed downward, and conduct criminalised because of what it threatened economically rather than because of the harm it inflicted.

Critics have not left the study unexamined, and the traditional position is stronger for absorbing their points. Historians have noted charitable, religious and genuinely order keeping motives tangled inside vagrancy legislation, and have questioned whether labour scarcity alone explains the earliest statutes in all their severity. Others observe that a law can serve an interest without having been created for that interest, and that Chambliss sometimes moved quickly from function to origin. Defenders answer on two levels. Mixed motives do not dissolve the pattern, because the question is which motives prevailed when provisions were drafted, sharpened or enforced, and on that question the labour discipline provisions remain stark. Function and origin, moreover, converge in the later history: whatever the original mixture, authorities repeatedly retained and adapted the laws because of the control they offered over the mobile poor, and retention under changing conditions is itself evidence about the interests a legal instrument serves. The study survives criticism in the way strong demonstrations do, narrowed in scope, clarified in mechanism, and still standing as the example every student of the tradition meets first.

Generalisations drawn from vagrancy reach well beyond vagrancy. If law could criminalise a status to secure labour in one era, analogous questions arise wherever legal categories coincide suspiciously with economic convenience: offences that keep public space clear for commerce, benefit rules whose breach becomes fraud, migration offences that create a workforce too frightened to complain, drug prohibitions whose enforcement concentrates in neighbourhoods marked for redevelopment. The traditional method is to ask, of any offence category, who benefits from its existence, who bears its costs, and whether the harm it names would still be named if the powerful engaged in it. That method does not guarantee true answers. Applied carelessly it becomes a reflex that finds class interest everywhere and therefore explains nowhere in particular. Applied with Chambliss's patience, following texts, dates and enforcement records, the method remains one of the most productive in criminology, and its productivity is a large part of why the traditional position, for all its vulnerabilities, refuses to disappear.

### What makes corporate harm central to the traditional argument?

Corporate harm tests the claim that law protects property above persons. Deaths, injuries, frauds, and pollution arising from business decisions exceed conventional crime in damage yet attract negotiation, fines, or inaction. If definition and enforcement were neutral, scale of harm would predict severity of response. It rarely does.

Scale is the first element. A single industrial disaster can kill more people than a year of homicides in the community that hosts the plant. A fraudulent financial scheme can destroy more savings than every burglary in a city combined. Pollution released over decades can shorten lives across generations, and unsafe products distributed widely can injure children in numbers no street gang could approach. Traditional writers insist on placing such cases beside the conventional crime figures that dominate public debate, not to minimise the terror of violent crime, but to expose the selectivity of the comparison itself. Public fear is trained on the dramatic and the interpersonal. Institutional harm is statistical, delayed and authorless in appearance, and the appearance is cultivated by organisational design: decisions divided among departments, warnings softened in memoranda, responsibility dissolved into process. When no single hand holds the weapon, law built around individual intent falters, and the faltering is treated as a regrettable limitation rather than as a design feature of a legal order created by and for corporate forms.

Response is the second element. Conventional offenders face arrest, charge, trial and custody through processes designed to be swift and exemplary. Corporate offenders, in the traditional account, face inspection, negotiation, undertakings, deferred agreements and fines calibrated to be absorbable as business costs. Premises are rarely raided at dawn. Executives are rarely held in cells awaiting charge. Names are shielded by the corporate veil, and statements express regret for outcomes while denying the decisions that produced them. Where prosecution does occur, it frequently settles on junior employees or on the corporation as an abstract entity, whose fine is paid ultimately by shareholders, workers or customers rather than by the decision makers who profited. Traditional writers treat that asymmetry as the cleanest available evidence for their thesis, cleaner even than the history of statutes, because the asymmetry is visible in the present operation of enforcement and cannot be explained by the drafting intentions of long dead legislators. Whatever the law says, the machinery moves differently when the suspect wears a suit and retains counsel by the hour.

Defenders of corporate practice reply with a set of arguments that deserve hearing in full. Business activity produces social benefit, employment, goods, tax revenue and innovation, and a legal regime that treated every harmful outcome as crime would chill enterprise and punish people for risks that society implicitly accepted. Causation in organisational settings is genuinely complex, and criminal conviction requires proof of a culpable mind that may honestly not exist at any single point in a large firm. Regulation, compensation and insurance may repair harm more effectively than prosecution, which arrives late, costs heavily and often fails. Traditional writers answer each point without denying its partial force. Benefit does not distinguish corporate harm from conventional crime, since many conventional offenders also support families and contribute to their communities, yet receive no discount for it. Complexity is partly manufactured, because firms choose structures that diffuse responsibility and then plead the diffusion in their defence. Regulation and compensation are valuable precisely where they are strong, funded and feared, and the traditional complaint is that they are routinely weak, underfunded and negotiable, with penalties set below the profit the violation earned. A deterrent priced as a cost of business is not a deterrent but a licence fee.

Richard Quinney pushed the traditional position toward its most uncompromising formulation. For Quinney, crime is not a natural category of behaviour but a definition applied by those with power to protect a particular social order, and law functions as an instrument of the ruling class for maintaining the conditions of its dominance. Criminal definitions, in his account, are constructed to serve ruling interests, applied selectively by enforcement agencies that act as agents of those interests, and internalised by a public taught to fear the crimes of the poor while accepting the arrangements that produce them. Quinney also directed attention to the crimes of domination themselves: the violence, exploitation and repression through which an unequal order is created and preserved, acts that law does not merely neglect but actively authorises. Later Quinney moved toward more philosophical and critical positions, yet the instrumental core of his criminology remains the sharpest statement of the traditional view, and therefore the version opponents most often quote and supporters most often qualify.

Qualification became necessary because the uncompromising version invites obvious counterexamples. Laws against fraud, pollution, unsafe products and corrupt dealing exist, were often passed over business resistance, and have sometimes been enforced with real severity against prominent firms and executives. Welfare provision, workplace rights and public health regulation all impose genuine costs on capital. Courts occasionally rule for claimants against employers, tenants against landlords, communities against developers. A theory that predicts uniform service to ruling interests must explain why the service so often arrives disguised as defeat. Traditional defenders developed several replies. Some laws, they argued, discipline individual capitals in the interest of capital as a whole, preventing the reckless firm from destabilising markets or provoking unrest. Some reforms are concessions extracted by organised labour and social movements, real victories that nonetheless stabilise the wider order by making it tolerable. Some enforcement against the powerful is exemplary and theatrical, sacrificing a conspicuous offender to restore confidence in a system left unchanged. Each reply has force in particular cases. Stretched to cover every case, the replies risk making the theory unfalsifiable, since victory and defeat, enforcement and neglect all become evidence for the same conclusion. That risk is the deepest methodological wound in the traditional position, and later positions in the tradition defined themselves largely by how they treated it.

Crime generated from below completes the traditional picture, and here the argument turns from law to the offender. Capitalism, on this account, manufactures criminal motivation as reliably as it manufactures commodities. Competition teaches that worth is relative and displayed, so that the person who cannot acquire lawfully experiences not only deprivation but humiliation, a status injury that illegal acquisition promises to repair. Advertising and the general culture of consumption widen desire while wages and security stagnate, producing the strain that earlier sociologists described in other vocabularies and that Marxist writers reclaimed as a product of class structure rather than individual weakness. Insecure and degrading work weakens the bonds that make conformity rewarding. Communities stripped of industry lose the institutions, clubs, unions, chapels, settled routines, through which informal control once operated, and illegal markets move into the vacancy, offering income, identity and a parody of entrepreneurship to young people abandoned by the legal economy. The drug seller, in this reading, is the purest product of the market order: self employed, risk taking, profit driven, working in the one sector where no credentials, connections or inherited capital are required for entry. Condemning the seller while celebrating the values the seller embodies strikes traditional writers as the central hypocrisy of the order.

Acquisitive values also dissolve restraint inside lawful enterprise, which is why the traditional position treats street crime and suite crime as siblings rather than opposites. Both arise where success is worshipped and failure shamed, where other people appear as competitors, marks or obstacles, and where rules present themselves as challenges to be gamed rather than obligations to be honoured. The difference lies in opportunity and in the legal shelter available. Give the ambitious person a trading desk and the result may be manipulation dressed as innovation. Give the same disposition a street corner and the result is robbery. Society then punishes the robbery as crime and debates the manipulation as ethics, though the motivational structure is shared and the aggregate damage may run the other way. Bonger saw this clearly at the start of the tradition, and the clarity remains startling. A criminology that studies only the corner and never the desk has accepted, in advance, the distribution of suspicion that the powerful would have chosen for it.

Material need, finally, should not be spiritualised away by talk of values alone. Hunger, rent, debt and the care of children exert pressure that moral philosophy conducted in comfort tends to underestimate. Offences of acquisition rise where lawful income collapses, and the connection between destitution and theft is among the oldest observations in social thought. Traditional writers add that need in a consumer society is partly relative and manufactured, since people measure their deprivation against the abundance displayed around them, but relative need is still need in its effects on conduct and self respect. A parent who steals to feed children, a worker who pilfers from an employer paying poverty wages, an addict whose habit was formed inside a market that targeted the neighbourhood: the traditional account does not canonise any of them, and it recognises the victims their acts may create, often neighbours as poor as themselves. What the account refuses is the official story in which such offences spring from individual wickedness while the distribution of income, housing and security that surrounds them counts as innocent background. Background, for this tradition, is where the explanation lives.

Workplace harm deserves a fuller hearing within the traditional argument, because the workplace is where the largest number of people spend the largest portion of their waking lives under the authority of others. Injuries from machinery, falls, vehicles, dust, chemicals and exhaustion accumulate quietly, recorded in compensation files and hospital admissions rather than in crime statistics. Investigations, where they occur, tend to ask whether a guard was fitted, a procedure followed, a warning given, questions that locate failure at the point of production and often at the level of the injured worker or an immediate supervisor. Questions that travel upward, toward production targets, staffing levels, maintenance budgets and delivery deadlines set by distant executives, encounter the familiar diffusion of responsibility. Traditional writers ask why a death caused by a knowingly removed safety device is so rarely treated with the moral gravity reserved for a death caused by a weapon in a street. Answers involving intent only deepen the puzzle, since a decision to save money by accepting a foreseeable risk to life is a decision, taken by identifiable people, with consequences that actuaries can predict in advance. Foreseeability without prosecution is, for this tradition, one of the signatures of class power inside legal doctrine.

Fraud tells a parallel story at the level of property. Conventional theft is pursued as a violation of the social bond itself, dramatised in courtrooms and campaigns, while frauds committed through companies, through false accounting, mis sold products, manipulated benchmarks or concealed liabilities are narrated as failures of governance, lapses of culture or excesses of a few individuals who betrayed an otherwise sound system. The vocabulary matters because vocabulary allocates blame. A culture cannot be imprisoned. A lapse cannot be sentenced. When wrongdoing is described in the passive voice, profits were overstated, controls were bypassed, customers were misled, the grammatical subject vanishes along with the legal one. Traditional analysis restores the subject: directors decided, managers concealed, auditors looked away, regulators accepted assurances, and each participant acted inside incentives that rewarded the concealment and penalised the whistleblower. Restoring the subject does not by itself prove criminal liability in any particular case, and the tradition at its best respects the difference between moral responsibility and provable guilt. Restoring the subject does prove that the absence of prosecutions cannot be read as the absence of culpable conduct, which is the inference public debate draws most often.

State harm forms the darkest extension of the argument. Violence by officials, deaths in custody, abusive detention, the destruction of communities through forced clearance and the harms of war conducted or supplied by governments all fall, in the traditional reading, under the same principle: the institution that defines crime exempts its own characteristic harms from the definition. Quinney pressed this point hardest, treating crimes of domination as the foundation on which ordinary criminal law rests, since an order created by dispossession and maintained by coercion cannot present its own violence as mere administration while condemning the violence of the dispossessed as crime. Critics respond that states also protect, rescue, heal and restrain private violence, and that collapsing all state action into domination erases distinctions citizens rely on daily. Traditional defenders accept the distinctions and dispute the accounting. Protection and domination, they argue, are distributed to different populations, and the same force that rescues one neighbourhood occupies another. A criminology that counts only the protection has accepted the state's own ledger, in which its harms appear as regrettable incidents and its benefits as the essence of its mission.

Gender and race expose both the reach and the blind spots of the traditional position, and honest treatment requires holding both in view. Domestic violence was long minimised by legal systems that treated the household as a private realm, and that minimisation served a patriarchal order intertwined with, but not wholly explained by, property relations. Racialised policing has histories rooted in empire and enslavement that predate industrial capitalism and shaped its development from within. Traditional writers sometimes subordinated such harms to class too quickly, as if naming the economic base settled the analysis. Feminist and critical race scholars working partly inside the Marxist tradition corrected the subordination while retaining its central insight: that law's silences are patterned by power. The corrected traditional position therefore speaks of interlocking dominations, in which class remains fundamental because it organises access to housing, income, legal help and physical safety, the very resources that determine whether a victim of any kind of harm can escape it, report it or survive its consequences. Money does not explain everything about violence inside a home. Money explains a great deal about whether the person enduring that violence can leave, be believed and be housed afterwards, and a criminology that ignores those material conditions will mistake formal rights for real ones.

The media complete the traditional circuit between definition and consent. News values favour the sudden, the violent and the personal, and crime reporting built on police sources reproduces the enforcement pattern as a picture of the world. Readers learn to fear the offences that fill bulletins and to regard the offences that fill inquests, tribunals and regulatory reports as a separate, duller category of event, hardly crime at all. Traditional writers did not claim that editors receive instructions from owners in every instance, though ownership concentration and advertising dependence form part of their account. The stronger claim concerns selection: stories that dramatise threats from below and promise safety through punishment are cheap to produce, easy to dramatise and congenial to the institutions that supply them, while investigations into corporate or state harm are expensive, legally dangerous and resisted by public relations machinery. Output shaped by those economics will flatter power without any conspiracy needing to exist. Later work on hegemony and moral panic developed this insight into a full theory of consent, yet the seed lies here, in the traditional insistence that the public picture of crime is manufactured under material constraints that favour the powerful.

Reform, within the traditional position, therefore aims higher than better enforcement of existing categories. Some traditional writers argued for redirecting prosecution toward the boardroom, with penalties that reach decision makers personally and fines set against profit rather than against turnover in the abstract. Others argued that such redirection, while worth attempting, will always be blunted by the structure it operates inside, and that the deeper remedy lies in changing the relations that generate both the harms and the blindness to them: democratic control of workplaces, decommodified housing and care, and forms of accountability that do not depend on the victim hiring a lawyer. Still others concentrated on decriminalisation from below, urging the repeal of status offences, the end of imprisonment for poverty linked conduct such as fine default and rough sleeping, and the transfer of resources from punishment to provision. The disagreements are genuine, and they prefigure the splits that produced the later positions. What unites the traditional reformers is the conviction that tinkering with sentencing while leaving definition untouched merely rearranges the machinery of selective control. Change who counts as a criminal, the tradition insists, and the statistics, the headlines and the politics of crime all change with it. Leave the categories alone, and every reform is absorbed by them.

Sceptics outside the tradition offer a challenge that any honest restatement must include. If law simply serves owners, why do owners so often complain about regulation, taxation, liability and prosecution, and why do business associations spend so heavily resisting measures they supposedly control? If the state were a reliable instrument, the resistance would be theatre, yet the expenditures and the occasional genuine defeats suggest real conflict. Traditional writers reply that a class is not a single mind. Individual firms resist costs that the class as a whole may need imposed for its long term survival, from limits on fraud that preserve market confidence to safety rules that preserve a healthy workforce, and the state earns its keep precisely by imposing the collective interest against short sighted members. Conflict between government and business is therefore compatible with the thesis, provided the outcomes never threaten the underlying relations of ownership themselves. Whether that proviso saves the theory or insulates it from refutation is the question the neo-Marxist position took up, by granting the state and the law a relative autonomy that the traditional picture lacked. The argument between the two remains the engine room of the whole tradition, and it begins from the evidence assembled here.

Enforcement in daily life gives the traditional argument its most ordinary and therefore its most persuasive illustrations. A shoplifter is detained in public, searched, recorded and banned, while a wage payment system that quietly shortchanges thousands of employees is corrected by repayment without admission, penalty or publicity, though the aggregate taking may dwarf the value of goods lifted from shelves in an entire district. A tenant who damages a landlord's property faces prosecution and eviction, while a landlord who leaves tenants without heat, water or secure doors faces a slow administrative process that the tenants must initiate and sustain. A street trader without a licence is moved on within the hour, while a firm trading without adequate safety provision negotiates a timetable for improvement. Each pairing can be explained individually, by immediacy, by evidence, by the difference between criminal and civil jurisdiction. Traditional writers ask readers to look at the pairings together, as a pattern, and to notice that the pattern never seems to run the other way. Speed, publicity, stigma and custody gather at the bottom of society. Delay, privacy, negotiation and repayment gather at the top. A distribution so consistent, across so many unrelated fields of law, demands a structural explanation, and class is the explanation the traditional position offers.

Education and early intervention extend the pattern into childhood. Schools in poor districts operate under inspection regimes and discipline policies that treat disorder as a security problem, excluding pupils whose behaviour reflects unmet need, unstable housing or undiagnosed disability, and each exclusion raises the excluded child's exposure to street life and police contact. Children of the comfortable, displaying comparable behaviour, are more likely to meet counsellors, tutors and private treatment, purchased shields that keep adolescent trouble inside the family and out of the record. By adulthood the two groups carry different histories into every encounter with authority: one documented, flagged and suspected, the other undocumented and presumed respectable. Traditional analysis calls this the manufacture of the criminal population, not because the offences recorded are invented, but because the surveillance and recording that convert behaviour into criminality are allocated by class from the earliest years. A society gets the criminals it looks for, in the places it looks, with the instruments it builds for looking. The instruments were built under the influence of the interests this section has described, and their output is then cited as independent proof that the looking was justified.

Punishment completes the daily pattern. Custody falls on those who cannot pay fines, cannot secure stable addresses for bail, cannot absorb the loss of work that remand imposes, and cannot buy the treatment or supervision packages that persuade courts toward leniency. Community penalties demand time, transport and documentation, resources unevenly distributed in ways that convert poverty itself into non compliance. Breach then returns the poor offender to court for failing conditions that a wealthier offender would have met without effort. Traditional writers treat breach statistics as a quiet confession by the system: a large share of imprisonment punishes not the original act but the inability to perform compliance on a budget of nothing. Wealth, by contrast, purchases the conditions of its own mercy.

That refusal explains both the endurance and the limits of the traditional position. Endurance, because the core observations keep being rediscovered by each generation that examines who fills prisons and who escapes them, which harms command headlines and which command only compensation schemes. Limits, because a position built for exposure is less well built for government, for the design of institutions that must protect poor communities from the offenders in their midst as well as from the powers above them. The internal correction, when it arrived from within the left itself, pressed exactly that point, and its force came from accepting most of the traditional analysis while denying that the analysis was sufficient. Victims in working class neighbourhoods, the correction argued, cannot wait for the transformation of capitalism to be safe in their homes and streets, and a criminology that treats their fear as ideology has betrayed the people in whose name it speaks. How the tradition answered that charge, through neo-Marxist complication, through the analysis of panic and hegemony, and through left realism itself, belongs to the sections that follow. The traditional position remains the foundation: law made for property, enforcement aimed downward, harm from above discounted, and crime from below generated by the very order that punishes it. Every later refinement is a footnote to that foundation, or a rebellion against it, and usually both at once.

## The Neo-Marxist Turn: A Fully Social Theory of Deviance

Something decisive shifted when a generation of criminologists refused to choose between structure and meaning. Earlier traditions had tended to pull in opposite directions. One side traced offending to large forces such as poverty, inequality and blocked opportunity, yet left the person strangely empty, as if conduct followed automatically from position. Another side followed encounters, labels and negotiations in fine detail, yet often left the wider distribution of wealth and power outside the frame. The neo-Marxist turn promised a theory capacious enough to hold both ends of that tension together without collapsing either into the other. Deviance would be explained neither as mechanical output nor as free floating invention. It would be located inside a social order that shapes choices, limits horizons and supplies vocabularies, while still leaving room for actors who interpret, resist, improvise and sometimes deliberately transgress.

A book published in 1973 gave that ambition its most programmatic statement. The New Criminology, written by Ian Taylor, Paul Walton and Jock Young, set out to construct what its authors called a fully social theory of deviance. The adjective mattered as much as the noun. A theory could be social in a thin sense by mentioning groups, neighbourhoods or families. Their demand was thicker. Social meant historical, economic and political all at once. It meant asking where rules come from, whose interests rule making serves, how enforcement agencies acquire their mandates, why some harms provoke outrage while other harms pass as ordinary business, and how a person caught inside those arrangements understands what he or she is doing. Nothing in the sequence was to be treated as natural or self explaining.

Ambition of that scale explains the polemical energy of the work. Taylor, Walton and Young wrote against several targets at the same time, and the force of the argument comes from that multi front engagement. Against positivist criminology they rejected the search for criminal types, inherited defects or measurable pushes that supposedly separate offenders from non offenders in kind. Against a narrow strain tradition they argued that frustration alone explains little unless one also examines the cultural solutions available, the moral meanings attached to them and the political economy that distributes frustration so unevenly. Against administrative criminology they objected to research that takes official categories as given and then asks only how to manage the populations sorted into them. Each critique pointed toward the same positive claim: a satisfactory account must connect biography to history and interaction to political economy.

Labelling theory received a more complicated treatment, part gratitude and part farewell. From labelling, the new criminology learned to distrust official definitions, to watch the labellers as closely as the labelled, and to see reaction as part of the phenomenon rather than an afterthought added once the real act has occurred. Those lessons were indispensable. Yet labelling, in their reading, remained an interactionist perspective rather than a Marxist one, brilliant on encounters and careers but underdeveloped on the origins of rules, the structure of power and the material conditions that make some labels stick while others fade. A brief clarification belongs here because confusion persists in textbooks: labelling is interactionist, not Marxist, in its theoretical roots, however often the two are shelved together under a loose heading of critical thought. The neo-Marxist project borrowed the insight about reaction and then insisted on grounding it in a theory of the state, of class and of capitalist social relations.

Romanticism was another danger the authors tried to avoid, though critics later accused them of falling into it. Celebrating every transgression as rebellion would merely invert the old pathology, replacing the sick offender with the heroic outlaw. Taylor, Walton and Young were more careful than that caricature suggests. Some deviant acts carry oppositional meaning, others are predatory, confused, self destructive or harmful to neighbours who share the same deprived conditions. A fully social theory had to preserve the possibility of political content without conferring political virtue automatically. Meaning had to be investigated case by case in the older scholarly sense of careful inquiry, not assumed from the mere fact that a rule had been broken. That discipline separates analysis from applause.

Underneath the programme lay a particular view of human beings. People were to be treated as creators of their social world who nevertheless create it under conditions inherited from the past and weighted heavily against many of them. That formulation allowed the authors to speak about constraint without determinism and about freedom without fantasy. A young person growing up amid insecure work, poor housing and aggressive surveillance faces a field of choices narrowed in advance, yet narrowing is not the same as scripting. Within a narrowed field, individuals still deliberate, form loyalties, adopt styles, tell stories about themselves and select among available courses of action for reasons that make sense inside their world. Explaining deviance therefore requires reconstructing those reasons rather than replacing them with variables.

Political economy supplied the widest lens. Capitalist societies generate systematic inequalities in income, security, status and exposure to harm, and they also generate cultural pressures toward acquisition, competition and display. Deviance cannot be sealed off from those pressures. Property offences, workplace fiddles, drug markets, public order conflicts and corporate harms all arise inside a common order organised around private accumulation and unequal life chances, even though each form has its own local logic. A theory that begins with the isolated offender misses the order that produces both the temptation and the prohibition, both the illicit market and the agency charged with suppressing it. Starting from the whole does not dissolve the particular. It gives the particular a location.

Culture formed the second lens, inseparable from the first. Subcultures, styles and collective solutions mediate between structural position and individual act. Groups elaborate shared meanings that dignify some choices and shame others, provide techniques, supply audiences and convert private frustration into public identity. The neo-Marxist writers wanted those meanings taken seriously as evidence, not treated as decorative colour added after causal work has been done elsewhere. If an act expresses contempt for authority, loyalty to peers, hunger for respect or refusal of humiliating work, those meanings belong inside the explanation. At the same time, culture was not to float free. Styles emerge somewhere, under specific material pressures, and they change as those pressures change.

Power formed the third lens, directed at reaction. Rules do not enforce themselves, and enforcement is never evenly spread. Agencies select targets, legislatures define offences, courts translate categories into outcomes, and media institutions amplify some dangers while softening others. Reaction also feeds back. A label can close employment doors, deepen identification with a deviant group, provoke defiance or teach caution, thereby altering the very behaviour it purports merely to record. Any theory stopping at the act therefore tells half a story. Any theory starting only with reaction tells the other half. The programme demanded a circuit rather than a segment.

Method followed from theory. If deviance is meaningful action inside structured conditions, research must listen to actors, reconstruct settings and trace connections outward to institutions and inward to motives. Ethnographic attention mattered, but so did historical inquiry into how categories were made and political analysis of who benefits from particular definitions. No single technique could carry the whole burden. Counting has its place when categories are interrogated first. Listening has its place when accounts are set against conditions. Archival work has its place when a panic or a campaign needs its genealogy exposed. Plural methods were not eclecticism for its own sake. Each method answered a different link in the circuit the theory described.


### What are the seven components of a fully social theory?

The seven components trace a complete circuit: wider structural origins of the deviant act, its immediate origins, the act itself, immediate origins of social reaction, wider origins of that reaction, effects of reaction on subsequent action, and the character of the whole process.

Naming the circuit slowly reveals how demanding the proposal was. Consider first the wider structural origins of the deviant act. Before any individual lifts, strikes, deals, deserts or defaces, a social order has already distributed resources, honours and insecurities in patterned ways. Class position, labour market prospects, housing conditions, educational sorting and racialised disadvantage belong at this level, not as background decoration but as generative conditions. Such conditions do not manufacture a specific act on a specific evening. They shape the terrain on which evenings are lived, the company kept, the grievances accumulated and the opportunities that appear plausible or absurd. Structural origins explain pressure and probability at the level of populations and milieux. Treating them as direct causes of single acts would repeat the determinism the authors opposed.

Immediate origins occupy a different scale. Here the focus narrows to the setting in which a person moves toward an act: a quarrel gathering heat, a debt falling due, a group daring one another, an empty afternoon stretching ahead, a perceived insult demanding answer. Biography enters as well, since people carry prior experiences, commitments and self understandings into each encounter. Two individuals under similar structural pressure may diverge sharply at this level because their immediate worlds differ in company, mood, opportunity and interpretation. A fully social theory refuses to skip this scale. Without it, structure becomes abstraction and explanation loses the moment where conduct actually takes shape.

Then comes the act itself, restored to the centre after traditions that dissolve it into either causes or labels. The act has form, skill, risk and meaning. It may be planned or impulsive, solitary or collective, skilled or clumsy, expressive or instrumental, often several of those at once. Actors frequently know rules are being broken and offer accounts of why breaking them is justified, necessary, amusing or unavoidable. Those accounts deserve analytic respect even when they cannot be accepted at face value. Studying the act also means studying its victims and its harms honestly. Restoring agency does not require minimising damage done to other people, and the strongest passages of the programme keep agency and harm in view together.

Reaction requires the same two level treatment given to action. Its immediate origins lie in encounters and decisions close to the event: a complaint made or withheld, a patrol choosing to intervene, a teacher deciding whether an incident becomes a record, a manager handling misconduct quietly or formally. Discretion saturates these moments. Similar conduct can meet indifference in one setting and severity in another depending on demeanour, reputation, place and the relationship between observer and observed. Analysing immediate reaction exposes the practical work through which abstract rules become lived consequences. It also shows why official records cannot be read as transparent mirrors of behaviour. Records document decisions as much as deeds.

Wider origins of reaction reach behind those decisions to institutions and ideologies. Legislatures, ministries, professional bodies, campaigning organisations and news institutions help define which problems demand action and which solutions count as realistic. Moral entrepreneurs raise alarms, experts supply vocabularies, and agencies compete for budgets and jurisdiction. Behind such activity stand deeper assumptions about order, property, respectability, danger and desert. A Marxist framing asks whose order is being defended and whose conduct is most readily imagined as threat. That question does not imply a conspiracy meeting where every reaction is scripted. Consent, habit, institutional interest and genuine fear all play parts, often without coordination. Wider origins name the pattern that emerges from many uncoordinated parts acting inside shared assumptions.

Effects of reaction on subsequent action close the loop between society and biography. Sanctions, stigma and surveillance alter future options and identities. Exclusion from work or education can push a person toward the very associations and income sources that reaction aimed to suppress. Public shaming can produce withdrawal in one person and defiant commitment in another. A deviant identity, once organised around a label, may supply belonging, status and a ready interpretation of hostile encounters. Yet amplification is not inevitable. Support, diversion, forgiveness and changed circumstances can interrupt the spiral. The component matters because it converts reaction from an ending into a cause of what follows, making deviance a career shaped over time rather than a single event frozen at the moment of detection.

Finally, the character of the whole process asks the theorist to step back and describe how the preceding elements relate. Are action and reaction loosely connected or tightly coupled? Does reaction amplify, contain, displace or merely record? Which meanings dominate at each stage, and how do they change as a person moves from primary transgression into organised deviance or back toward conformity? This seventh element prevents the list from becoming a checklist. It demands synthesis. A theory earns the word fully only when the connections are specified, since isolated chapters on structure, action and reaction could sit side by side without ever explaining their interaction. The whole process is the object. Components are instruments for grasping it.

Worked through an ordinary example, the circuit shows its value. Imagine pilfering from a workplace stockroom. Structural origins include low wages, insecure shifts and a culture celebrating consumption while withholding means. Immediate origins might involve a supervisor whose favouritism has bred resentment and a colleague who knows which door stays unlocked. The act itself carries meanings of compensation, revenge and thrill in proportions only inquiry can uncover. Immediate reaction depends on whether loss is noticed, who is suspected first and whether informal settlement seems preferable to scandal. Wider origins include property law traditions, managerial authority and public narratives separating honest workers from thieves. Effects may range from dismissal and blacklisting to a warning absorbed and behaviour abandoned. Characterising the whole process means showing how workplace order produced both the grievance and the prohibition, and how reaction redistributed the costs.

Another example clarifies the cultural side. Consider a street style that authorities read as menace while participants read as dignity. Structural origins involve exclusion from respectable routes to status. Immediate origins involve gatherings where reputation is won and lost in public view. The act might be posture, dress, noise or territorial display rather than acquisitive crime. Reaction begins with complaints and patrol attention, widens into campaigns about disorder, and returns as stops, records and reputations that harden the style into an identity. A theory missing any component would misread the phenomenon: pure structuralism would miss performance, pure interactionism would miss exclusion, and a focus on reaction alone would miss why the performance mattered to performers in the first place.

Drug use offers a third illustration of range. Structural inquiry examines illicit markets, prohibition regimes, neighbourhood abandonment and the legal industries selling relief from pain and boredom. Immediate inquiry follows friendship networks, rituals of preparation and vocabularies that frame use as pleasure, medicine, courage or escape. The act itself varies enormously by substance, setting and competence. Reaction ranges from medicalisation to punishment, often distributed unequally across social groups for similar conduct. Feedback effects include criminal records that narrow legitimate options and treatment encounters that open alternative identities. Seeing the whole process stops the analyst from treating use as either private pathology or simple effect of availability. Both simplifications fail the circuit test.


### Why did the neo-Marxists insist that deviance involves choice?

They insisted on choice because determinist accounts turned people into passive products of pressure, erasing meaning, intention and responsibility. Preserving choice allowed deviance to be understood as purposeful action, sometimes carrying proto-political protest against unequal conditions, rather than mere pathology or mechanical response to strain.

Determinism came in several costumes, and the critique addressed each in turn. Biological positivism located conduct in constitution, as if bodies carried instructions that social inquiry merely needed to decode. Psychological reduction located conduct in drives, complexes or deficits, translating social conflict into interior malfunction. Sociological versions could be subtler while remaining deterministic in effect: strain, anomie or disorganisation were sometimes written as forces that push actors along tracks laid down in advance, leaving deliberation as decoration on a process already decided. Even radical writing could slip into that pattern when class position was treated as a stamp producing consciousness and conduct automatically. Against every version, Taylor, Walton and Young asserted a human subject who interprets conditions and acts for reasons.

Choice, in their usage, never meant unconstrained selection from an open menu. Menus differ brutally. One person chooses among careers, neighbourhoods and forms of self presentation cushioned by wealth. Another chooses among humiliating work, illicit income, endurance and flight, with each option carrying penalties. Insisting on choice under unequal menus directs attention to both the chooser and the menu maker. Responsibility therefore becomes layered. Actors answer for what they do, and a social order answers for the alternatives it supplies, the rewards it attaches to conformity and the degradations it attaches to refusal. Collapsing either layer produces bad theory and bad politics: excuse everything and victims vanish; blame everything on individuals and structure vanishes.

Meaningful action also changes what counts as evidence. If people act for reasons, their accounts, jokes, boasts, silences and rituals become data about how a world is understood from inside. A theft narrated as getting back what was owed expresses a moral economy worth reconstructing. Vandalism narrated as revenge against an institution expresses a theory of that institution, however crude. Refusal of work narrated as defence of dignity expresses a judgement on available employment. None of those narratives settles justification. Each reveals the normative world inside which the act made sense, and explanation ignoring that world will mistake its object. Verstehen, the interpretive recovery of meaning, thus took its place alongside political economy rather than competing with it.

Proto-political content names a possibility handled with deliberate caution. Some deviance articulates, in inchoate form, grievances that organised politics also expresses: anger at exploitation, resentment of arbitrary authority, refusal of deference, hunger for respect denied through respectable channels. Squatting can assert a claim about housing as need against housing as commodity. Poaching traditions historically asserted customary rights against enclosure. Industrial sabotage can embody a judgement about who owns effort and time. Pilferage can enact an informal wage claim. Such readings illuminate dimensions invisible to pathology models. Yet the prefix proto does heavy work. Inchoate protest is not a programme, its targets can be mischosen, its methods can harm fellow sufferers, and its energy can dissipate into style or self destruction. Granting possible political content is an invitation to examine, never a certificate of virtue.

Opponents sometimes heard the choice argument as voluntarism, a fantasy of sovereign individuals inventing themselves from nothing. That hearing ignores how thoroughly the seven components embed action. Wider origins set distributions. Immediate origins set scenes. Reaction sets consequences that reshape later choices. Culture sets the symbolic materials from which choices are assembled and recognised. A person selects, but selects with a vocabulary learned somewhere, before audiences trained to read signals in particular ways, under risks distributed by institutions. Freedom inside the theory is therefore situated and relational. It resembles the freedom of a speaker using a language not of personal invention to say something never said before. Grammar constrains, grammar enables, and utterance remains genuinely the speaker's own.

Responsibility posed the sharpest practical puzzle. Courts and public debate often demand a simple fork: either an act was caused and the actor is excused, or an act was chosen and the actor is wholly blameworthy. The new criminology refused that fork. Causal understanding and moral judgement operate at different levels, and enriching the first does not abolish the second. Knowing why a neighbourhood produces certain adaptations does not require pretending that a violent act inside that neighbourhood lacks a perpetrator. Conversely, holding a perpetrator responsible does not require pretending the neighbourhood is irrelevant. Holding both truths strains legal common sense, which prefers single level answers, but strain is preferable to falsification. A mature theory can explain without absolving and condemn without ignoring conditions.

Gender exposed limits in the original formulation that later writers pressed hard. The deviant actor imagined in much early radical work was implicitly male: the pilferer, the street corner youth, the political rebel. Women's deviance, women's victimisation and the policing of femininity received far less attention, and domestic harms sat awkwardly inside frameworks built around public order and property. Feminist critics argued that choice itself is gendered, since options, risks and meanings differ under patriarchal relations inside households as well as labour markets. That criticism extends rather than destroys the programme. A fully social theory worthy of its name must include gendered structure among wider origins, gendered scenes among immediate origins and gendered reaction among institutional responses. The circuit survives by being completed where its founders left gaps.

Race and colonial history raised a parallel demand. Policing of minority communities, migration controls and racialised images of danger cannot be derived from class alone without remainder. Radical criminology at its best recognised that state power sorts populations through intertwined hierarchies, yet early statements sometimes subordinated every division to capital in ways that flattened distinct histories. Later scholarship within the tradition corrected course by treating racial formation as a structuring force in its own right, interacting with class rather than dissolving into it. Choice, too, looks different where surveillance presumes guilt and where respectable conformity offers no reliable protection from suspicion. The insistence that actors interpret their world becomes more, not less, important under those conditions, because official readings of the same acts diverge so sharply from lived readings.


Moving beyond labelling theory required specifying exactly what labelling could and could not do. Its strengths were real. It shifted attention from the supposedly distinctive qualities of offenders to the processes by which conduct gets defined, detected and recorded. It showed how a deviant status can become a master status, reorganising how every later action is read. It exposed the moral careers of rule enforcers and the negotiated character of official knowledge. Any Marxist criminology discarding those gains would regress toward naive realism about crime categories. Taylor, Walton and Young instead proposed absorption and transcendence: keep the analysis of definition and career, then ask the questions labelling rarely asked about the origins of the rules themselves, the political economy of enforcement priorities and the material interests served by particular definitions of order.

Several gaps justified the move. First, labelling often began too late, at the moment of successful definition, leaving the act and its motives underexplored, as if what happened before reaction were sociologically thin. Second, its account of power tended toward the local and interpersonal, strong on officials and audiences, weaker on ownership, state formation and ideology at societal scale. Third, its relativism could slide toward symmetry, treating all definitions as equivalent constructions while neglecting that some definitions command prisons, budgets and broadcasting systems while others command only a street corner. Fourth, its politics remained ambiguous: sympathy for the underdog does not by itself explain the order producing underdogs in batches. A Marxist completion addressed each gap by widening the frame in time, scale and material depth.

Interactionism more broadly shared both the virtues and the limits. Its micro sociology recovered texture that structural writing often lost: glances, negotiations, staged performances, repairs after embarrassment. The neo-Marxist complaint was never that such texture is trivial. Complaint targeted sufficiency. An encounter between officer and youth condenses histories neither participant created: histories of territory, employment, law making, professional training and public fear. Reading only the encounter mistakes condensation for origin. Reading only the histories mistakes origin for encounter. The fully social ambition was precisely a refusal of that forced choice, an insistence that explanation travel the full distance between epoch and episode and then travel back again with the episode's consequences in hand.

Social reaction, accordingly, was theorised as more than labelling by another name. Reaction includes legislation, budgeting, professional ideology, media amplification, architectural design, welfare provision and punishment, arranged in systems that sometimes reinforce and sometimes contradict one another. A label is one moment inside that larger machinery. Focusing on machinery changes research design. Instead of asking only how a tag sticks to a person, the analyst asks how a category becomes thinkable, fundable and enforceable, which agencies gain from its expansion, which populations become its routine raw material and which harms remain outside its reach. Corporate fraud, occupational injury and environmental damage become test cases: their relative invisibility beside street offences reveals the selectivity that a purely interactionist account can describe locally but struggle to explain systematically.

The state occupied an uneasy place in the argument. A crude instrumental view, in which rulers simply order repression to suit owners, could not survive contact with institutional complexity, professional autonomy and genuine public demand for safety. A purely pluralist view, in which the state merely balances competing pressures, could not explain persistent skew in whose safety counts and whose conduct is criminalised. The new criminology groped toward a relational view: state agencies act within capitalist social relations that limit agendas and reward certain solutions, while officials retain discretion, publics exert pressure and crises open unexpected paths. That groping was unfinished, and later work on hegemony would give it sharper tools. Unfinished, however, is not empty. Naming the problem correctly was itself an advance over theories where the state appeared only as neutral referee or as puppet on visible strings.

What would research look like if the programme were honoured in practice? It would begin by refusing official problem definitions as its own. It would reconstruct an act from participants on several sides, including those harmed. It would trace the category applied to that act backward through campaigns, professional literatures and legislative debates. It would map enforcement patterns against social divisions and ask why attention concentrates where it does. It would follow labelled persons forward through institutions and informal consequences, watching for amplification, desistance and reinterpretation. It would then return to political economy, asking how the whole sequence stabilises or unsettles a social order. Few single studies can do everything. A programme, though, can judge partial studies by the connections they acknowledge and the connections they suppress, which is how research traditions actually guide work: less by dictating each project than by defining what a satisfying explanation must eventually join together.

Critics from the political right heard the programme as excuse making dressed in theory. If structure explains and reaction amplifies, personal guilt supposedly evaporates and punishment loses foundation. That reading confuses explanation with acquittal, a confusion the choice component was designed to block. Critics from parts of the left heard the opposite fault: too much sympathy with deviant meanings, too little attention to working class victims who bear the costs of burglary, violence and disorder most directly. That second criticism gained force over time and eventually pushed one of the original authors toward a very different politics of crime, centred on victims and local control. The split is instructive. A framework capacious enough to hold structure, meaning and reaction will generate internal tensions about emphasis, and those tensions can pull collaborators apart when public debate forces priorities. Frameworks do not settle politics. They clarify what politics must argue about.

Administrative criminology posed a quieter challenge by offering usefulness. Governments fund research that promises measurable reductions, manageable risks and techniques that fit existing institutions. A fully social theory promises understanding first and warns that some techniques deepen the problems they claim to solve. That warning rarely wins grants. Yet usefulness depends on diagnosis. Interventions aimed at immediate origins alone may displace conduct. Interventions aimed at reaction alone may widen nets. Interventions ignoring wider origins may polish the surface of an order generating the same pressures elsewhere. The programme therefore retains practical bite even when it refuses the consultant's role: it predicts side effects that narrower theories discover only after budgets are spent and evaluations puzzle over perverse results.

Durability came from that diagnostic reach. Long after vocabularies changed, the habit of asking connected questions persisted inside critical criminology, cultural criminology, zemiology and abolitionist scholarship, each developing different parts of the inheritance. Cultural criminology expanded the study of meaning, style and emotion. Zemiology widened attention from crime to social harm. Abolitionist work pressed the reaction components toward questions about whether penal institutions can be reformed at all. None of those descendants reproduces the 1973 statement whole. Descent rarely works by reproduction. It works by selective intensification, where later writers take one strand, pull it further than founders imagined and argue with siblings over the estate. Judged by fertility rather than fidelity, the neo-Marxist turn succeeded abundantly.

Assessment must also record genuine weaknesses beyond the gender and race gaps already noted. The programme was stronger as manifesto than as method, sketching connections more confidently than it demonstrated them. Its Marxism sometimes functioned as orientation rather than analysis, invoking political economy without always specifying mechanisms linking ownership patterns to a particular offence or campaign. Its treatment of victims remained underdeveloped, especially where victims and offenders shared neighbourhoods and disadvantages. Its optimism about proto-political meaning occasionally outran evidence, tempting sympathetic readers to find resistance wherever they looked. Naming those weaknesses honours the founders better than reverence does, because the seventh component, attention to the whole process, licenses criticism of any partial telling, including the founding telling itself.

Teaching the theory creates a characteristic temptation worth resisting: turning seven components into boxes on a slide and inviting students to fill each box for an assigned topic. Box filling produces coverage without explanation. The components earn their keep only when arrows matter more than boxes, when a change in reaction is shown altering immediate origins of later acts, when wider origins of reaction are shown selecting which structural pressures become public problems, when meanings formed inside subcultures are shown feeding back into politics. A seminar exercise honouring the spirit would therefore ask for a narrative circuit with turning points, evidence at each turn and explicit claims about connection strength. Where evidence thins, the narrative should say so. A theory of the whole process should model intellectual honesty about wholes that remain partly unmapped.

Another temptation involves treating the founders as prophets of everything later called critical. Precision serves better. The 1973 intervention arrived after labelling had already unsettled orthodoxies, after conflict theory had already politicised rule making, and alongside phenomenologically inclined work already restoring meaning to action. Originality lay in synthesis with a Marxist spine: political economy plus meaning plus reaction plus feedback, held in one frame and aimed at a socialist politics of transformation rather than piecemeal reform. Synthesis is a genuine form of originality, often undervalued beside the invention of isolated concepts. Most mature sciences advance by joining what partial traditions separated. Criminology, a field assembled from law, sociology, psychology and politics, advances that way more than most.

One further implication deserves emphasis because it reaches beyond criminology. If deviance is a process joining action and reaction inside a social order, then crime itself cannot be treated as a natural kind awaiting a single cause. Categories gather unlike behaviours under administrative headings, while similar harms scatter across legal, regulatory and private handling. The object of study becomes unstable in a productive way. Analysts must defend why they study burglary beside assault but apart from dangerous working conditions, or drug selling beside drug marketing but apart from aggressive selling of legal intoxicants. Such defence cannot rest on statute alone without circularity. It must engage harm, power and public meaning together. That destabilisation prepared ground for the hegemony analysis taken up in the next section, where a single category of street offence becomes a window onto the government of an entire society in crisis.

Standing back, the neo-Marxist turn can be summarised as a change in the unit of analysis. Orthodox work studied offenders. Labelling studied transactions. The new criminology studied a social process stretched across structure, act and reaction, with human subjects interpreting their way through it. That change explains both the excitement the book generated and the difficulty of operationalising it in tidy projects. Big units resist tidy methods. They also resist tidy refutation, since a programme can absorb findings that would embarrass a single hypothesis. Sympathetic readers call that resilience. Sceptical readers call it elasticity. Either way, the questions permanently altered what a serious explanation must attempt: origins in two directions, meaning at the centre, feedback over time and an account of the whole that shows why the parts belong together.

Read as a whole, the programme also redefined what failure in explanation looks like. Failure is not only a wrong prediction or a weak correlation. Failure includes asking a question so narrowly that the answer, however accurate, misleads. A flawless count of arrests can mislead when enforcement patterns produced the count. A rich interview can mislead when the world outside the interview room never enters the analysis. A elegant model of strain can mislead when meanings and reactions are assumed away. By that standard, much technically competent research fails socially, and the adjective in the programme title becomes a criterion of adequacy rather than a slogan. Work counts as social only insofar as it traces conduct into conditions, categories into power, and consequences back into lives over time. That criterion still judges the field, still embarrasses partial accounts that present themselves as complete, and still explains why a manifesto written for a different era continues to organise argument. Its seven components are less a museum exhibit than a set of debts every explanation owes. Paying those debts in full may exceed any single project. Acknowledging them, mapping the unpaid portions honestly and designing collaborations across the circuit remain possible for any project, which is why the demand endures as discipline rather than nostalgia.

## Hegemony and Moral Panic: Policing the Crisis

Few works of criminology have travelled as far beyond their home discipline as the study published in 1978 under the title Policing the Crisis. Written by Stuart Hall together with Chas Critcher, Tony Jefferson, John Clarke and Brian Roberts, the book took an apparently modest subject, a moral panic about street robbery, and used that subject to interrogate the government of an entire society. Its central claim was audacious. A panic over a particular form of street offence helped manufacture consent for more authoritarian forms of policing and social discipline at a moment when established authority faced a deepening crisis of hegemony. Crime talk, on this account, was never only crime talk. Talk about robbery on the street became a language through which wider anxieties about economic decline, social change, race, youth and national identity could be expressed, organised and answered with calls for stronger control.

Scale distinguishes the argument from simpler accounts of exaggeration. Nobody needed Hall and colleagues to learn that newspapers dramatise, that politicians exploit fear or that public alarm can outrun evidence. Their contribution lay in connecting those familiar observations to a theory of how societies are ruled. Moral panic became a mechanism inside a larger process: the construction of consent to coercion. When a ruling order can no longer secure agreement through prosperity, deference or shared purpose, it may secure agreement through protection. Citizens frightened by disorder may welcome measures they would otherwise resist, may demand severity they would otherwise question, and may experience expanded state power as relief rather than loss. Panic, analysed this way, does political work far exceeding the offences that occasion it.

Hegemony supplies the key concept, borrowed and adapted from Gramscian analysis. Hegemony names leadership secured substantially through consent: through education, religion, media, common sense and the routines of everyday life, a social order presents its arrangements as natural, inevitable or broadly beneficial, so that coercion can remain in reserve for most people most of the time. Consent never eliminates force. Prisons, courts and police powers stand behind even the most settled order. Hegemony describes the balance, the degree to which rule feels like agreement rather than imposition. A crisis of hegemony arrives when that feeling thins: when economic promises falter, when subordinate groups withdraw deference, when ruling institutions lose moral authority and when no convincing story binds private troubles to a shared future. Readers seeking the fuller account of the concept itself can follow the [Gramsci hegemony complete guide](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/), since the concern here is application rather than exposition of Gramsci in general.

Applying hegemony to criminal justice changes the questions asked about law and order campaigns. Instead of asking only whether a campaign responds to real danger, the analyst asks what kind of authority a campaign rebuilds, which groups it unites, which groups it casts outside protection and which solutions it renders unthinkable. A panic can assemble a coalition broader than any governing party: anxious householders, ambitious officials, professional experts, editorial writers and ordinary people whose fear is genuine and whose experience of victimisation deserves respect. Consent manufactured through such a coalition is not fake feeling. Fear is felt. Losses are suffered. The analytic point concerns organisation: how scattered fears are gathered, named, aimed at a folk devil and converted into support for a programme of control whose scope extends well beyond the original danger.

Folk devil is the term the panic literature gives to the figure onto whom anxieties condense. In the study at hand, the figure was the mugger, a label imported, adapted and loaded with associations that exceeded any individual offender. The word did cultural work. It suggested predatory strangers, urban decay, racialised threat and youthful contempt for ordinary decency in a single compressed image. Once such a figure circulates, complex social change acquires a face, and policy acquires a target. Punishing the figure promises symbolic repair of fractures that punishment cannot actually mend: industrial decline, strained families, fraying neighbourhoods, distrust of institutions. Symbolic repair is not therefore unimportant. Societies often govern through symbols precisely where material repair seems blocked, expensive or politically divisive.

Moral panic theory before Hall had already described amplification: an incident sparks concern, media inventory and dramatisation follow, experts and officials propose remedies, and a deviant group finds itself watched more closely, which produces more recorded deviance, which seems to confirm the original alarm. Policing the Crisis absorbed that spiral and then refused to stop there. A spiral explains escalation. It does not explain timing, selection or function at societal scale. Why does one danger ignite while comparable dangers smoulder? Why does alarm peak during periods of economic and political instability? Whose definition of the problem prevails, and what form of state does the prevailing definition legitimate? Those questions push analysis from communication processes toward political economy and ideology. The panic becomes a window, not merely a distortion.

Primary definers occupy a strategic place in that account. Police spokespeople, judges, senior officials and accredited experts enjoy privileged access to media routines, so their interpretations frame stories before alternative interpretations can gather. Journalists, dependent on authoritative sources for copy, deadlines and legal safety, tend to reproduce those frames even while believing themselves independent. Hall called the resulting relationship structured in dominance: media possess real autonomy in presentation, yet operate inside definitions largely set elsewhere. That nuance matters. The book never reduces journalism to dictation. Editors select, dramatise and sometimes dissent. Audiences interpret actively and unevenly. Power works through routines, incentives and shared assumptions more reliably than through orders, which is precisely how hegemonic power generally works when it is working well.

Race runs through the analysis as a structuring presence rather than a decorative theme. The mugger figure drew force from longer histories in which black youth had been represented as threat to national cohesion, and policing of black communities had already generated conflict and distrust. Economic crisis sharpened those representations. Where jobs vanish and public narratives search for causes, racialised scapegoats offer a story that displaces blame from markets and governments onto neighbours. Hall and colleagues treated race, class and youth as mutually shaping categories: the panic targeted young working class men, but its emotional charge depended on racial imagery, and its policy consequences fell unevenly on communities already subject to intensive surveillance. Later scholarship on race and crime repeatedly returned to this intersection, often finding the original framework more durable than frameworks treating prejudice as individual attitude alone.

Historical depth gave the argument its sweep. The authors traced law and order themes across a long arc, showing how successive panics prepared ideological ground: each episode left behind expanded powers, hardened categories and a public more rehearsed in hearing social problems as crime problems. Preparation matters because consent is cumulative. A dramatic authoritarian turn rarely arrives from nowhere. It arrives along paths cleared by smaller campaigns, emergency exceptions that became normal, and vocabularies that migrated from fringe agitation into ministerial speech. By the time crisis deepens, the repertoire of strong measures feels familiar, even traditional. The book therefore reads panic diachronically, as sedimentation, where looser studies read episodes in isolation and wonder at their intensity without explaining their readiness.


### How does a moral panic manufacture consent?

A moral panic manufactures consent by magnifying a threatening figure, repeating alarming stories, and presenting stronger control as common sense protection. Fear narrows debate, unites otherwise divided audiences behind authority, and makes coercive measures appear necessary, popular and legitimate rather than imposed from above.

Manufacture deserves careful handling, since the metaphor can mislead. Consent here is not fabricated in a workshop by hidden engineers who feel no fear themselves and cynically programme a passive public. Officials often believe their warnings. Editors often share the anxieties they amplify. Victims speak from real experience, and residents demanding safer streets pursue a legitimate good. Manufacture names a process of assembly rather than a conspiracy of invention. Raw materials, genuine incidents, genuine losses, genuine unease about change, are selected, arranged and narrated until they support a particular conclusion about what must be done and who must be empowered to do it. Assembly can proceed through sincere actors at every station. Sincerity does not neutralise power. Indeed, hegemonic processes usually require sincerity, since messages believed by their senders travel farther than messages delivered with a wink.

Selection comes first. A society contains more suffering than any public agenda can hold, so selection decides whose suffering becomes emblematic. A robbery victim may become a national symbol while an injured worker, a defrauded pensioner or an abused partner remains a private sorrow. Selection follows news values, institutional access and cultural resonance. Incidents fitting an available story, stranger predation, urban danger, youth out of control, receive saturation treatment. Incidents complicating that story receive brief, local or technical treatment. Over time, availability does cognitive work: audiences judge risk by the examples easiest to recall, and policy follows the judged risk rather than any careful comparison of harms. No statistics need be falsified for distortion to occur. Proportion can be lost through emphasis alone, which is why the analysis attends to framing, placement and repetition rather than hunting only for factual errors.

Naming comes next, and naming is never innocent. A label gathers disparate incidents into a single phenomenon with implied causes and remedies. Street robbery committed by acquaintances, opportunistic snatching, organised theft and violent assault differ in motive, setting and prevention, yet a panic label compresses them into one wave attributed to one kind of person. Compression creates governability. A wave can be met with a crackdown. A person type can be stopped, searched, sentenced and displayed. Heterogeneous social problems resist such theatre. The label also travels backward, teaching audiences to reinterpret ambiguous encounters through its lens, and forward, teaching agencies to record borderline incidents under its heading. Category and count then grow together, each certifying the other, in a loop that looks like discovery while functioning partly as production.

Amplification follows through mutually reinforcing institutions. A courtroom warning becomes a headline. A headline becomes a parliamentary question. A question becomes an official review. A review becomes fresh headlines announcing that authorities have recognised the menace. Each participant can honestly claim merely to be responding to others, and the circle acquires momentum no single participant needs to intend. Deviancy amplification adds a material loop beneath the symbolic one: intensified patrols in targeted areas produce more stops, more arrests for minor offences and more recorded incidents, which are then cited as proof that targeting was justified. Visibility generated by surveillance masquerades as prevalence independent of surveillance. Breaking that circle analytically requires asking what would be seen if attention were distributed differently, a counterfactual question panic discourse rarely poses about itself.

Authoritative closure narrows the range of respectable response. Once primary definers agree that a menace exists and that firmness constitutes realism, dissent can be reframed as softness, naivety or indifference to victims. Experts who urge proportionality risk losing access. Politicians who counsel patience risk electoral punishment. Residents who describe police conduct as part of the problem risk being heard as defenders of robbery. Closure does not require censorship. It works through career incentives, news formats and moral pressure, until the debate concerns only how much coercion, how fast, administered by whom. Questions about housing, employment, youth provision or the distribution of safety itself move outside the frame marked serious. Hegemony is visible exactly there, in the boundary between arguments that must be answered and arguments that need not even be heard.

Consent then attaches to a broader programme than the panic nominally concerns. Powers introduced for the folk devil remain available for protesters, migrants, benefit claimants, football crowds or whichever group next occupies the position of internal threat. Sentencing severity justified by exceptional cases becomes baseline. Surveillance justified by emergency becomes infrastructure. The panic subsides, as panics do, but the settlement it ratified persists. That persistence explains why analysts should judge a panic less by peak rhetoric than by residue: budgets altered, doctrines entrenched, careers advanced, expectations reset. A society may forget the headlines that frightened it while continuing to live inside institutions those headlines legitimised. Forgetting is part of the mechanism, since powers acquired as exceptional responses come to feel like ordinary equipment once their originating alarm fades from memory.

Audiences, however, are not clay. Hall's wider work on encoding and decoding insisted that messages carry preferred meanings which audiences may accept, negotiate or oppose depending on position and experience. Communities targeted by a panic often read it oppositionally from the first headline, recognising their sons in the folk devil and their streets in the promised crackdown. Victims may accept the preferred reading while rejecting particular police practices. Workers may demand protection from crime and protection from employers in the same breath, refusing the package deal panic politics offers. Consent is therefore contested terrain, never a finished product rolled out uniformly. Manufacture remains a useful word only when paired with resistance, leakage and partial failure. Hegemonic projects aim at closure. Social life keeps prying them open.

Emotion binds the process together at a level purely institutional analysis can miss. Panic condenses grief for a lost sense of safety, anger at visible disorder, shame about national decline and longing for a world imagined as orderly. Such feelings attach to personal biography: ageing, parenting, neighbourhood change, precarious work. A campaign speaking to those feelings offers recognition as well as policy. It tells frightened people that their unease has a name, a culprit and a remedy. Rival accounts that begin by correcting exaggerations may win arguments while losing audiences, because correction alone offers no recognition and no remedy. Effective counter politics would need its own account of safety, one that honours fear while widening its causes and distributing protection more equally. The book implies that lesson even where it concentrates on diagnosis. Diagnosis shows why technically accurate reassurance so often fails against a story that organises emotion.


### What connects a street robbery panic to a crisis of hegemony?

A street robbery panic connects to a crisis of hegemony because disorder on the street becomes a condensed symbol for wider economic, social and political breakdown. Governing through crime lets authority rebuild consent, discipline dissent and justify stronger state power when its broader leadership falters.

Condensation is the pivotal mechanism linking a street offence to a crisis of rule. In condensation, one vivid phenomenon gathers meanings belonging to many separate troubles and lets them be felt as a single threat. A robbery can thus carry anxieties about jobs disappearing, prices rising, unions asserting themselves, families changing shape, deference weakening and cities feeling unrecognisable to older residents. None of those troubles caused the robbery in any simple sense, and solving robbery solves none of them. Yet once condensation occurs, political energy flows toward the symbol. Demands to restore order on the street become demands to restore order in general, and a government unable to deliver prosperity can still deliver visible punishment. Symbolic governance substitutes for material governance at exactly the moment material governance loses credibility.

Economic background therefore belongs inside the analysis, not as scenery. The postwar settlement that underwrote consent in many capitalist societies rested on growth, employment security, expanding public provision and rising consumption. As those foundations weakened, the settlement's promises began to sound hollow. Governments faced industrial conflict, fiscal strain and declining trust while lacking a new story capable of commanding broad agreement. Hegemony thins under such conditions in observable ways: elections become volatile, established parties fragment, unofficial strikes and protests multiply, and everyday deference toward judges, broadcasters, managers and police loses its automatic quality. Authority still commands force. What authority lacks is the taken for granted belief that its leadership expresses a general interest. That lack is the crisis. Everything else follows from attempts to repair it.

Governing through crime offers one repair strategy with distinctive advantages. Crime unites where class divides. A campaign against robbery can address shopkeeper and worker, pensioner and parent, as fellow potential victims, suspending conflicts among them over wages, rents or public spending. Crime also personalises structural failure. Empty streets at night can be blamed on leniency rather than on disinvestment. Youth unemployment can be recoded as youth idleness and menace. Police budgets can grow while other budgets shrink, presenting the state as simultaneously smaller and stronger: withdrawn from social provision, expanded in discipline. That combination later acquired names such as authoritarian populism in Hall's subsequent writing, where popular consent is actively mobilised for a more coercive order rather than merely extracted by manipulation from above. Populism here signals genuine popular involvement. The project succeeds only when real grievances are articulated into its story.

Displacement operates alongside unification. Attention fixed on street predation is attention withdrawn from boardroom predation, unsafe workplaces, polluted neighbourhoods and frauds whose victims number far beyond any street wave. Displacement need not be planned to be functional. News routines, legal categories and political incentives reliably favour harms that are episodic, visual and attributable to identifiable villains over harms that are chronic, systemic and attributable to respected institutions. A hegemony analysis asks what a society's crime agenda systematically excludes, and treats the exclusions as evidence about power equal in weight to the inclusions. Silence, in this framework, is data. The harms never panicked about reveal the boundaries of imaginable disorder, boundaries that tend to protect the arrangements producing the widest harms from the scrutiny applied to the narrowest.

Discipline forms the third link. A crisis of hegemony involves not only belief but conduct: workplaces experiencing resistance, streets experiencing new claimants, households experiencing changing authority, colonies and former colonies reshaping global relations. Law and order discourse translates that diffuse indiscipline into a single moral failing, insufficient respect for authority, and prescribes a single remedy, restored firmness across institutions. Teachers, parents, managers and magistrates are invited to see their local difficulties as fronts in one campaign. Such translation has practical effects. Sentences lengthen in spirit even before guidelines change. Negotiating postures harden. Welfare becomes more conditional and suspicious. Protest becomes easier to portray as criminality's cousin. The panic thus radiates outward from its nominal target into the general government of conduct, which is why a book about robbery can legitimately discuss the state as a whole without inflating its subject.

A common misreading must be corrected plainly, because it shrinks the book into a subfield it deliberately exceeded. The analysis is about hegemony, not about media effects. Media effects research typically asks whether exposure changes individual attitudes or behaviour, isolating messages from the institutional order that produces and deploys them. Hall and colleagues asked a different order of question: how does a whole society talk itself into a more authoritarian settlement during a crisis of leadership, and what role do media institutions play inside that societal process alongside courts, police organisations, political parties and economic transformation? Media matter enormously in the account, but as mediating institutions within hegemony, not as syringes injecting opinion into passive heads. Reducing the thesis to newspapers exaggerate crime converts a theory of rule into a complaint about journalism, and then refuting the complaint with evidence of real crime appears to refute the theory. That refutation misses its target entirely. The existence of real offences was never in dispute. The dispute concerns what societies build upon offences, which fears get organised, which solutions get authorised and which crises get governed through the building.

Evidence for the thesis therefore looks different from evidence in an effects study. Correspondence between panic intensity and recorded offending matters, and the authors examined official series critically, showing how categories and recording practices complicate any simple reading of a wave. But the deeper evidence is relational and historical: timing against economic deterioration, resonance with earlier panics, convergence among primary definers, racialised imagery in representation, legislative and institutional residue, and the migration of law and order language toward the centre of political competition. A critic demanding a single decisive variable misunderstands the kind of claim being made. Hegemony is a configuration. Configurations are supported by patterned convergence across domains, and weakened when domains diverge: when panics fail to ignite during crises, when authoritarian remedies are rejected by frightened publics, or when crime discourse remains sealed off from wider politics. Stating defeat conditions keeps the theory honest and distinguishes configuration analysis from a mood in search of illustrations.

Oppositional readings also test the configuration. Where targeted communities organise, where alternative media name the panic as it forms, where victims refuse recruitment into punitive campaigns, consent frays visibly. The book emerged from a milieu connected to such organisation, and its scholarship carries an implicit wager that naming a mechanism weakens it. Exposure does not automatically dissolve power, since audiences can recognise manipulation narratives and still demand severity for reasons of their own. Still, a public equipped with the concept of moral panic possesses a resource: a pause between alarm and legislation, a question about who defined the problem first, a suspicion toward convenient folk devils. Concepts entering ordinary political vocabulary alter the terrain on which later panics must operate, one reason the phrase itself became a small piece of counter hegemonic equipment far beyond seminar rooms.


Standing among the most influential works in the discipline is now a settled description rather than promotional exaggeration. Policing the Crisis reshaped criminology by joining cultural studies methods to Marxist state theory, and its influence ran outward in several directions at once. Within criminology it legitimised the study of representation, ideology and state formation as core concerns rather than optional context. Within sociology it modelled conjunctural analysis, the study of a specific historical moment as an articulation of economic, political, cultural and racial forces, none reducible to another and none intelligible alone. Within political debate its vocabulary of authoritarian populism and law and order society supplied tools for interpreting later decades of penal expansion, preventive powers and surveillance growth, whether or not interpreters shared the authors' politics. Influence on that scale changes syllabi, research programmes and public language together, a rare combination for an academic monograph grounded in one national conjuncture and one offence category.

Media studies absorbed the book just as deeply, in some respects more lastingly than criminology itself. The account of primary definers entered journalism studies as a standard framework for analysing source power. The structured in dominance formulation offered a middle path between liberal pluralism, in which media mirror society, and crude propaganda models, in which media obey owners or governments directly. Encoding and decoding, developed in Hall's related work of the same period, combined with the panic analysis to found an approach where texts, institutions and audiences each receive serious attention. Students encountering news as constructed through routines rather than bias alone are walking ground this book helped clear. Its uptake in media studies also corrected disciplinary memory in one direction: a work sometimes cited as criminology's classic is equally a founding text for the critical study of news, representation and ideology.

Methodologically, the study demonstrated how much can be built from patient assembly of heterogeneous materials. Press coverage across an extended episode, courtroom statements, official publications, parliamentary debate, historical writing on earlier panics and theoretical reflection were woven into a single conjunctural account. No dataset decided the argument. Judgement did, exercised openly, with theoretical commitments declared rather than laundered through procedural neutrality. That openness invites disagreement on equal terms. A reader can contest the reading of a headline, the weight given to race, the characterisation of economic crisis or the leap from episode to epoch, because the materials and reasoning lie exposed. Scholarly influence often follows such exposure. Works that hide their machinery can be cited. Works that reveal machinery can be used, adapted and argued with across generations.

Criticisms accumulated alongside influence, and a balanced account should give them substance. Empirically minded critics questioned whether the robbery wave was as constructed as the analysis suggested, pointing to victim accounts and recorded trends that indicated real increases in some forms of street violence. Others questioned the functionalist undertow: describing a panic as serving hegemony can slide toward implying that hegemony needed it, produced it and benefited uniformly, whereas institutions often blunder, publics often resist and outcomes often disappoint their supposed beneficiaries. Feminist critics noted that street danger and domestic danger received unequal attention, with women's safety inside homes marginal to a drama staged in public space. Victim focused critics argued that left analysis risked treating fear of crime as ideological raw material first and lived suffering second. Each criticism identifies a real tension. None dissolves the central achievement, since a framework can require correction on evidence, gender and function while remaining the most generative account of how crime discourse and state power interlock.

Functionalism deserves a paragraph of its own because it shadows many Marxist explanations. To say a panic served consent is to describe an effect reconstructed after the fact. Effects do not prove intentions, and benefits do not prove design. The strongest reading of the book respects that distinction. Institutions selected responses from available repertoires under pressure. Some responses resonated, spread and hardened. Others failed. Hegemony names the pattern visible in the survivors, not a plan drawn before the episode began. Read that way, the analysis resembles evolutionary explanation more than conspiracy: variation, selection by institutional and cultural fitness, retention in law and habit. Weak readings that posit a ruling class composing panics like campaigns collapse under documentary scrutiny and embarrass the tradition. Strong readings survive scrutiny precisely because they need no hidden composer, only structured incentives, shared ideologies and unequal access to definition.

Comparison with the fully social theory discussed in the preceding section clarifies both projects. Taylor, Walton and Young supplied a programme for explaining deviance as a circuit of action and reaction. Hall and colleagues supplied a worked example at societal scale, tracing reaction upward into ideology and state formation with a richness the programme had demanded but not itself delivered. Where the earlier work listed wider origins of reaction as a component, the later work showed what investigating those origins actually entails: archives, discourse, institutional history and a theory of the conjuncture. Where the earlier work warned against labelling's limits, the later work demonstrated a Marxist analysis of definition in action, with primary definers doing empirically what labelling had described abstractly. The two books together mark a tradition maturing from manifesto toward method, from declaring connections to tracing them through documents and decades.

Subsequent history treated the thesis as a standing hypothesis rather than a sealed verdict. Penal populism research examined how electoral competition rewards severity. Governing through crime literature traced crime frameworks colonising schools, workplaces, immigration systems and urban design. Security studies followed preventive logics into digital surveillance. Each literature found the 1978 analysis anticipatory without treating it as prophecy. Anticipation differs from prophecy in a crucial respect: a framework anticipates when it identifies mechanisms that later operate in new materials, whereas prophecy merely guesses outcomes. Mechanisms identified here, condensation, primary definition, amplification, displacement, authoritarian residue, recur across panics concerning drugs, gangs, migration, terrorism and online harms, with local variations that test and refine the original formulation. A mechanism that travels while changing is alive. A slogan that travels unchanged is merely repeated.

Limits of travel deserve equal emphasis. Digital media environments fragment primary definition, since officials compete with influencers, leaked footage and algorithmic amplification that no institution fully steers. Panics can ignite from below, target institutions themselves or fracture audiences into sealed publics experiencing different alarms simultaneously. Hegemony in such environments may be harder to assemble and quicker to dissipate, or may form inside platforms through metrics and moderation rather than through broadcast consensus. Applying the framework now requires updating its media sociology while retaining its political question. Who defines first still matters, but first may mean first to trend rather than first to brief a correspondent. Consent still matters, but consent may be harvested as engagement data and converted into policy pressure through channels the original authors never encountered. Updating a classic in this fashion honours it better than embalming it.

Another limit concerns the state itself. The analysis presumed a state capable of coherent authoritarian drift, whereas fragmented governance, private security markets and transnational pressures complicate any single centre of hegemony. Private actors now manufacture consent for their own products and platforms, selling safety as subscription and suspicion as a feature. Neighbourhood applications digitise the folk devil process, circulating suspicion at street level without waiting for editorial framing. A hegemony account extended to these materials would examine how public and private definers interlock, how data brokers and platform designers become unacknowledged legislators of visibility, and how consent to surveillance is renewed through convenience rather than fear alone. The original questions survive translation. Their answers multiply, which is the fate of good questions in changed conditions.

What, finally, should a reader carry away from the study beyond its historical importance? A discipline of reading alarms. When a new menace dominates headlines, ask who defined it first and what evidence would count against the definition. Ask which older anxieties the menace condenses and which unrelated troubles vanish from view while attention lasts. Ask what powers are being requested, whether those powers sunset or persist, and onto whom their burdens will predictably fall. Ask who is invited to feel protected and who is assigned to feel watched. Ask what material settlement, what failure of leadership, what thinning of consent forms the background against which alarm becomes politically useful. Those questions do not dismiss victims, deny danger or excuse offences. They refuse the conversion of danger into blank cheques. Refusal of blank cheques, argued through archives and theory alike, is the book's enduring civic contribution, separable from agreement with its Marxism and available to any citizen willing to read a panic slowly while everyone else is being hurried.

Conjuncture, a word the authors used with technical intent, deserves unpacking because it prevents a lazy reading where crisis becomes a permanent backdrop invoked whenever convenient. A conjuncture is a specific balance of forces at a specific moment, produced by longer histories moving at different speeds. Economic cycles, party fortunes, racial formations, generational change and institutional decay rarely peak together by necessity. When they do align, a period acquires its distinctive volatility, and actions taken inside the alignment acquire effects disproportionate to their size. A panic landing in a settled conjuncture may flare and die. A panic landing in an unstable alignment can redirect a polity. Analysis must therefore date its claims, map the forces in play and resist exporting one moment's diagnosis to every moment. Hall's later career repeatedly returned to conjunctural reading, treating each new period as a fresh problem rather than a rerun, and that habit may be the most transferable element of the whole method.

Signification spirals, another technical notion in the book, explain how separate concerns get mapped onto one another until each intensifies all the others. Thresholds are crossed in public imagination when an offence is linked to a wider category of threat, when that category is linked to a group, when the group is linked to national decline, and when decline is linked to permissiveness said to infect courts, schools and families alike. At each linkage the emotional temperature rises and the proposed remedy broadens, from punishing offenders to reforming sentencing, from reforming sentencing to disciplining youth culture, from disciplining youth culture to reversing a generation of social change. Tracing linkages one by one slows the spiral down on paper, revealing the joints where evidence thins and association replaces argument. Propaganda rarely announces its joints. Scholarship earns its keep by finding them.

Policing itself, in the title's deliberate phrasing, meant more than the activity of uniformed officers. Policing the crisis described a society increasingly governed through its crime problem, with police organisations as symbolic centre and cutting edge of a broader disciplinary settlement. Other institutions absorb policing logics under such a settlement. Schools adopt exclusionary discipline and surveillance. Welfare offices adopt suspicion and conditionality. City design adopts hostile architecture and managed visibility. Employers adopt testing and monitoring. Families are urged to treat suspicion as care. The uniformed officer remains important, not least because the burdens of direct coercion fall through that office onto particular streets and bodies. Yet the thesis reaches its full force only when policing is read as a template spreading across social life, converting political questions about distribution and belonging into security questions about risk and exclusion wherever it lands.

Consent from below completes the picture and guards against a purely top down misreading. Authoritarian populism, as Hall developed the idea, involved active popular mobilisation: movements, campaigns, letters, petitions and votes demanding firmness, often against elites perceived as liberal, distant or indulgent. Hegemony is built with such energy, not merely imposed over it. That recognition complicates democratic sentiment in a productive way. Majorities can demand measures that injure minorities and eventually injure majorities themselves. Popularity therefore cannot serve as the sole test of legitimacy for penal policy, any more than unpopularity proves a policy just. The book forces readers to hold two commitments together: respect for popular fears and experiences, and refusal to treat every popular demand as self justifying. Democratic theory lives inside that tension permanently. Criminology that ignores the tension will oscillate between technocratic contempt for publics and populist surrender to whichever alarm rings loudest.

## The Internal Correction: Left Realism Breaks Ranks

Every intellectual tradition carries a point at which loyalty and honesty pull in opposite directions. For Marxist criminology that point arrived when the people most exposed to street crime began to be described, inside the tradition itself, as the people the tradition had forgotten. Left realism grew from that discomfort. Its leading figures, Jock Young and John Lea, set out their case in What Is to Be Done About Law and Order?, published in 1984, and the question in the title was itself a rebuke. A movement that prided itself on asking what was to be done had, on this subject, largely answered with what was to be denied. Crime, in much earlier writing, appeared mainly as an artefact of definition, a label fixed by the powerful upon the powerless, or as a distorted form of resistance whose political meaning mattered more than its human cost. Young and Lea argued that such answers had ceased to persuade because they had ceased to describe the world their intended audience inhabited.

Calling the argument an external attack misses its force entirely. Hostile critics had always accused Marxist criminology of softness toward offenders, and those accusations could be dismissed as predictable. Left realism could not be dismissed in the same way, because it spoke the tradition's own language, shared its account of inequality, and claimed its political inheritance. That is why this section names the development the internal correction. The phrase matters. A correction implies that something valuable required repair rather than abandonment, and an internal correction implies that the repair came from people who wanted the tradition to survive contact with reality. The strongest objection to traditional Marxist criminology, on the question of ordinary crime, came from inside the tradition, and its strength lay precisely in that origin. Outsiders could be accused of misunderstanding. Insiders had to be answered.

### What is left realism in criminology?
a current within Marxist criminology arguing from the 1980s that crime is a real problem whose principal victims are working-class people, that earlier Marxist accounts romanticised offenders, and that any adequate analysis must address the offender, the victim, the state, and the public together.

Consider first what the realists refused to give up. Material inequality remained, in their account, a central condition of crime. Relative deprivation, the felt gap between what people are encouraged to expect and what their circumstances allow, retained explanatory weight. Marginality, the condition of being pushed to the edge of secure work and recognised standing, retained weight as well. Nothing in the correction required pretending that a society organised around competition and unequal reward would produce calm and contentment as its natural fruit. Where the realists broke ranks was over what followed from those premises. Earlier writing had often moved from the structural origin of an act to a kind of absolution for the actor, as though explaining a burglary by pointing to unemployment settled every question the burglary raised. Young and Lea insisted that explanation and excuse are different operations, and that collapsing one into the other had damaged both analysis and politics.

Offenders, in the romantic version, acquired a glamour they rarely possessed in life. The young man who stole from a neighbour's flat became, in sympathetic retelling, a primitive rebel striking at property relations. The group that intimidated people on an estate became an inchoate expression of class anger. Such readings required a great deal of charitable invention. Most working class crime, the realists observed, is committed by working class people against working class people, within the same streets and often within the same households. Its targets are seldom the distant owners of capital. Its proceeds are modest, its methods frequently cruel, and its meaning for the person harmed is not rebellion but loss, fear and humiliation. To redescribe predation as proto-politics was, in their view, an intellectual evasion dressed as solidarity.

Predation is the word that changes the temperature of the argument. A predatory act takes from someone who can ill afford the taking. It exploits proximity and vulnerability rather than challenging power. An elderly resident robbed on a familiar stairwell has not encountered the contradictions of capitalism in human form, whatever role those contradictions played in forming the person who robbed her. She has encountered a man who decided that her purse was available to him. Left realism demanded that criminology keep both truths in view at once, the formation and the decision, the structure and the act. Holding only the first produced a sociology without victims. Holding only the second produced a moralism without causes. The correction proposed holding both, however uncomfortable the combination proved for inherited pieties.

Victimisation therefore moved from the margins of the account to its centre. Earlier Marxist writing had treated concern for victims with suspicion, as though sympathy for a burgled family were a concession to punitive politics. Realists replied that the suspicion itself revealed a distance from working class life. People in neighbourhoods where work was scarce and housing was poor did not experience crime as an abstraction invented to justify authority. They experienced it as noise through a thin wall, as a child threatened on a familiar route, as savings taken from a drawer, as a shopkeeper beaten for a till. Fear altered how they used public space, whom they trusted, and whether they let children play outside. Any analysis that waved such experience away as false consciousness insulted the very constituency in whose name the analysis claimed to speak.

Abandoning that constituency carried a political price, and the realists named the price without flinching. Where a left politics cannot speak credibly about victimisation, another politics will. Promises of order, however harsh or dishonest, find an audience among people who feel unprotected, and an audience found on that ground is not easily recovered by lectures about the social construction of deviance. The ground of law and order is not surrendered in a single dramatic contest. It is surrendered gradually, conversation by conversation, each time a resident raises a genuine fear and receives a seminar in reply. Young and Lea treated that surrender as a strategic catastrophe and a moral failure at once. Strategic, because it delivered working class voters to opponents who offered recognition alongside punishment. Moral, because it told people that their suffering counted for less than the coherence of a theory.

Recognition matters here as more than electoral calculation. A theory that claims to begin from lived experience cannot select which experiences qualify. Prosperous commentators could afford to treat crime as a text to be decoded, since the decoding happened at a safe distance from broken locks and hospital visits. Residents of hard pressed estates had no such luxury, and the realists, who worked closely with local surveys of victimisation, heard a different criminology in their testimony. Women described routes avoided after dark. Older people described a shrinking world bounded by fear. Families described the particular bitterness of being harmed by neighbours rather than strangers, because the harm poisoned the solidarities on which survival in poverty depends. Crime within a community corrodes the community itself, and a politics devoted to communal strength cannot treat that corrosion as a distraction.


### What does it mean to say the left romanticised the offender?
Romanticising meant treating an offender primarily as a rebel against an unjust order, so that theft, violence or intimidation appeared as confused protest. The description softened predatory conduct, displaced attention from the person harmed, and made explanation sound like excuse, even when the act expressed no politics beyond taking from a neighbour.

Romanticism of that kind had a history worth understanding rather than merely condemning. Early radical criminology formed itself against a conservative tradition that treated offenders as a separate and lesser breed of human being, and against official accounts that located all disorder inside defective individuals. Rescuing the offender from demonology was honourable work. Sympathy corrected cruelty. Attention to circumstance corrected the fiction that every act springs from a free and equal choice among identical options. Problems began when correction hardened into reflex, so that every offender arrived in the text pre forgiven and every victim arrived, if at all, as an inconvenience. A humane impulse became an analytical habit, and the habit survived long after the polemical situation that had produced it had changed.

Generational memory played a part in the habit as well. Writers formed by struggles over political protest naturally read rule breaking through the lens of dissent, and some rule breaking does carry that meaning. Trouble follows when a lens fitted to one phenomenon is applied to all phenomena sharing a superficial shape. Breaking a law against assembly and breaking into a pensioner's home share the formal property of illegality and almost nothing else. One act addresses power in public. The other takes advantage of weakness in private. Romantic reading erased that distinction because the distinction complicated a satisfying story, in which the state stood on one side and everyone it prosecuted stood, by that fact alone, on the other. Left realism restored the distinction and accepted the complication.

Sentimentality also distorted the portrait of offenders themselves. Real people who offend are rarely the folk heroes of radical folklore. Their lives often contain genuine hardship, addiction, violence suffered as well as violence done, and choices narrowed by circumstances they did not select. None of that converts their acts into politics. Treating a man as a symbol does him a strange disservice, since symbols are not held responsible for anything and therefore are not fully treated as persons. Accountability, handled without cruelty, recognises agency. Excuse, handled without limits, dissolves agency into structure until the offender becomes a puppet of forces, which is only a more flattering version of the conservative puppet moved by wicked impulses. Realists wanted a harder and more respectful picture, in which people act for reasons, within constraints, and answer for the difference their acts make to others.

Alongside the offender stood another figure the older literature handled awkwardly, the working class victim who demanded protection. Such a person could appear in radical writing as a dupe of punitive campaigns, clamouring for the very powers that would be used against her own community. Sometimes that suspicion had substance, since fear can be cultivated and directed. Often, though, the demand for protection was exactly what it appeared to be, a request that public authority perform its most basic task in streets where private security was unaffordable and informal controls had frayed. Dismissing the request required believing that residents understood their own lives less well than theorists did. Realism reversed the presumption. Residents were treated as witnesses first and as political problems only later, if at all.

Gender sharpened the correction considerably. Violence and intimidation inside households, harassment in public places, and sexual offences had never fitted comfortably into a story about property and protest. A framework built around theft from the rich had little to say about a woman harmed by a partner whose class position matched her own. Feminist criticism had already exposed that silence, and left realism absorbed much of the exposure. Harm patterned by gender could not be waved aside as a secondary matter awaiting resolution after larger transformations. It was happening in kitchens and bedrooms and bus queues, and its victims were asking for acknowledgment rather than postponement. A criminology unable to hear them forfeited credibility with half the class it claimed to represent.

Race and locality complicated the picture in parallel ways. Communities marked by disadvantage often carried a double burden, suffering high levels of victimisation while also suffering heavy handed and distrustful treatment from the agencies meant to protect them. Romantic criminology noticed the second burden clearly and the first only dimly. Administrative criminology, its mirror image, noticed the first and used it to excuse the second. Realists insisted on the doubleness as the defining fact. People could want more protection and better protection at the same time, could fear the burglar and resent the raid, could ask for presence without asking for occupation. Holding that doubleness required listening at a granularity that grand theory tends to skip, street by street and estate by estate, which is one reason local victim surveys became such an important realist method.

Surveys changed the evidential basis of the argument. Official records capture what gets reported and recorded, filtered through trust, expectation and administrative practice. Household surveys, carefully done, capture the dark mass of incidents that never reach a record, along with the fear that outlasts any single incident. When realists pointed to working class victimisation, they were not trading anecdotes against theory. They were claiming a better instrument for seeing the phenomenon. Critics could dispute the interpretation of survey findings, and many did, but the methodological challenge stuck. A tradition that prided itself on materialism had been reasoning about crime with remarkably little material about who was harmed, how often the same households were harmed again, and how harm clustered where disadvantage clustered.

Repeat victimisation deserves particular attention in the realist telling. Harm rarely distributes itself evenly like rain. The same flat is entered again because the first entry revealed its weaknesses. The same shop is targeted because its routines are known. The same person is assaulted because the assailant has learned that no consequence follows. Concentration transforms crime from a background risk into a governing condition of life for a minority who are, disproportionately, already poor. Averages conceal that concentration and thereby conceal the moral urgency of the subject. Politics conducted at the level of averages will always sound calmer than life conducted at the level of the repeatedly burgled street.


### Why does victimisation decide who holds political ground?
Victimisation decides political ground because fear of crime shapes daily choices about movement, trust and public space. A politics that dismisses that fear as manufactured leaves anxious residents to whoever promises order. A politics that begins from lived harm can contest both predatory crime and punitive responses without surrendering the conversation.

Ground, in that formulation, means something concrete. Political ground is the set of subjects on which a movement is believed when it speaks. Belief is earned by recognition before it is earned by programme. A resident who feels recognised in her account of a dangerous stairwell may listen to an argument about prevention, housing or youth provision. A resident who feels corrected, told that her fear is a media product or a moral panic, stops listening at the first sentence. Realists grasped that sequence with unusual clarity. Acknowledgment is not a concession tacked onto analysis for tactical warmth. Acknowledgment is the doorway through which analysis enters a life.

Fear itself required finer treatment than either dismissal or amplification. Some fears are cultivated by sensational reporting, and some anxieties attach to groups rather than to conduct. Realists were not obliged to endorse every fear simply because it was felt. Their point concerned the starting position of inquiry. Felt fear is data about a life, even when its object turns out to be misjudged, because fear reorganises behaviour regardless of its accuracy. Women who avoid a park lose the park. Older people who stop going out lose company and health. Shopkeepers who close early lose income and the street loses light and presence, which makes the street less safe for everyone else. A politics that begins by honouring the cost can then argue, credibly, about proportion and remedy. A politics that begins by disputing the cost argues with the sufferer and loses.

Remedies proposed by the realists followed from their diagnosis in ways that surprised readers expecting a simple turn toward severity. Prevention through better housing design, lighting, youth provision and local employment sat alongside demands for accountable and responsive public protection. The emphasis fell on making agencies answerable to the communities they served, with local priorities set by local experience rather than by distant performance targets alone. Punishment as spectacle held little interest. What mattered was whether a victim felt believed, whether a repeat target received practical help, whether known trouble spots drew sustained attention, and whether young people on the edge of offending found routes back into standing and income. Realism in this sense meant administrative seriousness, the unglamorous work of making protection function for people accustomed to being processed rather than served.

Democratic accountability supplied the bridge between protection and the tradition's suspicion of state power. Marxist analysis had taught, with good reason, that agencies of order serve dominant interests and reproduce inequality in their daily operations. Realists accepted much of that teaching and then asked a further question. Given that such agencies exist and will be used, who sets their priorities and who can call them to account? Absence of democratic control does not weaken an agency. It merely leaves its power unexamined. Bringing protection under local democratic pressure was therefore not a betrayal of the critique of the state. It was the critique applied to practice, an attempt to bend an imperfect instrument toward the people who needed it most and trusted it least.

Tension inside that proposal should not be smoothed away. Accountability mechanisms can become decorative. Consultation can ratify decisions already taken. Local priorities can turn against unpopular minorities, and a majority's fear can demand measures that a just politics ought to refuse. Realists knew these hazards in outline even when their answers remained incomplete. Their wager was that engagement with safeguards beat abstention without safeguards, and that a left absent from the room would not prevent harsh measures but merely prevent its own influence over them. Abstention preserves purity at the price of irrelevance. Engagement risks contamination for the chance of protection. The internal correction chose engagement and accepted the argument that followed.

Defenders of the traditional position deserve a hearing at full strength, because their reply identifies real dangers in the realist turn. Begin with the strongest version of that reply. Crime categories, the traditionalist says, are not neutral containers. What counts as crime reflects decisions about which harms matter, and those decisions track property and power. Street theft draws energetic attention while wage theft, dangerous workplaces and corporate fraud draw a fraction of that energy, although the losses involved can dwarf a burglary. A criminology that starts by accepting the category of crime as given has already surrendered its most distinctive insight. Realism, on this reading, risks becoming a respectable sociology of the already criminalised, fluent about estates and silent about boardrooms.

Scepticism about the state forms the second strand of the reply. Agencies of enforcement, the traditionalist continues, do not distribute their attention evenly, and greater reliance on them predictably burdens the same young men, the same neighbourhoods and the same minorities already marked as suspect. Inviting more protection into a poor estate may deliver more surveillance, more stops, more records and more young lives narrowed by contact with a system that rarely apologises. Victims and suspects are often drawn from the same streets and sometimes from the same families, so a politics that imagines a clean division between a community to be protected and an offender population to be controlled misunderstands the social fabric it claims to defend. Protection and punishment arrive in the same vehicle more often than realist rhetoric admitted.

Media dynamics supply a third strand. Fear of crime does not grow in a sealed local vessel. Reporting selects the shocking over the typical, political entrepreneurs amplify selected incidents into emblems, and public anxiety can be steered toward punitive ends by actors who care little for victims beyond their usefulness. A left that validates every anxiety in the name of listening may find itself validating a manufactured climate, then struggling to oppose the measures that climate is used to justify. Traditionalists warned that the vocabulary of realism could travel. Words coined to demand accountable protection could be quoted in support of longer sentences and wider powers, detached from the preventive and democratic context that gave them meaning.


Fourth, the traditionalist questions the theory underneath the turn. Relative deprivation explains a great deal, yet it can become elastic enough to explain anything, invoked for theft and violence alike without specifying why one deprived person offends and a hundred neighbours do not. Marginality likewise describes a condition rather than a mechanism. Unless realism specifies the pathways from condition to act, through family, peer group, opportunity and masculine honour among other mediations, it risks replacing one slogan with another. Structural sympathy swapped for victim sympathy still leaves the causal middle of the story thin. That objection lands, and honest realists conceded that their programme was stronger as a correction of attention than as a finished theory of causation.

Fifth, there is the matter of what gets crowded out. Attention is finite in scholarship and in politics. A movement absorbed by street crime may neglect the slower harms that never produce a frightened witness or a dramatic incident, harms delivered by spreadsheet and contract and deferred maintenance. Victims of those harms are also disproportionately working class, and they too can claim that a left which cannot speak about their injuries abandons ground. Traditionalists therefore presented their priorities not as indifference to the burgled pensioner but as a ranking of harms by scale, in which the pensioner's case, though real, should not monopolise a tradition built to analyse a whole economic order. Ranking is a legitimate intellectual act. Every research programme performs it, openly or by default.

Fairness also requires stating what the traditional position got right about offenders. Humanising people whom respectable opinion preferred to monster was a genuine achievement. Insisting that punishment does not repair the conditions that produce offending was another. Warning that crime statistics and crime categories carry the fingerprints of the institutions that compile them was a third, and no serious realist programme could afford to unlearn those lessons. Where the older position weakened was in its silences and its tone. Silence about victims read, outside the seminar room, as indifference. A tone of knowing better read as condescension. Politics punishes both failings without waiting for footnotes.

Realists, for their part, had answers that deserve equal steel. Categories can be constructed and still name real events, they replied. A burglary is a legal category and also a ransacked room. Pointing to the category's history does not reassemble the room. Corporate harm deserves far more attention, and the realist programme at its best said so plainly, but the existence of greater harms elsewhere does not shrink a smaller harm at home, any more than a flood elsewhere dries a flooded kitchen. Scale arguments work for allocating research effort. They fail as consolation, and they fail as politics when the person offering them appears to be grading suffering from a height.

On the state, the realist answer turned the traditionalist's own realism back upon itself. Refusing engagement does not abolish enforcement. Enforcement continues, shaped by whoever does engage, and the communities most burdened by its excesses are also the communities most in need of its protection. Dual demand is not incoherence. It is the ordinary position of citizens who want a service performed well and lawfully. Organised pressure for accountability, complaint procedures with teeth, and local priority setting are imperfect instruments, yet imperfection is an argument for improving instruments rather than for leaving power exactly as it stands. A tradition fluent in analysing institutions should be capable of reforming one without worshipping it.

On fear and media, realists drew a line between validating experience and endorsing every interpretation placed upon it. Listening comes first in sequence, not in authority. A resident's account of an incident is evidence. A newspaper's account of an epidemic may be theatre. Confusing the two served traditionalists rhetorically but not analytically, since the surveys on which realism rested asked people about their own lives rather than about headlines. Where fear exceeded risk, realists could say so with more credibility than sceptics who had doubted the fear from the outset, precisely because their general posture was trust. Credibility is a resource earned by acknowledgment and spent on correction. Movements that never earn it cannot spend it.

On causation, the realist reply conceded ground and then reframed the concession. No single mechanism was on offer because offending is plural. Theft for resale, violence over status, vandalism from boredom and cruelty inside a household do not share one pathway, and a theory pretending otherwise buys elegance with falsehood. Relative deprivation and marginality were starting conditions, to be filled in by local and situational analysis of each offence type. Plurality looked like vagueness from the standpoint of a grand unified theory. From the standpoint of the estate and the survey, plurality looked like accuracy. The correction traded a certain theoretical neatness for descriptive honesty, and it regarded the trade as overdue.

Underneath the exchanges sat a disagreement about the purpose of the discipline itself. Traditional Marxist criminology often wrote as though its task were unmasking, showing that crime talk concealed class power. Left realism wrote as though its task also included repair, reducing the harm that crime does to people with the fewest resources for absorbing harm. Unmasking without repair leaves the burgled flat burgled. Repair without unmasking leaves the causes untouched and the powerful unexamined. Mature versions of the tradition need both operations, sequenced intelligently, and the quarrel between the camps is best read as an argument about sequence and emphasis rather than as a war between truth and betrayal. Read that way, the internal correction did not end the tradition. It forced the tradition to grow a second hand.


Historical timing explains much of the correction's urgency. Deindustrialisation was hollowing out the employment on which many working class communities had rested, weakening informal controls that stable work and dense kinship once supplied. Public housing concentrated disadvantage in estates where design faults, poor maintenance and thin services compounded one another. Youth faced a widening gap between the consumption standards displayed all around them and the lawful means available for reaching those standards. None of these conditions made crime inevitable, and most residents under identical conditions offended against nobody. Conditions did, however, load the dice, concentrating temptation, grievance and opportunity in the same few streets while withdrawing the buffers that elsewhere absorb shocks quietly. Romantic theory, formed in a more confident period, had not reckoned with the texture of that decline. Realism was, among other things, fieldwork conducted amid decline, and its tone carries the imprint.

Political competition sharpened the reckoning further. Law and order had become a language in which anxieties about change itself could be expressed, economic change, cultural change, the changing composition of neighbourhoods. Opponents of the left spoke that language fluently and connected it to a broader story about permissiveness and decline. Silence from the left did not deprive that story of material. Silence merely left the story uncontested among the audiences most affected by its subject. Young and Lea understood that a counter story had to begin from the same facts of fear and harm, then travel in a different direction, toward prevention, accountability and social provision rather than toward exemplary punishment. A counter story that begins by denying the facts convinces nobody who lives among them.

Working class conservatism on crime, so called, looks different under realist light. Survey conversations repeatedly found attitudes more nuanced than the punitive label suggests. Residents wanted action against repeat offenders who made estates miserable, and simultaneously wanted jobs, youth clubs, repairs and decent lighting, often ranking practical prevention above theatrical sentencing. What looked from a distance like a simple appetite for severity was frequently a demand to be taken seriously by institutions that had ignored polite requests for years. Severity becomes attractive when it is the only dialect in which authority appears to listen. Offer another dialect, one in which reports are answered and priorities are negotiated, and the appetite often moderates. Realism treated that moderation as an opportunity rather than as an embarrassment to be explained away.

Women's testimony pressed the argument into territory where class language alone proved insufficient. A woman assaulted by a partner does not experience the assault as an episode in political economy, even though economic dependence may trap her in the household where it happens. Her need is immediate, safety, belief, somewhere to go, and a response that does not treat the incident as a private disturbance. Earlier radical frameworks, centred on public crime between strangers, had underbuilt their account of the household, and feminist critics had documented the underbuilding long before realists absorbed it. Absorption mattered. Once domestic harm counts fully as crime, the romantic offender thesis collapses for a large class of offences, because there is no plausible reading in which a violent partner rebels against property relations by terrorising the person nearest to him.

Elders formed another constituency whose experience corrected theory. For an older person, a single robbery can end independent living more effectively than illness, because confidence, once broken, rebuilds slowly at an age when rebuilding anything is hard. Fear among elders is therefore not a soft variable to be discounted against harder data. It is itself a harm with measurable consequences in isolation, nutrition, mobility and health, even though this section offers no figures for any of them. A tradition attentive to material life should recognise confidence as material. Losing the ability to walk to a shop without dread is a loss of world, and the person who caused that loss has taken something larger than the contents of a handbag.

Youth, meanwhile, occupied both sides of the realist account in a way that kept the account honest. Young people appeared as the group most associated with street offending and simultaneously as a group heavily victimised by peers, by older offenders and by violence that adults undercounted because it happened among the young themselves. Holding both facts prevents the lazy slide into generational blame. It also directs remedy toward the settings where young lives are actually organised, schools, clubs, streets and homes, rather than toward abstract denunciations of youth culture. Realists who had begun by correcting sympathy for offenders ended with a more differentiated sympathy altogether, distributed by evidence rather than by role.

Local knowledge became, through all of this, an epistemological commitment. National aggregates flatten the very variations that explain events. One estate suffers a spate of burglaries tied to a single entry method. Another suffers violence clustered around a closing time and a corner. Remedies that ignore such granularity waste effort and then cite their own failure as proof that nothing works. Realist method insisted on mapping harm where it happens, asking residents where it happens, and revisiting the map after intervention. That insistence sounds modest. Against a tradition accustomed to deducing the local from the structural, it amounted to a quiet revolution in how criminological knowledge was to be made.


Readers of this series will recognise the shape of what happened next, because the shape recurs across the tradition. Authoritarianism, within Marxist thought, drew its sharpest reassessment from writers inside the tradition who had watched movements excuse coercion in the name of necessity, and who insisted that the excuse itself required analysis. Legal theory followed a comparable path, when internal critics argued that dismissing law as a mere mask for power left the tradition unable to explain why legal forms mattered to the people who fought through them and sometimes won. Criminology now joined the pattern. Three areas, authoritarianism, legal theory and crime, display the same internal correction at work, in which the most consequential criticism arrives not from opponents eager for the tradition's funeral but from heirs determined to make the inheritance usable.

Parallels of that kind are more than an elegant observation for a series overview. A pattern repeated across domains suggests something structural about how this tradition learns. External criticism tends to be met with defence of the whole edifice, since accepting one external point feels like conceding the critic's larger case. Internal criticism bypasses the siege mentality. The critic shares the premises, cites the founding commitments, and shows that the failing position betrays those commitments rather than fulfilling them. Young and Lea argued, in effect, that a Marxism deaf to working class victims had betrayed Marxism's own starting point in lived material experience. That framing made the correction hard to expel. Expelling it would have required defending deafness as orthodoxy.

Loyalty, then, is not the enemy of correction but its vehicle. Only a writer visibly committed to the tradition could accuse the tradition of romanticism and be heard as a physician rather than as an assassin. Only writers who retained inequality and deprivation at the centre of their account could move victims to the centre without being read as converts to conservatism, although some readers read them that way regardless. Reception was often hostile. Accusations of betrayal, of selling out to administrative criminology, of providing intellectual cover for punitive politics, followed the realists for years. Hostility itself testified to the correction's internal character. Families reserve their fiercest arguments for their own members.

Betrayal accusations deserve scrutiny rather than automatic respect. Every correction that costs a movement some comforting belief will be called betrayal by the belief's holders. A better test asks what the correction preserved and what it discarded. Left realism preserved the structural explanation of crime, the critique of selective enforcement, and the insistence that order imposed without justice is brittle. Discarded were the romantic offender, the dismissal of victims, and the strategic silence on law and order. Judged by preservation, the programme looks like renovation. Judged by discard, it looks like loss only if the discarded items were load bearing, and the realists' whole case was that those items were rot mistaken for timber.

Renovation metaphor aside, genuine losses accompanied the gains and should be recorded plainly. A criminology centred on local surveys and accountable protection speaks less fluently about the global organisation of harm, about finance, empire and the slow violence of extraction, than the older tradition at its best could speak. Fluency lost in one register is not automatically regained in another. Successive writers have tried to hold the local and the global together, with mixed success, and the difficulty is real rather than rhetorical. Scale is the permanent problem of this field. Zoom toward the street and the system blurs. Zoom toward the system and the victim blurs. The internal correction chose a focal length and accepted the blur at the other end as a cost to be managed by other work, including the social harm programme examined in the following section.

Management of that cost begins with honesty about what each focal length reveals. Street level inquiry reveals concentration, repetition, fear and the texture of institutional neglect. System level inquiry reveals why the neglect is patterned, why some harms are criminalised and others are licensed, and why protection itself is distributed unequally. Neither revelation refutes the other. A complete Marxist criminology would be binocular, and the quarrels chronicled here can be read as the painful adjustment of two lenses that had each been used alone for too long. Adjustment is slow because careers, journals and reputations attach to single lenses. Ideas change at the speed of institutions, which is to say unevenly, with long plateaus and sudden shifts when a formulation like the internal correction gives scattered dissatisfactions a common name.

Naming does political work inside scholarship as well as outside it. Before a dissatisfaction has a name, each dissenter supposes the discomfort to be private. After naming, discomfort becomes a position, with texts, conferences and students. Left realism acquired that solidity quickly, and solidity brought its own distortions, as later writers flattened a varied body of work into a few slogans about taking crime seriously. Slogans travel farther than arguments. Taking crime seriously is a slogan. The argument underneath it, about deprivation, marginality, victim concentration, democratic accountability and the four sided analysis of any offence, is harder to carry and easier to lose. Much of the subsequent debate argued with the slogan and missed the argument, a fate the tradition's other internal corrections suffered in their turn.

Students encountering the debate fresh often ask which side won. Victory is the wrong unit of measurement for a correction. Elements of realism entered the common sense of the field so thoroughly that their origin is forgotten, including the centrality of victims, the reality of intra class harm, and the need to address public protection as a legitimate demand rather than as a coded embarrassment. Elements of the traditional critique likewise survived intact, including suspicion of crime categories and of enforcement patterns. What disappeared was the permission to ignore either set of elements. After the correction, a Marxist account of crime that never mentions a victim reads as incomplete on its face, and an account that never mentions power reads as naive. Raising the minimum standard on both sides is what a successful internal correction looks like.

Standards raised in scholarship still leave politics with its original dilemma, and the dilemma should be stated without decoration. People want safety in the streets where they live and justice in the institutions that police those streets, and they want both without being forced to trade one against the other. Political traditions earn trust by refusing the trade. Conservative politics has often offered safety at the price of justice, and a certain radical politics offered justice at the price of dismissing safety. Left realism, whatever its shortcomings, attempted the refusal. It said that working class people deserve protection from predation and from unaccountable power in the same breath, because in their lives predation and unaccountable power are not abstractions from rival textbooks but neighbours in the same week.

Breath, however, is short, and programmes are long. Translating the refusal into durable institutions defeated many attempts, as consultation exercises faded, funding cycles turned, and national politics rewarded simpler stories. Failure of translation does not invalidate the diagnosis. It locates the difficulty where the realists themselves, at their most candid, located it, in the hard middle distance between a correct analysis and a state apparatus willing to be democratised by the people it serves. That distance remains the working space of any criminology that wants to be both Marxist and realist. Occupying the space requires tolerating permanent tension, between structure and agency, between category critique and victim recognition, between suspicion of power and the demand that power do its job.

Permanent tension is an uncomfortable inheritance, and comfort explains much of the resistance the correction met. Romanticism was comfortable. It offered clean roles and a ready moral. Administrative severity is comfortable in the opposite direction, for the same reason. Realism offered neither comfort, only the estate as it was, the survey as it read, and the victim as she spoke. A tradition that began by insisting that philosophy must change the world rather than merely interpret it could hardly complain when some of its own members applied the insistence at home. The internal correction is that application, recorded in criminology with unusual clarity, and its clarity is why this article places the episode at its centre.


Centre placement also reflects a judgement about where readers most need guidance. Surveys of this field often present left realism as a footnote, a brief deviation before the narrative returns to grander theories of control. Footnote treatment understates the episode twice over. First, it understates the human stake, since the argument concerned who gets believed when they report being harmed. Second, it understates the methodological stake, since the correction reordered what counted as evidence and whose testimony counted as knowledge. A footnote cannot carry either stake. Placing the correction at the centre, with its defenders and its critics both heard at length, restores the proportions that participants themselves experienced, for whom nothing about the quarrel felt minor.

Minor quarrels do not rearrange a field's conscience. This one did, because it touched the nerve connecting scholarship to allegiance. Every researcher in the tradition had to decide, implicitly or openly, whether solidarity pointed toward the offender, the victim, or toward the harder discipline of refusing to choose between people drawn largely from the same class and the same streets. Refusal to choose is not indecision. It is a claim that the choice itself was badly framed, manufactured by a debate that assigned each camp a single client. Estates contain no single clients. They contain grandmothers and grandchildren, shopkeepers and shoplifters, sometimes within one household across one year of family life, and a criminology worthy of its subject has to address the household whole.

Addressing the household whole also changes how success is judged. A policy that lowers recorded offending while leaving victims unheard has succeeded administratively and failed humanly. A campaign that hears victims eloquently while widening unaccountable powers has succeeded rhetorically and failed politically. Realist criteria demand both ledgers at once, harm reduced and power answerable, protection delivered and dignity intact. Double ledgers make for slower victories and fewer triumphant announcements. They also make for victories that survive contact with the people in whose name they were won, which is the only kind the tradition, on its own best account of itself, should be interested in claiming.

Claiming less, then, can amount to claiming more honestly. The internal correction taught Marxist criminology to claim less on behalf of offenders as a class, less on behalf of categories as a critique, and less on behalf of silence as a strategy. In exchange it claimed more on behalf of evidence gathered close to the ground, of victims as witnesses to their own lives, and of democratic pressure as a legitimate tool for shaping protection. Exchange rates in intellectual history are rarely so favourable. When they are, the episode deserves more than a footnote. It deserves the centre, and the centre is where this article leaves it, with the argument still open and the tension still instructive.


Openness, in this context, is not vagueness about conclusions already reached. Several conclusions stand firmly. Crime is real in its effects even though its boundaries are made. Victims are concentrated among the poor even though concern for victims can be exploited. Offenders are formed by conditions even though formation never cancels responsibility. Protection is a legitimate demand even though the agencies supplying it require relentless scrutiny. Each conclusion was contested, and each survived contest in a stronger form than the one in which it first appeared, because the contest came from colleagues who shared the premises and therefore aimed at the joints rather than at the caricature. That is the deepest lesson the episode offers to every other branch of the tradition, and it is why the phrase the internal correction deserves to travel beyond criminology into the series vocabulary as a whole.


Travel, for a phrase of this kind, means adoption by writers who never met its originators and who apply it to material its originators never studied. Phrases that travel earn their passage by naming an experience that many people had privately and few had articulated. Scholars in distant fields recognise the moment when a trusted colleague, sharing every premise, says the unsayable thing about a shared inheritance and thereby makes the inheritance stronger. Criminology's version of that moment has now been given its name and its place in this series, at the centre of the article where its consequences belong.

## The Square of Crime and the Turn to Social Harm

Arguments about attention eventually require an instrument, something a researcher or a resident can actually use when facing a concrete offence. Left realism supplied such an instrument in the square of crime. The device is simple to state and demanding to apply. Any offence, the realists argued, sits inside four relations at once, linking the offender, the victim, the state and the wider public. Analysis that seizes one relation and forgets the others will mistake a part for the whole, and policy built on that mistake will lurch toward whichever corner supplied the partial view. Completeness is the square's entire discipline. Each corner interrogates the rest, and the offence becomes intelligible only when the interrogation has run in every direction.

Simplicity of statement should not be mistaken for shallowness. Diagrams in social theory usually decorate arguments made elsewhere in prose. The square instead performs the argument, forcing its user to ask four sets of questions in a fixed rotation. Who acted, under what pressures and for what reasons? Who was harmed, with what consequences and with what support afterward? Which agencies responded, by what rules and with what effects beyond the immediate incident? What did the surrounding public do, in reporting, witnessing, sheltering, gossiping, demanding or looking away? An offence processed through all four sets of questions looks different from the same offence processed through one, and the difference is the realist programme compressed into a method.

### How does the square of crime organise an offence?
The square organises an offence as a relation among four elements rather than a single act. Offender and victim meet in a concrete encounter, while state agencies and public responses shape what that encounter becomes, whether it is recorded, pursued, forgiven, amplified or allowed to fade without consequence.

Start with the offender corner, where traditional analysis felt most at home. Motives live here, along with the pressures that formed them, deprivation, marginality, peer expectation, opportunity and the immediate circumstances of the act. Realism kept this corner fully furnished. Nothing in the square licenses a return to the fiction of the self created offender choosing evil in a vacuum. Pressures, however, are examined in relation to a specific victim rather than in the abstract, and that relation disciplines sympathy. Explaining why a young man needed money or status does not float free once the analysis must also describe the neighbour whose door he forced. The corner retains causal depth while losing the romantic haze that depth once carried.

Victim, the second corner, transforms the first by proximity. Harm acquires a face, a household, a set of consequences unfolding across weeks rather than minutes. Lost property matters, and so does lost confidence, disturbed sleep, a child's new reluctance to walk a familiar corridor, a shopkeeper's decision to close a business that anchored a street. Support, or its absence, belongs in this corner as well. Whether a victim was believed, compensated, protected against repetition or left to improvise recovery shapes the offence's total meaning as surely as the act itself. Two identical thefts diverge completely when one victim receives swift practical help and the other receives a reference number and silence. The square insists that divergence is criminological data, not administrative trivia.

State, the third corner, gathers every agency whose action or inaction frames the event. Recording practices decide whether an offence officially exists. Response times decide whether protection feels real or theoretical. Investigative priorities decide which victims discover that their harm ranks low in an institutional queue. Prosecution and sentencing decisions signal to offenders, victims and publics alike what the harm is taken to be worth. Realists examined this corner with the tradition's inherited suspicion intact, alert to selective attention and unequal treatment, while adding a demand the older critique had neglected. Agencies should be answerable for the quality of protection they deliver to the least powerful, judged partly by the testimony of those on the receiving end.

Public, the fourth corner, is the square's most original contribution and its most easily underestimated. Offences happen inside a social atmosphere that helps decide whether they happen again. Neighbours who witness and report, who shelter a victim or challenge a known offender, alter the local calculus of risk. Neighbours who withdraw, through fear or distrust of agencies or exhaustion, leave space in which offending becomes safer. Media coverage belongs here too, as public reaction at a distance, capable of informing a community or of stampeding it. So does political debate, which converts individual incidents into symbols and sometimes into policy. A square missing its public corner treats crime as a private transaction between two people and a bureaucracy, when in lived reality a whole street participates in every outcome.

Relations between corners matter as much as the corners themselves. Offender and victim stand in the most visible relation, yet even that relation is mediated by the other two. Trust between public and state decides whether offences are reported at all, which decides whether the state corner can function, which decides whether victims receive protection, which decides whether offenders face consequences, which decides whether the public trusts the state. The chain is circular, and circles can turn virtuous or vicious. In a vicious turn, distrust suppresses reporting, low reporting starves agencies of information, weak response deepens victimisation, and deepened victimisation confirms distrust. Naming the circle is the first step toward interrupting it at its weakest link, which varies by place and can only be found by local inquiry.

Dropping the offender corner produces a criminology of pure reaction, in which acts have no causes and prevention shrinks to deterrence and incapacitation. Such analysis cannot explain why offending clusters where it clusters, and its policies reproduce the conditions they police. Dropping the victim corner produces the older radical failure in a new form, a sophisticated account of everything except the harm that made the offence matter. Dropping the state corner produces folk criminology, blind to how recording, response and prosecution construct the visible pattern of crime itself. Dropping the public corner produces technocracy, in which agencies and individuals are modelled while the community that reports, witnesses and absorbs the consequences vanishes from the calculation. Each amputation has a constituency. Each constituency mistakes its favourite corner for the building.


Worked slowly, the square also exposes how a single incident changes character as it travels. A scuffle outside a shop is, at the offender corner, a dispute over respect among young men with few other currencies of respect available. At the victim corner it is an injury, a frightened assistant and a till left untended. At the state corner it becomes a classification decision, recorded one way or another depending on injury, history and the judgement of whoever attends. At the public corner it becomes a story a street tells itself about safety, retold in queues and kitchens, shaping whether parents alter routes and whether the shop hires help or installs shutters. None of the four versions is the true one in isolation. The offence is the sum of its travel, and policy that intervenes at only one point of the journey should expect the other three to undo or redirect the intervention.

Intervention design is where the device earns its keep most visibly. Prevention aimed at offenders, through employment, mentoring or treatment, addresses one corner. Support and target hardening for victims address a second. Reform of recording and response addresses a third. Community organisation, tenant associations and credible local information address the fourth. Programmes built on the square therefore tend to arrive in bundles, untidy compared with single lever policies and more faithful to the phenomenon. Untidiness is a cost in a political culture that rewards announceable simplicity. Realists accepted the cost as the price of correspondence between the map and the territory. A tidy programme aimed at the wrong corner fails tidily, and its failure is then blamed on the intractability of crime rather than on the poverty of the map.

Critics found weaknesses in the device, and the weaknesses are instructive. A square can become a checklist, four boxes ticked with a paragraph each, generating the appearance of completeness without the labour of tracing actual relations. Corners can also imply a symmetry that reality refuses. Offender and victim are not always distinct populations, state and public bleed into one another, and power is distributed across the diagram with gross unevenness that a neat geometric figure tends to flatter. Corporate and state offences strain the device hardest, since there the state corner may contain the offender, the victim may be a dispersed public, and the public corner may never learn that an offence occurred. Realists acknowledged much of this. Their defence was that a heuristic should be judged by the questions it forces, and the square forces questions that single corner theories never ask.

Generosity toward the device should still stop short of worship. Geometry can seduce its users into believing that naming four elements amounts to explaining their interaction, when explanation requires mechanisms, histories and evidence specific to each case. The square tells an analyst where to look. It cannot tell the analyst what will be found. Used humbly, as scaffolding for local inquiry, it remains one of the most transferable tools the Marxist tradition in criminology has produced, precisely because it travels without demanding allegiance to a full doctrine. Workers in housing, health and youth services have all found versions of it usable, usually without knowing its provenance. Tools that outlive their doctrinal home have passed a test that manifestos often fail.

Provenance nevertheless matters for the story this article is telling, because the square embodies the internal correction in miniature. Victim and public, the two corners older theory neglected, stand in the diagram with the same size and dignity as offender and state, the two corners older theory privileged. Equality on the page announced equality of attention in the field. Nobody encountering the diagram could afterwards claim that victims were an afterthought to the programme. They were load bearing. Remove them and the figure collapses, which is exactly the claim the realists were making about the tradition itself.

| Hegemony and moral panic | A crisis of consent seeking an authoritarian outlet | Panic manufacturing consent for coercive policing | The link between crime control and hegemony | The ordinary experience of victimisation |
| Left realism | Real predation rooted in relative deprivation and marginality | A state answerable to the victims it protects | The victim, and the internal correction | A full theory of corporate and state harm |

Reading across the rows of the grid clarifies what the narrative sections established in sequence. Each position adds something its predecessors lacked, and each addition exposes a further absence. Traditional analysis explained criminalisation while stumbling over harm inside the working class. Later syntheses joined act and reaction while leaving the forms of reaction underexplained. Hegemony accounts connected control to consent while the ordinary victim waited outside the frame. Realism brought the victim inside and then stopped, by its own admission in the final cell, before the largest harms of all. A grid cannot argue, but it can display an argument's shape, and the shape here is a staircase, each step built from the criticism of the step below.


Staircases invite the question of the next step, and the next step proposed within this broad tradition was zemiology, the study of social harm. Associated above all with Paddy Hillyard and Steve Tombs, the proposal was deliberately provocative in its phrasing. Criminology, they argued, should study harm rather than crime, and should be willing to follow that substitution wherever it leads, even if it leads out of the discipline's inherited boundaries altogether. Provocation had a precise target. A discipline organised around a legal category inherits the state's selection of what matters, however critical the discipline imagines itself to be. Marxist criminology had exposed that selection for generations and then, in much of its daily practice, gone on studying the selected category anyway. Zemiology proposed ending the contradiction by changing the object.

### Why study harm rather than crime?
Studying harm rather than crime starts from damage to people instead of from a legal label. Unsafe work, polluted air, ruined savings and preventable illness can injure more deeply than many offences, yet remain outside criminal categories. Harm as the organising idea exposes that selection and widens inquiry beyond it.

Unsafe work offers the clearest entry into the argument. People are killed and maimed in workplaces through decisions about speed, maintenance, staffing and protective equipment, decisions taken by identifiable organisations with foreseeable consequences. A fraction of such events are prosecuted as crimes. Most are processed through regulatory and compensation channels that soften their moral character, converting a death into a compliance failure and a bereaved family into claimants. Zemiologists ask why a discipline devoted to harm done by one party to another should accept that conversion at face value. The legal label differs. The crushed body does not. Starting from harm rather than from crime restores the body to the centre and treats the label as a question instead of as a boundary.

Environmental damage extends the same reasoning across time. Pollution released lawfully under permit can sicken a neighbourhood across decades, while an unlawful release of the same substance becomes a crime by virtue of paperwork rather than by virtue of consequence. Rivers, air and soil carry harms that no single victim can narrate as an incident, which is one reason such harms struggle for attention in a field built around incidents. A harm based approach has no such structural embarrassment. Damage is damage whether it arrives in an event or in an accumulation, whether a prosecutor can use it or not, and whether it photographs well or merely registers, years later, in lungs and bloodstreams and shortened lives.

Financial misconduct supplies a third field of evidence. Savings destroyed by deception, pensions hollowed by mismanagement, households ruined by products designed to be misunderstood, all produce consequences that victims describe in the language of theft because theft is the honest word for the experience. Legal systems process much of this conduct through civil and regulatory routes, where the vocabulary of wrongdoing dissolves into the vocabulary of mis selling, imprudence and market failure. Zemiology treats the dissolution itself as an object of study. Who benefits when a harm loses its criminal name? Which institutions perform the renaming, and what would a ledger of social damage look like if the renaming were refused and every serious injury to livelihood were entered in the same book as a burglary?

Breadth is the proposal's glory and its problem in a single package. Harm, uncritically used, can expand until it covers every disappointment and offence against taste, at which point the concept loses the cutting edge that made it attractive. Zemiologists have answered with attempts at classification, distinguishing physical harms, financial harms, psychological harms and harms to autonomy and standing, while insisting that severity and preventability should rank attention. Ranking reintroduces judgement, and judgement reintroduces politics, which the harm approach never truly escaped in the first place. Deciding which harms count most is a political act wearing methodological clothing. Honest zemiology admits the clothing. Dishonest versions pretend that harm is a neutral measuring rod lying outside the disputes that crime, for all its distortions, at least conducts in public through legislatures and courts.

Democratic objection deserves its strongest form here. Crime, whatever its biases, is defined through processes that are at least formally public and contestable. Harm, defined by scholars, risks substituting expert judgement for public definition, empowering a new priesthood to declare which sufferings are real objects of concern. Hillyard, Tombs and their collaborators have generally welcomed public argument about the harm catalogue rather than fearing it, treating the catalogue as an invitation to debate what a society should protect its members against. Whether publics would engage on those terms at scale remains an open question. Seminar rooms have embraced zemiology warmly. Courtrooms, newsrooms and parliaments have adopted its vocabulary only in fragments, usually where a campaign had already done the political work of naming a harm in public.


Marxist readers will notice that zemiology, for all its novelty of branding, recovers an ambition present near the tradition's source. Analysis of capitalism was always an analysis of harm, of bodies worn out early, of lives narrowed by insecurity, of wealth extracted through arrangements that legality blessed. Somewhere in the professionalisation of criminology, that ambition narrowed into a debate about a legal category, conducted with increasing technical refinement. Social harm writing widens the aperture back toward the original scale. Its proponents sometimes present the widening as a break with criminology altogether, a new discipline for a new object. A longer view sees continuity under the provocation. The questions are the tradition's oldest ones, asked again after a period in which the category of crime had quietly fenced them in.

Continuity should not be overstated into absorption, because zemiology also rebukes the Marxist tradition at a sensitive point. Radical criminology prided itself on unmasking the selectivity of criminal law, yet its research agenda remained oddly loyal to the selection. Journals filled with studies of policing, sentencing and street offending, while the harms of the powerful appeared mainly in programmatic statements and in the work of a persistent minority. Tombs spent a career documenting safety crimes and corporate harm in empirical detail, and the relative marginality of that documentation within the field is itself evidence for the zemiological charge. A tradition is judged by its bibliography more honestly than by its manifestos. By that measure the charge lands, and lands harder because the tradition's own premises predict the pattern it failed to escape.

Escape, where it has happened, has usually come through alliance with neighbouring fields. Occupational health, environmental sociology, public health and the study of corporate regulation all possess methods for tracing harms that never pass through a criminal process. Zemiology borrows those methods freely and gains empirical range by the borrowing. Borrowing also dilutes disciplinary identity, which some zemiologists celebrate and others regret. A field defined by an object as broad as harm may dissolve into a general sociology of suffering, losing the specific expertise about law, enforcement and punishment that made criminology worth consulting. Balance between range and identity is not a problem that gets solved once. It gets renegotiated by each generation of scholars deciding what to study and what to leave, deliberately, to others.

Deliberate leaving returns the discussion to the square, because the two devices answer different questions and work best in harness. The square organises the analysis of offences, events that have entered, or should enter, the field of public response. Zemiology organises the prior question of which harms deserve to enter that field at all. An analyst equipped with both can ask why a workplace death never acquired a square of its own, no offender corner filled, no state response of the criminal kind, no public reckoning beyond a local headline, and can then use the square, once the case for criminal attention is made, to structure the response the harm deserves. Sequence matters. Harm first, as the criterion of attention. Square second, as the anatomy of response. Together they describe a criminology that chooses its objects democratically and then refuses to analyse any chosen object partially.

Refusal of partiality is an exacting standard, and this section should end by acknowledging how rarely any real institution meets it. Agencies specialise because specialisation is efficient. Scholars specialise because careers require a defensible patch. Publics attend in bursts because attention is finite and grief is exhausting. Against those pressures, devices like the square and concepts like social harm function as disciplines of memory. They remind their users of the corners habitually skipped and the harms habitually renamed, and they make the skipping and the renaming visible enough to argue about. Argument, in the end, is what the Marxist tradition in criminology has contributed most reliably across all its phases, traditional, synthetic, hegemonic, realist and zemiological alike. Each phase argued that the obvious object of study was not the whole object, and each phase was right often enough to keep the argument, and the tradition, alive.

Alive, but never settled, which is the condition this article has traced from its opening sections to this point. A settled criminology would have chosen between structure and victim, between category and harm, between suspicion of the state and demand upon the state. The tradition at its best refused every one of those choices and paid for the refusal in coherence, in factional quarrels and in programmes that satisfied nobody completely. Payment bought something valuable. It bought a field of inquiry in which a burgled pensioner, a maimed worker, a frightened street and a poisoned river can appear in the same analysis without any of them being treated as a metaphor for another. Holding that company together is difficult, unfinished work. It is also, on the evidence assembled here, the work this tradition was always for.


Further angles on the square repay attention before the harm discussion closes over it. Time runs through all four corners and changes each of them. An offender's reasons at the moment of an act differ from the reasons offered months later in an interview room, and both differ from the reasons a court accepts. A victim's needs shift from the first night, when safety and belief dominate, to the following season, when compensation, information and the wish to see the same harm prevented for others may dominate instead. Agencies move through cycles of attention, in which a category of offence is a priority until a new priority displaces it. Publics forget, then remember sharply when a similar incident revives an older fear. A static diagram applied to a moving process will mislead unless its user animates it, asking not only what each corner contains but how each corner is changing and at what speed relative to the others.

Speed differences generate many practical failures. Support services that arrive after confidence has collapsed are experienced as indifference even when their eventual quality is high. Prevention programmes that mature over years are judged against incident patterns that shift over months, and are cancelled for failing a test no honest designer would have accepted. Public consultation conducted after an agency has settled its plan teaches residents that their corner of the square is decorative, and the lesson is remembered long after the plan is forgotten. Realist method, with its insistence on local and repeated inquiry, was partly a technology for catching these mismatches of tempo. A single survey is a photograph. Repeated surveys become a film, and films show movement, including the movement of trust, which is the square's most fragile and most consequential variable.

Trust deserves a paragraph of its own in any account of the device. Between public and state, trust decides whether the entire apparatus of response receives the information it needs to function. Between victim and offender's community, a thinner and stranger trust decides whether informal resolution is possible without humiliation or revenge. Between agencies themselves, trust decides whether information travels or pools in separate files while a repeat pattern goes unrecognised across organisational boundaries. Programmes that ignore trust treat the square as four boxes of activity. Programmes that cultivate trust treat the lines between the boxes as the real infrastructure, harder to build than any office and slower to repair than any fence. The realist literature returned to that infrastructure repeatedly, in discussions of accountability, responsiveness and local priority setting, because its authors had watched technically competent interventions fail on a street that simply did not believe the interveners.

Belief also governs the zemiological programme more than its founders sometimes acknowledged. Renaming an injury as a social harm is an act of persuasion before it is an act of classification. A workforce must come to believe that a death at work is a killing rather than an accident of fate. A neighbourhood must come to believe that its air is a harm rather than a background condition of living where it lives. A saver must come to believe that a ruined pension is a wrong rather than a personal failure of financial literacy. Each belief has opponents with an interest in the older vocabulary, since accident, background and failure all distribute blame toward the harmed and away from the harmer. Zemiology at its most effective is therefore a rhetoric as well as a research programme, supplying words in which dispersed injuries can recognise themselves as a pattern. Where the words catch, campaigns follow. Where they do not, the harm continues under its anaesthetic older name.

Anaesthetic names are everywhere once the habit of noticing them forms. A redundancy programme, a restructuring, an industrial incident, a compliance matter, a negative outcome, a market correction. Language of that kind is not decoration on harm. It is part of the machinery by which harm is made tolerable to audiences who might otherwise demand a reckoning. Marxist analysis has always been alert to such machinery in the vocabulary of crime, where words like disorder and menace do political work. Zemiology extends the alertness symmetrically, to the vocabulary in which powerful actors describe their own conduct. Symmetry matters. A critique of language that examines only the words applied to the powerless, while accepting the words the powerful apply to themselves, has understood only half of its own method.

Half understandings recur throughout this history with almost comic regularity. Traditionalists understood criminalisation and half understood victimisation. Realists understood victimisation and half understood the harms of the powerful, a gap their own grid concedes in its final row. Zemiologists understand harm and sometimes half understand the institutional question of what follows from naming, since a harm identified still requires agencies, remedies and sanctions if identification is to change anything in a life. Each generation's half understanding becomes the next generation's research programme, which is a hopeful way to describe a sequence that participants experienced as polemic and betrayal. Hopeful descriptions are permitted when the sequence genuinely produced knowledge, and this one did, at every stage, including the stages its successors remember mainly for their errors.

Errors, catalogued honestly, form a useful syllabus. Romanticism taught the field how sympathy becomes distortion. Administrative capture taught it how listening becomes legitimation when safeguards are weak. Category loyalty taught it how a critique of selection can coexist with obedience to the selection. Harm inflation taught it how a liberating concept becomes useless when it is stretched to cover everything. A student who masters the syllabus possesses something more valuable than allegiance to any single position in the grid. The student possesses a set of antibodies, each developed against a specific failure that real scholars, acting in good faith, actually committed. Antibodies do not guarantee health. They improve the odds when the next attractive simplification arrives, as it reliably does, wearing the vocabulary of whichever position happens to be ascendant.

Ascendant vocabularies also decide what a generation of researchers can get funded, published and promoted for studying, and a materialist tradition should be the last to ignore those incentives when explaining its own history. Street crime offered data, access and policy relevance. Corporate harm offered litigation risk, closed doors and documents obtainable only through long campaigns. The distribution of scholarly attention followed the distribution of professional reward more faithfully than it followed any manifesto, which is precisely the kind of explanation Marxist analysis applies to every institution except, too often, its own. Zemiology's difficulty in displacing established criminology is therefore overdetermined. The concept challenges not only ideas but careers, and careers defend themselves with the quiet weapons of peer review, syllabus design and hiring preference. Naming that defence is not cynicism. It is the tradition's method applied at home, one more time.


Home application of the method yields a final observation about the grid with which this section organised its middle. Grids, like squares, are instruments, and instruments shape the hands that use them. A reader who has absorbed the grid will approach the next criminological claim with four questions ready. What does the position explain about the act? What does it explain about control? What does it add that earlier positions lacked? What can it not explain, stated as plainly as its proponents would state their strengths? That fourth question is the tradition's conscience in interrogative form. Positions that welcome it tend to age into contributions. Positions that evade it tend to age into slogans, and slogans, however stirring, are where analysis goes to stop.

Stopping, though, is sometimes a social necessity disguised as an intellectual failure. Campaigns need slogans because movements cannot march behind a literature review. Scholars who supply movements with ideas therefore live with a permanent translation loss, watching careful arguments become banners. The loss can be managed by keeping the careful version in print, cited and taught, so that the banner can be corrected against the text when the banner begins to mislead. Marxist criminology has kept its careful versions in print with unusual tenacity, which is why an article like this one can reconstruct the argument across a century of polemic without inventing a tidier past. Tenacity of the archive is a scholarly virtue with political consequences. A tradition that preserves its own corrections can learn from them. A tradition that edits them out is condemned to repeat them as discoveries.

Discovery, repeated across the positions surveyed here, finally suggests the note on which a long article on this subject should close its middle sections. Every generation of this tradition believed that it had found the missing element, the offender's reasons, the reaction, the crisis of consent, the victim, the harm beyond crime. Every generation was partly right, and the parts accumulate. Accumulation, rather than replacement, is the honest model of the field's progress, untidy as accumulation always is. The square keeps four corners because four generations each proved that one corner could not be removed without collapse. The harm programme keeps the category of crime in view, as a special and instructive case of selection, even while refusing to be governed by it. Nothing essential was ever simply refuted. It was situated, bounded and joined to the element it had excluded, and the joining is the story.

## The Criticisms: What the Framework Cannot Explain

Criticism deserves its strongest form before any answer begins. A framework that claims to explain crime through inequality, property and class power invites a particular kind of test, and that test is not whether inequality exists or whether enforcement can look uneven in a single anecdote. Serious critics ask whether the explanation does the work assigned to it. Does inequality cause offending, or does it merely sit behind offending as a broad condition that accompanies almost everything else in a stratified society? Does the tradition explain why offences happen, or does it explain something narrower and then borrow credit for the wider question? Put plainly in that sharpened form, the objection has real force, and any honest exposition should let that force land before attempting a reply.

David Greenberg, writing from within a broadly sympathetic engagement with radical criminology rather than from hostility toward it, pressed versions of this worry with care. Internal critics of that kind matter more than distant opponents, because they share enough of the project to know where its joints are weak. Functionalist writers, approaching from a very different direction, pressed a complementary worry about order, consensus and the functions that control performs for a society as a whole. Pluralist critics added another angle, doubting whether power concentrates as tightly as the traditional position supposes. Taken together, such criticisms do not amount to a single refutation. Rather, they converge on one uncomfortable finding. Where the framework is asked to explain individual offending in detail, its answers grow thin, general and difficult to distinguish from background description.

Consider first the pattern that troubles the theory most directly. Most offences recorded within any stratified order are not directed upward against owners, managers or holders of wealth. Theft, assault, criminal damage and intimidation fall overwhelmingly upon neighbours, acquaintances, partners, workmates and strangers who occupy a similar economic position to the person who harms them. Homes broken into belong, in the main, to other households with modest possessions. Goods taken from shops pass their cost, in diffuse ways, onto fellow shoppers and low paid staff rather than onto some distant abstraction called capital. Violence in streets, pubs and homes wounds bodies that carry the same insecurities as the body that strikes. A theory built around conflict between classes must explain why so much harm travels sideways, within a class, rather than upward across the divide that supposedly structures it.

Strain language can be stretched to cover that sideways pattern, yet stretching is not explaining. Blocked opportunity might account for frustration in general terms, but frustration alone cannot specify a target. Why does a frustrated person strike a partner instead of an employer, steal from a neighbour instead of a warehouse, or damage a bus shelter that fellow residents rely upon? Each answer imported to fill the gap, whether it invokes masculinity, alcohol, local status competition or learned habits, comes from outside the core framework. Borrowing explanations from psychology, subcultural theory or routine activity thinking may produce a fuller account in practice. Still, every borrowing concedes the same point. Core concepts of property, exploitation and class domination, taken alone, underdetermine the act, the victim and the moment.

Variation between societies deepens the difficulty. Societies with broadly comparable levels of inequality display markedly different patterns of offending, different dominant offence types and different public anxieties about disorder. Conversely, societies with quite different distributions of wealth sometimes resemble one another in the harms that dominate everyday life. If inequality were doing direct causal work, closer correspondence would be expected between its level and the shape of offending. Culture, family structure, welfare provision, housing patterns, religious traditions, urban design and historical experience all intervene between a distribution of resources and any particular offence. Once every intervening factor is admitted, inequality begins to look like one condition among many, setting a scene within which other mechanisms operate, rather than a mechanism in its own right.

Historical comparison presses harder still. Offending persisted in societies that abolished capitalist private property in the means of production and proclaimed class antagonism overcome. Theft did not vanish when factories changed owners on paper. Violence did not end when markets were suppressed. Black markets flourished precisely where official distribution failed, corruption attached itself to administrative privilege, and domestic harm continued behind closed doors that no change in ownership reached. Defenders sometimes respond that such societies retained hierarchy, scarcity or authoritarian habits inherited from an earlier order. Each response may contain some truth, yet each also weakens the original claim. If crime survives the abolition of the property relations said to generate it, then those relations cannot be its sufficient cause, and perhaps cannot be its principal cause either.

Generality itself becomes a problem at that point. An explanation that can absorb every outcome explains very little in a discriminating way. Prosperity produces acquisitive crime through consumer pressure, while poverty produces acquisitive crime through need, so both abundance and scarcity confirm the theory. Falling offending shows control working through hegemony, while rising offending shows control failing under strain, so both directions confirm the theory again. Peaceful periods demonstrate successful ideological incorporation, whereas turbulent periods demonstrate contradictions surfacing. A critic is entitled to ask what observable pattern would count against the framework. Without an answer to that question, explanation slides toward interpretation, a way of redescribing events in favoured vocabulary after they have occurred.

Functionalist criticism approaches the same weakness from the opposite shore. Where Marxist accounts treat control primarily as domination imposed from above, functionalist accounts ask what shared norms make social life possible at all. Most people refrain from harming others most of the time, and that restraint cannot be explained solely by fear of punishment or by ideological deception. Attachment, conscience, habit and mutual dependence all bind people who have every material reason, on a crude reading, to prey upon one another. Offending within working class communities then looks less like distorted class conflict and more like a breakdown of bonds that ordinarily protect those communities from within. Such an account has its own blind spots, especially around whose norms prevail and whose interests order serves. Nevertheless, it names something the traditional position handles awkwardly, namely the moral life of ordinary people taken seriously as a cause in its own right.

Pluralist objections widen the field further. Power in complex societies disperses across many centres, including professions, bureaucracies, media organisations, local institutions and organised publics whose interests do not align neatly along a single class axis. Criminalisation sometimes follows campaigns by groups with little wealth, moral entrepreneurs with strong convictions, or officials pursuing institutional goals of their own. Public anxiety can push enforcement in directions that embarrass governments and inconvenience businesses rather than serve them. None of that fits a simple picture in which a unified ruling class writes its preferences into law and directs enforcement accordingly. Sophisticated versions of the framework never held that simple picture, of course. Yet sophistication purchased through endless qualification carries a cost, because a theory of tendencies, mediations and relative autonomy can become compatible with almost any institutional outcome.

Gender exposes another gap that class centred explanation long neglected. Offending and victimisation are patterned by sex in ways that cross class lines and persist across very different economic arrangements. Violence within households, sexual coercion and intimidation in public space follow hierarchies of gender that cannot be derived from ownership of productive property without considerable auxiliary argument. Feminist critics, including writers sympathetic to materialist analysis, insisted that patriarchy operates with a logic that interacts with class without reducing to it. Working class women suffer harm from working class men as well as from economic structures, and a framework that treats the first harm as a displaced expression of the second risks explaining away the experience it claims to champion. Intersectional work later complicated the picture further, showing how race, migration status, age and disability shape exposure to harm and to control in ways no single axis captures.

Race presents a parallel challenge and a parallel temptation. Disproportionate contact between minority communities and agencies of control can be read through class composition, and part of that reading holds. Another part concerns racialised perception, institutional culture and histories of empire and migration that have their own weight. Collapsing race into class loses explanatory precision and can sound dismissive to communities whose experience of being watched, stopped or disbelieved does not feel like a disguised version of something else. Treating race as wholly separate from material position loses just as much. Careful work holds both together without subordinating either by decree. Traditional statements of the framework, written before such care became standard, often failed that test, and critics were right to mark the failure.

Age patterning adds a quieter but persistent puzzle. Offending concentrates heavily in adolescence and early adulthood across societies with very different property systems, inequality levels and political orders. A curve that stable across contexts suggests developmental, peer related and neurobiological influences that class analysis does not supply. Why would exploitation produce its sharpest behavioural expression in the teenage years and then fade as people enter the very labour market relations said to generate it? Answers invoking youthful exclusion from legitimate status have some purchase. School failure, dependence and blocked transitions into adult roles are socially structured, after all. Even so, similar age concentration appears where transitions are organised quite differently, which again points toward mechanisms operating beneath the level at which the framework pitches its claims.

Victim choice raises a related embarrassment for any romantic residue in the tradition. Early radical writing sometimes treated the offender as a primitive rebel, a figure whose defiance contained an inarticulate politics. Experience of victimisation inside poor communities dismantled that image more effectively than any academic critique. People who live with burglary, robbery and violence rarely experience the perpetrator as a rebel against property relations. Fear restricts movement, corrodes trust between neighbours and drives demands for protection that may include demands for stronger enforcement. A theory that cannot hear those demands without translating them into false consciousness has stopped listening to the class in whose name it speaks. Left realism emerged from exactly that refusal to stop listening, as a later part of this section will show.

Measurement troubles compound the theoretical ones. Official records reflect reporting practices, recording rules and enforcement priorities as well as underlying behaviour, a point the tradition itself taught criminology to respect. Self report evidence, gathered independently of enforcement, tends to show rule breaking spread more widely across social classes than conviction patterns suggest, especially for minor offences committed during youth. Defenders reply that serious, persistent and harmful offending remains class concentrated, and that distinction matters. Critics answer that the reply quietly changes the claim. A framework introduced as an account of crime becomes, under pressure, an account of serious crime, then of recorded serious crime, then of the processing of recorded serious crime. Each narrowing may be defensible. Cumulatively, they reveal how much ground the original ambition has surrendered.

Causation at the individual level resists the framework in another way. Within any deprived neighbourhood, most residents commit no serious offences, while a small minority accounts for a large share of harm. Neighbours share streets, schools, labour market prospects and exposure to consumer pressure, yet their conduct diverges sharply. Sibling studies and life course research direct attention toward temperament, early attachment, school experience, peer selection and turning points such as stable work or partnership. None of those factors floats free of social structure, since structure distributes risks and opportunities unevenly. Still, distribution of risk is not commission of acts. Explaining why harm clusters in a district differs from explaining why a particular person harms a particular victim on a particular night, and the framework speaks far more confidently to the first question than to the second.

Reverse causation deserves mention among the harder objections. Areas acquire reputations through offending, after which investment withdraws, employers relocate and services deteriorate, deepening the deprivation that explanation treats as the starting point. Individuals acquire records that exclude them from work, housing and credit, manufacturing the marginality later invoked to explain their conduct. Untangling cause from consequence in such loops is methodologically demanding for every criminological tradition, not only for this one. Fairness requires granting that point. Granting it does not rescue a monocausal reading, though. Loops of that kind suggest reciprocal processes in which offending itself does causal work, something a purely structural account struggles to grant without revising its picture of offenders as products of circumstance.

Even the concept of crime creates difficulty for a theory that wants both to criticise the category and to explain its contents. Radical writers argue, persuasively, that the legal category bundles together unlike acts while excluding harms of comparable or greater seriousness. Acts as different as shoplifting, assault and fraud share little beyond prohibition. If the category is an ideological construction, then asking for a single material cause of everything inside it may be a malformed question. Some writers embraced that conclusion and redirected attention toward harm, power and definition. Others continued offering causal claims about crime as though the category were natural. Critics reasonably charge the tradition with wanting both moves at once, dissolving the category when its heterogeneity embarrasses the theory and treating it as real when a causal story is required.

### Can the framework explain crime within the working class?

No, not adequately on its own terms. Strain, exclusion and blocked opportunity may describe pressures surrounding an offence, yet they seldom explain why one neighbour steals from another, why violence falls on kin and acquaintances, or why most people facing identical pressures never offend at all in any period.

That short answer needs unpacking, because partial resources do exist inside the tradition and fairness requires naming them. Relative deprivation can sharpen resentment when people compare their circumstances with near neighbours rather than with distant elites, and comparison with those nearby may help explain why envy and conflict ignite within streets rather than across class boundaries. Status frustration can turn local hierarchies of respect into contests settled by force. Informal economies can draw neighbours into disputes that lack legal channels for resolution, so debts, territory and reputation get enforced violently. Consumer culture can make modest possessions into potent symbols, so theft from a similar household carries meaning beyond the value of goods taken. Each resource illuminates something real about intra class harm.

Limits appear when those resources are asked to carry the full explanatory load. Relative deprivation explains a feeling, not a deed, and many people who feel deprived acutely never steal or strike anyone. Local status contests occur in affluent settings as well, expressed through litigation, exclusion and reputational manoeuvre rather than fists, which suggests that available repertoires and controls mediate any link between status anxiety and violence. Informal economy disputes explain some assaults and some retaliation, but they say little about coercion inside families, cruelty toward children or opportunistic theft from strangers. Household violence in particular follows patterns of entitlement, control and learned conduct that economic pressure may aggravate without creating. Pressure aggravates many things it does not cause, from illness to marital breakdown, and criminology should keep aggravation and causation conceptually separate.

Honesty about that boundary strengthens rather than weakens the wider tradition. A framework can illuminate the conditions under which harm becomes more likely, the forms harm takes when legal remedies feel inaccessible, and the reasons some neighbourhoods carry heavier burdens of both offending and victimisation. Claiming more than that invites refutation by a single street where deprivation is deep and serious offending is rare, or by a single household where comfort coexists with cruelty. Variation at close range is the standing rebuke to grand explanation, and every mature version of the theory has had to absorb that rebuke by narrowing its promises. Absorption came at a price, because narrower promises sound less like a theory of crime and more like a theory of context.

Attention now turns to what the framework explains better, because a balanced verdict depends on separating its strong ground from its weak ground. Criminalisation comes first. Why do some harmful acts attract prohibition, prosecution and imprisonment while other harmful acts attract regulation, compensation schemes or quiet administrative handling? Wage theft, dangerous working conditions, pollution, mis selling and reckless financial conduct can injure large numbers of people, yet they have historically occupied a different moral and legal register from street theft or burglary. Property offences committed by the poor tend to be defined as crime in the full expressive sense, complete with individual blame and custodial punishment. Harms flowing from organisational decisions tend to be fragmented into compliance failures, settled without any equivalent stigma attaching to identifiable persons. That asymmetry is not an invention of theory. Any citizen can observe it by comparing the language used for benefit fraud with the language used for tax evasion, or by comparing the urgency devoted to shoplifting with the patience extended to employers who underpay.

Enforcement patterns form the second area of strength. Laws written in general language still require choices about where patrols concentrate, which complaints receive investigation, which suspects appear credible, which cases merit prosecution and which sentences feel proportionate. Discretion at every stage creates room for class, race, neighbourhood reputation and perceived respectability to shape outcomes without any official ever announcing a discriminatory purpose. Traditional Marxist writing described that shaping in terms of ruling class interest, sometimes too crudely. Stripped of crudity, the underlying observation survives contact with evidence from many sources. People with resources can purchase legal expertise, meet bail conditions, present themselves in settings that invite leniency and absorb penalties that would destroy a poorer household. People without resources meet the system at its most coercive points and with the fewest buffers. Explaining that gradient is something the framework does well, arguably better than any rival that treats enforcement as a neutral response to behaviour alone.

Agenda setting forms a third strength that connects criminalisation with public feeling. Which harms become symbols of social decline, which victims become emblematic and which offenders become folk devils depends partly on media selection and political entrepreneurship. Moral panic analysis, developed within the neo Marxist moment and refined by later writers, showed how a modest pattern of disorder could be amplified into a national drama about youth, race or national character. Amplification was never presented, in careful versions, as fabrication from nothing. An incident occurs, a reaction builds, and the reaction reorganises enforcement priorities, sentencing rhetoric and public expenditure far beyond the scale of the original incident. Understanding that disproportion requires concepts of ideology and hegemony that purely behavioural theories lack. Here the framework earns its keep by explaining the career of a crime problem rather than the occurrence of a crime.

Distribution of victimisation, handled carefully, offers a fourth and more qualified strength. Poor households face higher exposure to many forms of harm because they live with weaker physical security, thinner insurance cover, longer waits for repairs, greater dependence on public space and less capacity to move away from trouble. Offenders and victims often share neighbourhoods because opportunity, proximity and vulnerability are socially patterned. A materialist lens connects those facts to housing allocation, labour markets and public investment rather than treating them as unfortunate coincidences. Left realist writers built directly on that connection when they insisted that victimisation inside working class communities was a material injury demanding material remedies. Where the lens fails is in moving from patterned exposure to individual motivation. Exposure explains where harm lands more readily than it explains why a hand is raised.

Predictive direction deserves careful statement because it captures the asymmetry at the heart of a fair verdict. Given a new harm, the framework predicts, with reasonable success, which end of the social hierarchy will bear the heavier burden of criminalisation for it. Harms associated with the powerless will be named as crime sooner, policed harder and punished more severely than comparable harms associated with the powerful. History offers repeated illustrations of that direction, from the treatment of vagrancy and combination in earlier centuries to modern contrasts between street level drug possession and organisational misconduct. Predicting the direction of enforcement is a genuine achievement. Confusing that achievement with an explanation of why individuals offend is the characteristic error of weaker presentations of the theory, and critics perform a service when they force the distinction into the open.

Falsifiability returns here in a constructive form, because the strongest part of the theory can be stated in a way that evidence could genuinely defeat. Selective enforcement is not a mood or a metaphor. Claims about selectivity assert that, harm held roughly equal, the social position of the person causing harm influences the likelihood and severity of legal response. That assertion can be examined by comparing matched harms, tracing decisions stage by stage and asking where divergence enters. Do complaints from some neighbourhoods receive slower responses than identical complaints from others? Do organisational harms get routed toward regulators while individual harms of similar scale get routed toward prosecution? Do sentencing outcomes diverge for comparable conduct once legally relevant factors are held constant? Questions of that kind admit evidence, and evidence can disappoint the theory as readily as confirm it.

### What would defeat the selective enforcement claim?

Evidence that enforcement intensity tracks harm rather than the class position of offenders would defeat it. If comparable harms drew comparable attention, investigation and sanction regardless of who caused them, the traditional claim about patterned selectivity would lose its central support and require fundamental revision or abandonment altogether.

Spelling out a defeater matters for examination answers as much as for scholarship. Weak presentations treat selective enforcement as self evident, pointing at any single disparity as though disparity alone settled causation. Stronger presentations specify the comparison that would test the claim. Harm must be measured independently of enforcement, otherwise the argument circles back on itself by defining seriousness through the response it seeks to explain. Social harm scholarship attempts exactly that independent measurement, cataloguing injury, loss and premature death across both conventionally criminal and conventionally lawful activities. Writers in that tradition often conclude that the comparison supports selectivity, since many grave harms attract minimal legal response. Other researchers contest their measures, arguing that intent, detectability, consent and the practicalities of proof differ between street offences and organisational harms in ways that legitimately affect response. Either way, the debate is empirical and checkable in principle, which places this component of the framework on firmer scientific footing than its claims about motivation.

Practical difficulties in testing should not be hidden. Matching harms across contexts is contentious work, because a fraud that spreads small losses across thousands of victims differs phenomenologically from a robbery that concentrates terror in one victim, even where aggregate loss is equal. Intent matters morally and legally, and organisational harm often involves diffused responsibility that complicates attribution to any single decision maker. Enforcement also responds to complaint patterns, and complaint patterns themselves reflect trust, resources and expectations about whether reporting will help. Each complication gives defenders room to explain away an inconvenient comparison. Room to explain, used sparingly, is legitimate scholarship. Room used automatically, so that no comparison could ever count against the claim, converts a testable proposition back into dogma, and examiners reward candidates who notice the difference.

Societies without capitalist property relations pose the objection in its most concrete historical form, and the point deserves a section of its own within this evaluation. Twentieth century states that expropriated private owners and planned production through administrative command still maintained extensive criminal codes, large enforcement apparatuses and crowded places of confinement. Theft of socialised property became a distinct and heavily punished offence category, which is itself revealing. Goods held in common were stolen by individuals who plainly did not experience collective ownership as their own in any motivating sense. Queues, shortages and privileged access for officials generated resentment, illicit trade and corruption that reproduced, in administrative form, many behaviours the theory attributes to markets. Alcohol related violence, hooliganism and domestic abuse persisted across decades of full employment guarantees and officially egalitarian ideology. Whatever caused those harms, private ownership of factories was absent while they occurred.

Responses available to defenders each carry a price. One response argues that such societies were not genuinely classless, since a bureaucratic stratum controlled resources and privileges in ways that functioned like a class system. That move preserves a materialist spirit by widening the concept of class beyond legal ownership toward effective control. Widening has plausibility, since control over allocation can matter more than title deeds. Yet the rescue transforms the theory from an account tied to capitalism into a general account of hierarchy and privilege, which is a different and less distinctive claim. Another response blames scarcity, arguing that crime flows from unmet need under any system that cannot provide abundance. Scarcity plainly mattered in shortage economies. Still, affluent capitalist societies also generate extensive offending, so neither scarcity nor abundance maps cleanly onto harm. A third response blames cultural lag, the survival of habits formed under an earlier order. Lag may explain a transitional decade. Stretched across generations born entirely within the new order, lag becomes an all purpose solvent that dissolves falsification altogether.

Left realism supplies the most credible internal correction, and its existence should count in the evaluation rather than being treated as an embarrassment to hide. Writers in that current began from inside the radical tradition and accused its romantic wing of neglecting the lived reality of crime for the very communities radical politics claimed to serve. Working class victims, they argued, experience burglary as loss they cannot absorb, violence as fear that narrows daily life and disorder as a tax on people with the fewest options for escape. Dismissing concern about such harm as manufactured panic betrayed both evidence and political principle. Square of crime thinking followed, placing offender, victim, state agencies and public reaction in one frame and insisting that an adequate account must hold all four corners together. Relative deprivation survived in that frame, but disciplined by attention to individual choices, local conditions and the legitimacy that enforcement needs if communities are to cooperate with it.

Significance of that internal correction extends beyond one school. When a tradition produces its own sharpest critics and revises itself in response, blanket dismissal becomes harder to sustain. Left realists did not abandon materialist commitments. Relative deprivation, marginalisation and the unequal distribution of security remained central to their analysis. What they abandoned was the inference that structural explanation could substitute for taking offences seriously as events with victims, motives and local dynamics. Their work therefore marks the precise boundary this section has been tracing. Structure shapes the distribution of risk, the availability of legitimate routes to status and income, and the response that harm receives. Structure does not, without help from other levels of analysis, generate the individual act.

Concessions should be gathered explicitly, since evaluation writing gains credit by stating them without flinching. First, the account of individual offending is the weakest component of the framework, and writers inside the tradition, especially the left realist current, say so in substance even when their vocabulary differs. Second, inequality functions in most defensible versions as a background condition that raises or lowers pressures, rather than as a proximate cause that produces specific offences. Third, crime in societies without capitalist property relations cannot be explained by the traditional position in its unmodified form, and every available modification dilutes the distinctiveness of the original claim. Fourth, intra class victimisation remains only partly explained, with the strongest supplementary resources borrowed from relative deprivation, subcultural analysis and feminist work on gendered power. Fifth, the framework handles gender, race and age as additions more readily than as derivations, which signals limits in its core architecture.

Balance requires gathering the surviving strengths with equal explicitness. Criminalisation remains uneven in ways that track power, and no rival tradition explains that unevenness as economically. Enforcement remains patterned by resources and respectability at every discretionary stage, and the claim that it is so patterned can be tested, contested and refined with evidence. Public reaction to crime remains disproportionate in patterned directions that ideology and hegemony help to decode. Victimisation remains materially distributed, falling hardest where buffers are thinnest, and that distribution connects to housing, work and public provision in ways a purely individual account cannot see. A candidate who can hold both lists together, concessions on one side and survivals on the other, writes the kind of evaluation that distinguishes analysis from allegiance.

Ranking the components helps a conclusion about relative merit, even though any ranking invites argument. Strongest stands the account of criminalisation, because the historical record of which harms receive criminal labels shows persistent asymmetry aligned with power. Next stands the account of enforcement, strong in direction though variable in magnitude and always requiring stage by stage evidence rather than assertion. Public reaction and moral panic analysis follow, illuminating amplification while needing discipline against overreading every campaign as orchestration. Distribution of victimisation comes next, real but heavily mediated by opportunity and proximity. Weakest, by a considerable distance, stands the explanation of individual motivation, where the framework gestures toward pressures that many experience and few translate into offences. That ordering, enforcement and definition first, motivation last, is the single most useful sentence a student can carry away from the critical literature on this tradition.

Examination technique follows naturally from that ordering. Answers that present criticism as a contest between a theory that is wholly right and rivals that are wholly wrong misrepresent a field in which every tradition explains some slice of the problem better than others. Better answers allocate explanatory territory. Let strain and life course accounts handle much of motivation, let interactionist work handle definition and reaction at the level of encounters, and let the Marxist tradition handle the political economy of criminalisation and enforcement where its evidence is strongest. Allocation is not evasion. Precision about what a theory cannot do is part of understanding what it can do, and markers reward that precision because it mirrors how researchers themselves divide labour when they design studies, choose measures and interpret awkward results that refuse to fit a single lens.

Final judgement, then, is neither dismissal nor defence but placement. Placed as a theory of why particular people commit particular offences, the framework disappoints, and its own left realist critics supply the most authoritative statement of that disappointment. Placed as a theory of how societies select harms for criminal treatment, distribute enforcement across a hierarchy and amplify some dangers while normalising others, the framework remains formidable and, in places, unmatched. Confusing those placements produced decades of talking past one another, with defenders citing enforcement evidence to answer motivation objections and critics citing motivation failures to dismiss enforcement insights. Keeping the placements separate dissolves much of that confusion. What remains is a tradition with a genuine explanatory core, a known and admitted weak flank, and a testable central claim about selectivity that future evidence could strengthen or defeat.

One further criticism deserves space because it concerns method rather than conclusions. Historical materialist writing on crime sometimes selects its illustrations from periods of open conflict, when class language was explicit and enforcement wore its purposes openly. Generalising from such periods to quieter periods, when legitimacy is broader and coercion more procedural, requires argument that is not always supplied. Institutions develop routines, professional norms and legal constraints that acquire momentum independent of any originating interest. Officials who enforce a rule may believe sincerely in its fairness, and that sincerity is a social fact with consequences, not a mask to be waved aside. Ideology critique that treats every sincere belief as deception loses the ability to explain why some rules command genuine consent while others provoke resistance. Consent, uneven and conditional as it is, belongs inside a complete account of order, and functionalist critics were not wrong to insist upon its presence even when their own treatment of power was naive.

Economic reductionism names the recurring temptation behind several of these failures. Reducing law, culture, gender, race and individual psychology to reflections of an economic base offers parsimony at the price of truth. Later Marxist writers themselves developed richer accounts of relative autonomy, overdetermination and articulation precisely because the crude version failed empirically. Each enrichment improved realism while complicating the clean causal story that made the tradition distinctive. Critics can therefore pose a dilemma that still has bite. Keep the theory simple, and it fails against intra class harm, cross national variation and post capitalist offending. Make the theory complex, and it converges with multi factor criminology, retaining a vocabulary of class and power but surrendering any claim to a unique causal engine. Defenders answer that retaining class and power at the centre of a multi factor account is itself a distinctive contribution, since other traditions marginalise both. That answer has merit, provided the centre is specified tightly enough to generate expectations rather than slogans.

Generational change inside criminology has quietly altered the terms of debate in ways that favour a measured verdict. Few researchers defend a neutral, power free picture of criminalisation, because the evidence against such a picture accumulated across many independent studies and many theoretical camps. Equally, few researchers defend a purely structural account of offending, because life course, developmental and situational evidence accumulated with equal independence. Convergence on both points suggests that the sharpest criticisms of the framework were partly absorbed by the field as a whole, while its sharpest insights were absorbed at the same time. Absorption is a form of vindication and a form of defeat simultaneously. A tradition can win its best arguments and lose its claim to be a complete theory in the same historical movement, and that dual outcome describes the position of Marxist criminology with unusual accuracy.

Teaching the criticisms well matters because students otherwise meet them first as hostile caricatures and learn to swat them away rather than weigh them. Weighing produces a different intellectual posture. A student who has felt the full weight of the intra class objection reads enforcement evidence more carefully, asks sharper questions about causation and stops expecting any single tradition to explain definition, motivation, reaction and distribution in one movement. Criminology as a field advances through exactly that division of explanatory labour, with each tradition strongest where its concepts bite deepest and weakest where its concepts stretch. Recognition of stretch, honestly admitted and precisely located, is not a surrender of the framework. Recognition of that kind is what separates a living research tradition from a catechism, and the Marxist tradition at its best, including its internal critics, has repeatedly chosen the living option over the catechism when forced to decide.

## The Misattribution: Labelling Theory Is Not Marxist

Revision material often files labelling theory under Marxist criminology, and the filing is wrong. Plain statement of the correction should come first, before any nuance softens it. Labelling theory is an interactionist theory. Its questions, its methods, its leading figures and its picture of how social order works all belong to a tradition that explains deviance through encounters, meanings and reactions rather than through property relations and class power. Grouping the two together because both discuss definition confuses a shared topic with a shared explanation. Topics can be shared by theories that disagree about almost everything else. Astronomy and astrology share a topic, after all, without sharing a framework, and the analogy, though deliberately sharp, captures why topic alone cannot settle classification.

Howard Becker stands at the centre of the labelling tradition, and nothing in his intellectual formation makes him a Marxist. His work grew from the Chicago tradition of urban ethnography, from symbolic interactionism and from close observation of musicians, teachers and users of prohibited substances as they negotiated reputations and rules in everyday settings. Edwin Lemert, whose distinction between primary and secondary deviance gave labelling one of its most durable concepts, likewise worked within an interactionist and social psychological frame. Neither writer grounded explanation in ownership of productive property. Neither treated the state as an instrument or expression of class domination. Neither organised analysis around exploitation, accumulation or hegemony. Calling such writers Marxist because they noticed that powerful people influence rule making stretches the term until it covers anyone who has ever observed inequality of influence.

Shared interest in definition is real, and acknowledging it explains how the confusion starts. Both traditions ask who gets to define an act as deviant, whose definition sticks and what follows for the person defined. Both reject the naive assumption that deviance sits inside an act as a natural property waiting to be discovered. Both direct attention toward audiences, officials and institutions rather than toward the offender alone. Agreement stops at that threshold, however. From the shared question, each tradition walks in a different direction. Interactionism follows the encounter, tracing how a label is applied, internalised or resisted across a career of interactions. Marxist analysis follows the structure, asking why some groups command the institutions that define, why law takes the shape it does and whose material interests the resulting order protects.

Difference in scale follows from difference in direction. Labelling research works at close range, in courtrooms, classrooms, clinics and streets, watching negotiation happen between identifiable people. Meaning emerges in that negotiation, fragile and revisable, dependent on setting, sequence and the skill with which participants present themselves. Structural explanation works at a distance from any single encounter, mapping relations that predate the participants and will outlast them. A magistrate and a defendant meet as individuals, yet the resources, presumptions and institutional roles they bring into the room were distributed long before either entered it. Interactionism brackets that prior distribution to study the meeting. Marxist analysis makes the prior distribution its main object and treats the meeting as one site where distribution becomes visible.

Consequences for method are practical rather than merely philosophical. Ethnography suits labelling because its claims concern process at close range, and a researcher must watch process unfold to describe it credibly. Historical and comparative analysis suit structural explanation because its claims concern patterns across periods and societies that no single observation site can reveal. Political economy, legal history and institutional analysis therefore dominate the Marxist shelf, while field notes and interview transcripts dominate the interactionist shelf. Students who merge the traditions also merge their evidence, citing an ethnography of a subculture as though it demonstrated a claim about class power, or citing a history of criminalisation as though it demonstrated a claim about identity formation. Each citation misses the level at which its source actually works.

### Why is labelling theory not a Marxist theory?

Because its explanatory centre lies elsewhere. Labelling theory asks how audiences define acts and how reacted identities harden, following interaction between people in concrete encounters. Marxist explanation asks how property relations, class power and state institutions structure definition itself, a different question pursued through different concepts and commitments.

Elaboration of that answer begins with the concept of power, which the two traditions use in almost opposite ways. For interactionists, power appears as an uneven resource within encounters, visible when one participant succeeds in imposing a definition on another. Moral entrepreneurs campaign, rule creators draft prohibitions and enforcers decide where to look, yet each step remains contingent, open to failure, ridicule or reversal. For Marxist writers, power is anchored in control over productive resources and over the state, giving it a durability and direction that encounter level contingency lacks. Definitions succeed or fail, on the structural account, partly because some definers stand behind institutions with budgets, coercive capacity and ideological reach that no individual entrepreneur commands alone. A campaign against a pastime and a campaign embedded in a governing project for social order are different phenomena, even when both involve energetic moral advocacy.

History supplies the clearest proof that the traditions understood themselves as distinct. When a group of British criminologists published a landmark attempt at a fully social theory of deviance in 1973, their explicit ambition was to move beyond labelling. Interactionism, in their assessment, described reaction elegantly while neglecting the wider political and economic context that made some reactions systematic. Their proposed synthesis retained labelling insights about definition and amplification while subordinating them to a political economy of crime, a social psychology of action and an account of state response. Whatever one thinks of that synthesis, its authors plainly did not regard themselves as restating labelling theory. They regarded themselves as correcting its limits by embedding it inside a structural frame it had never possessed. A correction of that scale only makes sense between theories that are genuinely different.

### Where does interactionism part company with structural explanation?

Interactionism parts company at the level of explanation itself. It begins with meanings negotiated in encounters and builds outward toward careers, identities and reputations. Structural explanation begins with enduring relations of property and power that predate any encounter, constrain what meanings become authoritative, and distribute the consequences of definition unequally.

Agency looks different when viewed from each side of that divide. Interactionist writing grants actors considerable room to manage impressions, resist labels, drift into and out of deviance and construct identities through reflected appraisals. Deviance becomes something people do within situations, shaped by commitments and contingencies that shift across a lifetime. Structural writing grants actors room as well in its sophisticated forms, but it stresses how the menu of available identities, the cost of resistance and the likelihood of being watched in the first place are set by position within a hierarchy. Resistance that succeeds for a person with money, credentials and articulate advocates may fail for a person with none of those assets, facing the same formal rule in the same formal setting. Both descriptions capture something true. Blending them without noticing the tension produces accounts in which labels are simultaneously free floating social constructions and predictable instruments of class rule, a combination that cannot be sustained without careful argument about levels.

Origins of rules provide another clean separation. Becker famously directed attention toward moral entrepreneurs, people and groups who crusade for new prohibitions or stricter enforcement out of conviction, organisational interest or humanitarian concern. Crusades in his account are contingent historical episodes whose success depends on organisation, publicity and political opportunity. Marxist accounts of rule creation look instead for alignment between a prohibition and the requirements of an economic order, asking how a rule protects property, disciplines labour, manages surplus populations or secures conditions for accumulation. Entrepreneurial energy matters in the second account as a mechanism, never as a sufficient explanation. Where interactionism sees a campaign that happened to succeed, structural analysis asks why that campaign found receptive institutions while other campaigns, equally energetic, died without trace. Receptiveness, on the structural view, is patterned rather than accidental.

Treatment of the state separates the traditions just as sharply. Interactionist research tends to encounter the state in fragments, as individual officials exercising discretion in particular settings, with motives ranging from routine habit to personal prejudice to institutional convenience. No unified state purpose is assumed, and none is needed for the analysis to proceed. Marxist analysis treats the state as a structured whole whose parts, however quarrelsome in practice, tend to reproduce the relations on which the social order rests. Courts, enforcement agencies and legislatures may disagree about tactics while converging on outcomes that leave property relations intact. Critics dispute how tight that convergence is, and internal debates about relative autonomy occupy a large literature. Even the loosest version of the structural claim, though, asserts a patterned tilt that interactionism neither asserts nor requires.

Victims occupy an ambiguous place in labelling work, and that ambiguity marks another boundary. Because labelling focuses on the process by which people acquire deviant status, the harm that prompted intervention can recede into the background of the analysis. A burglary becomes an occasion for studying how a suspect is processed, how a record accumulates and how identity shifts under official reaction. Marxist work in its traditional form shared some of that recession, which is why left realism had to correct both traditions at once by restoring victims to the centre. Shared weakness, however, is not shared identity. Two theories can neglect the same thing for different reasons, one because its lens points at reaction, the other because its lens points at structure, and the correction each requires will differ accordingly.

Examination consequences of the confusion are concrete and worth stating without embarrassment. Candidates who attribute labelling concepts to Marx or who describe Becker as a Marxist criminologist lose marks for accuracy before their argument is even assessed. Candidates who explain the distinction, name the interactionist lineage, identify the 1973 neo Marxist attempt to move beyond it and show where the traditions overlap without merging usually gain marks for discrimination. Discrimination, in the academic sense of distinguishing carefully between similar things, is a skill examiners are trained to reward. Stating the distinction earns marks because it demonstrates that a candidate knows what each theory claims, where each came from and why textbooks shelve them near one another despite their differences. Proximity on a shelf is a librarian decision, not an intellectual identity.

Repetition of the correction in fresh words may help it survive the pressure of revision timetables. Labelling theory is not Marxist because its founders were interactionists. Its engine, moreover, is the encounter rather than the economy. Its object is the career of a label, not the structure of a society. And the Marxist writers who learned from it said plainly that learning from it was not enough, building a different framework around political economy instead. Four sentences, four routes to the same destination, each route emphasising a different ground for classification: intellectual biography, explanatory mechanism, object of study and self understanding of the traditions involved. Any one route suffices to correct the error. Together they make the error hard to repeat.

Influence travelled widely beyond the tradition that produced it, and that travel creates a second family of confusions. Concepts forged inside Marxist criminology now circulate as standard tools among researchers who hold no Marxist commitments at all. Moral panic analysis offers the clearest illustration. Media scholars use the concept to study amplification, folk devils and disproportionate reaction in fields ranging from health scares to technology anxieties, often without any reference to hegemony or class. Criminologists of liberal, conservative and purely empirical orientations use it to describe cycles of anxiety and enforcement that they explain through electoral incentives or news values rather than ideology. A concept can outgrow its birthplace. Origin still matters for intellectual history, yet present use cannot be read backwards as evidence of present allegiance.

Selective enforcement analysis travelled along a similar path into socio legal studies. Researchers examining regulation, compliance and enforcement discretion across fields such as environmental protection, workplace safety, financial supervision and food standards routinely ask why some violations attract prosecution while others attract advice, warnings or negotiated settlement. Many such researchers frame their answers through organisational capacity, regulatory culture or political pressure rather than class domination. Findings about patterned discretion therefore circulate in literatures that cite the radical tradition respectfully, critically or not at all. Shared findings do not imply shared theory. A liberal scholar who documents uneven enforcement and a Marxist scholar who explains uneven enforcement through class power agree on a phenomenon while disagreeing about its deepest causes, and conflating their positions flattens a live debate into a false consensus.

Social harm approaches extend the pattern into a broader rethinking of what criminology should study. Writers in that field argue that the discipline should organise itself around harms, including harms that law permits, tolerates or produces, rather than around the legal category of crime alone. Roots of that argument reach into radical critiques of criminalisation, and the debt is often acknowledged openly. Users of the approach nevertheless include scholars whose politics centre on human rights, public health, environmental protection or welfare provision rather than on any Marxist programme. Treating every social harm researcher as a Marxist repeats the original labelling error at a larger scale, assigning a whole identity on the basis of one borrowed tool. Tools migrate. Identities, theoretical commitments and explanatory frameworks migrate only when a researcher actually adopts them, which is a claim requiring evidence about that researcher rather than inference from a bibliography.

Media studies absorbed the moral panic concept with particular enthusiasm because it solved a problem internal to that field. Audiences were known to be active interpreters rather than passive recipients, yet coverage of some groups plainly followed hostile and repetitive patterns with real consequences for policy and public feeling. Panic analysis offered a middle position, granting coverage a role in constructing a problem without claiming that coverage mechanically injected beliefs into audiences. Hall and colleagues had built hegemony into the original formulation, linking amplification to a governing project during a period of economic and political crisis. Later media scholars often retained the amplification machinery while dropping the hegemony argument or replacing it with accounts centred on news production routines and commercial pressures. Whether that dropping impoverishes the concept is a genuine scholarly dispute. Either way, the stripped version in wide circulation is no longer distinctively Marxist, and students should describe its lineage accurately rather than assume that every use carries the full original theory inside it.

Correcting the remaining misreadings begins with the idea that Marxist criminology names a single position. Plurality defines the tradition from its early statements onward. Traditional work treated law and enforcement as expressions of ruling class interest with varying degrees of directness. Neo Marxist work complicated that picture with hegemony, relative autonomy and cultural analysis, insisting that consent and meaning mattered alongside coercion. Left realism broke with romantic readings of offending and rebuilt the tradition around victims, local surveys and practical questions of protection. Feminist and anti racist writers inside and alongside the tradition challenged its class first habits and forced race and gender into the centre of serious analysis. Abolitionist currents questioned whether criminal justice institutions could be reformed at all, while other currents pursued reformist programmes within them. A candidate who writes about the Marxist view of any contested question without specifying which current is speaking has already made an error, because the currents disagree with one another about offenders, victims, the state and the very possibility of justice under capitalism.

Romanticisation forms the second misreading in need of correction. Early radical writing did sometimes portray offenders as resisters whose acts contained an embryonic politics, and critics fastened onto those passages with justification. Treating that portrayal as the position of the whole tradition ignores what happened next. Left realism arose precisely to correct romanticisation from within, arguing that predatory offending inside working class communities deserved condemnation and practical response rather than celebration. Writers in that current interviewed victims, documented fear and insisted that a politics claiming to represent working class people could not romanticise the people who burgled their homes or terrorised their estates. Even traditional writers were more varied than the caricature suggests, distinguishing between political resistance, survival strategies and predation in ways that popular summaries flatten. Accuracy requires the narrower claim. One current romanticised some offending for a period, another current corrected that tendency explicitly, and the correction is part of the tradition rather than an external refutation of it.

Moral panic analysis suffers a third misreading when it is described as a theory of media effects. Effects language suggests a stimulus and response model in which coverage causes audiences to believe or behave in direct proportion to exposure. Panic analysis in its original form concerned hegemony, the process by which a governing bloc constructs consent for an authoritarian turn by presenting itself as the voice of ordinary moral feeling. Media organisations mattered in that account as institutions that reproduced preferred definitions, structured by ownership, professional routines and dependence on official sources. Audiences were never imagined as empty vessels, and later formulations stressed negotiation, scepticism and the decay of panics as well as their ignition. Reducing the argument to a claim that newspapers frighten readers misses its political core. A panic, properly understood in the tradition that coined the analysis, is a moment when the boundaries of acceptable coercion shift, when exceptional measures gain popular licence and when a society rehearses a harder version of order that may outlast the scare that introduced it.

Neglect of victims forms the fourth misreading, and it survives because it was once partly true. Traditional statements concentrated on offenders, law makers and enforcers while victims appeared mainly as occasions for state action. Feminist critics exposed a parallel neglect inside radical work on violence against women, where victims were subordinated to arguments about policing and imprisonment. Left realism exists to address exactly that neglect, placing victimisation surveys, repeat victimisation and the material consequences of harm at the centre of radical analysis. Square of crime thinking formalised the correction by giving victims a permanent corner in the explanatory frame alongside offenders, agencies and publics. Claiming that the tradition ignores victims therefore requires a date and a current to be accurate. As a blanket description of a tradition that contains left realism, the claim is simply false, and repeating it in an examination signals reliance on a caricature rather than engagement with the literature as it developed.

Comparison with functionalism sharpens every distinction drawn so far, because functionalism shares with Marxism a structural scale while reversing its evaluation of order. Both traditions look beyond individual encounters toward institutions, norms and the organisation of society as a whole. Both ask what crime and control do for a social system rather than only what they do to individuals. Agreement on scale makes the disagreement about power and consensus easier to isolate. Functionalist accounts treat shared values as the binding agent of society and deviance as a boundary marking exercise that can, within limits, perform useful work. Marxist accounts treat apparent consensus as a contested achievement, secured partly through ideology and coercion, with law and enforcement tilted toward dominant interests. Students who want the fuller contrast set out systematically should work through [Marxism vs functionalism](/2021/08/01/marxism-vs-functionalism/) alongside this article, since the comparison organises differences in assumptions about human nature, order, change and the role of the state into a single frame that revision can actually use.

Legal theory connections deepen the structural side of the contrast and prevent criminology from floating free of its parent discipline. Debates about whether law expresses class relations directly, enjoys relative autonomy or follows an internal logic of its own run through the wider literature surveyed in [Marxist theory of law](/2021/04/01/marxist-theory-of-law/), which traces how different generations of writers handled the relationship between economic base and legal form. One especially demanding contribution to that debate argued that the very form of law, with its abstract equal subjects exchanging equivalents, mirrors the commodity form at the heart of capitalist exchange. Readers who follow that argument through [Pashukanis commodity form of law](/2021/04/15/pashukanis-commodity-form-of-law/) will see why some Marxist criminologists treat legal categories themselves as objects of explanation rather than as neutral tools. Labelling theory contains nothing comparable, which is further evidence of the distance between the traditions. Interactionism takes legal categories as the starting point for studying reaction. Commodity form analysis asks why categories take that shape at all.

Genealogy, carefully kept, protects against a cluster of smaller errors that examiners notice even when candidates do not. Deviance as a field passed through phases in which successive theories reacted against the limits of their predecessors, and each reaction makes sense only when the sequence is preserved. Positivist confidence in measurable causes provoked interactionist attention to meaning and reaction. Interactionist neglect of power and political economy provoked the neo Marxist synthesis of 1973. Romantic excesses within radical work provoked left realism. Feminist and anti racist critiques provoked reconstructions that no earlier phase anticipated. Compressing that sequence into a single box labelled radical theories erases the arguments that give each position its identity. Erasure feels efficient during revision and proves expensive in the examination room, where questions are often designed to test whether candidates can separate positions that textbooks print on facing pages.

Terminology discipline offers a practical safeguard against compression. Reserve the word Marxist for work that grounds explanation in material relations, class power and the state, and that situates crime within a wider critique of capitalism. Reserve interactionist for work that grounds explanation in meaning, encounter and reaction. Reserve radical as an umbrella only when the argument genuinely spans several traditions, and specify which traditions are meant as soon as the umbrella opens. Reserve critical as a still broader label for scholarship that questions taken for granted categories, a set that includes feminist, abolitionist, postcolonial and queer work with distinct foundations of their own. Precision of that kind is not pedantry. Labels in an examination answer function as promises about the content that follows, and broken promises cost marks in ways that candidates rarely trace back to a careless first paragraph.

Influence without identity also describes the relationship between Marxist criminology and several neighbouring fields that borrowed its questions while developing independent answers. Socio legal scholars who study how law operates in everyday life inherited an insistence that formal rules and lived practice diverge, then pursued that divergence through ethnographies of workplaces, households and advice agencies. Scholars of regulation inherited an interest in why enforcement varies, then answered through theories of organisational behaviour and political accountability. Cultural criminologists inherited an interest in meaning, style and resistance, then combined it with ethnographic methods closer to interactionism than to political economy. Each inheritance is genuine. Each borrower transformed what was borrowed. Intellectual history that credits the source while respecting the transformation serves students better than a family tree in which every descendant carries the founder name as a surname.

One more distinction completes the correction, concerning what counts as evidence for classifying a theory. Classification rests on explanatory commitments, not on the political sympathies a reader projects onto an author, not on the company a theory keeps in an anthology and not on whether a theory criticises authority. Many traditions criticise authority, including liberal, conservative, feminist, anarchist and religious traditions that disagree profoundly with one another. Criticism of power is a stance. Marxism is a framework, with specific claims about production, class, ideology and historical change that a theory either makes or does not make. Becker and Lemert did not make those claims. Their work makes different claims, valuable on their own terms, about definition, career and identity. Honouring their work means describing it accurately, and describing it accurately means refusing the Marxist label that revision shortcuts keep attaching to it.

Closing the section where it began may give the correction its best chance of being remembered accurately under pressure. Labelling theory and interactionism are frequently grouped with Marxist criminology in revision material, and the grouping is wrong. Shared concern with definition explains the temptation behind the grouping without excusing it. Leading figures in the labelling tradition were not Marxists, their framework was interactionist rather than structural, and the neo Marxist position articulated in 1973 was explicitly an attempt to move beyond them toward a fuller social theory grounded in political economy. Adjacent fields borrowed Marxist concepts widely, other misreadings about unity, romanticisation, media effects and victims collapse once the currents inside the tradition are distinguished, and comparison with functionalism shows what a genuinely structural rival looks like. Stating the distinction earns marks. Blurring it loses them. That asymmetry alone should settle how a careful candidate writes.

A final classroom illustration may fix the distinction more firmly than any definition. Imagine two researchers observing the same young person being stopped, searched and sent on with a warning that a record has been made. One researcher follows the young person home, interviews friends and family, reconstructs how the encounter alters self image and watches whether a deviant identity consolidates over following months. That researcher is doing interactionist work, whatever personal politics accompany the project. Another researcher leaves the scene to examine why patrols concentrate on that estate, how the power to stop was legislated, which harms elsewhere in the city attract no patrols at all and how the resulting pattern maps onto class and property. That researcher is doing structural work in the Marxist lineage, whatever methods the project employs. Same street, same encounter, divergent questions, divergent literatures, divergent conclusions. Classification follows the question and the explanatory machinery built to answer it. Nothing about sharing a street corner makes the two projects one theory, and nothing about separating them prevents a thoughtful scholar from reading both with profit.

Revision routines that respect the boundary tend to share one habit. Notes keep traditions in separate columns, with founders, core concepts, methods and criticisms entered under the correct heading before any comparison begins. Comparison then becomes a deliberate second step rather than an accident of filing, and examination answers built from such notes inherit their clarity.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination on this topic is won or lost on a single discrimination: whether the script in front of the examiner treats Marxist criminology as one doctrine or as four positions arguing with each other. Build every answer on the four positions grid. Open with the traditional claim about law and property, widen through the fully social theory, turn to hegemony and the moral panic, and then deliver the internal correction, because a conclusion that arrives through left realism shows the examiner a candidate who can evaluate from inside a tradition rather than reciting criticisms from outside it. The correction is the highest-value move available on this topic, and it costs nothing to deploy once its shape is learned: earlier accounts romanticised the offender, the principal victims of working-class crime are working-class people, and a politics unable to address victimisation cedes the ground. Candidates preparing the wider sociology paper should work alongside this series' guide to [Marxist sociology exam answers](/2021/08/15/marxist-sociology-exam-answers/), which sets out how evaluation points of this kind are distributed through an answer rather than saved for a closing paragraph.

Three separations protect marks that weaker scripts surrender. Separate labelling theory from Marxist criminology every time the two appear near each other, and state the grounds rather than merely asserting the difference: interactionist framework, structural question, leading figures who were not Marxists. Separate explaining crime from romanticising the offender, using the directional test of which party's experience a passage invites its audience to share. Separate the pattern of enforcement, which the framework predicts impressively, from the explanation of individual offending, which remains its weakest component, and concede the weakness before the examiner has to find it. A VaultBook card built on the four positions, one row per position with its addition and its blindness, converts the whole topic into a drillable object, and the Marxism study notes tool at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is designed for exactly that conversion. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The seminar this material wants is a trial in which the tradition prosecutes itself. Set the traditional position first and let it make its strongest case, the vagrancy demonstration and the selective enforcement pattern, until the room feels its pull. Then introduce the victimisation evidence and watch the case develop its crack, and only then hand the prosecution to left realism, whose authors shared the defendants' premises and therefore cannot be waved away as hostile witnesses. Students remember the session because the reversal arrives from an unexpected direction, and they learn the deeper lesson the series keeps teaching across its topics: that the sharpest criticism of a living tradition is usually an internal affair, and that recognising an internal correction when one appears is a transferable scholarly skill. The square of crime makes an excellent whiteboard exercise in the second half of the session. Take any offence the class proposes, populate the four corners in turn, and require the room to notice how the analysis changes each time a neglected corner is filled, particularly the public, whose fear and reporting habits students habitually omit.

Two teaching traps deserve advance planning. The first is the student who arrives with labelling theory filed under Marxism, a filing the textbooks themselves encourage by shelving the topics together. Correct it early and kindly, with the three separations, because the error compounds: a candidate who thinks Becker was a Marxist will misread the nineteen seventy-three programme, whose whole purpose was to move beyond the position it is then confused with. The second trap is tonal. Crime teaching attracts both the student who wants every offender excused by structure and the student who wants every structural claim dismissed as excuse-making, and the material's gift is that it disappoints both in instructive ways. Left realism gives the first student a left authority for taking victims seriously, and the selective enforcement evidence gives the second a pattern that pure individualism cannot explain. Teachers who stage that double disappointment deliberately will find the discussion afterwards unusually honest, since nobody in the room has been allowed to keep the comfortable version of their prior view.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier this article leaves open sits where the social harm programme meets the enforcement question. Zemiology has established its provocation, that the legal category misdescribes the distribution of serious injury, but the programme's constructive half remains contested ground: harm requires a metric, metrics require judgements about severity and preventability, and those judgements reintroduce through the back door the politics the category of crime was accused of smuggling through the front. Researchers working this seam will find the verdict defeater a useful discipline. The selective enforcement claim stands or falls on whether enforcement intensity can be shown to track harm rather than the class position of offenders, and designing studies that could in principle return either answer is harder, and more valuable, than accumulating further illustrations of the expected result. The older literature rewards archival return as well. The vagrancy demonstration of nineteen sixty-four modelled a method, reading legal change against the labour requirements of the powerful, that transfers to any field where regulation presents itself as neutral housekeeping, and the method's limits are as instructive as its successes.

For historians, the four positions double as a period document of the discipline's own development, each row registering the pressure that produced it: postwar confidence in structural explanation, the nineteen seventies encounter between Marxism and the sociology of deviance, the Gramscian turn toward consent, and the reckoning with victimisation that followed. The enforcement apparatus behind those debates belongs to the wider account of the state given in this series' [Marxist theory of the state guide](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/), and reading the two together shows how criminology borrowed its state theory and then, in the left realist years, lent some hard experience back. Journalists will find the moral panic analysis the most portable instrument in the article, provided it is used with its original discipline: the claim is never that alarm is fabricated from nothing, but that alarm is selected, amplified and converted into consent for arrangements that outlast the episode. Applied that way, with the hegemony frame kept in view and the media effects misreading avoided, the analysis remains the sharpest available tool for covering a crime scare without becoming part of its machinery.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Marxist criminology?

Marxist criminology is a family of positions that explains offending, and the response offending provokes, through the social relations of capitalism. Its shared starting point holds that how crime gets defined is itself a product of power, since law is made within a society divided by ownership, and that enforcement falls unevenly across that division. Beyond the starting point the family quarrels. The traditional position stresses law made for property and enforcement aimed downward. The neo-Marxist position joins the act to the reaction against it. The hegemony analysis reads crime control as consent manufacture. Left realism, the internal correction, insists that victimisation is real and concentrated among the poor. A candidate who treats the family as one doctrine has missed the examinable content, which lives in the quarrels.

### Q: What is left realism in criminology?

Left realism is the internal correction that Marxist criminology delivered to itself, emerging in the nineteen eighties from writers who shared the tradition's premises and rejected its evasions. It holds that crime is a real problem rather than a label imposed on harmless conduct, that those harmed most by working-class offending are themselves working-class, and that earlier accounts romanticised offenders by treating predation as proto-politics. Its device, the square of crime, requires any offence to be analysed through four parties held in relation: whoever offended, whoever suffered, the agencies of the state, and the surrounding public. Politically it argues that a left unable to speak to victimisation abandons frightened communities to whoever promises order, so taking crime seriously is a condition of the tradition's credibility rather than a betrayal of it.

### Q: What is the fully social theory of deviance?

The fully social theory of deviance is the programme set out in The New Criminology, published in nineteen seventy-three by Taylor, Walton and Young. It demands that an adequate account join seven elements in a single frame: the broad structural roots of an act, the immediate setting that precipitates it, the act itself, where the reaction against it begins, the deeper sources of that reaction, what the reaction then does to the person's later conduct, and the nature of the entire sequence taken together. Two commitments distinguish it from older determinism. Deviance involves meaningful choice rather than mechanical compulsion, and the act may carry a proto-political content that a purely pathological reading erases. The programme was built to move beyond labelling theory while absorbing its insight about reaction.

### Q: What is the moral panic analysis of crime?

The moral panic analysis, in its Marxist form, reads an episode of public alarm about a category of offending as political work rather than as a simple response to danger. Its canonical statement is Policing the Crisis, published in nineteen seventy-eight by Stuart Hall and his collaborators, which examined alarm about street robbery. The argument holds that the panic displaced a wider crisis of hegemony, a faltering of consent for the existing order, onto a figure who could be policed, and that the alarm thereby manufactured consent for a more authoritarian mode of crime control. The analysis is therefore about hegemony and the state, not about media exaggeration as an end in itself, and mistaking it for a media effects claim is the standard error the examiners wait for.

### Q: What is the square of crime?

The square of crime is left realism's analytical device for refusing partial accounts of any offence. It places four elements in relation: the person who offends, the person harmed, the state acting through its policing and judicial agencies, and the public at large, whose definitions and reactions shape what gets reported and pursued. Change any corner and the offence itself changes character, because an act unreported is socially a different event from the same act prosecuted, and a public demanding severity alters what the state supplies. The square's discipline is completeness. Accounts built on the offender alone romanticise, accounts built on the state alone conspiracise, and accounts built on the victim alone moralise. Holding all four corners at once is what makes the resulting analysis realist rather than rhetorical.

### Q: Why is corporate crime under-enforced?

Marxist criminology answers at three levels. At the level of law-making, the conduct of enterprises is regulated through frameworks designed with the regulated parties' participation, so much damaging behaviour is defined as infraction, non-compliance or misfortune rather than as crime at all. At the level of enforcement, agencies charged with oversight are resourced and oriented toward negotiation, while agencies policing street offending are resourced for detection and prosecution, so the intensity of scrutiny tracks the class position of the likely offender rather than the scale of harm. At the level of ideology, the corporate offender does not match the cultural image of the criminal, which lowers public demand for severity. The verdict defeater applies here: a demonstration that enforcement intensity follows the scale of injury, wherever it occurs, instead of following social position would defeat the claim, and the tradition's own social harm literature keeps that test in view.

### Q: What is the social harm approach?

The social harm approach, also called zemiology, proposes that the study of crime be rebuilt as the study of harm. Its reasoning begins from a mismatch: the legal category of crime captures a selection of damaging acts shaped by the interests that wrote the law, while many of the gravest injuries people suffer, through unsafe working conditions, environmental damage, impoverishment and financial misconduct, sit outside the category entirely or attract only regulatory attention. A discipline organised around crime therefore studies what power selected, not what people endure. Writers such as Paddy Hillyard and Steve Tombs argue for harm as the organising concept, graded by severity and preventability rather than by legal status. Critics reply that harm is a contested and elastic category, and the exchange between the two camps is where the approach earns its assessment value.

### Q: Does Marxism explain crime against the working class?

Honestly graded, only in part, and the tradition's own left realist current is the source of that grading. The traditional position explains the conditions surrounding such crime well: scarcity, competition for insecure work and housing, and acquisitive values pressed on people denied legitimate routes to them. What it explains badly is the direction of the harm, since the victim is usually a neighbour in the same position rather than the owner whose interests the framework places at the centre. Left realism rebuilt the account around that admission, treating intra-class victimisation as the central fact any credible theory must face rather than as an embarrassment at its edge. So the strongest available answer distinguishes the positions: the framework explains the production of offending conditions better than the distribution of its victims, and saying so with the internal correction named demonstrates command rather than weakness.

### Q: Is labelling theory Marxist?

No, and the correction is worth marks because revision material so often groups them together. Labelling theory belongs to the interactionist tradition associated with Howard Becker and Edwin Lemert. Its question is how acts and people come to be defined as deviant, and its explanatory machinery works at the level of interaction, audience and identity, where a label applied can amplify the very conduct it names. Marxist criminology asks a structural question instead: whose interests the definitions serve, and how law-making and enforcement distribute their burdens by class. The two traditions share an interest in definition, which is why they are confused, but sharing a topic is not sharing a theory. The neo-Marxist programme of nineteen seventy-three was written explicitly to move beyond labelling, absorbing its account of reaction into a structural frame it lacked.

### Q: What are the criticisms of Marxist criminology?

Four criticisms carry the assessment weight. First, the traditional position struggles with the direction of victimisation, since most working-class offending harms working-class victims, a difficulty the internal correction pressed from inside the tradition. Second, it struggles with offending in societies where capitalist property was abolished, where the framework's engine is absent yet the behaviour persists. Third, cross-societal variation embarrasses simple versions, because places with comparable inequality display markedly different offending patterns, suggesting that inequality names a background condition rather than a sufficient cause. Fourth, the romanticising charge: treating offending as proto-political ignores the people it injures, which left realism stated more sharply than any external critic. The balanced verdict the evidence supports is asymmetrical. The framework predicts the pattern of criminalisation and enforcement more successfully than it explains why a given individual offends, and strong answers say exactly that.

### Q: How does the internal correction grade the earlier Marxist accounts of crime?

The internal correction grades them as half a theory. On its reading, the traditional position understood law-making and enforcement with real precision and then squandered that achievement by refusing to look at victimisation, treating recorded crime as a construct of policing and the offender as a rebel in embryo. The neo-Marxist programme earns a better mark for joining act and reaction, yet still fails on the same point, since its sympathy flows toward the deviant and away from the deviant's neighbours. Left realism presents itself not as a rejection of the inheritance but as its completion under pressure of evidence: keep the structural account of where offending conditions come from, discard the romance, and add the victim, the state and the public through the square of crime. That self-grading, delivered by insiders against their own side, is what makes the correction the strongest evaluative instrument on the topic.

### Q: What should a student separate between romanticising offenders and explaining crime?

The separation is between an explanation and an alibi. Explaining crime locates its conditions: the scarcity, competition, humiliation and blocked opportunity that make offending intelligible as a response to a situation, and explanation of that kind is the ordinary business of social science, available for any conduct whatever. Romanticising begins where explanation quietly changes sides, recasting the offender as a resister, the offence as a statement, and the victim as an inconvenience to the interpretation. The test a student can apply to any passage is directional. Ask whose experience the passage asks the reader to inhabit. If the burgled household, the assaulted neighbour and the intimidated witness never come into focus while the offender's motives glow with significance, the passage has crossed from analysis into romance, and naming that crossing, with left realism as the authority for it, is a distinction examiners reward.

### Q: Why did left realism break with the tradition it came from?

Because the tradition's evasions had become politically and intellectually untenable for writers who still shared its premises. Intellectually, the evidence of victimisation surveys made the constructivist shrug impossible: working-class communities were suffering real and concentrated harm, and a theory that could only discuss the state's response to crime had nothing to say to the people living with it. Politically, the vacuum was being filled. Where the left offered scepticism about the figures, its opponents offered protection, and communities under pressure chose protection. Jock Young, John Lea and their collaborators concluded that the break had to come from inside, since an external attack could be dismissed as hostility while an internal correction had to be answered. The break was therefore an act of loyalty performed as criticism, an insistence that the tradition's structural insights deserved a version of the theory that could survive contact with a victim's testimony.

### Q: How does the four positions grid order the criminologies?

The four positions grid orders the field as an argument unfolding across four rows. The traditional position opens by asking who makes law and whom enforcement strikes, and it answers with property and class. The neo-Marxist row widens the frame, joining the act to the reaction through the fully social theory and restoring choice to the deviant. The hegemony row turns to consent, reading moral panic as the mechanism by which a crisis of authority converts alarm about crime into permission for coercion. The left realist row closes the grid by turning the tradition's weapons inward, demanding the victim be counted and the offender de-romanticised. Read down the columns and the grid also grades: each row adds what its predecessor lacked and inherits a blindness the next row names, which is why the grid works as an essay plan as readily as a revision aid.

### Q: What separates labelling theory from Marxist criminology?

Three separations, each independently sufficient. The first is the level of explanation: labelling theory works at the level of interaction, where audiences define acts and identities form around definitions, while Marxist criminology works at the level of structure, where ownership and class interest shape which definitions become law. The second is the question asked: labelling asks how deviance is constructed, Marxism asks in whose interest the construction proceeds and why enforcement distributes itself as it does. The third is intellectual allegiance: Becker and Lemert wrote within symbolic interactionism and were not Marxists, a biographical fact that matters because the traditions' remedies differ, one favouring tolerance and decriminalisation of labels, the other a transformation of the relations producing both crime and its control. A student who can state all three separations has converted a common confusion into marks.
