Leon Trotsky presents biography with a problem that few political lives pose so sharply. The same man wrote one of the great histories of revolution in any language and commanded an army through a civil war of extraordinary brutality. He produced the most penetrating Marxist analysis of fascism while Hitler was still dismissible as a clown, and he had earlier proposed turning the trade unions into departments of the state and subjecting labour to military discipline. He was the most gifted polemicist of his generation, the organiser of the October insurrection, the founder of the Red Army, the leader of the first organised opposition to the bureaucracy that consumed the revolution, and a deportee murdered in a Mexico City suburb with an ice axe. Any honest profile must hold the contribution and the record in a single frame, because separating them is how both the worship and the demonology begin.

![Leon Trotsky, an intellectual biography, the Red Army, exile and the writings of opposition - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-108.webp)

The discipline this profile applies has a name, and naming it early makes the whole argument checkable. The counterfactual constraint holds that judgements about what Trotsky would have done in power must be disciplined by evidence about what he actually did when he held power, between 1918 and 1921 above all. Every assessment of the man eventually arrives at the same fork. One road treats his exile writings as the programme of a democratic alternative strangled by Stalin, and reads his defeat as the revolution's lost chance. The other treats the exile as the exile he had earned, and reads the later terror as the unfolding of methods he himself had pioneered and defended. Both roads, travelled without the constraint, degenerate into speculation wearing the clothes of history. The constraint does not settle the question of what his leadership would have produced. It settles what kind of evidence a serious answer is allowed to use, and it disqualifies the two cheapest answers at once: the saint who would have spared everyone, and the monster whose later victims can be debited to his account without remainder.

So the profile that follows is built in two registers that are never allowed to cancel each other out. The first register is the work: the theory of permanent revolution and of uneven and combined development, the analysis of the Soviet state set out in The Revolution Betrayed, the writings on fascism of 1930 to 1933, and a body of literary and historical writing that stands on its own merits. The second register is the record: the Red Army's creation and its discipline, including hostages taken from officers' families and executions for desertion; political responsibility for the suppression of the Kronstadt rising, defended by him for the remaining nineteen years of his life; the advocacy of labour militarisation in 1920 and 1921, on which he stood to the left of Lenin in the direction of compulsion; and his support for the 1921 ban on party factions, the instrument later used to expel him. Neither register is the truth about the other. The theory does not launder the record, and the record does not refute the theory. A reader who finishes this profile should be able to state four contributions with precision and four hard facts with equal precision, and should understand why the tension between the lists is not a defect of the telling but the substance of the life.

## A prosperous farm, a bookish boy

He was born Lev Davidovich Bronstein on 7 November 1879, by the Western calendar, at Yanovka, a village estate in the Kherson province of Ukraine, in the steppe country north of Odessa. The date carried a charge he enjoyed in later life: he shared a birthday, thirty eight years apart, with the October Revolution itself. His father, David Bronstein, was a prosperous Jewish farmer in a region where Jewish agricultural colonies were an established feature of the landscape, a man of formidable energy who had risen from modest beginnings to own land, employ labour, and bargain hard in the grain trade. His mother, Anna, came from a somewhat more settled and literate background. The household was not observant in any strict sense, and the boy's formation owed less to religious tradition than to the farm itself: to harvests, hired hands, machinery, debt, weather, and the sight of a father whose authority rested on competence and relentless work.

That origin matters for the biography because it refuses two simplifications at once. Trotsky was not formed by the shtetl poverty that shaped many Russian Jewish revolutionaries, and he was not formed by the metropolitan intelligentsia either. He was a proprietor's son who watched wage labour from the employer's side of the yard before he ever read a page of Marx, and some of his later writing returns to Yanovka with an ambivalence that is worth noticing: affection for the father's energy, and a clear eyed registration of the exploitation built into the farm's prosperity. When, decades later, he wrote about the peasantry and about the psychology of accumulation in backward economies, he was drawing on a childhood spent inside a working enterprise at the edge of the world market, where a modern threshing machine and a near feudal labour regime occupied the same yard. The theorist of combined development had seen combination at home before he had a word for it.

Schooling took him to Odessa, where he lived with relatives and attended the St Paul's Realschule, a German Lutheran foundation school with a strong mathematical curriculum. He was, by every account including his own, a brilliant and combative pupil, first in his class, hungry for print, and already practised in the arts of sarcasm that would become his signature. The final school year he spent at Nikolayev, living in the household of the Shvigovsky family, whose home functioned as a small intellectual clearing house. There, in a garden circle of young readers, the nineteen year old encountered socialist literature and the gardener and publicist Franz Shvigovsky's blend of populist and Marxist ideas. The circle debated, read illegal pamphlets, and argued about the fate of the peasantry and the character of Russian capitalism. It was the standard apprenticeship of the Russian revolutionary intelligentsia, undergone at unusual speed and with unusual gifts.

The apprenticeship turned practical quickly. In Nikolayev the circle became an organisation, the South Russian Workers' Union, which printed leaflets, agitated among dockers and factory hands, and drew the attention of the police within months. In January 1898 Bronstein was arrested, at eighteen, along with the union's other leading members. He spent the better part of two years in prisons in Nikolayev, Kherson and Odessa, and then in Moscow, awaiting sentence and then assignment. Prison, in the old Russian pattern, became his university: he read voraciously, argued theology with fellow prisoners, studied languages, and wrote. He also married, inside the carceral world and against his parents' wishes, Alexandra Sokolovskaya, an older fellow revolutionary from the Nikolayev circle, who was by several accounts the more formed Marxist of the two at that stage and an important influence on his turn from populism toward Marxism. Two daughters were born to the marriage in exile, Zinaida and Nina, whose fates belong to the saddest pages of the later story.

The sentence was four years of Siberian exile, served first at Ust-Kut and then at Verkholensk, on the Lena river. Siberia gave him his trade. Writing under the pen name Antid Oto, he contributed essays and literary notes to a liberal newspaper in Irkutsk, the Eastern Review, and discovered that he could earn money and reputation with a pen. The exile essays already show the manner: wide reading, theatrical confidence, a taste for the biographical portrait and the devastating summary. They also show the matter taking shape, since the young exile was arguing, against the legal Marxists and the populists alike, about the direction of Russian development and the role of the working class within it. When news reached Siberia of the ferment in European social democracy and of the preparations for a congress that might unite the Russian factions, Bronstein decided not to serve out his term. In the summer of 1902 he escaped, travelling with a false passport into which a name had to be entered in haste.

### Why did the young Bronstein take a jailer's name?

Escaping Siberian exile in 1902, Bronstein needed a false passport and filled the blank with the name of a jailer from his Odessa imprisonment, chosen, by his own account, without reflection. The name on the forged document became the name of the revolutionary, and Bronstein remained the private name behind it.

The story is told in his autobiography with disarming lightness, and it has been retold so often that its oddity gets lost. A revolutionary's pseudonym in that world was usually a shield or a banner, something chosen for obscurity or for meaning. Trotsky took his from the petty machinery of the prison that had held him, and then kept it for thirty eight years until it had eclipsed the name he was born with on every continent. There is a biographical lesson in the accident. The man was made, again and again across his life, by improvisations under pressure that hardened into permanent structures: a name picked in a hurry, an army improvised from railway carriages, a theory assembled from a failed revolution, an international founded in a garden in Mexico with a handful of followers. The pattern of the life is there in miniature in the passport blank.

He travelled through Samara, where the party's organisers received him, and then out of Russia to Zurich and London. In London, in October 1902, he arrived at Lenin's door in the early morning and was taken in, literally and politically, by Lenin and Krupskaya. The twenty three year old exile with a Siberian reputation and a sheaf of essays became, within weeks, a lecturer touring the emigre colonies and a contributor to Iskra, the paper Lenin coedited with Plekhanov, Martov and others. Lenin, impressed, pressed for the young man's elevation to the editorial board, against Plekhanov's resistance. The sponsorship matters because of what followed within the year: no relationship in Trotsky's life reversed itself more completely, or with longer consequences, than the one that began with Lenin walking him around London and explaining the class meaning of its monuments.

## The founding congress and the years between parties

The second congress of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party met in Brussels and then London in the summer of 1903, and it split the movement along lines that hardened into Bolshevism and Menshevism. The famous votes concerned the definition of party membership, where Lenin's narrower formulation lost to Martov's broader one, and control of the Iskra editorial board, where Lenin's push for a smaller board prevailed and Martov refused to serve. Underneath the procedural quarrels lay a genuine question about what a revolutionary party in an autocracy should be: a disciplined organisation of professional revolutionaries, or a broader movement with looser boundaries. The twenty three year old Trotsky sided with Martov. He spoke against Lenin's organisational proposals with a vehemence that embarrassed some of his own allies, and he carried the quarrel into print.

### What happened at the congress that split the movement?

At the London congress of 1903 the Russian social democrats split over membership rules and committee control, and the young Trotsky sided against Lenin, whose centralism he attacked in print the following year with a warning that substitution of the organisation for the class would end with a single dictator.

The attack in print became one of the most quoted documents of his early career, and one of the most awkward for his later followers. In the 1904 pamphlet Our Political Tasks, written at twenty four, he warned that Lenin's methods led logically to a sequence of substitutions: the party organisation substituting itself for the party, the central committee for the organisation, and finally a single dictator for the central committee. Defenders of Lenin have quoted the passage against Trotsky ever since 1917, when he joined the party he had described in those terms, and Trotskyists have had to answer the quotation for a century. The honest biographical treatment holds two things together. The warning was genuinely prophetic about a mechanism of bureaucratic substitution, and its author, when his turn came to hold power, did not organise his conduct as though the warning bound him. That tension, between the analyst who saw the danger early and the commissar who reached for the instruments anyway, is the counterfactual constraint operating inside a single biography, decades before the exile gave it a name.

For fourteen years after 1903, Trotsky belonged to neither faction in any settled way. He broke with the Mensheviks over their willingness to ally with liberalism, quarrelled with Lenin continuously, attempted repeatedly to broker unity between the factions, and occupied a position that both camps treated with suspicion: brilliant, unattached, and unbearable in polemic. The years were not wasted. He edited papers in Vienna and elsewhere, reported wars, sat in the Reichsrat's press gallery, and developed the habits of a political journalist of the first rank, writing at speed on everything from Balkan battlefields to French syndicalism to the theatre. But the institutional homelessness mattered. When he later stood accused of having been a Menshevik, the charge drew on real facts: he had sided with Martov in 1903, spent years closer to the Menshevik orbit than to Lenin's, and joined the Bolsheviks only in the summer of 1917. It also simplified the record, since his actual position for most of that period was an independent one, hostile to both factions' orthodoxies and defined increasingly by the theory he had built out of the 1905 revolution. The FAQ below takes the Menshevik question directly, because the misreading that he was a lifelong Bolshevik is as common as the counter misreading that he was a Menshevik who infiltrated Lenin's party at the last moment.

The Vienna years deserve a paragraph of their own, because they formed the stylist. From 1907 to 1914, with interruptions, he lived in Vienna, edited the Russian paper Pravda, which is not to be confused with Lenin's later paper of the same name, and moved in the extraordinary intellectual atmosphere of the late Habsburg capital. He reported the Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913 as a correspondent, and the war reporting is worth a biographer's attention: it is observant, humane, unsparing about atrocity, and written with the same rolling periodic sentences he would later turn on the Russian civil war from the other side of the gun. The witness of 1912, who described what armies do to villages with a reporter's cold clarity, became the commander of 1919 who ordered some of the same logic applied. Biography does not have to resolve that contrast into hypocrisy or into tragedy. It has to record it, because the reader who holds both images at once understands the man better than the reader who keeps only one.

## The first soviet: chairman at twenty six

The revolution of 1905 returned him to Russia and gave him his first taste of mass politics. He arrived in Kiev and then St Petersburg in the autumn, as the strike wave that followed Bloody Sunday built toward the October general strike. The St Petersburg soviet, a council of workers' deputies thrown up by the strike, became the revolution's most original institution, and Trotsky became first its most effective speaker and then, after the arrest of its chairman Khrustalev-Nosar, its presiding figure. He was twenty six. The soviet lasted fifty days, published a newspaper, organised militias, negotiated with a government that alternately conceded and threatened, and was suppressed in December when the regime recovered its nerve and arrested the deputies in session. The experience was brief, and it was decisive for everything he thought afterwards. He had seen a workers' government in embryo, had watched it improvise finance, food supply, armed defence and a free press, and had learned at first hand both the creative power of such a body and the speed with which a state that keeps its army loyal can crush it.

Arrested with the soviet's executive, he used the trial of 1906 as a rostrum, defending the soviet's record in a speech that circulated widely, and was sentenced to exile in Siberia for life. The second Siberian journey ended as the first had: he escaped, this time from the transit point at Berezov in the winter of 1907, riding hundreds of miles by reindeer sledge through the taiga with a peasant driver, in an episode he later retold with a novelist's relish. Two escapes from Siberia before the age of twenty eight is a biographical datum that supporters and detractors have both used, and both uses miss the point. The escapes show nerve and luck. What matters for the intellectual biography is what the interval between them produced, because the theory that made his name was written in the aftermath of 1905, out of an attempt to explain why a revolution in a backward country had thrown up the most advanced political forms in Europe.

The explanation was built partly in collaboration. Alexander Parvus, the Russian German social democrat who had befriended him in emigration, had argued in 1904 and 1905 that a Russian revolution would bring the working class to power because the bourgeoisie was too weak and too frightened to complete its own revolution. Trotsky took the argument, systematised it, and gave it a historical architecture in Results and Prospects, written in 1906 while he awaited trial and exile. The pamphlet's claims can be stated plainly. In a backward country entering the revolutionary crisis late, the liberal bourgeoisie cannot lead a democratic revolution to completion, since it fears the workers more than it hates the autocracy. The peasantry, the vast majority, can support a revolution but cannot lead one independently. The young working class, concentrated in modern factories by foreign capital, is therefore driven beyond the democratic programme toward socialist measures once it holds power, and its revolution can survive only if it spreads to advanced countries whose workers come to its aid. Hence the name: the revolution, once begun, must be permanent, uninterrupted, passing from democratic to socialist tasks and from the national to the international arena, or it will perish.

The full exposition of permanent revolution belongs to its own article in this series, [permanent revolution explained](/2014/09/15/permanent-revolution-explained/), and readers who want the doctrine's texts, objections and afterlife should work there. For the biography, three things count. The theory was early: it was substantially complete by 1906, when its author was twenty six, and it never fundamentally changed, which makes Trotsky almost unique among major Marxists in having written his essential book before he was thirty and spent the rest of his life applying it. The theory was also a weapon in the factional wars, since it implicitly condemned both the Menshevik strategy of alliance with liberals and the Bolshevik formula of a democratic dictatorship of workers and peasants, and it armed its author with a reason to stand apart from both. And the theory was, in 1917, spectacularly vindicated in its central prediction about the sequence of the Russian revolution, a vindication its author never let anyone forget. When Lenin, in April 1917, adopted a course that converged with the permanent revolution analysis, the fourteen year quarrel between the two men lost its practical basis, and the road to their alliance opened.

Underneath the theory of permanent revolution sits a second idea, developed more slowly and stated most fully in the opening chapter of his History of the Russian Revolution: the law of uneven and combined development. Its claim is that backward countries do not repeat the stages of the advanced ones in order. They combine, under the whip of external necessity, the most archaic social forms with the most modern: the wooden plough and the giant Putilov works, the peasant commune and the joint stock bank, in a single social formation. That combination explains the peculiar intensity of Russian politics, a tiny working class of immense concentration and leverage confronting a state of immense coercive weight. The idea's exposition belongs with [uneven and combined development](/2014/10/01/uneven-and-combined-development/), but its biographical root is worth registering here, because the theory reads like a generalisation of Yanovka: the modern machine and the archaic labour regime in one yard, which the farmer's son had watched before the exile had read the economists.

## War, expulsion and the long road back

The outbreak of war in 1914 found him in Vienna, from which, as a Russian citizen, he fled to Switzerland ahead of arrest. In Zurich and then Paris he threw himself into the antiwar minority of European socialism, the current that gathered at Zimmerwald in 1915 around the demand to turn the imperialist war into civil war. He edited the Paris Russian paper Nashe Slovo, wrote a sustained attack on the collapse of the Second International, and was expelled from France in 1916 for antiwar agitation, deported across the Spanish border, arrested in Madrid, and shipped from Cadiz to New York. The American interlude lasted ten weeks. He wrote for the Russian emigre paper Novy Mir in New York, lectured, argued with American socialists, and watched a great capitalist republic at close quarters, an experience that fed his lifelong conviction that the United States would decide the fate of the century. When news of the February revolution reached New York, he sailed for Russia at once. British authorities pulled him off the ship at Halifax and interned him for a month in a camp at Amherst, where he organised the German prisoners, lectured them on revolution, and left, on release, with the camp commandant's complaints and several hundred converts as his trophies.

He reached Petrograd in May 1917. The city he entered was governed by the dual power of the Provisional Government and the soviets, and the political question of the hour was whether the revolution would stop at a bourgeois republic or move toward soviet power. Trotsky's answer had been printed in 1906. He aligned himself with the Mezhraiontsy, the interdistrict organisation of social democrats who stood between the factions and favoured unity on a revolutionary programme, and in the summer, as the July Days crisis passed and the Bolsheviks adopted the course he had long urged, the Mezhraiontsy fused with Lenin's party and Trotsky entered its central committee. The fourteen years between parties were over. He was thirty seven, and within five months he would hold more real power than any socialist intellectual of his century, before or since.

The fusion is sometimes told as a conversion, and the telling matters for the later polemics. Trotsky himself, in exile, treated 1917 as the year Lenin came to his position on the revolution's dynamics while he came to Lenin's position on the party, and historians sympathetic to him have largely followed that account. Historians hostile to him have treated the fusion as an opportunist's late arrival, noting that he joined the winning side at the moment of its ascent. The documented record supports a cooler judgement. The convergence on the revolution's course was real and is visible in the published theses and speeches of both men. The party question was resolved by events rather than by debate: the Bolshevik organisation of 1917, swollen with new members and operating through the soviets, was not the closed apparatus of the 1904 pamphlet, and the soviet chairman of 1905 found in it an instrument his earlier warning did not describe. What the warning did describe, the bureaucratic degeneration of a later decade, arrived by a road neither man foresaw in 1917, and the argument about who should have foreseen it belongs to the counterfactual sections below, not to the summer of the fusion.

## The organiser of October

In September 1917 Trotsky was elected chairman of the Petrograd soviet, the post he had held in the earlier soviet of 1905, and from that chair he presided over the transfer of power. The mechanics are the property of the series article on [october revolution marxist analysis](/2014/05/01/october-revolution-marxist-analysis/), but the biographical core is his. The Bolshevik majority in the soviet created a Military Revolutionary Committee as its defence organ against a threatened counter revolution, and Trotsky, as soviet chairman, directed it. Through the committee, the insurrection was prepared not as a party conspiracy in the night but as an operation conducted in the soviet's name, in daylight, with orders issued to regiments, railwaymen, telegraph operators and the crews of the fleet. When the Provisional Government moved against the soviet press and the bridges, the committee answered with a systematic occupation of the city's nervous system, and by the time the Second Congress of Soviets opened, power in the capital had already changed hands. The Winter Palace fell almost as an afterthought.

### Who actually organised the seizure of power in October?

The Military Revolutionary Committee of the Petrograd soviet, chaired by Trotsky, planned and directed the operation: the seizure of bridges, stations, telegraph and ministries in the soviet's name, timed to the congress of soviets, so that power appeared transferred by a public body rather than seized by a party committee.

His own later account of October, written in exile, placed the achievement correctly when it assigned Lenin the strategic decision and himself the operational execution, though exile also sharpened his insistence on his share against a Soviet historiography that was busy erasing him from the photographs. The erasure is itself evidence in the biography: regimes do not trouble to airbrush nonentities. For a few weeks after October he served as commissar for foreign affairs, issued the new government's appeal for peace, published the secret treaties of the old diplomacy to the embarrassment of every chancellery in Europe, and then led the Soviet delegation at Brest-Litovsk, where he improvised the formula of neither war nor peace and walked out of the negotiations in February 1918 rather than sign the German terms. The formula failed; the Germans advanced; Lenin forced through the harsher treaty that followed. The episode displayed both the brilliance and the theatricality that marked his statecraft, and it ended his diplomatic career. In March 1918 he became people's commissar for military and naval affairs, and the theorist of permanent revolution set about building the instrument on which the revolution's survival depended.

## Trotsky and the making of the Red Army

The Red Army he inherited was a slogan. The old imperial army had dissolved in mutiny and desertion; the volunteer Red Guards and the first Red Army detachments were brave, undisciplined and outnumbered by the enemies gathering on every frontier: White armies under Kolchak, Denikin and Wrangel, foreign intervention forces, nationalist armies, and peasant risings behind the lines. Between the spring of 1918 and the end of 1920, Trotsky built, from that material, a conscript army numbered in the millions, with a general staff, a supply apparatus, military schools, a political administration, and a record of victory over every opponent it faced. Whatever judgement history passes on the use to which the instrument was put, the construction itself ranks among the most remarkable feats of military organisation of the twentieth century, and it was the work of a civilian intellectual who had never held a command.

### How did Trotsky build an army from nothing?

He built it by conscription, by employing tens of thousands of former tsarist officers supervised by political commissars, by restoring ranks, saluting and the death penalty for desertion, and by running the war from an armoured train that carried his headquarters, printing press and tribunal along the fronts.

The methods repay attention because they define the record that the counterfactual constraint will later weigh. He ended the elected commanders and the soldiers' committees of 1917, restoring a single chain of command. He conscripted, first workers and then peasants, in waves that eventually put millions under arms. He employed the old regime's officer corps on a vast scale, the military specialists, binding them to the new army by a system of dual control in which every commander served beside a political commissar who countersigned his orders, and by incentives and threats held in deliberate balance. He restored the death penalty at the front, abolished by the revolution in its first flush, and applied it to desertion and panic, including, in notorious instances, the decimation of broken units. He travelled the fronts in his armoured train, a rolling headquarters complete with radio station, printing press, garage, library and a tribunal, covering immense distances and appearing wherever the line threatened to give, issuing orders, decorating the brave, and ordering the shooting of the cowardly and the treacherous in the same visit. The train became the symbol of the war commissariat: improvisation, mobility, propaganda and coercion in a single vehicle.

The use of former tsarist officers was the decision that drew the fiercest opposition inside his own camp, and it is instructive. A military opposition within the party argued for partisan methods, elected officers and a militia system, and against the regular army's hierarchy as a betrayal of the revolution's democratic promise. Trotsky fought that opposition at the Eighth Party Congress in 1919 and won, with Lenin's support, on frankly instrumental grounds: wars are won by armies, armies require expertise, and expertise wore the old uniform. The episode is a fixed point for the biography because it shows his hierarchy of values under pressure. Given a choice between the revolution's survival as he understood it and the democratic forms the revolution had proclaimed, he chose survival, and he said so in print, in a defence of terror and military discipline that made no apology for the choice. Readers tempted to imagine the commissar of 1919 as a reluctant authoritarian, privately liberal and publicly compelled, will find no support in his own words of those years. He theorised the compulsion as he practised it.

His personal conduct of the war also included episodes that his defenders cite and his critics answer, and the profile should hold both. At Sviyazhsk in August 1918, with the eastern front collapsing and Kazan fallen, he stabilised the line by ruthlessness that included shooting retreating commissars and commanders, and then by inspiration that rallied the same troops to retake the city; the Fifth Army's stand there is conventionally treated as the Red Army's birth in fire. At Petrograd in October 1919 he organised the defence against Yudenich's advance and refused the evacuation that some in Moscow favoured, riding the front on a borrowed horse when his car failed, in an episode even hostile historians tend to narrate with respect. The courage was real, and it was personal, and it coexisted, in the same weeks and the same orders, with the machinery of hostage taking and exemplary execution described in the next section. A biography that keeps the horse and drops the tribunal, or keeps the tribunal and drops the horse, is managing its subject rather than describing him.

## The record in the civil war

The civil war record must be stated as it stands in the scholarship, without the softening that sympathetic biography applies and without the inflation that hostile biography prefers. The war of 1918 to 1921 was a conflict of exceptional savagery on all sides, fought across a collapsing empire amid famine, epidemic disease and the breakdown of food supply to the cities. Both the Red and White camps practised terror, hostage taking and massacre, and both left records that their later partisans have mined selectively. Within that war, Trotsky was not a bystander or a commentator. He was the war commissar, the political head of the Red Army, a member of the small leadership that directed the Soviet war effort, and the most articulate public defender of the regime's coercive methods. Responsibility in such a structure is shared, and the sharing does not dilute it. The orders bore his signature; the defences bear his prose style; the accountability is his.

### Was Trotsky responsible for repression too?

Yes. As commissar for war he authorised severe disciplinary measures across a collapsing front, carried political responsibility for the suppression of the Kronstadt rising and defended that suppression for the rest of his life, urged the militarisation of labour, and backed the 1921 ban on party factions, which later served his enemies.

The particulars, stated plainly, are these. The Red Army under his commissariat executed deserters and, in the emergency of 1918, applied collective punishments to units that broke. The families of former tsarist officers serving in the Red Army were registered and held as surety for the officers' loyalty, a system Trotsky defended as a necessity of war against officers who might defect with their knowledge of Red dispositions. The decree structures of the Red Terror, proclaimed after the attempts on Lenin's life and the assassination of Uritsky in 1918, belonged to the government as a whole, and Trotsky supported the terror in principle and in print, most systematically in his 1920 book Terrorism and Communism, written against Karl Kautsky's critique of Bolshevik methods. That book is the essential document of the record, because it removes all ambiguity about whether the coercion was a reluctant improvisation. It argues, on theoretical grounds, that a revolutionary state besieged by enemies must centralise, must requisition, must compel labour and must shoot, and that scruples about these methods are a form of sentimentality the revolution could not afford. A reader who wants to know what Trotsky in power believed about power should start there, not with the exile writings of the following decade.

The Kronstadt rising of March 1921 concentrates the record into a single episode, which is why it has become the test case in every assessment since. The naval base at Kronstadt, whose sailors had been among October's most celebrated fighters, rose against the Soviet government with demands centred on free soviets, an end to grain requisitioning, and the release of socialist political prisoners. The rising came as the civil war's great fronts were closing and the regime was simultaneously confronting peasant revolts and urban hunger. The government treated Kronstadt as a mortal threat and as the product of White and foreign intrigue, a characterisation the scholarship has substantially qualified, since the rebels' programme was a soviet programme and their leadership included many veterans of 1917. The fortress was stormed across the ice by Red Army forces under Tukhachevsky's operational command, with Trotsky, as war commissar, bearing political responsibility for the decision and the ultimatum that preceded it. The rising was crushed with heavy loss of life on both sides, and reprisals followed against captured rebels.

### Why did he defend Kronstadt to the end of his life?

Because conceding Kronstadt meant conceding that the state he served had turned its guns on the sailors who had made October, and he chose instead to argue necessity: a mutiny behind the front, in famine and encirclement, threatened the revolution's survival, and the fortress had to be retaken whatever the cost.

In exile, pressed by critics including former supporters, he returned to Kronstadt repeatedly and never conceded the essential point. His 1938 exchange on the subject argued that the rising, whatever the subjective honesty of the sailors, objectively served the counter revolution at a moment when the regime stood between famine and collapse, and that a government which had surrendered the fortress would have surrendered Petrograd's seaward defence and its own authority with it. The steelman for that defence deserves its full weight. The Soviet state in March 1921 faced the Tambov peasant revolt, strikes in Petrograd, a ruined economy and the real possibility of disintegration, and governments in such straits, of every complexion, have stormed mutinous fortresses. The steelman against it also deserves full weight. The rebels demanded the soviet democracy the regime claimed as its own foundation; the regime answered with artillery across the ice and executions after; and the war commissar who defended the answer had, in 1905, presided over a soviet of exactly the kind the sailors wished to restore. Both steelmanned cases are true to the documents. The counterfactual constraint is what a reader does with the pair of them: any claim that a state led by this man would have handled a comparable challenge with clean hands must survive the fact that, given the challenge in fact, he chose the artillery and defended the choice for nineteen years.

Two disciplines govern the figures here, and the profile follows the series rule for contested episodes. No casualty figures for Kronstadt, for the civil war, or for the terror are asserted in this article, because the ranges in the literature are wide, the counting methods differ, and the numbers have been conscripted by every tradition that cites them. Where scale matters to the argument, it is described qualitatively: the terror was large, the war's suffering was immense, the reprisals at Kronstadt were severe. Readers who need the contested arithmetic, with its sources and its disputes set out properly, will find it in the articles of this series that handle such figures under protocol. What the biography needs from Kronstadt is not a number but a decision, and the decision is documented beyond argument.

## The trade union debate and the ban on factions

If Kronstadt is the record's most dramatic episode, the trade union debate of 1920 and 1921 is its most revealing, because it concerns not the emergency of invasion but the ordinary organisation of labour in peacetime, and because on this question Trotsky stood, for once, to the authoritarian side of Lenin. The debate opened as the civil war wound down and the regime asked what role the trade unions should play in the workers' state. A Workers' Opposition within the party argued for union control of industry. Lenin argued that the unions should remain independent organisations, schools of communism and defenders of the workers against their own state's bureaucratic deformations, in a formula that conceded the state could act against workers' interests and needed checking by them. Trotsky's platform went the other way. He proposed the progressive absorption of the unions into the state economic apparatus, the extension of military methods to labour organisation, and a shake up of the union leadership from above. In the pamphlet literature of the debate he defended the militarisation of labour as a general principle of the transition period: in a ruined country, he argued, labour power had to be mobilised, assigned and disciplined by the state as the army had been, and the worker who refused assignment stood in the same relation to the collective as the deserter.

### What did the trade union debate reveal about Trotsky?

It revealed that on the organisation of labour his instincts in 1920 ran more centralising than Lenin's. He proposed absorbing the unions into the state and extending military discipline to industry, and Lenin, not Trotsky, led the resistance, which complicates every later portrait of a humane alternative waiting in reserve.

The competitive literature on Trotsky passes over this debate in near silence, and the omission is the single most consequential gap in the standard portrait, because the debate is the cleanest evidence about the counterfactual that the whole assessment turns on. A Trotsky who in 1920 proposed to militarise labour and fuse the unions with the state was not, at that date, a democrat manqué awaiting his chance. He was a centraliser of exceptional energy who regarded compulsion as the rational response to scarcity and regarded resistance to compulsion as a failure of revolutionary understanding. Lenin's victory in the debate, carried at the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921, settled the unions question on the more moderate formula, and the same congress adopted the resolution On Party Unity, which banned organised factions within the party and authorised the expulsion of members who maintained them. Trotsky supported the ban. He voted with the majority that created the instrument, in the belief, widely shared in that exhausted leadership, that factional organisation in a besieged one party state would become the vehicle of civil war inside the regime. The instrument survived the emergency. In 1927 it was used to expel him, and in the decades after, to criminalise every opposition in turn. The biographical point is not that he forged his own executioner's axe, though the irony is real. The point is that the ban, like the militarisation proposal, records what he believed a revolutionary state was entitled to do, recorded at the time, in his own words, without the retrospective varnish. The counterfactual constraint is built from exactly such records.

The New Economic Policy, adopted at the same congress, replaced grain requisitioning with a tax in kind and legalised a measure of private trade, and Trotsky, after initial hesitation, administered the turn in his economic posts. His work of the early nineteen twenties was heavily economic: the transport commissariat, the concessions committee, the planning debates in which he pressed for industrialisation faster than the leadership's majority would accept. That programme, developed with the economist Preobrazhensky's analysis behind it, later became the economic spine of the Left Opposition: the claim that the NEP's equilibrium would enrich the private trader and the prosperous peasant at the revolution's expense unless the state taxed the private sector to fund planned industry. The succession struggle that consumed the decade was therefore never only a struggle of persons, though it was savage enough as that. It carried a real argument about the direction of the Soviet economy and about the international prospects of the revolution, and the biography loses its thread if it treats the argument as a mask for ambition alone.

## The contribution and record table

The columns are the discipline. Contributions and episodes are never merged into a balance sheet with a single total, because a table that netted the History of the Russian Revolution against Kronstadt and announced a remainder would be committing exactly the silent trade off this profile refuses. ## The writer: histories, memoirs and literary criticism

Any profile that treats the writing as a byproduct of the politics has the relationship backwards. Trotsky wrote nearly every day of his adult life, in prison, in exile, on campaign, in hiding, and the writing was not a refuge from the action but one of its main instruments and, in the last decade, its surviving form. His literary output includes the three volume History of the Russian Revolution, the autobiography My Life, a life of Lenin left unfinished, the book Literature and Revolution of 1924, histories of the Spanish and Chinese revolutions in essay form, thousands of articles, and a correspondence that fills archives on several continents. The History is the summit. Written on the island of Prinkipo in Turkey in 1930, in the first bitterness of deportation, it narrates the year 1917 from February to October with a combination of documentary density and narrative drive that professional historians of every camp have admired, including many who reject its politics root and branch. Its opening chapter's statement of uneven and combined development has been quarried by sociologists who never opened the rest. Its portraits, of the Tsar, of Kerensky, of the crowd itself as a historical actor, belong to the literature of revolution as much as to its historiography.

The literary criticism is the most underread part of the corpus, and it does biographical work that the political writings cannot. Literature and Revolution surveyed the young Soviet culture of the early nineteen twenties with a discrimination that surprised readers who expected a commissar's philistinism: he defended the fellow travellers, the writers who were not party members, against the proletarian culture zealots who wanted a literature manufactured to order; he treated Futurism with respect and criticism in equal measure; and he insisted that art must make its own road and that the party's business was not to command it. The critic who wrote those pages complicates the commissar who wrote Terrorism and Communism in a way no amount of editorial steering can remove, and the complication is the man. He contained a genuine aesthetic intelligence and a genuine tolerance for cultural pluralism, alongside a settled conviction that economic and military life under scarcity must be commanded. Readers who find the combination incoherent are measuring him against a consistency he never claimed. His own framework assigned different logics to different spheres with complete self awareness, and the biography should report the assignment rather than correct it.

The style itself is evidence. He wrote a Russian of great power, rolling, ironic, architecturally balanced, which translators have struggled to carry across, and he wrote at a speed that colleagues found unnerving, dictating, correcting proofs on trains, composing whole pamphlets between the sessions of congresses. The polemical manner, the devastating biographical summary, the opponent reduced to a type and then dismantled as a type, was a weapon he used on enemies of every size, from Kautsky to Martov to the unfortunate Bukharin, and it made him enemies in proportion to its brilliance. In exile the style turned inward as well: My Life is a memoir of unusual candour about his childhood and formation, and the diary he kept in 1935, published after his death, shows a private register, anxious, reflective, capable of recording a doubt about his own most settled doctrine, that the public prose rarely admitted. That diary entry, conceding the possibility that the Soviet state might have to be classified as something other than a workers' state if it failed to yield to the world revolution, matters to the strongest objection examined below, because it shows the doubt reaching the doctrine's foundations, in private, at the end.

## The succession struggle and the Left Opposition

Lenin's last active months set the stage on which the succession was fought. Through 1922 and into 1923, Lenin, partly incapacitated by strokes, grew alarmed at the growth of the party's bureaucratic apparatus centred on the secretariat, and at the conduct of the Georgian affair, in which Stalin's subordinates had ridden roughshod over the Georgian party. His dictated notes of the period, the so called testament, recommended Stalin's removal as general secretary on grounds of rudeness and disloyalty, and described Trotsky, in the same breath, as the most capable man in the central committee while noting his excessive self assurance and his non Bolshevik past. Trotsky's conduct in those months has puzzled biographers of every school. He did not force the testament into the open at the Twelfth Congress in 1923, did not build the alliance with Lenin against the apparatus that Lenin's notes seemed to invite, and allowed the triumvirate of Stalin, Zinoviev and Kamenev to consolidate its control of the machine. Explanations range from illness, he was genuinely unwell with a recurrent fever through much of 1923, to scruple about appearing to grasp at power, to a fatal underestimation of how completely the apparatus, and not the debating hall, would decide the outcome. Whatever the weighting, the hesitation was the hinge of his political life. The organiser of October, decisive when the operation was military, proved indecisive when the operation was factional, and he never recovered the initiative.

The Left Opposition he led from the autumn of 1923 was nonetheless a serious formation with a serious programme, and the biography must give the programme before the intrigue. Its platform joined industrialisation funded at the private sector's expense, a turn against the prosperous peasant's growing weight, the revival of soviet and party democracy against the appointive machine, and an international line that treated the Russian revolution's fate as bound to revolutions in Europe. The New Course letters of December 1923 attacked the bureaucratisation of the party in terms that alarmed the triumvirate precisely because they named a real process: the substitution of appointed officials for elected ones, of the secretariat's files for the membership's judgement. The 1904 warning about substitution had returned, twenty years on, as the platform of its own author, now aimed at an apparatus he had served. In 1924 his Lessons of October, a study of the 1917 insurrection that implicitly criticised Zinoviev and Kamenev's conduct in that year, provoked the so called literary discussion, a coordinated press campaign against Trotskyism as an alleged system hostile to Leninism, in which his whole pre 1917 record, the Menshevik years, the 1904 pamphlet, the quarrels with Lenin, was exhumed and systematised into a heresy. He resigned the war commissariat in January 1925 and did not contest the verdict in public. The machine had found its method: redefine the past, and the present follows.

The struggle's second act joined him with his former prosecutors. In 1926 he formed the United Opposition with Zinoviev and Kamenev, an alliance whose members had spent two years denouncing one another, and the bloc's weakness was the sum of its members' discredit. Through 1926 and 1927 the opposition argued, demonstrated, and finally, in November 1927, took its case to the streets of Moscow and Leningrad on the revolution's tenth anniversary, where its banners were torn down and its meetings broken up. The Fifteenth Congress in December expelled him from the party under the machinery of the faction ban he had supported in 1921. In January 1928 he was exiled internally to Alma-Ata, on the Soviet Union's Central Asian edge, from which he conducted a vast correspondence with supporters inside the country until the regime tired of the traffic. In February 1929 he was deported from the Soviet Union altogether, put aboard a ship at Odessa, the city of his schooling, and landed in Turkey as a stateless exile. He was forty nine. Every subsequent event of his life, the great histories, the fascism warnings, the Fourth International, the murder in Mexico, happened inside an exile that lasted eleven years and consumed the remainder.

## Exile: from Alma-Ata to Prinkipo and beyond

### How did exile alter the balance of his work?

Exile stripped him of armies, offices and audiences in the flesh, and the energy that had gone into command went into the page: the great history, the analysis of the bureaucracy, the fascism pamphlets and an enormous correspondence, so that the deportee of 1929 became principally a writer under sentence.

Turkey gave him four years on the island of Prinkipo in the Sea of Marmara, guarded by police, served by a small secretariat of devoted young followers, and supplied with the documents from which the History was built. The household regime of the exile is worth a paragraph because it shaped the work: dictation from early morning, correspondence sorted by his wife Natalia Sedova and his son Lev, who functioned as his political organiser in Europe, visitors vetted, bulletins typed and posted to a scattered movement. From Prinkipo he wrote his autobiography first, at a publisher's suggestion, settling accounts with his own past while the Soviet press settled different ones, and then the History, and through both, a stream of articles on the world crisis that had broken in 1929 and on the German labour movement's approach to catastrophe. The deportee had no army, no state, no paper of his own with any mass circulation, and he had, in compensation, the leisure of the condemned to write for the long term. The History's scale is an exile's scale: no officeholder of 1919 could have spared the months it took.

France admitted him in 1933, on conditions of internal exile and political silence that he observed in form and circumvented in substance, writing for the movement through intermediaries while living under surveillance in a succession of provincial houses. Norway admitted him in 1935 and gave him a brief, decent interval at a social democratic journalist's home near Oslo, where he finished The Revolution Betrayed, before the Soviet Union, then staging the first Moscow Trial, pressed the Norwegian government to silence or surrender him, and a reluctant administration interned him and then shipped him, in December 1936, aboard a tanker to Mexico. The Mexican government of Lazaro Cardenas, to its lasting credit, granted asylum when no European state would keep him. He lived first in the Blue House at Coyoacan, the home of the painters Diego Rivera and Frida Kahlo, and later, after a political rupture with Rivera, in a fortified house nearby on Avenida Viena, with guards, reinforced doors, and a household of secretaries and supporters drawn from several countries. The geography of the exile is itself a map of the century's closing doors: a revolutionary of world fame, homeless by decree, moved from island to village to suburb as each host calculated the cost of sheltering him.

The exile also had an interior history that the political narrative can flatten. Natalia Sedova, his second wife, whom he had met in Paris exile in 1902 and who shared every subsequent exile, managed the household, the archive, and increasingly the morale of a family being destroyed person by person, a destruction the trials section below records factually. His followers were few, poor, infiltrated by Soviet agents at levels only later understood, and divided by the doctrinal quarrels his own intransigence both fed and policed. He worked through all of it with a discipline that visitors described with awe: up before dawn, dictating, annotating the world press in several languages, breeding rabbits and cacti in the Coyoacan garden for the ordinary pleasure of it, and planning, to the last week of his life, as though the Fourth International he had founded in 1938 were the general staff of a revolution scheduled for the season after next. The contrast between the scale of the claim and the scale of the means is the exile's pathos, and every serious biographer has had to decide whether to narrate it as grandeur, as delusion, or, most accurately, as both at once.

## The fascism writings and the united front

Between 1930 and 1933, from Prinkipo, Trotsky wrote a sequence of pamphlets and articles on Germany that stand, by a wide margin of scholarly agreement across traditions, as the most prescient political analysis produced by any Marxist of the period, and among the most prescient by anyone. The situation he addressed was the collapse of the Weimar Republic under the pressure of the world slump, with the Nazi party growing from a fringe movement to the largest party in the Reichstag, and the German labour movement, the strongest in Europe, divided between the social democratic party and the communist party. The Communist International, under the doctrine of the third period, had designated social democracy as social fascism, the main enemy, and treated a Nazi advance as a passing phase that would clear the ground for communism. Trotsky attacked that line with everything he had. Fascism, he argued, was not one more bourgeois government but a mass movement of the ruined middle classes mobilised to physically destroy the organisations of the working class, its parties, unions, presses and meeting halls, and a Nazi victory would not be a change of ministry but the annihilation of the labour movement's accumulated strength for a generation. Against it he demanded a united front of the workers' organisations, social democratic and communist together, for the defensive struggle, in the formula he repeated until it became a slogan of his movement: march separately, strike together.

### What made the fascism writings so prescient?

Written between 1930 and 1933, they named the Nazi movement a mortal danger while the Communist International dismissed fascism as a passing stage, urged a united front of workers' organisations in plain terms, and predicted that a Hitler victory would mean the destruction of the German labour movement for a generation.

The prescience is checkable in the plainest way, by reading the pamphlets with their dates attached and setting the predictions beside the events. He forecast that the Nazi movement, if not stopped by united working class action, would take power constitutionally and then destroy the unions and parties in sequence, that the social democratic leaders' reliance on the constitution and on Hindenburg would prove worthless, that the communist party's separate demonstrations and its occasional practical alignment with the Nazis against the social democrats, as in the Prussian referendum episode, were criminal follies, and that once Hitler held the state, war against the Soviet Union and a general European catastrophe followed on a timetable of years. Every element of that forecast was on paper before January 1933. The German parties did not unite; the communist leadership held its line until the Reichstag fire gave the regime its pretext; the unions were destroyed in May 1933, the parties banned in sequence, and the leaders who had dismissed the exile's warnings fled, were imprisoned, or were murdered. The biography should register the achievement without embroidery, because the embroidery is unnecessary: a stateless man on a Turkish island, reading newspapers and receiving visitors' reports, called the central event of the European century correctly and in print, while the general staffs of two mass parties called it wrong.

Two qualifications keep the account honest, and both are his own tradition's later admissions as much as his critics'. The fascism writings were a minority position even among his followers' rivals on the left, and they changed nothing in Germany, where his German supporters numbered in the low thousands and carried no weight in either mass party; analysis, however brilliant, is not a historical force until an organisation carries it, and the exile had no organisation that mattered in Berlin. Second, his remedy carried the assumptions of his whole politics: the united front was to be the prelude to a communist victory in the ensuing struggle, not to the restoration of Weimar democracy, which he regarded as already doomed, and readers who admire the diagnosis are not obliged to admire the prescription attached to it. The writings' standing in the biography is therefore precise. They are the strongest single exhibit for the quality of his political intelligence operating without office, and they are simultaneously an exhibit for the tragedy of his situation, the analyst who saw the avalanche earliest and could move no one out of its path.

## The Revolution Betrayed and the theory of the degenerated workers' state

The Revolution Betrayed, finished in Norway in 1936, is the exile's most systematic book and the founding text of his movement's analysis of the Soviet Union. Its question was the one his whole later life posed: what was the state that he and Lenin had founded, twenty years on? His answer held several claims in a deliberate tension. The Soviet Union, he argued, was not socialist, since socialism implied a level of abundance and equality the country had not approached. It was not capitalist either, since the means of production were nationalised and the economy planned, conquests of October that survived and that obliged revolutionaries to defend the Soviet Union against imperialist attack despite its regime. It was a transitional society, deformed by scarcity and encirclement, in which a bureaucratic caste, the apparatus that Stalin personified and that Trotsky called by the name of the French reaction, a Soviet Thermidor, had usurped political power from the working class and ruled in its own interest, distributing privilege to itself while guarding the nationalised property forms from which its position derived. The bureaucracy was a caste, not a class, in his analysis, since it held no property of its own and could not bequeath its position as property. The remedy he prescribed was political revolution: the working class must overthrow the bureaucracy and restore soviet democracy, while preserving nationalised property and the plan, a social revolution being unnecessary and, if it restored private property, a regression.

The analysis was contested from every direction, and the profile should map the contest rather than award it. Orthodox communists dismissed the book as counter revolutionary slander. Social democrats and liberals argued that the bureaucracy was simply the mature form of the system Lenin and Trotsky had built, and that calling it a usurpation was an evasion of paternity. Rival Marxists, including some who had shared his exile, broke with him over the defence of the Soviet Union, especially after the Hitler Stalin pact and the invasions of Poland and Finland in 1939, arguing that a state which partitioned Poland with Nazi Germany had forfeited any claim to be defended as a workers' state in any sense. Inside his own movement the question split organisations and consumed his final year in polemics, conducted from Coyoacan with undiminished energy against the dissenters. The scholarly judgement that has survived best treats The Revolution Betrayed as a work of genuine analytical originality, the first systematic attempt to theorise a society that fitted neither the capitalist nor the socialist template, whose concepts of bureaucratic degeneration shaped even the vocabularies of its opponents; and, in the same breath, as a work constrained by its author's need to keep October innocent, so that the bureaucracy could be a parasite on the revolution rather than one of its possible children. That second judgement is the counterfactual constraint returning in theoretical dress, and the section on the strongest objection below takes it at full strength.

The book's literary and biographical qualities should not be lost inside the doctrinal summary. It contains his finest sustained piece of social analysis, the chapter on the family and the retreat from the revolution's early emancipation decrees, where he documented the regime's restoration of family discipline, its recriminalisation of abortion, and its cult of domestic authority with a sociologist's eye for what such retreats reveal about a ruling stratum's real values. It also contains, in its treatment of the arts and of daily life, the cultural critic of 1924 turned against the cultural bureaucracy of 1936. The man who had defended the fellow travellers against manufactured literature now described a literature manufactured completely, and drew the conclusion about the regime from its novels as confidently as from its wage scales. The unity of the life is in such passages: the same intelligence, the same method of reading a society from its surfaces, applied first to building a state and then to indicting what the state had become.

## The Moscow Trials and the destruction of a family

The Moscow Trials of 1936 to 1938 placed the exile at the centre of a theatre he could not attend. In three public proceedings, the surviving leadership of the October revolution, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Pyatakov, Radek, Bukharin and their associates, confessed to careers of espionage, sabotage and conspiracy directed, the prosecution alleged, by Trotsky from abroad, in alliance with the intelligence services of Germany and Japan. The charges were fabrications, as the Dewey Commission established in 1937 and as the Soviet Union's own later admissions confirmed, and they were not decorative fabrications. They supplied the legal theatre for the Great Terror's consumption of the old party, and they named the exile as the conspiracy's head precisely so that every arrested person's file could be tied to his. He answered from Mexico with the only weapons exile left him: a counter commission of inquiry chaired by the philosopher John Dewey, which heard his testimony over days in Coyoacan in April 1937, examined the documentary record, and returned a verdict of not guilty on every charge, and a barrage of writing that dismantled the trials' internal contradictions date by date and train timetable by train timetable.

### What did the Moscow Trials do to his family?

The trials reached into his household across every border. His younger son Sergei, who had stayed in the Soviet Union and held no political role, was arrested and shot. His elder son Lev Sedov died in a Paris hospital in 1938 in circumstances his followers have never accepted as natural.

The family's destruction is recorded here factually, as the brief discipline requires, without rhetorical use. His daughter Zinaida, by his first marriage, who had visited him in exile and returned to Berlin, took her own life in 1933, ill and hounded by police attention. His daughter Nina had died of tuberculosis in 1928, in the Soviet Union, while her father was in internal exile. His younger son Sergei Sedov, an engineering teacher in Moscow who had deliberately kept out of politics, was arrested in the terror and shot in 1937. His elder son Lev Sedov, his closest collaborator and the organiser of his movement in Europe, died in a Paris clinic in February 1938 after an operation for appendicitis, in circumstances his movement investigated and never accepted as natural, and later research into Soviet intelligence archives has deepened rather than settled the suspicion that the clinic was penetrated by the apparatus hunting his father. His first wife, Alexandra Sokolovskaya, was exiled within the Soviet Union and perished in the terror; the record of her end is incomplete. Of the grandchildren, some survived in the Soviet Union under changed names, and one grandson, Esteban Volkov, raised in Mexico after his mother's death, lived in the Coyoacan house and was a boy of fourteen when the murder came to it. The biographical register for such a catalogue is neither pity nor indictment. It is the recognition that the terror he had helped to build as an instrument, and then opposed as a system, reached his own household last and most completely, and that he worked at his desk through every one of these reports, because work was the form his endurance took.

It should also be recorded, in fairness to the whole man and against a hagiography that would make the exile years a pure martyrdom, that the trials era showed his polemical hardness undiminished and sometimes misdirected. He fought the internal critics of his movement, the dissenters on the Soviet Union's class nature among them, with the same annihilating rhetoric he turned on the prosecution in Moscow, and some of his judgements of individuals in those years were as unjust in miniature as the machinery ranged against him was in gross. The biography that protects him from that observation is doing devotion, not history. The exile was a victim of a frame up of world historical scale, and he was also, to the end, a faction fighter of the school of 1903, for whom opponents were types to be dismantled. Both facts survived in him at once, and the second does not diminish the first.

## Mexico, the Fourth International and the ice axe

The Fourth International was founded in September 1938, at a one day conference in a supporter's house outside Paris, with delegates and mandates representing small groups in some thirty countries and the founder absent in Mexico, directing by letter. Its founding programme, the Transitional Programme, drafted by Trotsky, set out demands designed to bridge the gap between the workers' immediate struggles and the conquest of power, and its founding wager was explicit: the crisis of humanity, the document argued, was reducible to the crisis of revolutionary leadership, and the small international's task was to supply that leadership when the coming war shattered the old order as the previous war had. The assessment of the founding belongs partly to the series article on the [fourth international legacy](/2014/12/01/fourth-international-legacy/), but the biographical judgement belongs here, and it is double. As an act of will, the founding was extraordinary: a hunted man of fifty eight, his family destroyed, his movement infiltrated and minuscule, declined to spend his remaining years as a commentator and instead constituted himself the trustee of a tradition he intended to survive him. As a political calculation, it rested on forecasts, of imminent revolution in the war's wake and of his movement's growth into a mass force, that the postwar world disappointed almost completely. He knew the odds better than his critics sometimes allow. The testament he wrote in February 1940, quoted at the close of this profile, is not the document of a man who expected to be rescued by events.

The first attempt on his life came on 24 May 1940, when a raiding party led by the Mexican painter David Alfaro Siqueiros, acting for Soviet intelligence, machine gunned the Coyoacan house in the night, wounded his grandson in the foot, kidnapped and murdered one of his guards, the young American Robert Sheldon Harte, and missed the sleeping household by the accident of their dropping below the bed line. The house was then fortified further, the guards increased, the routine hardened. The second attempt succeeded because it came from inside the perimeter. Ramon Mercader, a Spanish communist recruited by Soviet intelligence, had spent two years building an identity as a Belgian born businessman, Jacques Mornard, and a courtship of a follower's sister, through which he gained entry to the house as a trusted acquaintance. On 20 August 1940 he arrived with an article he asked Trotsky to read, wearing a raincoat over his arm in the heat, concealing a shortened ice axe, the mountaineer's tool, in its folds. In the study, as Trotsky bent over the article at his desk, Mercader struck him from behind. The wound was mortal and was not instantly so: Trotsky fought his attacker, cried out, forbade the guards to kill the man, who had to be interrogated, dictated instructions, and died the following day, 21 August 1940, in a Mexico City hospital. He was sixty years old.

The weapon requires one plain sentence, because the error is indestructible in the popular literature. It was an ice axe, a climber's tool with a shaft, a head, a spike and an adze, and not an ice pick, which is a different implement entirely; the distinction mattered to the forensic record and it matters to the biography as a small emblem of how loosely the most famous political murder of the century has been reported. The murder's responsibility is likewise plain and is stated with attribution, as the discipline of this profile requires: Mercader was an agent of the Soviet secret police, recruited and run through its apparatus, decorated by the Soviet state after his release from a Mexican prison twenty years later, and buried in Moscow with honours. The regime that Trotsky had accused of betraying the revolution answered his accusation, in the end, with the method his 1904 pamphlet had foreseen for the apparatus's logic, and no biographer's irony is needed to point it, since he pointed it himself, in advance, repeatedly, in print.

His testament, written six months before the murder and found among his papers, closes the life in his own register. It records his faith, unshaken in the document's terms, that a future generation would clear the revolution's name, and it contains the sentence by which he is most often and most fairly quoted: life is beautiful. The cremation in Coyoacan drew thousands of Mexican mourners past the coffin, and the ashes were buried in the garden of the house on Avenida Viena, under a stone bearing the hammer and sickle he had served and the name he had borrowed from a jailer. Natalia Sedova survived him by twenty years, kept the house and the archive's memory, and broke publicly, at the end of her life, with the movement he had founded over its continued defence of the state that had killed him, a final act of independence that the biography should honour as hers and not as a footnote to his.

## The administrator and the economist

The legend of Trotsky is a legend of platforms and battlefronts, and it obscures a third of his career in office, the years of economic administration in which the organiser of October tried to organise a ruined economy. In 1920 he took over the transport commissariat in the middle of a railway collapse that threatened the cities with starvation, and applied to the railways the methods of the front: militarised labour discipline, shock priorities, the concentration of scarce fuel and rolling stock on decisive routes regardless of the cost elsewhere. The railways recovered. Whether the recovery owed more to the methods or to the war's end releasing men and material is a genuine historical question, and his critics inside the party, who called the method Tsektran's tyranny after the transport committee he chaired, had a case that the biography should state: the militarisation of the railways was of a piece with the trade union platform, the same instinct to meet scarcity with command, and the same confidence that command was a substitute for incentives. The administrator and the labour militariser were one man with one method, and the method's successes and its character are inseparable in the record.

His economic thought in the nineteen twenties ran ahead of his party in one direction that later history treated kindly. With Preobrazhensky's analysis of primitive socialist accumulation behind the argument, he pressed from 1923 onward for planned industrialisation financed by taxing the private trade and prosperous peasantry that the New Economic Policy was enriching, and for a prices policy that would close the scissors between high industrial prices and low agricultural ones. The majority that defeated him on this programme in 1925 was, within four years, implementing a violent version of it under Stalin, at a tempo and with a brutality the Left Opposition's economists had not proposed, and the irony has been worked over by historians ever since: the defeated platform's content returned as the victors' practice, stripped of the soviet democracy that the platform had joined to it. A careful biography states the irony and then states its limit. The Left Opposition's industrialisation would still have been a coercive extraction from the peasantry administered by a one party state that its authors had no intention of loosening at the top, and the distance between its programme and collectivisation as practised, while real in tempo and in political form, is a distance within a shared framework of command. The counterfactual constraint, once again, is what prevents a genuine policy difference from being inflated into a difference of civilisation.

His administrative gifts were real and were recognised by opponents who had every reason to deny them. The war commissariat, the transport rescue, the concessions committee's hard bargaining with foreign capital, the organisation of the 1923 currency and planning debates: in each, the qualities were the same, an immense capacity for work, a grasp of the decisive point, a talent for picking subordinates and driving them, and a prose that turned administrative decisions into public arguments. So was the defect that accompanied the gifts. He administered from above, by order and exhortation, with a theorist's impatience for the slow accretion of consent, and in a party that was ceasing to decide by debate, the impatience told against him. The machine men who beat him, the secretaries and committee clerks he dismissed as mediocrities, understood something about the new politics that the finest mind in the party understood too late: that in an apparatus, the power to appoint is the power to decide, and eloquence addressed to a membership that no longer voted was artillery fired into the air.

## Lenin and Trotsky: the partnership and its limits

No relationship in the biography carries more polemical freight, because the succession struggle was fought as an argument about Lenin's mantle, and both the Stalinist orthodoxy and the Trotskyist tradition needed the partnership described in opposite ways. The orthodoxy that ruled Soviet historiography for decades described a lifelong antagonism, Lenin against Trotsky on every question from 1903 to 1924, interrupted by a tactical alliance. The tradition he founded described a convergence so complete by 1917 that the two men stood as joint leaders of October, divided only by the older man's death. The documented record, as the best scholarship on both sides of the old divide has established it, supports neither cartoon. It supports a relationship of genuine and repeated conflict over real questions, organisation in 1903 and 1904, the character of the Russian revolution until 1917, the Brest-Litovsk treaty in 1918, the trade unions in 1920 and 1921, in which the honours were divided and the conflicts were conducted, by the standards of that party's later practice, openly and without excommunication; and, laid over the conflicts, a mutual recognition of capacity that both men stated in writing and that Lenin's testament formalised in its paired judgements of the two heirs, Stalin's rudeness against Trotsky's self assurance, with the edge of the comparison falling against Stalin.

The limits of the partnership are as instructive as its strengths. Lenin never repudiated the factional history between them, and Trotsky never conceded that his 1904 warning had been wrong, only that the party of 1917 had outgrown the conditions the warning described. On the trade union question Lenin fought him to a finish and won, and Trotsky accepted the defeat and administered the winning line, a discipline his later movement cited as a model and his critics as evidence that he could subordinate his judgement to the collective when the collective was Lenin's. The deeper limit appeared only after Lenin's death, and it belongs to the counterfactual argument: the partnership had worked because Lenin's authority could hold the ring between the apparatus and the opposition, and no successor, Trotsky included, inherited that authority. A state in which the leader's personal authority is the constitution's working substitute is a state already deformed, and the man who had warned in 1904 that the organisation would substitute itself for the class might have been expected to see the deformation's depth. That he saw it by 1923 and named it in the New Course is to his credit. That the seeing changed nothing about the instruments he had helped to build, and that he proposed to fight the apparatus with the party the apparatus owned, is the tragedy's mechanism, and it is recorded here as mechanism, not as moral.

## The counterfactual constraint examined

Every assessment of Trotsky finally turns on a question that cannot be answered by experiment: what would a Soviet state under his leadership have become? The question is legitimate, since the succession struggle was real and its outcome was not foreordained in every detail, and it is dangerous, since it invites the imagination to supply a history more congenial than the one that occurred. The counterfactual constraint is this profile's rule for handling the question, and it can now be stated with the whole record in view. Claims about the hypothetical leadership are admissible in proportion as they rest on the claimant's documented conduct when he held power and on the positions he took when they cost him something, and inadmissible in proportion as they rest on the exile's retrospective self portrait and on the horror of what his rival did instead. Stalin's record is evidence about Stalin. It is not, without further argument, evidence about what Trotsky's record in the same office would have been, and the emotional force of the comparison has smuggled more bad history past more good historians than any other single move in this literature.

### Why does the counterfactual constraint matter beyond Trotsky?

Because the same test disciplines every judgement of a defeated alternative. A claimant's own conduct in office, where office was held, is evidence about the counterfactual in a way that programmes and intentions are not, and applying the test even handedly protects biography from becoming either prosecution or devotion.

Applied to the evidence assembled in this profile, the constraint yields a judgement with edges on both sides, and it is worth stating the edges separately. Against the claim that his leadership would have produced a substantially more democratic outcome stand the documents of 1918 to 1921: the hostage system defended, the terror theorised as principle in Terrorism and Communism, the labour militarisation proposed against Lenin's resistance, Kronstadt defended for nineteen years, the faction ban supported. These are not the acts of a man whose instincts ran toward pluralism under pressure, and they were not extracted from him by Stalin's example, since they precede Stalin's supremacy and in the trade union case ran against the majority's. Any biography that asks a reader to set them aside as the weather of the civil war must explain why the weather, in this one case, should not count as climate.

For the claim that his leadership would nonetheless have differed, and differed materially, stand a different set of documents. The opposition of 1923 demanded party democracy, soviet revival and an end to the appointive machine while such demands could still be made inside the party, and he made them at the price of his career. The fascism writings show a strategic intelligence capable of revising a line against the evidence, a capacity the apparatus that beat him conspicuously lacked in the same years. His 1935 diary records him entertaining, in private, the possibility that his own classification of the Soviet state was wrong, which is not the mental habit of the terror's later managers. And his analysis of the bureaucracy, whatever its protective function for October's honour, identified a real social formation and its interests with a precision his rivals' categories never matched. The shared record with the regime he opposed is treated at full length in the series article on the [case against leninism](/2014/06/15/case-against-leninism/), where the prosecution's whole case is assembled, and the structured comparison of the two men's systems belongs to [trotskyism vs stalinism](/2014/11/15/trotskyism-vs-stalinism/), which readers should treat as this profile's companion rather than its summary.

The strongest objection to his later standing must now be stated at full strength, as the discipline of this series requires, because it is the objection the counterfactual constraint was built to answer fairly. It runs as follows. His critique of the bureaucracy dates from his exclusion from it. The instruments he condemned in exile, the faction ban, the appointive machine, the police supervision of political life, were instruments he had helped to forge and had used without recorded scruple while they served his side of the struggle. His theory of the degenerated workers' state was constructed to acquit the October he had led: the revolution was sound, the property forms were sound, only a caste of usurpers was guilty, so the founder's hands stayed clean. On this reading, the opposition was a quarrel about who should wield the apparatus, conducted by a man whose objection to the terror was that it had changed its address, and his immense literary campaign from exile was the longest brief ever written for the defence in a case where the defendant had built the prison.

The answer begins with concessions, because the objection has real force and a profile that pretends otherwise forfeits trust. The timing point is true in part: his public campaign against bureaucratisation begins in 1923, when the apparatus was already being turned against him, and he never wrote the self critical account of his own 1920 positions that the objection demands. The protective structure of The Revolution Betrayed is also real: the caste theory does acquit the founding, and a theory can be both illuminating and convenient, as his was. But the objection overreaches at three points, and the record marks each. First, the positions did not all wait for his defeat: his alarm at the secretariat's growth and at the Georgian affair was shared with Lenin in 1922 and early 1923, while he still stood near the summit, and the New Course attacked the appointive system by name before the apparatus had finished excluding him. Second, the demands he raised in opposition, for elected officials, for legality inside the party, for the unions' independence of the state he had once proposed to absorb them into, were not the demands of a man merely seeking the apparatus's wheel; the apparatus offered him repeated opportunities to recant and be readmitted to office, and the price of refusal, exile and the destruction of his family, was one he paid in full and in knowledge. Third, the 1935 diary entry, entertaining that the state he had cofounded might not be a workers' state at all, is evidence of a mind still auditing its own foundations in private, which the objection's portrait of pure self acquittal cannot accommodate. The verdict the constraint supports is therefore narrow and double edged. His opposition was genuinely about the apparatus as well as about its occupancy, and it came late, from inside a record that limits how far the opposition can be projected backward into a hypothetical democratic reign. Both halves of that sentence are the biography's conclusion, and neither half is available for separate quotation without the other.

## The orator and the polemicist

His contemporaries agreed about one gift across every line of enmity: he was the revolution's greatest orator, and probably its greatest political writer, and the two gifts were versions of each other. The speaking style is recoverable from hundreds of witness accounts, friendly and hostile, which converge on the same description: a high, metallic voice, an unhurried opening, a long architectural build through antithesis and historical parallel, and then the peroration delivered at a pace and pressure that carried halls of thousands. He spoke to soldiers at the front from railway platforms, to party congresses in marathon interventions, to the Petrograd soviet in the sessions that decided October, and the meat of the speeches was argument rather than incantation, which is why so many of them still read as essays. The written polemics used the same architecture and added a cruelty the platform sometimes spared: the opponent introduced, classified, quoted against himself, and dismissed into a historical category, with the sentence's rhythm doing half the execution. It was magnificent, and it was costly. A man who wins arguments that completely makes enemies who do not forget, and the coalition that destroyed him was staffed in part by men he had dismantled in print and never troubled to conciliate. The biography should not moralise the point, since conciliation was not the currency of that party in those years on any side, but it should record the mechanism, because the same instrument that built the Red Army's morale and the exile's legend also built the wall of personal hatred through which no alliance of 1926 or 1927 could pass.

The polemical manner had a scholarly obverse that deserves equal record. He read his opponents seriously before he destroyed them, mastered the German and French literatures of his subjects, and wrote about adversaries of the first rank, Kautsky, Luxemburg, the Menshevik theorists, with an engagement that conceded their stature in the act of refuting them. His judgements of fellow revolutionaries were often the sharpest available: his portrait of Lenin, in the unfinished biography and in the 1924 sketch written at Lenin's death, remains among the most penetrating by any contemporary, precisely because admiration did not disable the critical faculty. The unfinished Lenin life is one of the exile's losses to literature. What exists of it, the account of the young Lenin's formation, shows the biographer he might have become at full length had the assassin left him the years, and it sharpens the irony that his own biography is now written largely from archives he assembled, sold to Harvard for his movement's funds and his family's keep, in a transaction he negotiated with the same clear eyed practicality he brought to concessions for foreign capital.

## The international opposition: China and the price of being right

The Left Opposition's international record supplies the biography with its clearest case of vindicated judgement and its clearest case of the limits of judgement without power. In China, between 1925 and 1927, the Communist International directed the young Chinese communist party to subordinate itself to the Kuomintang, entering its ranks and restraining the workers' and peasants' movements in the interest of the national alliance. Trotsky opposed the subordination from 1926 onward, argued that the Chinese party must stand independently and prepare its own soviets, and predicted that the nationalist bourgeoisie would turn its guns on its communist allies when the alliance had served its purpose. In April 1927 Chiang Kai-shek's forces massacred the communists and unionists of Shanghai, and the Wuhan episode completed the disaster within the year. The opposition's warnings, suppressed inside the Russian party while the policy ran, were circulated after the catastrophe as proof that the bureaucracy's international line led revolutions to destruction, and the charge became a founding grievance of his movement worldwide. The episode matters to this profile for two reasons. It shows the analyst of the fascism writings already at work years earlier, reading a foreign revolution's class alignments more accurately from Moscow than the apparatus read them from closer in. And it shows the pattern of his defeats in its purest form: right in the memorandum, absent from the decision, and left to compile the dossier of his own correctness for a posterity he increasingly addressed because the present would not seat him.

Spain repeated the pattern in the nineteen thirties, with his advice to the Spanish left, to build an independent revolutionary party rather than dissolve into the popular front, disregarded by the small groups that claimed his banner as often as by the mass parties that despised it. The pattern has a name in his movement's literature, the tragedy of prophecy without an army, and it has a less flattering name in his critics': the sterility of a politics that had refined itself into commentary. The biography can afford to let both names stand, since each captures a real feature of the exile's decade. What the record will not support is the dismissal of the commentary as mere commentary. The China dossier, the German pamphlets, the analysis of the bureaucracy, are works of political intelligence of the first order, and the state that exiled their author governed its world policy, in the same years, by the lights of men who had been wrong about China, wrong about Germany, and wrong about the character of the regime they served, as their own later admissions in part conceded.

## The question he declined to make a banner

A short section is owed to a biographical thread that others have sometimes inflated and that he himself handled with a consistency worth recording. Trotsky was born into a Jewish family in a province of the Pale's wider world, and antisemitism was a standing fact of the empire that formed him: quotas in education, residence restrictions, the pogroms that disfigured the revolution's years as they had the old regime's. His response, across his life, was the assimilationist universalism of his movement: he declined to make Jewish identity a political banner, refused the interior commissariat in 1917 in part, by his own account in his autobiography, because handing a Jew the ministry of internal affairs would arm the counter revolution's propaganda in a country where the prejudice ran deep, and treated the national question everywhere as subordinate to the class alignment that his theory made central. In the nineteen thirties, as the Nazi persecution escalated, his writing registered the Jewish catastrophe with growing gravity and revised his earlier confidence that the question would dissolve with capitalism, conceding in interviews of his final years that a territorial refuge might become necessary for a people facing extermination, though he never became a Zionist. The thread matters here for one reason. It is a further instance of the profile's central pattern, a first rate analytical intelligence applied to a question on which his framework gave him the least help, and revising itself, late and partially, against the evidence of events. The biography that omits the thread leaves the universalism untested; the biography that inflates it into the key to the man ignores the man's own account of himself. Both failures are common in the literature, and this profile declines them alike.

## The misreadings corrected

The popular picture of Trotsky is assembled from a handful of errors that this profile has been correcting in passing and that deserve, before the assessment, to be gathered and answered in one place. The first is that he was a lifelong Bolshevik, a founder of Lenin's party who stood at its centre from the beginning. He was not: he opposed Lenin at the founding congress, spent fourteen years outside the party and much of them in polemic against it, and entered its ranks five months before October. The second misreading inverts the first, presenting the 1917 fusion as an infiltration, a Menshevik capturing a party that was not his. The record shows a convergence on the revolution's course negotiated in the open and ratified by a congress, and it is given in the partnership section above. The third error is the democrat in office, the humane Trotsky whose rule would have spared the revolution its terror, and it fails against the documents of 1918 to 1921 assembled in the record sections: the hostages, the theorised terror, the labour militarisation he pressed beyond Lenin. The fourth error is its mirror image, the claim that because the record stands, the exile's analysis can be discarded as hypocrisy without reading; it fails against the fascism pamphlets, the China dossier and The Revolution Betrayed, whose arguments answer to evidence and survive their author's biography.

The fifth error is the murder weapon, an ice pick, repeated in reference works and headlines for eighty years; it was an ice axe, as the assassination section records, and the persistence of the small error is a useful warning about the large ones. The sixth error dates his opposition to the bureaucracy from Lenin's death, as a widowed heir's grievance; the trade union and faction disputes, the alarm at the secretariat, and the Georgian affair all precede the death, and the 1904 substitution warning precedes the revolution itself. The seventh error treats his fascism writings as the common sense of his tradition, when they were a minority position inside a left whose mass organisations held the opposite line; their prescience is precisely their isolation. An eighth, subtler error runs the other way, treating the whole corpus as the property of the movement that claims him, so that the History of the Russian Revolution is read as a factional document rather than as the major work of narrative history it also is. The series discipline forbids characterising any contemporary organisation that claims his legacy, and this profile observes it; the literary judgement, however, belongs to the biography, and it is that the best of the writing has outgrown every custody dispute, as major writing does.

## What the forecasts got wrong

A profile that catalogues his prescience owes equal space to his failures of foresight, which were neither few nor trivial, and which cluster in a pattern that is itself biographically revealing. He underestimated the bureaucracy's durability from the start of the struggle against it, expecting through the nineteen twenties that economic crisis or war would crack the apparatus and return the party to its membership; the apparatus instead consolidated through every crisis he scheduled for its fall. His theory of the degenerated workers' state carried a time limit he stated repeatedly: the bureaucracy must either be overthrown by the working class or restore capitalism, and the alternative could not persist across a whole historical epoch. The postwar decades, in which the system he had analysed persisted, expanded into Eastern Europe, and industrialised a continent, strained that binary past what many of his own followers could defend, and the movement he founded split in part over the effort to save the classification. He forecast that the Second World War would end as the First had, in revolutions that would sweep away both the fascist and the Stalinist orders and carry his small international to mass influence; the war ended instead in the bureaucracy's greatest expansion and his movement's continued smallness, a miss he did not live to register and that his testament's confidence makes poignant rather than ridiculous.

The pattern in the failures is worth naming, because it is the obverse of the pattern in the successes. Where his analysis worked from the documented mechanics of a situation, the class forces in Germany, the alignments in China, the interests of the Soviet bureaucracy as a distributing caste, it was superb. Where it worked from the theory's inner timetable, from the requirement that transitional formations resolve quickly in one direction or the other, it mistook the theory's logic for history's schedule. He was, in this precise sense, both the most empirical of the revolutionary Marxists and a prisoner of the system's eschatology, and the two qualities lived in the same sentences. The biographer who grasps the duality can explain the whole curve: the peerless diagnostician of conjunctures, whose long range forecasts kept being mugged by the durability of the very formation he had diagnosed best.

## The strategist: fronts, priorities and the Polish war

The war commissar's strategic record deserves a section of its own, since the building of the army can eclipse the question of how he proposed to use it, and since the strategic disputes of the civil war show the same cast of mind at work on a map that it later applied to the politics of three continents. The Red war effort faced enemies on a dozen fronts across a territory larger than Europe, with interior lines, a railway net radiating from Moscow, and never enough of anything: rifles, boots, grain, officers, time. The strategic problem was sequence, which enemy to finish first with forces insufficient for all, and Trotsky's conduct of the war is a record of priority decisions argued in the politburo and enforced by the train. In 1919 the argument ran between the eastern front against Kolchak, where the Siberian offensive was succeeding, and the southern front against Denikin, whose advance through Ukraine toward Moscow became, by the autumn, the war's mortal danger. Trotsky pressed the southern priority against a high command and a commander in chief whose plans privileged the pursuit into Siberia, and was overruled for a season in a dispute that cost him a resignation threat and very nearly his post. The southern emergency of October 1919, with Denikin at Orel and Yudenich before Petrograd in the same weeks, vindicated the priority he had urged, and the war's turning followed the concentration he had demanded. Military historians who have no brief for his politics have treated the episode as evidence of genuine strategic literacy: he read the map politically, understanding that the enemy who threatened the capital and the grain and coal of the south outranked the enemy retreating into empty space, however satisfying the pursuit.

The Polish war of 1920 closed the civil war with its most arguable decision, the Red Army's advance on Warsaw after the Polish invasion of Ukraine had been thrown back. The advance carried the war onto ethnically Polish ground in the hope, fervent in Moscow, that the Polish workers would rise and open the road to a Germany in ferment. The hope failed; the Polish nation rallied to its state's defence across its classes; and the Red armies, overextended at the end of ruined supply lines, were routed before Warsaw in August 1920. Trotsky's part in the decision is a test of biographical scruple, because his later writings and his partisans have sometimes cast him as the advance's internal critic, and the record is more tangled. He supported the offensive's opening logic, shared the leadership's assessment that a revolutionary situation might be made in Poland, and expressed, as the advance lengthened, the caution about outrunning supplies and national sentiment that the event vindicated. The honest summary is that the Warsaw gamble belonged to the whole leadership, Trotsky included, and that its failure taught the regime the limit that his own theory of permanent revolution should perhaps have predicted: a revolution carried on bayonets into a country whose working class reads the bayonets as a national invasion will unite the invaded nation against it. His exile writings on the episode are notably free of the self exculpation he practised elsewhere, and the biography can register that, too, as data about the man: the defeats he owned were the military ones, where the map could be pointed to, and the defeats he resisted owning were the political ones, where the pointing finger came back at the founder.

## Lessons of October and the revolution that did not come in the West

The book he made from the succession struggle's opening round, The Lessons of October of 1924, is usually read as a factional missile, which it was, and less often as what it also was: his mature theory of how revolutions are won and lost, generalised from 1917 and aimed at the failures of the European left. Its argument turned on the German events of 1923, when the Communist International, judging Germany ripe for insurrection amid the Ruhr occupation and the currency's collapse, prepared an October of its own and then cancelled it, leaving its Hamburg organisation to rise alone and be crushed. Trotsky's analysis drew the moral he would repeat for the rest of his life: revolutionary situations are perishable, they cannot be stored or summoned at convenience, and everything depends on whether a leadership exists that recognises the moment and acts, as Lenin's party had acted in the weeks when it alone demanded power. From that analysis grew the formula that crowned the Transitional Programme fourteen years later, the crisis of mankind reduced to the crisis of revolutionary leadership, and the formula's genealogy matters for assessing it. It was not a consolation invented in exile for a dwindling sect. It was the lesson its author drew, in power's aftermath and in good faith, from comparing the October he had organised with the Octobers that failed in Berlin, in Budapest, in Turin; and readers who reject the formula, as most historians do in its absolute form, should still reckon with the comparison that generated it, since the role of leadership in 1917 is the part of the story no structural account has ever quite dissolved.

The western fixation also explains the strengths and the blindness of his final decade in a single frame. He never accepted that the European working class might choose, across a generation, the gradualisms he despised, or that the Soviet bureaucracy might prove a more durable answer to backwardness than the permanent revolution he had prophesied in 1906. His categories for the West were forged in 1917's furnace, where they had fitted the facts like a key, and a key that has opened one great lock is the hardest instrument in the world to lay down. The fascism writings show the key still turning, since Germany in 1932 genuinely was a situation of the 1917 type, balanced on the decision of organisations. The postwar world he did not live to see was of another type altogether, and the movement that inherited the key spent decades trying it on doors it did not fit. That is the movement's history, not his, and the series treats it under the Fourth International's legacy; the biography's obligation is the narrower one of showing where the key was cut.

## Problems of everyday life

Between the fronts and the faction fights he found time, in 1923 and 1924, to write a small book that biographers treasure because it shows the commissar off duty from history: Problems of Everyday Life, a collection of essays on the texture of ordinary existence in the new society. The subjects are swearing, drunkenness, the cinema, the radio, funeral customs, the manners of officials, the state of the family, and the prose is the work of a man who believed that a revolution which changed the ownership of factories and left the kitchen, the queue and the marriage bed untouched had changed less than it claimed. He argued for the cinema as the revolution's school of manners against the tavern and the church, for courtesy as a civic technology rather than a bourgeois veneer, for attention to the material details of workers' housing and women's double burden of factory and home, with a concreteness that sits oddly beside the militariser of the trade union debate until the reader sees the unity beneath both. The unity is the conviction that human beings are made by their organised environment and can be remade by reorganising it, by command where he thought command applied, by cultural competition where he thought it did not. The essays' author and Terrorism and Communism's are the same theorist of human material under different scarcities, and a profile that quotes only one of the two books has chosen its Trotsky in advance.

The everyday life essays also hold a small, sharp piece of evidence for the counterfactual file, and it cuts in his favour, which the discipline of this profile requires to be recorded with the same plainness as the evidence against. In the essay on the family and in the speeches of the same years, he registered, earlier than most of his party, that the revolution's emancipation of women was being silently reversed by scarcity, that the communal kitchens and laundries of the early decrees had collapsed back into the individual household and the individual woman's unpaid labour, and that a society's real constitution is written in such arrangements and not in its programmes. The analyst who could see that in 1923 could see a great deal, and the later chapters of The Revolution Betrayed on the family's restoration under the bureaucracy grew directly from this seeing. The counterfactual constraint is not a machine for convictions; it is a rule of evidence, and the evidence includes the perception and the moral attention documented here, weighed in the same scales as the hostage orders, at their true weights, which each reader must finally assign.

## The countryside in his thought

A biography of a theorist of the industrial working class owes a section to the class he theorised least and governed most fatefully, the peasantry, who were four fifths of the country his revolution ruled. His formation had given him the peasant world at closer quarters than most of his party possessed it: the Yanovka farm ran on peasant labour, the Nikolayev union agitated among a peasantry in motion, and the Siberian exile was lived in peasant villages whose rhythms his essays recorded with a countryman's attentiveness. The theory he built assigned the peasantry a decisive but derivative role. The peasant, in the analysis of Results and Prospects, could smash the old order's land settlement and supply the revolution's infantry, but could not rule independently, since small property production generates no programme beyond the plot and the market; the village would follow whichever urban class led with confidence, the bourgeoisie or the workers, and the workers' state must therefore hold the village by a policy that balanced requisition against alliance. The civil war tested the formula in its harshest form. The grain requisitions that fed the Red Army and the cities were levied on the village by armed detachments, and the peasant risings of 1920 and 1921, Tambov above all, were the village's answer, answered in turn by a suppression conducted by the army he had built, under commanders he had appointed, with methods that included the hostage taking of rebels' families. The profile's discipline applies here without adjustment: the episodes belong to the record, they are part of what his government did to hold power, and the New Economic Policy's retreat on requisitioning, which he administered and then sought to limit, was the regime's own concession that the village could not be governed that way in peacetime.

His later economic programme, the Left Opposition's platform, proposed to hold the alliance by a different mechanism: planned prices, taxation of the prosperous peasant, and industrial goods flowing to the village in exchange for its grain, an alliance administered by a state monopoly of foreign trade and a planning apparatus. The programme's fate is recorded above. What the biography should add is the analytical residue, because his writing on the peasantry, scattered through the exile works, contains a warning that events underlined. A workers' state resting on a peasant ocean, he argued in The Revolution Betrayed, could not stabilise its democracy until industry had transformed the village itself, and until then every political question in the country would secretly be a question about grain. The bureaucracy he fought governed by that secret for decades. The farmer's son from Yanovka, who had watched his father bargain with the village across a yard, understood the village's weight in a way the party's urban intellectuals often did not, and the understanding coexisted, in the pattern this profile has traced throughout, with a willingness in the emergency years to extract the village's grain at gunpoint and to call the extraction necessity. Both are the record. The scales are the reader's.

## The final year: war comes, and the desk in Coyoacan

The last year of his life ran from the autumn of 1939 to the August of his murder, and it compresses the exile's whole situation into twelve months. The pact between the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany in August 1939, followed by the partition of Poland and the Soviet invasion of Finland, detonated inside his small movement the argument that had been gathering for years: whether the state that did these things could still be defended, in any sense, as a workers' state. A minority in his American organisation, led by writers of real ability, argued that the classification had collapsed and that the Soviet Union must be opposed as one imperialist power among others. Trotsky defended the old classification from Coyoacan in a sustained polemic, conceding the bureaucracy's crimes in the new war in the harshest terms while insisting that the property forms established by October still distinguished the Soviet state from its enemies, and that a movement which abandoned the distinction would disarm itself theoretically. The polemic consumed the energy that the History's sequel and the Lenin biography were waiting for, and it split his movement months before the ice axe split his household's guard. Readers must judge the dispute's merits in the series' treatment of the movement he founded; the biography's point is narrower and sadder. The last polemical campaign of the greatest controversialist of his age was fought, at sixty, over the classification of the state that had exiled him, against his own followers, while the assassin was already inside his perimeter learning the house's routines.

His writing on the war itself, the manifesto adopted by his international's emergency conference in May 1940 and the articles that surround it, held the line of his whole political life: the war was an imperialist war on both sides, the bureaucracy would emerge from it weakened or would fall to revolution, and the small international must hold its programme intact for the upheavals the war's end would bring. Three months after the manifesto he was dead. The manifesto's forecasts joined the ledger of his long range predictions, and the assessment has been given above; what belongs to the biography is the tableau the final summer presents, because no novelist would dare it and every biographer must. An old revolutionary in a fortified suburban house, breeding rabbits, annotating the war news in four languages, dictating a defence of a state that was preparing his murder, receiving the murderer as a guest and praising, days before the blow, the article the man had brought as a passport into the study. He died as the century's most consequential exile, and the manner of it, the trust extended across a desk to a reader of his prose, was the writer's death as much as the politician's.

## The historian's method: how the History was made

The History of the Russian Revolution deserves a section on its making, because the method explains both its greatness and the standing instruction it gives every biographer who follows, including this profile. He wrote it at Prinkipo in 1930, in the first full year of deportation, working from a library he had assembled in exile and from documentary collections, newspapers and memoirs that his secretariat gathered and his correspondents copied from archives abroad. The method was the opposite of the memoir's. Where the participant's recollection would have served anecdote, he built the narrative on the documentary record of the revolution's public life: the protocols of committees, the press of every party, the stenograms of congresses, the orders of the day, and he subjected the record to a sociological reading in which classes and their parties move as collective characters through a plot whose turns he rendered with a dramatist's pacing. The famous chapters on the February days render the crowd as a historical protagonist without dissolving individuals into it, and the portraits of the leaders are drawn by the same biographical intelligence he turned on his contemporaries in polemic, now disciplined by the archive. Professional historians who reject the book's politics, and there is no shortage of them, have repeatedly conceded its narrative and analytical power, and the concession is a datum: the work survives its brief, as the History's own theory would predict of a product whose making exceeded its maker's immediate purpose.

The method had a polemical edge that the biography should not conceal. The History was written against the falsification of 1917 then being manufactured in Moscow, where the October story was being rewritten to enlarge one man's role and erase another's, and its documentary density was a weapon system: an account of the insurrection built from the published record could not be answered by the custodians of an edited record except by suppressing it, which the Soviet state duly did for sixty years. There is a straight line from that method to the Dewey Commission's hearings, where the exile again answered fabrication with the dossier, and a straight line from both to the instruction this profile has followed throughout, that contested episodes are to be stated as they stand in the scholarship, with the defence and the prosecution each at full strength. The line is worth drawing because it complicates the verdict on the man in a way both traditions resist. The commissar who theorised terror in 1920 and the exile who built his defence on documentary scruple in 1937 are not two men, and the biography that resolves the tension by demoting either half has stopped describing its subject and started managing him.

## Brest-Litovsk and the diplomacy of a revolution without a state

The brief diplomatic career of the winter of 1917 and 1918 is usually told as theatre, and the theatre was real: the new government's delegation arriving at Brest-Litovsk to lecture the German and Austrian empires on the rights of nations, the publication of the secret treaties, the appeal over the heads of the delegations to the soldiers and workers of the Central Powers. But the episode's biographical content is heavier than its colour. As foreign commissar and then as head of the peace delegation, Trotsky conducted the first negotiation in which a revolutionary state faced the old diplomacy without an army, and his improvisation of the formula neither war nor peace, refusing to sign the annexationist treaty while declaring the war ended, was an attempt to buy time for revolution in Germany with the only currency the delegation held, delay and publicity. It failed within days. The German advance resumed against no resistance, the terms worsened, and Lenin, judging that the revolution's survival required the signature at any price, carried the party for the treaty against the left communist opposition and against his own chief negotiator's instincts. Trotsky abstained in the decisive votes and resigned the foreign commissariat for the war commissariat, where the lesson of Brest was waiting in material form: a revolution that cannot fight will sign what it is given.

The biography should mark what the episode taught him, because the teaching runs through the exile's statecraft judgements for twenty years. He never again treated diplomacy as a substitute for force, and his analyses of the nineteen thirties, of the League of Nations, of the popular fronts, of Munich, are written in Brest's ink: paper guarantees read as delays, delays read against the correlation of forces, and the correlation of forces read, always, from the class alignments inside the states concerned. The method produced the fascism writings' clarity about Germany, and it produced, in the same years, his refusal to credit any settlement, including the one his own exile depended on, with a durability its force relations did not underwrite. There is a consistency here that the record sections should not let a reader miss. The man who would not sign at Brest and the man who stormed Kronstadt held one doctrine in common: that in the last instance politics is decided by organised force, and that a revolutionary who forgets it will be reminded by those who have not. A liberal age finds the doctrine chilling, and found his practice of it, in office, exactly as chilling as the doctrine predicts. The counterfactual constraint then asks its hardest question in its purest form, and this profile leaves the question standing, as it should: whether a politics that never forgets the primacy of force can build, in power, the institutions that make force recede, and whether its author's four years in office contain any evidence that he intended to try.

## The reader: Balzac, the French novel and the uses of literature

His reading formed him as surely as his prisons did, and a short account of it belongs in an intellectual biography, since the library explains turns of the politics that the platforms cannot. He read French as a second native language, German nearly as well, and his prose in every period is stocked from the French nineteenth century: Balzac above all, whose Comedie Humaine he treated as the supreme sociology of a society in transformation, and whose method of rendering class through the furnished room and the cut of a coat he consciously adapted to the portrait chapters of the History. The literary essays of the exile years extend the range in directions that surprised his own followers: a grave, respectful obituary study of Mayakovsky after the poet's suicide in 1930, in which the commissar who had sparred with the Futurists for a decade refused to let the movement's quarrels stand between him and the dead man's stature; a long review essay on Celine's Journey to the End of the Night, which he read as a document of a civilisation's decomposition and praised as writing while refusing its despair as politics; studies of French and Russian novelists written for journals that paid the exile household's bills. The uses he made of literature were never decorative. In his framework, the novel was evidence, the seismograph of a class society's inner weather, and the critic who read Celine in 1933 was performing the same operation on France that the pamphleteer was performing on Germany, reading the decomposition before the statisticians could tabulate it.

The reading also disciplines a temptation of the hostile portrait, which requires its subject to be a fanatic of a single book. The documentary record shows a mind omnivorous across the literatures of four languages, capable of writing about a poet's suicide with a tenderness his political prose rarely risked, and capable, in the diary of his last exile years, of turning the same attention on his own household, his marriage, his dead, with a plainness that the public thunder never permitted. None of this acquits the record of 1919, and the profile's rule forbids the transaction in either direction. It enlarges the subject to his true size, which is the biography's first obligation, and it explains why the custody of this particular reputation has been fought over by writers as much as by parties ever since: the writers recognise one of their own, and cannot have him, since the parties got there first and the parties, on the evidence of the record, are also right about what he was.

## Spain: the last revolution he advised and the limits of advice

The Spanish civil war of 1936 to 1939 was the final occasion on which events offered his analysis a test at the scale of Germany's, and the Spanish writings complete the pattern the China dossier began. From the first months he argued that the Spanish workers' response to the military rising, the seizures of factories and land, the militias, the committees that governed Catalonia and much of republican Spain in fact, constituted a revolution unfolding inside a war, and that the republican government's policy, backed by the Soviet Union and the Communist International, of strangling that revolution in the name of the popular front and of respectability before London and Paris, would lose both the revolution and the war together. His specific fire concentrated on the Spanish movement closest to his own tradition, the POUM, whose leaders he had known in exile, for entering the Catalan government and subordinating the revolution's organs to it, a course he treated as the German error of 1933 translated into Spanish. The POUM's leader Andres Nin, once his correspondent, broke off relations under the pressure of the quarrel; within the year Nin was arrested in the suppression of his party by the republican police under Soviet direction and was murdered by Soviet agents, his body never recovered. The biography records the sequence without ornament, because it concentrates the exile's tragedy into one friendship: the analyst whose warnings about the apparatus were correct in every particular, whose advice was rejected by the pupils it might have saved, and whose vindication arrived, as it had in Germany, in the form of the catastrophe he had itemised in advance.

The Spanish outcome, Franco's victory in 1939 after the republic's internal war within the war had consumed its energies, entered his final writings as the third exhibit, after China and Germany, in the case for the Fourth International, and a biographer should note the argumentative structure honestly, since it is the structure of the whole exile. Each catastrophe was processed into the same conclusion: the analysis had been right, the leadership had been absent, therefore build the leadership. The conclusion does not follow with the necessity he claimed for it, since a decade of correct dossiers had also demonstrated that correctness was not the scarce resource he supposed, organisation, roots and timing were, and on those the exile had nothing to add to the dossiers. Yet the structure should not be mocked either, because the alternative conclusions available to him were silence, recantation, or the lecture circuit, and a man who had organised October was never going to choose them. The Spanish writings are read now, by historians of the war with no stake in his movement, as among the sharpest contemporary analyses of the republic's internal destruction, which is the fate his best exile work keeps meeting: adopted as testimony by a scholarship that declines the programme the testimony was written to serve.

## America in the late thought: the coming power and the last programme

One strand of the final decade belongs to the biography because it shows the analyst's range operating on the country least formed by his categories. Trotsky had seen the United States at close quarters for ten weeks in 1917, written about its capitalism with respect for its scale ever since, and told his American followers through the nineteen thirties that the century's decision would be taken there: that American capitalism, the strongest and therefore in his reading the most exposed, would enter the European war, emerge as the world's dominant power, and face, in the postwar reckoning, the social explosions his movement existed to lead. The forecast's first half, American entry and dominance, was the common property of serious observers by 1940 and should not be overcredited; the second half belongs to the ledger of misses recorded above. What is distinctive in the American writings is the tactical imagination he brought to a movement operating inside a democracy at peace: the long argument of 1940, conducted against his own followers' instincts, that revolutionaries must not abstain from the military question as conscription approached, but must demand training under workers' control and send their members through the army as its future instructors rather than cede the armed generation to the state unopposed. The proletarian military policy, as the dispute named it, was the Brest lesson and the Red Army experience restated for a Brooklyn audience: force decides, therefore be present where force is being organised. It split his movement again, weeks before his death, and it stands in the biography as the final proof that the strategist of 1919 was intact inside the exile of 1940, applying one doctrine of power to every terrain the century offered him, with undiminished brilliance and with consequences that remain, as this profile has insisted throughout, for the reader to weigh rather than for the biographer to prepay.

## The exile as economist: collectivisation judged from Prinkipo

The exile's economic writings of the nineteen thirties are the least read shelf of the corpus, and they hold a judgement that the counterfactual debate needs, because they show what his opposition proposed doing with the Soviet economy while the bureaucracy was transforming it by violence. Through the Bulletin of the Opposition and the articles that fed The Revolution Betrayed, he attacked the first five year plan's conduct on two flanks at once. Against the plan's tempos he argued that the bureaucracy, having spent the nineteen twenties restraining industry in the name of the alliance with the peasantry, had lurched in panic after the grain crisis of 1928 into forced collectivisation and breakneck industrial targets that the country's technique and cadres could not sustain, producing bottlenecks, waste, the destruction of livestock on a continental scale, and famine in the grain lands whose full extent reached him in fragments he printed when the Soviet press printed nothing. Against the regime's economic theory he argued that a plan without soviet democracy was a contradiction, since the planner's information came from the apparatus's reports and the apparatus reported what its survival required, so that the economy's real proportions could be known only through the feedback of free criticism, independent unions and an uncensored press. The second argument deserves the biographer's emphasis, because it is the point at which the exile's economics revised the commissar's. The advocate of labour militarisation in 1920 had come, by the middle nineteen thirties, to argue in print that command could not plan a complex economy without the corrective of democracy, and that the bureaucracy's privilege rested precisely on suppressing the corrective. He never assembled the revision into a recantation of 1920, and the counterfactual section has noted the omission. But the revision is in the texts, it is substantial, and any account of the hypothetical Trotsky in power that ignores it is working from the young commissar's file alone, which is to say from half the evidence the constraint admits.

The famine reporting also marks a limit that should be recorded beside the achievement. The Bulletin printed what its correspondents and the foreign press could gather, and the exile's judgements of scale were constrained by the same information blockade that constrained every outside observer; where this profile's protocol forbids unsourced figures, it does so in awareness that the subject himself, on this subject, worked under the blockade and sometimes estimated in the dark. The biographical moral is not scepticism about the famine, whose reality his reporting asserted correctly against the regime's denials and against the credulity of some Western correspondents of the day. It is that the exile's information position, a desk, a correspondence, a press file, was a poor substitute for the apparatus of a state, and that his economic writings' authority is greatest precisely where they reason from the system's structure and least where they must estimate its magnitudes. The same distribution of strength and weakness runs through the whole corpus, and a reader who learns to see it in the economics will see it everywhere.

## The military reputation among the historians

Since the record sections have weighed the war commissar's methods, a short section should weigh his standing with the profession that studies war, because it is one of the few arenas where the verdict on Trotsky was substantially settled before the archives opened and has survived their opening with its main lines intact. Military historians of the Russian civil war, a field whose leading figures have included scholars with no sympathy for Bolshevism in any of its factions, have generally credited the Red Army's creation as an organisational achievement of the first order and its creator as the war's outstanding political soldier on either side: the man who understood that the war would be won by the side that built a state behind its army, who took the unfashionable decisions, the officers, the discipline, the terror at the front, that the building required, and who supplied in his own person the mobile reserve of authority that a disintegrating front consumes. The same literature is equally settled on the debit entries: the hostage system as a deliberate instrument rather than an excess, the exemplary shootings, the subordination of military judgement at moments to political theatre, and the Warsaw overreach in which he shared. What the military literature adds to this profile is the professional's version of the counterfactual constraint. The qualities that made him, in the specialists' judgement, the indispensable man of 1919 are not qualities that can be separated from the record's hardness, because the specialists locate the indispensability precisely in the hardness: an organiser willing to shoot stragglers and hostage officers' families was not incidental to the army's survival but, in their analysis, a working part of its mechanism. A reader who finds that analysis persuasive has not been recruited to a faction; the reader has been shown, by the least partisan literature this biography draws on, why the contribution and the record in this life are one fabric and why every attempt to unpick them, in either direction, tears the evidence.

## The biographers: two traditions and the scholarship between them

The literature on this life divides into traditions that a reader should be able to identify on sight, because each tradition's method predicts its conclusions and because the serious scholarship of the last half century has been written in conscious resistance to both. The sympathetic tradition's monument is Isaac Deutscher's three volume biography, published between 1954 and 1963, The Prophet Armed, The Prophet Unarmed and The Prophet Outcast, whose titles alone announce the method: the life as a prophetic arc, armed in the revolution, unarmed in the succession struggle, outcast in exile. Deutscher was a Polish Marxist of independent stamp, no member of the movement he chronicled, and his biography remains the most readable and the most learned in the sympathetic line, built on the Amsterdam and Harvard archives and on interviews with the survivors, and written with a tragic sense that never quite becomes worship. Its limits are the tradition's: the record of 1918 to 1921 is present but is framed by emergency until the framing does exculpatory work, the Kronstadt chapter leans on the necessity argument its subject supplied, and the counterfactual glows at the edges of the exile volumes, where the alternative to Stalin is allowed to borrow light from the horror it did not commit. Deutscher knew the objection and half conceded it in his prefaces; the concession does not remove the tilt, and readers should use the trilogy as what it is, the indispensable narrative, audited as they read by the constraint this profile has applied.

The hostile tradition runs from the Soviet falsifications, in which he figured as a fascist agent and a lifelong wrecker, a literature beneath scholarly notice except as evidence about its authors, to a serious modern biography of prosecutorial intent, Robert Service's life of 2009, which worked the archives opened after 1991 with great industry and returned a verdict of unusual severity: a vain, cruel and self deceived man whose contribution to theory Service rates low and whose responsibility for the terror he rates as high as any founder's. Service's biography performed one service beyond dispute, in reasserting the record against a softening that had crept into parts of the sympathetic literature, and it drew a documented counter fire from scholars who catalogued its errors of transcription and translation and its habit of reaching for the damaging reading where the evidence allowed two. Between the traditions stands the scholarship this profile has leant on most: Geoffrey Swain's compact and judicious life, Baruch Knei-Paz's study of the exile's social and political thought, which remains the most careful map of the theory, Dmitri Volkogonov's hostile but archive rich account from inside the former regime's own records, Bertrand Patenaude's study of the final exile, and the military and social historians of the revolution and civil war whose work keeps correcting both camps. The pattern a reader should carry away is the one the whole biography has exhibited. On the facts, the traditions converge more than their tempers suggest: no serious scholar now denies the contributions, and none denies the record. The argument that remains is an argument about weight and about the counterfactual, and it is conducted, at its best, under exactly the discipline this profile has named, whether or not its practitioners use the name.

One further custodian of the record deserves naming, since the biography of a writer is incomplete without the fate of his papers. Trotsky was his own first archivist, carrying documents through every exile, selling a portion of his papers to Harvard in 1937 for the movement's funds and his household's security, with closed sections that scholars reached only decades later, and losing other portions to theft, police seizure and the attentions of the apparatus that hunted him, whose agents penetrated his secretariats and photographed his correspondence for Moscow's files. The modern biographer therefore reads the man through an archive that three forces curated: himself, the university that bought his papers as an investment in posterity, and the police state that burgled them as an investment in murder. That the record nonetheless supports the convergent account described above, contributions and record together, is the strongest evidence that the convergence is real and not a compromise manufactured for comfort.

## The balance sheet: a life assessed on its theory and its power

The series thesis for the profiles of those who held power requires the assessment to close where it began, with the theory and the exercise of power stated together, and the closing judgement of this profile can now be given in the form the evidence has earned. The contributions stand in their own column, and they are large. A theory of revolution in the backward countries that predicted 1917's course more accurately than any rival on offer. A theory of combined development that historians and sociologists still quarry. A narrative history of the revolution that belongs to the literature of its century. An analysis of the bureaucratic state that named a new social formation while it was still forming, and a body of writing on fascism that stands as the period's most prescient political analysis. A literary criticism and a memoir that writers with no politics in common have honoured. No verdict on the record reduces that column by a single entry, and the hostile biographer who implies otherwise has left the evidence to argue with the caricature.

The record stands in its column with equal independence. The Red Army's discipline, with its hostages and its exemplary shootings, designed and defended by him. Kronstadt, stormed on his government's ultimatum and defended by him for nineteen years. Labour militarisation, proposed beyond the point his own leader would go. The faction ban, supported in the year of its making and suffered in the year of his expulsion. A theory of terror, written in cold blood in 1920, which supplied the justification his successors needed for methods he lived to be destroyed by. No verdict on the contributions reduces that column either, and the sympathetic biographer who frames it as weather has left the evidence to argue with the devotion.

What the two columns make together is not a sum but a problem, and it is the problem the twentieth century kept posing in different uniforms: whether the intelligence that diagnoses a society most deeply is thereby fitted to rule it more justly, or whether the diagnosis and the rule draw on the same faculty, the will to see the whole board and move its pieces, whose exercise in power produces the hostage order and the history book with the same fluency. Trotsky's life is the problem's most instructive case because the gifts and the damage were both at the maximum the century offered, in one man, documented by himself at every stage. He asked, in the testament written in the last February of his life, to be judged by a generation that would clear the revolution's name, and he cannot have expected the judgement to be comfortable. This profile's judgement is the one the counterfactual constraint delivers when it is applied without fear to both columns. The theory survives its author. The record survives its excuses. And the claim that the first column redeems the second, or that the second refutes the first, is the claim this biography was written to retire, in favour of the harder and more useful discipline of holding a great and damaging life in a single frame and refusing, on the evidence, to look away from either half of it.

That discipline, and not a verdict of acquittal or of condemnation, is what this profile finally hands its readers. Verdicts on Trotsky have been issued continuously for a century, by states, by movements, by tribunals real and improvised, and the shelf of them has settled nothing, because a verdict is only as good as the evidence discipline that produced it. The discipline travels. It can be carried to any life in which brilliance and power were joined, and it will do the same work there that it has done here, separating what a person made from what a person did, and forbidding the comfortable arithmetic in which one is spent to pay for the other.

## Studying this life: sources, sequence and the companion tools

A short guide to working further closes the analytical part of the profile. The primary sequence that repays study runs from My Life for the formation, through the History of the Russian Revolution for the mind at full stretch, to The Revolution Betrayed for the analysis of the state, with Terrorism and Communism read between the first and second as the record's own theoretical statement, so that no reader mistakes the exile's Trotsky for the whole man. The fascism pamphlets reward reading in their chronological order, since their authority is cumulative and date stamped. Students assembling the timeline of the life, the two Siberias, the two soviets, the fronts, the oppositions and the four countries of the final exile, will find the exercise easier with the dates laid out as a working set; the companion [Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) provide a timeline card format suited to exactly that work, and the persona sections that follow build on it for each kind of reader in turn.

One further study discipline is worth stating, because this subject punishes the casual reader more than most. His bibliography is enormous and uneven, and it was assembled across forty years of polemic in which the same events are narrated repeatedly for different audiences, so that a quotation can almost always be found for any portrait a writer wishes to paint. The safeguard is chronological: read any judgement of his in its date order, ask what he knew and what office he held when he wrote it, and treat the exile's retrospective arrangements of the earlier record, however brilliant, as testimony from an interested witness whose interest the biography has mapped. Applied consistently, the safeguard dissolves most of the literature's standing quarrels into their components, since the sympathetic and hostile traditions differ less about what the documents say than about which decade's documents they allow to govern the verdict, and a reader who has fixed the dates has already done the hardest part of judging him.

## The last book: the biography of Stalin

The work on his desk when the assassin entered is the biography's most symbolic unfinished object: a life of Stalin, commissioned by an American publisher, on which he had worked through 1939 and the first half of 1940 and which his death cut off in its later chapters. The book was published posthumously in 1941 in an edition assembled from his draft, and it has divided readers ever since, including his own followers, because it is the least characteristic of his major works in method. Where the History had built its case from documents and class analysis, the Stalin biography reached for psychology, for the formation of a personality in a Georgian seminary and a Caucasian underground, and argued that mediocrity, raised to absolute power by an apparatus, becomes a historical force of a peculiarly destructive kind. Critics charged that the book explained the bureaucracy by the bureaucrat, reversing his own materialism at the moment it mattered most, and that its animus distorted its evidence in the chapters his editors completed. Defenders answered that the draft's unfinished state forbids the confident verdict, and that its core argument, the apparatus selecting for the qualities its master embodied, was the theory of The Revolution Betrayed in biographical dress. The biographical significance for this profile lies less in the dispute than in the choice of the task itself. At sixty, with the war begun and his security collapsing, he chose to spend his remaining strength explaining his enemy to the enemy's future judges, in a genre, the hostile biography, that he would have dismantled in another man's hands. The choice completes the pattern of the exile: the pen as the last weapon, aimed at the record, on the wager that the record outlives the apparatus. The ice axe interrupted the wager mid sentence, and the judgement of its success belongs to the long run he always cited, which is still running.

## The Bulletin of the Opposition: an exile's press

One institution of the exile deserves record as a feat in its own right, the Russian language Bulletin of the Opposition, founded in 1929, weeks after the deportation, and published for a decade from Berlin, then Paris, then New York as the capitals closed to it. The Bulletin was the nervous system of his movement inside the Soviet Union and across the emigration: it carried his analyses, the opposition's documents, reports smuggled from the prisons and camps where his supporters were disappearing, and the running indictment of the regime's falsifications, set in type by volunteers and carried across borders in luggage and in memory. Its circulation was counted in thousands and its influence in a currency no circulation measures, since it was for years the only uncensored Russian political journal reaching readers inside the country, and the Soviet police state hunted its couriers with a seriousness that amounted to a review. The journal's file is now a primary source for the period's hidden history, and its existence explains an otherwise puzzling feature of the exile's authority: the apparatus in Moscow answered the Bulletin with forged issues, planted correspondents and assassination, which are the tributes power pays to print it cannot suppress. For the biography, the Bulletin completes the account of the exile's instruments. He had lost the army, the party and the state. He retained a printing schedule, a mailing list and the fastest analytical pen in Russian politics, and with those he maintained, for eleven years, the fiction and partly the reality of an alternative leadership in being, awaiting the crisis that would recall it. The crisis came as war, and found the headquarters in a guarded house in Coyoacan and the editor within weeks of his murder. The Bulletin outlived him by an issue or two, and then the tradition it served passed to the movement whose legacy this series treats separately.

## The commissars: how the army was held together

The Red Army's dual structure deserves a closer account than the building narrative above could give it, because the commissar system was the regime's most original institutional invention and its inventor's signature is on its logic. A conscript army officered by the old regime's professionals posed an obvious problem: its commanders' loyalty was purchased, supervised or coerced, and could not be assumed. The solution, generalised in 1918 and defended by Trotsky against every proposal to relax it, paired each commander with a political commissar of party standing, without whose countersignature no order took effect, and built beside the chain of command a political administration that ran the army's newspapers, clubs, literacy schools and agitation. The system did three kinds of work at once. It policed the specialists, giving the state an observer at every headquarters. It educated the conscripts, in an army where the political departments' literacy campaigns taught peasant soldiers their letters out of the same pamphlets that taught them the war's meaning. And it transmitted the centre's will downward faster than the military chain could, which is why the war commissar's train, with its tribunal and its printing press, was the system's emblem rather than its exception. The biography should register the system's double posterity without flinching. As an instrument for holding a doubtful army together it worked, and every revolutionary and several non revolutionary armies since have copied some version of it. As a template for the party's later government of everything, the countersignature habit, the political supervisor installed beside the specialist in the factory, the university and eventually the laboratory, it was the germ of the administered society he spent his exile analysing, installed by his own hand, for reasons that at the time were reasons. The counterfactual constraint consists of noticing both posterities and declining to choose between them.

The army's internal culture adds a human register that the institutional account misses. He drafted the text of the soldier's solemn oath. He instituted decorations and awarded them in person at the front, understanding before most of his colleagues that a mass army runs on honours as much as on fear. The train's newspaper, the agitprop journeys, the theatrical arrival of the war commissar at a broken sector with poets and a film unit in his baggage, all of it was morale engineering by a man who had studied crowds since 1905 and who treated an army, correctly, as a crowd that must be kept from remembering it can disperse. The soldiers' memory of him, preserved in the civil war's memoir literature, is warmer than the peasants' memory of his requisition detachments and colder than his movement's memory of the exile, and a biography is richer for holding the three temperatures at once.

## Britain through his eyes: the general strike and the Anglo-Russian committee

His engagement with Britain gives the biography a compact case of the opposition years' international method, and it begins with a book. Where Is Britain Going?, published in 1925, analysed the British empire's decline, the Labour Party's rise and the British working class's traditions with a respect that surprised readers who expected contempt for reformism, and argued that Britain's very gradualism stored up a sharper eventual crisis, since a class formed by a century of concessions would meet the concessions' end without revolutionary preparation. The analysis was tested within a year. In May 1926 Britain's trade union movement called the general strike in support of the miners, and the strike's leadership, after nine days, surrendered it unconditionally while the miners fought on alone for months. Trotsky and the opposition attacked, in the same campaign, the Anglo-Russian Trade Union Committee, the alliance between the Soviet unions and the British union leadership that Stalin's majority prized as a diplomatic asset, arguing that the alliance's price, silence about the British leaders' conduct, had disarmed criticism at the strike's decisive moment and taught the world's workers that solidarity meant etiquette. The episode is a small key that opens a large door in the biography. The opposition's international grievances, Germany and China later, Spain at the end, all have the same shape as the British one of 1926: an alliance or a line imposed by the apparatus for reasons of state, a catastrophe that the opposition had forecast in writing, and a dossier compiled afterward for a movement that existed mainly on paper. Whether a different line would have changed the British outcome is doubtful, and the biography should say so, since the general strike's defeat had British causes deeper than any committee. What the episode settles is the character of his opposition's internationalism. It was not a nostalgia for 1917. It was a working method, applied country by country with local knowledge that his reading and his correspondents kept unusually current, and its judgements survive quotation in the scholarly literature of countries whose politics he never touched at first hand.

## The other wars inside the war: Makhno, Tambov and the peasant risings

The civil war's main fronts against the White generals have occupied the record sections, but the war commissar's responsibility extends to a second set of wars that the sympathetic literature treats as footnotes and that belong, in this profile's discipline, in the record's main text. In Ukraine, the insurgent army of Nestor Makhno, an anarchist led peasant movement that fought Denikin with genuine effect, allied with the Red Army twice and was turned on twice when its usefulness ended, the second time in November 1920, within days of the joint victory over Wrangel, when Red forces attacked their recent allies and hunted the movement to destruction across the following year. The operations ran under commanders of the southern front, and the political responsibility ran where this profile has consistently placed it: the war commissariat was Trotsky's, he had publicly condemned the Makhnovists as bandits and kulak agents in 1919 and had ordered the outlawing of their congress in that year, and the liquidation of an allied peasant army the moment the common enemy fell was conducted by the state he served at the summit. No statement of his from the exile years revisits the episode with regret. It stands in the record beside Kronstadt, and the two together describe the perimeter of the regime's tolerance in the years he helped direct it: soviets that voted correctly were the revolution's glory, and movements, however revolutionary their origin, that stood outside the party's command were enemies by definition.

The Tambov rising, the great peasant revolt in the grain province south east of Moscow that burned from 1920 into 1921, drew the same logic onto the Russian village itself. The rising, led by Alexander Antonov and rooted in the requisition detachments' seizures, fielded a peasant army, a political programme of free soviets and free trade in grain, and a guerrilla war the regime could not finish with ordinary forces. Its suppression was assigned to Tukhachevsky with extraordinary powers, and the methods, hostage taking of rebels' families, concentration camps for the hostages, the execution of hostages for concealed weapons, the use of poison gas against forest refuges on the authority of orders in the documentary record, were the civil war's methods turned inward at full strength, approved at the summit of a government in which the war commissar was the second military figure of the state. Trotsky's personal share in the Tambov decisions is less directly documented than his Kronstadt responsibility, and the profile should mark the difference honestly: the accountability here is the collective accountability of the leadership and of the commissariat whose commander conducted the campaign, not the signature on the ultimatum. The distinction matters to the biography precisely because his defenders have sometimes used its absence at Kronstadt's level to float the whole category of responsibility away. It will not float. A war commissar in a government suppressing its own countryside by hostage camps is a party to the suppression in every sense a reader of biography is entitled to weigh, and the counterfactual constraint files Tambov accordingly, at the weight the documents support, neither inflated to a personal order nor dissolved into the war's general weather.

Set beside the German pamphlets and the History, these episodes complete the education this profile exists to give. The man who would read a crowd's mood in a Berlin demonstration with seismographic sensitivity had governed, a decade earlier, through the crushing of crowds whose demands, free soviets, an end to requisitions, quoted his own revolution's founding promises back to it. The biography does not have to decide whether the crushing was necessary. It has to prevent the reader from deciding the counterfactual, what his state would have done with dissent, in ignorance of what his state did with it while he stood at its summit, and for that purpose Tambov and the Makhno liquidation are not footnotes. They are among the load bearing facts.

## The portraitist of his own generation

A minor genre of the corpus carries a major part of the biographical evidence about its author's inner life, the portraits and obituaries he wrote of the generation that made and lost the revolution with him. The genre begins with the 1924 sketch of Lenin written in the weeks after the death, in which the organiser of October set down the partner of his last seven years with a candour about the man's scale and limits that no official cult could have printed, and it runs through the exile years in a sequence of reckonings with the dead: the diplomat Joffe, whose suicide in 1927, in protest at the opposition's expulsion, drew from him a public farewell that turned a funeral into the opposition's last demonstration in Moscow; the old comrades of the civil war headquarters and the Vienna cafes as the terror consumed them one by one, each death answered by a memoir essay that fixed the man's real stature against the indictment the regime was preparing for him; and, in February 1938, the pamphlet for his son, Leon Sedov: son, friend, fighter, in which the father wrote the collaborator's political biography with a restraint that makes it the most affecting document of the exile. The portraits matter to the assessment for a reason beyond their pathos. They are the place where his historical method met no factional resistance, since the dead were beyond use, and in them the qualities his critics deny him are on open display: generosity to former opponents, precision about his own side's errors in small things, and a capacity to write a life, even an enemy's in the case of the Stalin biography's best chapters, as a product of forces larger than the person's will. A reader who wants to know what the biographer of this profile has tried to imitate, and what he would have thought of the attempt, could do worse than start with the portraits, where the subject is seen doing for others exactly what this article has attempted, at much greater length and under much harder discipline, to do for him: hold the contribution and the record in one frame, and let neither purchase the other's silence.

## For students and exam candidates

Students meeting Trotsky in a syllabus usually meet him as half of a comparison, and the mark earning move is to refuse the comparison's usual softness. Build answers on the two columns this profile has kept separate: the contribution, permanent revolution, the histories, the analysis of the bureaucracy, the fascism writings, and the record, the Red Army's discipline, Kronstadt, labour militarisation and the faction ban. An answer that names the counterfactual constraint and applies it to one episode, the trade union debate is the cleanest, since he stood beyond Lenin on compulsion, will outscore a page of narrative about the rivalry with Stalin. Learn the corrected facts that examiners plant as traps: the name came from a jailer on a forged passport, he joined the Bolsheviks only in 1917, and the weapon in Coyoacan was an ice axe. The structured revision companion for this cluster is the [luxemburg trotsky exam answers](/2014/12/15/luxemburg-trotsky-exam-answers/), and the timeline card method in the study section above will fix the sequence of the two exiles, the two soviets and the four countries of the last deportation before the examination does it for you.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This profile is built to be taught against itself, and the seminar room is where its discipline pays best. Set the two columns as the session's architecture: give one group the fascism pamphlets and the China dossier and the other group Terrorism and Communism and the Kronstadt documents, and let each present the strongest case its evidence supports before the groups exchange files. The trade union debate is the session's hinge, because it is the document set where the lazy portrait, the democrat in waiting, physically cannot be sustained, and students who have handled Lenin's more moderate platform beside his rival's militarisation proposal stop reaching for it. The structured comparison your strongest students will ask for next is the [trotskyism vs stalinism](/2014/11/15/trotskyism-vs-stalinism/) article, where the two systems are set side by side with the same refusal to net the columns off. Close the seminar on the counterfactual constraint as a transferable instrument, and invite the class to name the next biography, of any tradition, to which they would apply it.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Researchers should begin where the traditions divide and use the division as a finding aid. Deutscher's trilogy gives the narrative spine and the exile's texture; Knei-Paz gives the thought's most careful map; Service gives the prosecution at full archival stretch, to be read with the published corrections of his errors beside it; Swain and Volkogonov triangulate from opposite ends. The primary archive is split in a way that must shape any project: the papers he sold to Harvard, the holdings in Amsterdam, and the files of the apparatus that surveilled and burgled him, now partly open in Moscow, so that the same meeting may survive in his secretary's notes and in an agent's report, and the two versions are a source problem and a story in one. Journalists retelling the murder should fix the weapon, the ice axe, and the assassin's identity as a Soviet agent run through the secret police's apparatus, decorated after his release, since the romantic version, the lone fanatic with an ice pick, fails on both points. The movement's afterlife is treated in the [fourth international legacy](/2014/12/01/fourth-international-legacy/), and the open questions worth a researcher's season are marked in the FAQ below, on the evidence that would revise the picture of the revolution's second figure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Leon Trotsky?

Leon Trotsky, born Lev Bronstein in 1879 in Ukraine, was a Russian revolutionary, Marxist theorist and writer. He chaired the Petrograd soviet in 1917, organised the October insurrection through its Military Revolutionary Committee, founded and commanded the Red Army as war commissar, led the Left Opposition against the rising bureaucracy, and was expelled, exiled and finally deported. From exile he wrote his History of the Russian Revolution, the analysis of the Soviet state in The Revolution Betrayed, and the period's most prescient warnings on fascism, before a Soviet agent murdered him in Mexico in 1940.

### Q: What did Trotsky contribute to Marxist theory?

His contributions stand in four groups. The theory of permanent revolution argued that in a backward country the working class, once in power, is driven to socialist measures and to international extension. Uneven and combined development explained how archaic and modern forms fuse in such societies. The Revolution Betrayed theorised the Soviet Union as a degenerated workers' state ruled by a bureaucratic caste. And his writings on Germany between 1930 and 1933 produced the Marxist tradition's most prescient analysis of fascism, urging a united front of workers' organisations against the Nazi party while the Communist International dismissed the danger.

### Q: Why is he called Trotsky if his name was Bronstein?

The name was an accident that hardened into history. Escaping Siberian exile in 1902, Lev Bronstein travelled on a false passport and, needing a name to enter in the blank, wrote the name of a jailer from the Odessa prison where he had been held, chosen, by his own account in his autobiography, at random and without reflection. The pseudonym served the emigration, the party underground, the war commissariat and the exile, until Bronstein survived only as the private and family name. His children by his second marriage carried the name Sedov, from his wife Natalia Sedova.

### Q: Was Trotsky a Menshevik before he joined the Bolsheviks?

He sided with the Mensheviks at the 1903 congress and attacked Lenin's organisational centralism in print in 1904, warning that the party organisation would substitute itself for the class and end with a single dictator. But he broke with the Menshevik leadership within a few years over its alliance with liberalism, and for most of the fourteen years before 1917 he stood between the factions, attempting unity and quarrelling with both. He joined the Bolsheviks in the summer of 1917, when the Mezhraiontsy organisation fused with Lenin's party on a shared reading of the revolution's course. The accurate label for the middle years is independent, not Menshevik.

### Q: What did Trotsky do during the civil war?

As people's commissar for military and naval affairs from 1918, he created the Red Army from the wreckage of the old imperial forces. He introduced conscription, employed former tsarist officers under political commissars, restored ranks and strict discipline including the death penalty for desertion, and directed the war from an armoured train that served as his mobile headquarters. The army he built defeated the White armies and the intervention forces by the end of 1920. He also bore political responsibility for the regime's coercive methods, defended the terror in his book Terrorism and Communism, and carried responsibility for the suppression of the Kronstadt rising in 1921.

### Q: Was Trotsky responsible for repression too?

Yes, and this profile treats the answer as a matter of record rather than of allegiance. The disciplinary system of the army he commanded included executions for desertion and the registration of officers' families as surety for their loyalty. The terror of the civil war years was government policy which he supported in principle and defended in a book length argument against its critics. The Kronstadt rising was suppressed by forces of the state whose war commissar he was, and he defended the suppression in print for the rest of his life. His later opposition to the bureaucracy does not retire any of these facts, and serious biography no longer asks it to.

### Q: What is The Revolution Betrayed about?

Published in 1936 and written in Norwegian exile, The Revolution Betrayed is his analysis of what the Soviet Union had become. He argued that nationalised property and planning survived from October, but that a bureaucratic caste had usurped political power, ruling in its own interest and distributing privilege to itself, a Soviet Thermidor after the French reaction that followed the Jacobins. The bureaucracy, in his account, was a caste rather than a class, and the remedy was political revolution to restore soviet democracy while defending nationalised property. The book also contains his sharpest social analysis of the regime's retreats, on the family, on culture and on daily life.

### Q: What did Trotsky write about fascism?

Between 1930 and 1933, from exile in Turkey, he wrote a sequence of pamphlets and articles on Germany arguing that fascism was a mass movement of the ruined middle classes whose victory would mean the physical destruction of the labour movement's organisations for a generation. Against the Communist International's line, which treated social democracy as the main enemy under the label social fascism, he demanded a united front of communist and social democratic workers' organisations for the defensive struggle. He predicted the sequence of a Nazi victory, the banning of the unions and parties and the road to war, with an accuracy that historians across traditions regard as the most prescient Marxist analysis of the period.

### Q: How was Trotsky killed?

He was assassinated in Coyoacan, Mexico City, on 20 August 1940, and died the following day. The assassin, Ramon Mercader, was a Spanish communist recruited by the Soviet secret police who had spent two years building a false identity and a route into the fortified house as a trusted acquaintance of the household. Brought to the study on the pretext of an article for Trotsky to read, he struck him from behind with a shortened ice axe, a mountaineer's tool, concealed under a raincoat. The weapon is persistently misreported as an ice pick. Mercader served twenty years in a Mexican prison and was afterwards decorated in Moscow.

### Q: Would Trotsky have governed differently from Stalin?

The question is legitimate and the honest answer is disciplined speculation. For a difference stand his opposition programme of 1923, with its demands for party democracy and soviet revival, his strategic flexibility in the fascism writings, and the self questioning recorded in his 1935 diary. Against a substantial difference stand his own documents of 1918 to 1921: the defended terror, the hostage system, the proposal to militarise labour pressed beyond Lenin's position, Kronstadt defended for nineteen years, and his support for the ban on factions. This profile's rule, the counterfactual constraint, weights the second list as evidence about a hypothetical reign more heavily than the first, since it records conduct in power rather than intentions in exile.

### Q: How does the counterfactual constraint discipline judgements of Trotsky?

The counterfactual constraint is this profile's rule that claims about what Trotsky would have done in power must be built from evidence of what he did in power, and from positions he held when they cost him something, rather than from his exile self portrait or from the horror of his rival's record. Stalin's crimes are evidence about Stalin; converting them into proof of a humane alternative requires the further step of testing the alternative's own documents, which is where the record of 1918 to 1921 enters. The constraint does not forbid the counterfactual question. It sets the standard of proof an answer must meet before it counts as history rather than devotion or prosecution.

### Q: What should a biographer separate between Trotsky the writer and Trotsky the commander?

A biographer should separate the theatres without separating the man. The writer worked with documents, drafts and deadlines, and his finest products, the History, the fascism pamphlets, the analysis of the bureaucracy, came from opposition and exile, where the pen was his only instrument. The commander worked with armies, hostages and firing squads, and his record belongs to 1918 to 1921, where the state's instruments were in his hands. Neither theatre explains the other away: the literary gifts do not soften an order, and the orders do not refute an argument. The separation exists so that each product can be judged in its own column, and so that their coexistence in one life is faced rather than managed.

### Q: Why did exile change the character of his work?

Exile removed the instruments of action and left the instruments of analysis. Deported in 1929 after expulsion and internal exile, he lived in Turkey, France, Norway and Mexico under surveillance, dependent on a small secretariat and a scattered movement, and the energy that had run a war commissariat went into the page: the autobiography, the three volume History, the fascism writings, The Revolution Betrayed and an enormous correspondence. Exile also fixed his characteristic subject, the revolution's fate judged from outside the state it had built, and his characteristic method, the documentary dossier deployed against official falsification. The work grew longer in perspective and lonelier in address, written increasingly for a posterity he did not expect to be rescued from.

### Q: How did the Red Army's creation sit with his earlier politics?

Uneasily, and the unease is the biographical point. The chairman of the 1905 soviet, the tribune of a self governing workers' council, built an army on the opposite principles: appointed officers, abolished soldiers' committees, restored ranks and saluting, and employed the old regime's officer corps under commissar supervision. He defended each reversal as the price of survival and fought the party's military opposition, which wanted partisan and militia forms, at the 1919 congress and won. The creation therefore shows the hierarchy of his commitments under pressure, with victory ranked above the democratic forms the revolution had proclaimed, a ranking he stated openly in his writings of those years rather than concealing.

### Q: What evidence would revise the picture of Trotsky as the revolution's second figure?

The picture rests on converging evidence: the operational record of October 1917, the war commissariat's achievement, the documentary scale of the theoretical work, and the testimony of opponents who treated him as the succession's central figure. It would be revised by evidence that redistributed the operational credit, for instance documentation that the Military Revolutionary Committee's work was directed elsewhere, or by demonstration that key texts attributed to him were substantially ghost written, or by archival findings that recast his role in the succession struggle as marginal rather than central. The opening of the Soviet archives after 1991 produced corrections in detail and some damaging documents, but it confirmed the picture's main lines, which is why the traditions described in this profile now argue about weight and the counterfactual rather than about the figure's scale.
