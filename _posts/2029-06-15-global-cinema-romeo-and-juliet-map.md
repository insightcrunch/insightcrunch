The screen life of Romeo and Juliet does not belong to Verona, or to London, or to Hollywood. It belongs equally to a Mexico City bus station where a rich girl dies in a poor boy's arms, to a Singapore hawker centre where two chicken rice stalls wage the feud, to a Rio de Janeiro beach kiosk where police bullets do the work of the poison and the dagger, and to a South African township where the lovers' deaths begin a community's healing. This article maps that worldwide screen life as one object: the global cinema of the play, surveyed territory by territory, graded row by row, and judged as a whole. The map covers the cinemas the Anglophone and Italian film articles in this series treat as the centre, and then keeps going, into Mexico, Brazil, Spain, Portugal, Egypt, India, Singapore, South Korea, Indonesia, South Africa and beyond, logging the versions that filmographies record, the retellings that scholarship documents, the titles that merely borrow the lovers' names, and the territories where a search found nothing at all.

The map's method is its argument in miniature. Every entry carries a grade. A documented adaptation stands on a filmographic record, a festival listing, a national database or a scholarly account. A documented retelling stands on the same evidence with a looser hold on the plot. A title citation borrows the names and nothing else, and the map says so. A reported entry rests on a single source and is labelled as such. A searched-and-not-found entry records the search itself, because a map of world cinema that hides its blank spaces is a sales brochure, not a map. Under this discipline the famous universality of the play becomes a testable claim rather than a compliment, and the result of the test is the verdict this article defends from its first section to its last.

## The Verdict First: A Skeleton Travels, the Play Stays Home

The verdict is this. Global cinema did not embrace the play; it rebuilt the play's skeleton inside local materials, and the rebuilds differ from one another more than they resemble the original. What travels is a structure of three parts: two groups locked in a feud neither can explain to an outsider's satisfaction, a pair of young lovers produced by those groups, and a machinery of missed messages and bad timing that carries the lovers toward a reckoning. What does not travel is nearly everything else. The verse stays home, with one parodic exception in Mexico. The Friar's cell, the Nurse's garrulity, Mercutio's Queen Mab flight, the architecture of the tomb: these dissolve at the border and are replaced by whatever the receiving cinema keeps in stock. A hawker stall replaces the great house. A football scarf replaces the family colour. A checkpoint replaces the failed letter. The play's own Prologue announces a story of "two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue, lines 1 to 2), and the world map's first finding is that almost no cinema keeps the households. Cinemas keep the alikeness and the dignity contest, and recast the households as classes, clans, clubs, regions, sects and stalls.

The endings prove the point with census force. In the play, the Prince closes the action over two bodies and pronounces the summary the whole tradition quotes: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3, closing lines). On the world map, that double death is one option among several, and the option a cinema chooses is predicted less by fidelity than by genre. The map's comedies marry the lovers or reconcile their families without exception. The map's melodramas kill at least one lover, and they distribute the killing with local precision: a suitor's stray bullet in Mexico City, police fire in Rio de Janeiro, a political war in Thokoza, the lovers' own hands in a Gujarat town of guns. A story whose ending each culture rewrites is not a universal story in the strong sense. It is a portable one, and the difference between those two words is the subject of this article.

The cliche that governs casual writing about the play keeps it Anglo-Italian: an English text, an Italian setting, a lineage running through the great screen versions of the last century. The map restores what the cliche crops out. Measured by documented production rather than by reputation, the play's screen life is polycentric, and several of its most instructive versions were made by industries that the standard histories of Shakespeare on film treat as footnotes. Mexico filmed the play in 1943, inside its golden age, as a verse parody built around the country's defining comic star. Spain built two flamenco tragedies on Roma clan feuds, one of them nominated for an Academy Award. Egypt's golden age produced a village analogue that entered a Berlin festival and, decades later, a library's canon of the hundred greatest Egyptian films. None of these works needs the adjective universal to justify its place on the map. Each needs only its record.

The sections that follow earn the verdict in order. The grading system comes first, with the artifact itself, because the map is only as good as its evidence discipline. The recurring localization pattern comes next. The territories then follow in clusters, each given its record and its grade. A census of endings gathers the pattern into numbers. Three named critics argue the universality question, and their disagreement is adjudicated against the map's own data. The blank spaces receive a section of their own, because they carry evidence too. The verdict returns at the end, restated with the census behind it.

### What does the map mean by calling a film an adaptation?

An adaptation, on this map, is a film whose debt to the play is documented and structural: the feud, the lovers and the reckoning all descend from the play, whether the film keeps the names, the setting or neither. A retelling keeps the structure with a documented but looser grip.

The distinction matters because the world's filmographies are generous with the play's title, and generosity is not evidence. Where a catalogue, a festival record, a national film database or a scholar documents the descent, the map grades the film an adaptation or a retelling. Where the only document is the title, the map grades a citation. The grades keep the census honest, and the census keeps the verdict honest.

## How the InsightCrunch Global-Cinema Map Grades Its Evidence

The artifact of this article is the InsightCrunch global-cinema map, and its rows are printed below in full. Five grades govern the rows. The first grade, documented adaptation, requires the play's structure and a record in a filmography, festival archive, national database or scholarly study. The second, documented retelling, requires the same record with a freer hand: the feud and the lovers survive, the plot is rebuilt. The third grade, title citation, is a warning disguised as an entry: the film borrows the lovers' names for their market value and nothing else, and two of the map's rows exist chiefly to teach readers to spot the species. The fourth grade, reported only, marks a single-source claim, carried because the territory matters and flagged because the evidence is thin. The fifth grade, searched and not found, records a territory where the survey looked in filmographies and scholarship and found no verifiable version; it is the grade that separates a map from a celebration.

The sources stand in a hierarchy, and the map states it plainly so that any reader can re-run the survey. Festival records and national film databases outrank trade press. Peer-reviewed scholarship outranks reference aggregation. A university archive's catalogue outranks a streaming site's synopsis. Where sources conflict, the map follows the stronger source and, where the conflict itself instructs, reports it. A researcher assembling a map of this kind keeps the working archive in a local store such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where each film's grade sits beside the catalogue record, festival listing or journal article that earned it, and where a downgrade can be recorded the day a stronger source demotes a row. The map's grades are therefore not verdicts carved in stone. They are the state of the evidence, dated by the survey and open to correction by better documents.

Two exclusions deserve statement before the rows. The first is the parallel: a story that resembles the play without descending from it. China supplies the great case, and it receives its own section below; the Butterfly Lovers legend is older than any contact between Chinese storytelling and Shakespeare, and resemblance is not descent. The second exclusion is the stage. The map is a cinema map. Stage adaptations enter only at the map's edge, where a country's screen record cannot be told without them, and they are labelled as stage each time. With those rules stated, the map follows.

### What separates a documented retelling from a reported one?

A documented retelling stands on at least two independent legs of the evidence hierarchy, or on one leg of the highest class: a festival record, a national database entry, a peer-reviewed study. A reported entry stands on one leg of a lower class: a single review, a single upload record, a single catalogue line.

The Brazilian and Mexican rows stand on all three legs. The map carries reported entries because territories such as Kenya and the Philippines would otherwise vanish from a survey they belong to, and it flags them because a single source can be wrong in ways a chorus of sources rarely is. The reader should trust the grades in proportion, and the map's census counts only the documented rows.

## The Localization Pattern: The Feud Changes First

Read down the map's feud column and a grammar emerges, consistent enough across five continents to count as the article's central finding. A receiving cinema changes the feud first, the names second, the plot machinery third, and the ending last and most freely. The feud changes first because the feud is the play's point of contact with a society: it must name a division the home audience already feels in its own streets, or the film has no pressure to work with. Verona's houses are "both alike in dignity" (Prologue, line 2), and that alikeness is the one element every localization preserves with care. The rival chicken rice stalls of Singapore are alike in dignity to the gram. The Persija and Persib supporter firms of Jakarta are alike in dignity and in danger. The Jeolla groom and the Gyeongsang bride of the Korean comedy carry regions alike in pride and unequal in the capital's esteem. Where the two sides are not alike, the story tilts into a different genre, the rich girl and the poor boy of class melodrama, and the map's class rows, Mexico's Amar te duele and Brazil's Era Uma Vez..., show the tilt at work: the feud of equals becomes the barrier of unequals, and the ending darkens in proportion.

The names change second because the names are the cheapest part of the inheritance. Hassan and Nayima, Gugu and Andile, Rangga and Desi, Fenson and Audrey, Dé and Nina: the map's lovers carry local names that do local work, marking language, faith, region and class in a syllable or two. The play itself authorizes the practice. Juliet's question from the orchard, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2, lines 43 to 44), is the most quoted speech in the play's afterlife precisely because every adapter needs its permission. The map's evidence adds a rider the quotation usually escapes: the name changes, but the naming pattern survives. Again and again the lovers' names rhyme with each other, pair off, or carry the initials of the originals, as if the films were keeping a receipt for a purchase they had otherwise transformed beyond recognition.

The machinery changes third, and its replacements are the most revealing details on the map. The play runs on failed communication: a letter entrusted to Friar John never reaches Mantua because plague seals the houses (5.2). Each cinema replaces the failure with its own era's characteristic obstruction. In the Palestinian school film In Fair Palestine, the messenger is stopped at a checkpoint, and the machinery of the tragedy becomes the machinery of occupation with a precision no allegory could improve. In the class melodramas, the obstruction is administrative: a father buys airline tickets, a family arranges removal to another country, a daughter is locked inside the house. In the football films, the obstruction is the fixture list and the away end. The machinery is where a localization confesses what its society believes actually separates people, and the confessions differ so widely that the map treats the machinery column as its most honest data after the endings.

The ending changes last and most freely because the ending is where genre collects its fee. Section by section below, the pattern repeats with a regularity that hardens into law: a cinema that receives the play as comedy returns the lovers alive, and a cinema that receives it as melodrama returns at least one of them dead. The play's own ending, in which the fathers shake hands over the bodies and promise statues of pure gold (5.3), survives intact almost nowhere. What survives instead is the ending's function, reconciliation purchased at a price, and each cinema sets the price in its own currency: a wedding, a handshake between stallholders, a community's first step toward healing, or two graves.

The map's scenes tell the same story at smaller scale. The first meeting survives everywhere and mutates everywhere: a wedding performance in Egypt, where Nayima first watches Hassan sing; a shopping mall in Mexico City, where class does the introducing; a street brawl in Jakarta, where the lovers meet mid-ambush; a college rehearsal in Singapore, where the play itself does the matchmaking. The balcony, the play's most portable image, fares worse. It survives as a stage inside other stages, in Singapore's rehearsal room and on Hong Kong's night-market boards, and it survives inverted in Rio, where the poor boy at his beach kiosk gazes up at the rich girl's apartment window in a sightline the film holds until the police close it. Elsewhere the balcony dissolves into terraces, bus stations and checkpoints, the vertical architecture of longing replaced by the horizontal architecture of movement and its permission. A scene census of this kind cannot be tabulated as cleanly as the endings, but its drift is unmistakable: the map's cinemas keep the meeting, translate the feud, and let the balcony go.

### Why does the feud change before anything else does?

The feud changes first because the feud is the only element of the play that must be believed before anything else can be felt. An audience grants the lovers their passion freely; young love needs no local papers. The opposition to that love must be drawn from the audience's map of the forbidden, or the film's pressure collapses into costume.

A Barcelona audience of 1963 knew what a Roma clan feud cost. A Jakarta audience of 2009 knew what a Persija shirt risked in Bandung. A Seoul audience of 2011 knew what a Jeolla accent confessed in a Busan drawing room. Directors reach for the local feud first because it is the load-bearing wall; the rest of the house can be rebuilt around it.

## Mexico: A Verse Parody in 1943, a Class Tragedy in 2002

Mexico holds the map's most instructive pair of rows, because the same industry filmed the play twice across six decades and changed its mind about everything except the story's usefulness. The first Mexican entry is also the map's earliest fully documented feature outside the Anglophone and Italian industries. Romeo y Julieta, released in September 1943, was directed by Miguel M. Delgado and built around Mario Moreno, known to the world as Cantinflas, with María Elena Marqués as his Juliet. The film belongs to the cycle of literary parodies that made Cantinflas the defining comic figure of Mexican cinema's golden age, following his Three Musketeers of the previous year, and it treats the play with a disrespect that scholarship has learned to take seriously. The dialogue is in verse, rhymed and tumbling, so that the parody keeps the play's music while discarding its reverence. A frame device sets a penniless taxi driver inside a scheme to pass an actor off at a rich man's house, and the tragedy unfolds inside the frame as a handsomely mounted production in which the clown keeps breaking the furniture of the verse. A duel is fought in front of Juliet's body in the tomb to heighten the silliness, and the closing gag turns on the performer's punishment by outraged custodians of the text.

Ignacio M. Sánchez-Prado's study of Cantinflas and world literature gives the parody its critical frame: comedic adaptation of this kind is a popular cosmopolitanism, a working-class audience's way of owning the canon by laughing at it. The map accepts that reading and adds the census point. Mexico's parody assumes total familiarity. A clown can only mug his way through a tomb scene for an audience that already knows what the tomb scene holds, and the 1943 film is therefore evidence of the play's deep domestication in Mexican popular culture a full generation before the art-house era. Alfredo Michel Modenessi, the dean of Mexican Shakespeare scholarship, places the film at the head of a national pattern: in Mexican cinema, Shakespeare arrives unabashed only as raw material for transformation, and the Cantinflas film stood alone for decades as the country's one integral screen derivation from a play. The parody is not a failure of respect. It is the local form respect took.

The second Mexican row reverses every term of the first. Amar te duele, released in November 2002, was written by Carolina Rivera and directed by Fernando Sariñana, and it rebuilds the play as an urban class tragedy for the neoliberal city. Ulises, played by Luis Fernando Peña, is the son of a market-stall clothing trader, a graffiti artist from the poor suburbs; Renata, played by Martha Higareda in her film debut, is an heiress of Santa Fe, the capital's glass-towered rich district. The feud is not between their families, who barely know each other, but between their classes, whose mutual contempt the film renders in the period's own vocabulary of slurs and gated distances. The Paris figure, Renata's boyfriend Francisco, carries the tragedy's weapon. At a bus station, as the lovers attempt flight, Francisco draws a gun he barely knows how to use and shoots Renata by accident. She dies in Ulises's arms. He survives her, and the film's last movement leaves him at her grave.

The halved death deserves the map's attention. Shakespeare's machinery kills both lovers because the feud's logic, once armed, spares neither house. Sariñana's film kills the rich girl and spares the poor boy, and the distribution is the film's thesis about its city: class violence in Mexico City falls on bodies in asymmetric patterns, and the survivor's punishment is to live inside the asymmetry. Sánchez-Prado's reading of the film within Mexican neoliberal cinema notes its commercial uniqueness, a class-crossing romance marketed upward to the classes it indicts. The map files the film as the class row against which the feud-of-equals rows can be measured, and its ending enters the census as the map's clearest proof that who dies is a local decision.

Argentina, the Spanish-speaking giant beside Mexico, supplies the map's cautionary row. The standard filmographies offer Romeo y Julita of 1954, directed by Enrique Carreras, and the title's diminutive is the tell: Julita is not Julieta, and the film is an apartment farce about an engaged couple and a flat that belongs to someone else. The play is present as a pun. Argentina's genuine screen life of the play runs through television, where the filmographies record a 1966 version directed by María Herminia Avellaneda and a full telenovela development as late as 2007, produced by Raúl Lecouna. The map grades the feature a title citation and the television record a documented presence, and draws the moral in the blank-spaces section below: a filmography is a list of titles until somebody checks the films.

## Brazil: Football Farce and Favela Tragedy from One Industry

Brazil is the map's only territory besides Mexico to hold two fully documented features, and the pair states the genre law in its purest form, because the same national industry produced the play as a wedding comedy and as a double killing within three years of each other. O Casamento de Romeu e Julieta, released in March 2005 and directed by Bruno Barreto, translates the feud into the São Paulo football rivalry between Palmeiras and Corinthians. Julieta, played by Luana Piovani, is the daughter of a Palmeiras board member; Romeu, played by Marco Ricca, leads the organised Corinthians support. He spends the film disguised as a Palmeiras devotee, an imposture that functions as the comedy's balcony scene, courtship conducted under a false flag, and the disguise collapses in a confession delivered on an aircraft returning from Palmeiras's defeat in Tokyo. The title announces the ending: Romeo and Juliet get married. The feud of the terraces, which in Brazilian life has its own casualty lists, is defused by the oldest comic device in the tradition, the enemy absorbed into the family by marriage.

The comedy's lightness should not be mistaken for triviality. Football allegiance in São Paulo is inherited like a surname, policed like a border and confessed like a faith, which makes it the most exact modern equivalent the map records for Verona's house loyalty: arbitrary in origin, absolute in practice, and invisible as a choice to those born inside it. Barreto's film understands the equivalence and plays it straight beneath the farce. Romeu's false allegiance is a genuine betrayal in the eyes of both camps, and the father's rage at the revelation is Capulet's rage in modern dress, the fury of a man whose daughter has made his public identity ridiculous. The map grades the film a documented retelling and enters its marriage in the census as the comedy pole of the Brazilian pair.

Era Uma Vez..., released in July 2008 and directed by Breno Silveira, occupies the tragedy pole, and its Verona is the vertical geography of Rio de Janeiro. Dé, played by Thiago Martins, grows up in the Cantagalo favela, works honestly at a hot dog kiosk on Ipanema beach, and falls in love with Nina, played by Vitória Frate, the rich girl whose apartment faces the kiosk. Screen Daily's review named the film's descent without hesitation: a Romeo and Juliet tale of doomed love between a favela boy and an Ipanema heiress. The machinery is the favela's own: a brother murdered in childhood over a football fight, another brother returned from prison to seize the hill's drug trade, a kidnapping staged for ransom, a media campaign that brands the innocent lover the criminal. The ending rewrites the play's with a bleakness the census records in its own category. Cornered at the kiosk, Dé acts out a surrender, pretending to release Nina as a hostage. The police shoot him dead in the act of surrendering, unarmed and posing no threat. Nina seizes his fallen gun, fires wildly at the crowd, and the police shoot her dead as well.

Neither lover dies by the feud's hand, by poison, by a dagger or by mistake of timing. Both die by the state's, in front of cameras, in a sequence that Brazilian audiences read against a domestic record of police killings in favela operations. The localization pattern completes itself here: the feud supplies the pressure, the class geography supplies the barrier, and the killing is outsourced to the institution that the receiving society most fears. The play's Prince arrives after the deaths to moralise them; Silveira's film offers no prince and no moral, only the news cameras that helped cause the catastrophe by publishing the lover's face as a kidnapper's. Between Barreto's wedding and Silveira's double execution, Brazil demonstrates the map's law with unmatched clarity: the story is constant, the genre is a choice, and the ending follows the genre as a shadow follows a body.

Two Brazilian footnotes belong to the record. The children's company of Mauricio de Sousa carried the story to film in 1979, in Mônica e Cebolinha: No Mundo de Romeu e Julieta, a live-action feature of the Monica's Gang characters that began as a stage production and a comic; the map notes it at the edge of the survey as a documented juvenile adaptation in the national record. And the community theatre of Nós do Morro in the Vidigal favela staged Romeu e Julieta: Ontem Verona, Hoje Vidigal in 2009, Verona relocated to the hillside by the community that lives there; the production is stage, not cinema, and the map logs it as evidence that the favela localization Era Uma Vez... filmed had a theatrical life of its own.

## Iberia: Flamenco Clans, an Oscar Nomination and a Fictional Derby

Spain gives the map its deepest European bench outside Italy, and its two documented tragedies share a single feud: the Roma clans of the flamenco world, whose honour codes, arranged marriages and inherited enmities offered Spanish directors a Verona that needed no translation at all. Los Tarantos, released in November 1963 and directed by Francisco Rovira Beleta, adapts Alfredo Mañas's stage play, itself inspired by the tragedy, and sets the feud in Barcelona's Somorrostro, the shantytown district on the city's edge where the Zoronga and Taranto families hold their enmity. Rafael the Taranto falls for Juana the Zoronga at a wedding, in an echo of the Capulet feast, and the love crosses the clan line under the eyes of Angustias, Rafael's mother, played by Carmen Amaya in her final film. Amaya was the most celebrated flamenco dancer of her century, and Antonio Gades, who plays Rafael, carried the form's future; the film's dance sequences are not decoration but the feud's native language, challenge and courtship conducted in rhythm. The Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences nominated the film for the Best Foreign Language Film award, a distinction no other row on this map can claim, and the scholarly record has kept pace: Anita Raychawdhuri's study in Shakespeare Bulletin reads the film as a negotiation of Romani Catalan identity conducted through Shakespeare, the borrowed tragedy giving a marginalised community the classical stage it was denied elsewhere.

Montoyas y Tarantos, released in 1989 and directed by Vicente Escrivá, returns to the same feud a generation later with the names promoted to the title: Ana Montoya and Manuel Taranto love across the flamenco divide, with Paco de Lucía, the form's supreme guitarist, writing the music and two Goya awards confirming the film's standing in its national industry. The repetition is itself a finding. Spanish cinema did not merely borrow the play once; it discovered that the Roma clan system and the Veronese house system were the same machine in different dress, and it ran the machine twice, in 1963 under a dictatorship that policed public morality and in 1989 in a democracy, with the tragedy intact on both occasions. The map's census reads the pair as the strongest evidence in Europe for the feud-of-equals model: where a society possesses a living honour feud of its own, the adaptation keeps the play's shape and its ending, because the local material requires no softening.

Portugal's single row supplies the comic counterweight and a lesson in verisimilitude. Star Crossed, released in June 2009 and directed by Mark Heller, transports the feud to Portuguese football, but the production fictionalized the clubs, inventing Invicta and Castelo where the Brazilian and Indonesian films used real allegiances. The film was shot in English as a co-production, cast across countries, and received by its home critics with a shrug that the map records without pleasure: the idea earned credit, the execution did not. The contrast instructs. Barreto's São Paulo comedy works because Palmeiras and Corinthians are real faiths whose derbies stop a city; the Portuguese film's invented clubs carry no inherited hatred, and a feud nobody believes in cannot bend a plot. Localization is not a paint job. The local material must be load-bearing, or the structure stands empty.

## Egypt, Jerusalem and Ramallah: The Analogue, the Retelling and the Checkpoint

The Arab world's rows divide into three kinds, and the divisions teach the map's grading system better than any definition. Egypt supplies the analogue. Hassan wa Nayima, directed by Henry Barakat and released in 1959, is described across the reference record in a formula of remarkable consistency: a love story similar to Romeo and Juliet, set in the Egyptian countryside. The similarity is structural at one remove. Hassan, played by Muharram Fouad, is a poor village singer; Nayima, played by Soad Hosny in the role that began her ascent to the summit of Arab stardom, is the only heir of a land-hungry father. The feud of two houses becomes the veto of one house: the father refuses the singer's suit because his cousin Atwah, the play's Paris rebuilt as a village strongman, is to have the girl and the land together. The lovers elope and marry; the father retrieves his daughter, imprisons her, promises the marriage he intends to break, and the conflict tightens around the broken promise. There is no second family, no feud of equals; the obstruction is property wearing a father's face.

The map grades the film a documented retelling and an analogue, and the grade matters more than the film's considerable pedigree, which includes entry into competition at the ninth Berlin International Film Festival and a place on the Bibliotheca Alexandrina's 2006 list of the hundred greatest Egyptian films. Egyptian melodrama of the golden age ran on paternal vetoes, village honour and the marriage market in land; it did not need Shakespeare to invent Hassan and Nayima, and the reference record's careful formula, similar to, records an affinity rather than a descent. A map that promoted the film to a direct adaptation would be claiming more than its sources claim, and the claim would then poison the census. The honest grade is the finding: the play's nearest Egyptian relative is a cousin, not a child, and the family resemblance tells the reader how thoroughly the story's skeleton matches the region's own melodramatic stock.

The analogue's weight inside its own cinema deserves a sentence of its own, because it reverses the usual direction of prestige. Hassan wa Nayima is not a footnote to the play's history that happens to be Egyptian; it is a pillar of Egyptian cinema that happens to resemble the play. Barakat stood among the defining directors of the industry's golden age, the film is catalogued as a musical drama in which the singer hero courts in song, and Hosny's Nayima began the career of the actress Arab audiences would treat for decades as their defining screen presence. The map's Egyptian row therefore records a meeting of equals: a national classic and a world classic, sharing a skeleton, neither one the other's source. That is precisely the relationship the lazy sentence about a Chinese or an Egyptian Romeo and Juliet always misses, and it is why the grade analogue, honestly applied, honours both films more than the promotion to adaptation would.

Jerusalem supplies the retelling proper, twice over, at two scales. David & Fatima, released in June 2008 and directed by Alain Zaloum, rebuilds the play in the divided city with a deliberateness its makers stated on the record: David Isaac, an Israeli Jew facing conscription, and Fatima Aziz, a Palestinian Muslim, fall in love across the religious line, elope in a jurisdiction where their marriage is impossible, and carry the hopes of a film that announced its peace-making intention as part of its design. The production was a diaspora construction, shot largely in Los Angeles with Martin Landau and Tony Curtis, in his final film, in support, and its director was a Cairo-born Copt chosen, by the producers' own account, because he belonged to neither side. Zaloum then supplied the map with its favourite dissent, telling interviewers that he disliked pushing the Romeo and Juliet angle, because the angle made the film a fairy tale. The map records the dissent and keeps the grade. A retelling does not require the maker's blessing; it requires the structure, and the structure, by the film's own synopsis and by every review the survey found, is the play's: the houses renamed as faiths, the secret union, the flight, the deadly stakes. The maker's objection enters the record as evidence of a different kind, proof that the label itself has become a marketing instrument that serious filmmakers reach for and recoil from in the same gesture.

The second Jerusalem row is smaller and, for the pattern argument, decisive. In Fair Palestine, produced by students of the Ramallah Friends Schools and premiered at the Ramallah Cultural Palace in January 2008 before an audience that overflowed the hall, is a documentary drama in which Palestinian teenagers restage the play inside their own city. The production follows the plot with a fidelity the professional versions rarely attempt, secret wedding performed by a sheikh included, until the machinery reaches the letter. In the play, plague stops Friar John's journey and the message dies in quarantine (5.2). In Ramallah, the messenger is stopped at an Israeli checkpoint. No line of dialogue could localise the tragedy faster. The students' teacher recorded the choice as the production's plain realism, and the map files it as the single most efficient localization on the entire survey: one substitution, and the abstract bad luck of the original stands revealed as the daily administration of movement in the students' own lives.

## South Asia at Map Scale: 1947, a Benchmark and a Borrowed Name

India's rows must be read under the fence this article observes: the Bollywood tradition has its own article in this series, and the map's business is breadth. Three rows mark the territory. The first is chronological and chastening. The standard filmographies of the play on screen list a Romeo and Juliet of 1947, directed by Akhtar Hussein, among the direct adaptations: an Indian feature of the play exists in the record from the year of independence itself, predating every industry structure the later rows assume. The survey could verify the listing and almost nothing else; no plot record, no cast list, no surviving print history presented itself in the sources consulted. The map carries the row at filmography grade and lets its thinness instruct. The global history of this play on film was being written in Bombay before it was being written in most of Europe, and the archive has kept the fact while losing the film, a distribution of survival that the blank-spaces section takes up.

The second row is the territory's benchmark, carried at map scale with its depth deliberately left to the series' Indian articles: Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela, released in 2013 and directed by Sanjay Leela Bhansali, restages the feud as a war of clan factions in a Gujarat town awash with guns, and its lovers die by the feud's logic and their own hands, in the map's Indian line the tragedy survives translation into spectacle without dilution. Readers who want the adjudication of that film, its box-office record and its place in the Hindi tradition will find it in the series' account of [Ram-Leela: Bhansali's Indian Romeo and Juliet](/2029/01/19/ram-leela-bhansali-adaptation/), which owns the film whole. The map needs from the row only its coordinates: the feud as clan, the ending as death, the grade as documented retelling.

The third row is the warning label. Romeo Juliet, a Tamil romantic comedy released in June 2015 and directed by Lakshman, carries the lovers' names on its poster and, by its own reference record's plain statement, is not based on the play at all: its hero is a gymnasium instructor mistaken for a rich man, its heroine an air hostess with a preference for wealth, and no feud, no families and no reckoning connect it to Verona. The film was a commercial success, remade in Odia two years later, which completes the lesson. The names alone, detached from every element of the story, retained enough market value in the world's largest film-producing country to title a hit and a remake. A census that counted titles would report the play's Indian empire as larger than it is; the map's grades exist to prevent exactly that arithmetic. Between the lost film of 1947 and the empty title of 2015, India's rows frame the survey's whole method: descent must be documented, or the map is merely a list of posters.

## East and Southeast Asia: Hawker Stalls, Home Provinces and Supporter Firms

Singapore's Chicken Rice War, released in November 2000 and directed by Chee Kong Cheah, known as CheeK, is the map's most economical localization: the feud entire fits inside a hawker centre. The Wongs and the Chans run rival chicken rice stalls side by side, competing for the same queue of customers with the same dish, and their enmity is a trade war conducted in steam and chilli sauce. The lovers, Fenson Wong and Audrey Chan, meet not at a feast but in rehearsal, cast as Romeo and Juliet in their college production, so that the play exists inside the film as the script the feud keeps interrupting. The multilingual record of the film, Cantonese for the elders and Singlish for the young, maps the generation gap onto the language gap, and the ending completes the comic pattern: the families reconcile, the stalls' war dissolves, and the lovers receive the chance the original pair were denied. The film travelled, taking a Discovery Award at Toronto and a jury prize at Miami by the national library's record, and the archive's own synopsis states the divergence from the source as the film's selling point, an ending in which the two sides reconcile to give the couple their happiness. On the census, Singapore is the reconciliation pole of the whole map: the feud was only ever about rice.

South Korea's row tests the feud-of-equals model in the register of regional pride. Clash of the Families, released in March 2011 and directed by Kim Jin-young, was the second most successful Korean film of its quarter by the specialist press's account, and its reviewers reached for the play without prompting: a variation on Romeo and Juliet, one called it, a lighter take on the famous conflict, said another. The houses are regions. Hyeon-joon, a gentle cartoonist from Jeolla in the southwest, loves Da-hong of Busan in the Gyeongsang southeast, and the courtship's central device is disguise: the groom must be trained out of his Jeolla accent and passed off as a Seoul man before her father, played by Baek Yoon-shik, a Gyeongsang patriarch whose greeting for a suitor involves a thrown baseball. Korean regional rivalry between Jeolla and Gyeongsang is a documented structure of the country's politics, voting and mutual caricature, and the film mines it as Verona's feud in modern dress: inherited, irrational to outsiders, and absolute at the family table. The ending is marriage, twice over in the sequel logic of the form, and the census files Korea beside Brazil and Singapore, the comedies that convert the feud into a wedding. No straight Korean feature adaptation of the play presented itself to the survey; the country's screen Romeo arrived, as in so many territories, wearing comic dress and carrying a regional passport.

Indonesia contributes two rows across thirty-five years. Romi dan Juli, released in 1974 and directed by Has Manan for Rapi Films, starred Rano Karno and Yessi Gusman, two of the golden-age industry's defining young faces, and circulates in the record as the local Romeo and Juliet of its era; the survey found the claim carried by a single presentation source, and the row stands as reported only, a standing invitation to Indonesian film scholarship to promote or demote it. The 2009 Romeo Juliet stands firmer. Its lovers are Rangga, a Jakmania, the organised support of Persija Jakarta, and Desi, a Viking of Persib Bandung, and they meet in the middle of a brawl, his firm ambushing her firm's bus, in the map's most literal staging of love at first sight inside a street fight. Desi's brother Parman leads the Vikings; Rangga's own firm brands him a traitor and ruins his merchandise trade; the lovers elope to a neutral city. Indonesian scholarship has taken the film seriously as a document of supporter fanaticism, the subject of at least one university thesis in semiotics, and the feud it records is among the deadliest on the map in life, whatever the film's melodrama does with it: the Jakarta and Bandung firms' enmity has a real casualty record. Beside Brazil and Portugal, the row completes a football triangle across three continents, and the census draws the obvious line: where football supplies the feud, the play is never far behind, because organised support is the modern world's most faithful reproduction of the Veronese house, hereditary, territorial, costumed and armed with songs.

The Philippines closes the cluster with the map's frailest feature record and one of its most suggestive. Si Romeo at si Julieta, released in October 1972, survives in the catalogue record as a farcical take-off on the story built as the first teaming of Dolphy, the country's comedy king, with a leading lady of the era; the survey found no director's credit and no fuller synopsis in the sources consulted, and the row stands at catalogue grade, a title, a date, a star and a register. The country's deeper appropriation is theatrical: Sintang Dalisay, Tanghalang Ateneo's adaptation directed by Ricardo Abad, sets Rashiddin and Jamillia inside a Muslim community of the southern Philippines, builds its movement from the igal dance of the Sama-Bajau people, and, by the account of the scholarship that studies it, transforms the tragic ending toward the utopian. The map logs the production at its edge, labelled stage, because it carries a datum the cinema rows need: the Philippines received the play most memorably by refusing its ending on stage, a refusal the census meets again in the comic cinemas and weighs in the verdict.

## The Chinese Parallel: Why the Butterfly Lovers Are Not an Adaptation

Every popular account of the play in Asia reaches, sooner or later, for the sentence that the Butterfly Lovers are the Chinese Romeo and Juliet, and the map's duty is to refuse the sentence in its lazy form. The legend of Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai, the girl who studies in male disguise, the sworn brother who learns the truth too late, the grave that opens and the two butterflies that rise from it, is documented in Chinese tradition centuries before any Chinese storyteller could have heard of Verona; its textual history runs through the Song dynasty and beyond, as Wilt Idema's edition of the legend's four versions establishes for the Anglophone reader. Resemblance is not descent. The legend and the play are two cultures' independent solutions to the same narrative problem, a love blocked by arrangement and ended by death, and the map excludes the legend's films from the adaptation count with the exclusion stated in the table, because counting them would manufacture a Chinese Romeo industry out of a Chinese classic that owes the play nothing.

The films themselves deserve their coordinates even in exclusion. Liang Shanbo yu Zhu Yingtai, released in 1954 and co-directed by Sang Hu and Huang Sha for the Shanghai Film Studio, filmed the legend as a Shaoxing opera with Yuan Xuefen as Liang, and holds a documented place in its national history as the first colour feature of the People's Republic. The Love Eterne, released in April 1963 and directed by Li Han-hsiang for the Shaw Brothers studio in Hong Kong, filmed the same legend as a Huangmei opera picture with Ivy Ling Po, a woman, as Liang and Betty Loh Ti as Zhu; it took six awards including Best Picture at the Golden Horse ceremony, served as Hong Kong's submission for the Academy's foreign language award, and ignited a devotion in Taiwan that the UCLA archive's account describes in terms of fans viewing the film a hundred times. Tsui Hark returned to the legend in The Lovers in 1994. These are major films by any measure, and the map's exclusion of them is a compliment to precision: they belong to the history of the Butterfly Lovers, a tradition with its own millennium, and annexing them to Shakespeare's filmography would repeat, in scholarship, the colonial gesture the map exists to correct.

Chinese-language cinema's documented dealings with the play itself are slighter and stranger, and Hong Kong supplies them. One Husband Too Many, released in 1988 and directed by Anthony Chan, is a parody whose object is not the play but the play's most famous film: the costumes copy the doublet-and-tights designs of the 1968 Franco Zeffirelli production, the score quotes that film's love theme with new Cantonese lyrics, and the tomb scene is restaged on a makeshift stage at a night market, where Juliet wakes inside the joke. Alexa Alice Joubin's account of Asian Shakespeare adaptations in the MIT Global Shakespeares project preserves the film for scholarship, and the map grades it a documented parody with a twist no other row shares: Hong Kong's Romeo is a quotation of a quotation, the play arriving through its Italian film rather than through any local feud. The sequence matters for the verdict. Where a cinema's own feud grammar does not seize the story, the story still arrives, but as cinema about cinema, affectionate, expert and second-hand.

### Why are the Butterfly Lovers excluded from the adaptation count?

The exclusion rests on chronology, and chronology is the one argument adaptation studies cannot argue with. The Butterfly Lovers legend circulated in Chinese ballad, tale and opera for centuries before the play existed in any form a Chinese artist could encounter. A story cannot descend from a source it precedes.

The films of the legend, in 1954, 1963 and 1994, adapt the legend, and their greatness changes nothing in the genealogy. The map therefore prints the rows, honours the films, and excludes them from the count, because a map that cannot tell a parallel from a descendant will mistake every resemblance in world culture for an influence, and influence is a claim that evidence, not similarity, must carry.

## The Axis, Briefly: What the Map Takes from the Centre

The map's benchmark row requires a short defence, because the Anglophone and Italian industries are the only territories this article deliberately under-reports. The series has given the axis its own run of articles: the Cukor production of 1936 as MGM's prestige monument, Renato Castellani's location-shot version of 1954, which took the Golden Lion at Venice, the Zeffirelli film of 1968, the Luhrmann film of 1996 and the Carlei film of 2013, with the head-to-head adjudications reserved to the film cluster. The map takes from the axis three coordinates and no more. The first is chronological: the axis industrialised the play on screen early and exported the results through the century's distribution networks, which is why a Hong Kong parody of 1988 quotes the 1968 film's costumes and music rather than any text, and why Iranian audiences in Kiarostami's short are watching the axis's Juliet die. The axis's deepest global influence runs through its prints, not through the play.

The second coordinate is generic. The axis's famous versions are tragedies that keep the double death, and their prestige taught the world's art cinemas to treat the death ending as the mark of seriousness, a lesson the map's melodramas absorbed and its comedies ignored. The third coordinate is the axis's own secret, which the map exists to expose: the axis films are localizations too. Cukor's Verona is a studio's idea of Renaissance Italy built for prestige in Culver City. Luhrmann's Verona Beach relocates the feud to an American gang geography of guns, gasoline and television news, as thorough a piece of local substitution as anything in Jakarta or Singapore, differing from the map's other rows chiefly in budget and in the export machinery behind it. Once the axis is seen as one province among many, with its own local materials and its own genre decisions, the map's reordering stops looking like revisionism and starts looking like cartography: the centre is a place on the map, not the map's maker.

## Japan and the Soviet Union at Map Scale

Two territories on the map are present by reference, because this series has already given them the depth the map format cannot hold, and the honest procedure is to fix their coordinates and point. Japan's screen life of the play runs from the shingeki stage through Yukio Ninagawa's productions to the Takarazuka Revue's staging of the French musical and the Gonzo studio's anime series of 2007, which relocates the feud to a floating continent and lets its lovers die saving their city; the full record, with its dates corrected and its kabuki chapter weighed, stands in the series' account of [Japanese Film and Stage Romeo and Juliet](/2029/04/06/japanese-film-stage-adaptations/). The map takes from Japan two coordinates: the anime's heroic double death, which enters the census, and the pattern of a culture that received the play first as a translated classic and then as raw material for its own most commercial forms, the all-female revue and the animated series.

The Soviet Union's coordinate is a single film with an outsized lesson. The Romeo and Juliet released in 1955, directed by Leo Arnshtam with Leonid Lavrovsky, filmed Lavrovsky's Kirov staging of Prokofiev's ballet with Galina Ulanova as Juliet, took the prize for lyrical film at Cannes, and remains the map's only row in which the state's flagship art form carries the story without a spoken word. Its depth, the Kirov creation of 1940, the Bolshoi transfer, the prize's exact name and the catalogue division between 1954 and 1955, belongs to the series' Soviet article; the map needs the row for two reasons. It proves the feud grammar is optional where the institution is total: the houses survive as choreography because the Soviet screen had no other Romeo to offer. And it gives the census its purest death ending, the play's own, preserved in the medium least able to argue with it.

## Africa: A Township Tragedy, a Kenyan Report and a Borrowed Nigerian Name

Africa's rows span the map's whole grading range in a single continent, which makes the cluster the survey's best exhibit of its own method. South Africa supplies the documented summit. uGugu no Andile, made for the SABC1 strand Shakespeare in Mzansi, adapted by Minky Schlesinger and Lodi Matsetela and premiered in June 2008 as a six-part mini-series, with a film version following, sets the lovers in Thokoza in 1993, in the last violent year before the first democratic elections. Gugu, played by Lungelo Dhladhla, is a Zulu-speaking girl of sixteen; Andile, played by Litha Booi, is an Xhosa boy of eighteen; their families stand on opposite sides of a township carved between political allegiances, hostel and street, in the period's low civil war. The adaptation keeps the play's contract to the letter and beyond: the lovers meet, love, and die, and the deaths begin the community's healing, the Prince's closing arithmetic restated as a nation's. The film version took three awards at the 2009 Africa Movie Academy Awards, including Best Film in an African Language, and the scholarly record has treated the work as a landmark: Chandré Botha and Chris Broodryk read it as a postcolonial adaptation in which the play is re-historicised against South Africa's own transition, the feud's reconciliation made to rehearse the country's.

The production's institutional frame matters as well. uGugu no Andile was the third of four mini-series in the SABC1 strand Shakespeare in Mzansi, a state broadcaster's commission that contemporised Macbeth as Entabeni, King Lear as Izingane zoBaba and Macbeth again as Death of a Queen alongside the lovers' story. A national broadcaster, a decade and a half after apartheid, chose the English canon as an instrument for telling the country's own stories in the country's own languages, and chose this play for the strand's tragedy of division. The decision is the South African row's deepest localization, made before a line was adapted: the feud play was commissioned because the nation was rehearsing reconciliation, and the lovers' deaths were broadcast as a civic lesson in what division costs. No other row on the map was commissioned by a state for that purpose, and the census notes the singularity.

The South African row matters to the census out of proportion to its size, because it is the map's clearest case of a cinema keeping the double death for a reason the play itself supplies. In Verona the deaths reconcile the houses; in Thokoza the deaths are asked to reconcile a polity, and the adaptation's wager is that the audience will accept the exchange because the audience's own recent history ran on it. Where the Brazilian tragedy outsourced the killing to the police and the Mexican to an accident of class, the South African version restores the deaths to the lovers and the feud, and draws from them the play's original dividend. The map also notes, as a reported entry in prose, an earlier South African signal: a 1991 production catalogued under the title Soweto, described in its single catalogue line as a Romeo and Juliette story against the background of the 1976 riots; the survey could not raise the source above a line, and the row stands in the text, not the table, as reported only.

Kenya's row stands at reported grade and says so in the table. Chaguo, a Kenyan feature released to streaming in 2022 and credited to Vincent Mbaya and Ravi Karmalker, was described in the review record the survey found as a contemporary adaptation of the play, its lovers Wendo and Mugeni drawn into an election struggle between historically opposed tribes and their families' political machines. One review is one leg, and the map prints the flag rather than the promotion; the territory is too significant in East African cinema for the survey to omit, and the evidence too thin for the survey to certify. Nigeria, the continent's largest film industry, supplies the cluster's third lesson. The survey found Nigerian stage appropriations in number and a busy traffic in the play's vocabulary, but at feature level the firmest document is a negative one with a title attached: Juliet Without Romeo, a Lagos-set drama released through streaming channels in 2025, whose own review record states that it borrows the heroine's name and nothing else, a healing story about a woman after heartbreak. Nigeria's row is a title citation, and the map reads it with respect: a Nollywood that can sell a film on Juliet's name alone has absorbed the play into its commercial vernacular as thoroughly as any industry on earth, by the route of the poster rather than the plot.

## Iran: The Country That Watches the Play

Iran is the map's most eloquent blank, because the survey found no Iranian feature adaptation or retelling of the play at all, and found instead a cluster of works about Iranians encountering the play as spectators, hosts and supplicants. The emblem is three minutes long. Abbas Kiarostami's contribution to the Cannes anthology Chacun son cinéma, titled Where Is My Romeo?, holds its camera on a cinema audience of Iranian women watching the closing movement of the 1968 film, their faces wet, while the tragedy happens off-screen in the soundtrack. Richard Brody's notice of the short in The New Yorker caught its doubleness, the women's grief indistinguishable from pleasure. The play, in Iran's most famous film about it, is not remade but received: a foreign object of feeling, wept over in the dark, in a country whose own screens could not stage its kisses.

The stage record explains the screen's silence without filling it. Tehran's Vahdat Hall mounted a celebrated Romeo and Juliet in 2000 under the director Ali Rafii, in a run reported internationally for its scale, tens of thousands of spectators across sixty performances, for its veiled Juliet, and for the official anxieties that trailed a production glorifying Western romance on a state stage. The play could be an event in Iran; it could not be a domestic film industry subject, and the survey's searched-and-not-found grade for an Iranian feature is recorded with the reason attached: the obstruction is not indifference but permission. The newest Iranian row bends the story once more toward the meta. Juliet and the King, an animated feature directed by Ashkan Rahgozar and shown at the Fantasia festival in 2025, follows a nineteenth-century actress engaged to perform Juliet for a Shah who has fallen for her in Paris, and smuggles the play into the Qajar court as a touring performance beset by harem intrigue. Iran's Romeo, across a quarter century of evidence, is always arriving from somewhere else and being watched, hosted or desired. The map knows no other territory where the play's presence is so strong and its local embodiment so completely refused, and it prints the blank with the confidence that a blank, honestly graded, is a finding and not a failure.

## The Ending Census: Who Kills the Lovers, Who Marries Them

The map's table can be read as a census, and the census is the article's hardest evidence. Counting only the documented adaptations and retellings, and only those whose endings the record states, twelve rows qualify. Eight end in death. Four end in survival, marriage, reconciliation or farce. The split is not random, and it is not national. Every one of the four survival rows is a comedy or a parody: the Brazilian wedding film, the Singapore reconciliation, the Korean marriage comedy, and the Mexican parody that plays the tomb for laughs, with Hong Kong's farce waiting just outside the count on a technicality of grading. Every one of the eight death rows is a melodrama, a tragedy, a ballet or a drama. Genre, not geography, decides whether the lovers live. A cinema's first decision about the play, the register in which to receive it, turns out to be a decision about the ending, taken in advance and rarely reversed.

The deaths, examined closely, divide by executioner, and the executioners are a census of their own. The feud itself does the killing in the Spanish clan tragedies, in the Gujarat of Ram-Leela and in the danced Verona of the Soviet film, where the play's own machinery survives translation intact. The state does the killing in Rio de Janeiro, where police bullets execute both lovers in front of news cameras, and the political war does it around the lovers in Thokoza, where the deaths are asked to found a peace. A suitor's incompetence does it in Mexico City, where the Paris figure's unfamiliar gun kills the heroine by accident and spares the hero for a life of mourning. The anime's lovers die as saviours, spending themselves for their city. Set against the play, where the lovers die by their own hands in a tomb built by misdelivered mail (5.2 and 5.3), the map's deaths read as a distribution of blame across the modern world: feud, state, accident, war and sacrifice each take their share, and the share a cinema assigns is the clearest statement it makes about where its own society locates fatal power.

The Anglophone rows that this map treats as its axis obey the same law and add a shading. The series' own account of Ferrara's [China Girl: Ferrara's New York Feud](/2029/02/09/china-girl-ferrara-new-york/) documents a lovers' death by a gang bullet in the open street, a double killing rather than a double suicide, the feud's violence arriving from outside the lovers' hands. The axis and the periphery agree more than the cliche allows: everywhere the play is filmed as tragedy, somebody kills the lovers, and the identity of that somebody is the adaptation's signature. Mercutio's curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), is the play's own census of blame in embryo. The map merely counts the houses.

### Which single change predicts whether the lovers survive?

The register. Change the genre and the ending follows with a regularity no other variable matches: not the country, not the century, not the feud substituted. Every documented comedy or parody on the map spares the lovers, by marriage, reconciliation or ridicule of the tomb, and every documented melodrama or tragedy kills at least one of them.

The finding flatters neither the universalists nor the sceptics. The story's survival machinery is indifferent to its ending, which means the ending was never the story's essence; it is the tax a genre levies, and each cinema pays in the coin of its own house style.

A second census, of the feuds themselves, can be stated in a breath. Organised football supplies three documented rows, in Brazil, Portugal and Indonesia, the largest single supplier on the map. Clan and honour supply three more, the two Spanish tragedies and the Gujarat benchmark, with Egypt's patrilineal veto standing adjacent as the analogue's variant. Class supplies the two great urban melodramas of Mexico City and Rio and shadows several others. Faith and political division supply Jerusalem twice and Thokoza once. A trade rivalry supplies Singapore, a regional rivalry supplies Korea, and the houses themselves survive chiefly where the axis industries or the ballet keep them. The distribution is the article's argument in miniature: the feud column is a census of what the world's societies are actually divided by, and the play's frame fits each division because the frame was always a mould for division as such, awaiting local metal.

## Universality on Trial: Burnett, Joubin and Hutcheon

Three named critics frame the question the map exists to answer, and their disagreement is genuine. Mark Thornton Burnett, whose Shakespeare and World Cinema mapped this field for scholarship, reads global Shakespeare films as the work of local industries negotiating a world market: the play travels because distribution, festivals and educational systems carry it, and each local version is an industry's bid within that economy. On Burnett's account, the map's spread is evidence about markets before it is evidence about the play. Alexa Alice Joubin, whose Chinese Shakespeares and Shakespeare and East Asia built the study of East Asian adaptation, reads the same films from the opposite shore: local appropriations are acts of authorship that produce knowledge about both the play and the locality, and a Singaporean hawker comedy or a Hong Kong parody of an Italian film is not the play's dilution but its continuation by other means. Linda Hutcheon, adaptation theory's central voice, supplies the mechanism both positions need: an adaptation is repetition without replication, an announced transposition whose pleasures and meanings arise in the gap between the source and the receiving work, so that asking whether a version is faithful is asking the wrong question in a disciplined way.

The universalist claim stands outside the three as the position they jointly refuse: the claim, heard wherever the play's spread is celebrated, that the map's breadth proves the story speaks a human constant, identical beneath its costumes. The map's data adjudicate the quarrel, and the adjudication splits. Against the universalist, the census is decisive: a story whose ending, feud, machinery and body count are rebuilt in every territory is not carrying an identical human constant from port to port; it is carrying a frame. For Joubin, the local evidence is decisive at the level of meaning: the checkpoint in Ramallah, the police volley in Rio, the reconciled rice stalls are acts of interpretation no market analysis predicts, and they are the parts of the films that matter. For Burnett, the map's own blanks are decisive at the level of circulation: which versions survive, travel and enter the archive tracks the economics of industries and the language of scholarship, not the worth of the films, as the lost Indian feature of 1947 and the single-sourced rows of Kenya and Indonesia attest. The verdict this article returns is therefore a compound. The play is not universal; it is available, and its availability is an industrial fact, and what local cinemas build inside the availability is authorship. Universality is the name the frame's portability gives itself when it wishes to be complimented. The map declines the compliment and keeps the frame.

One refinement of Hutcheon's mechanism earns its place before the verdict, because the census supports it. Hutcheon's theory distinguishes knowing audiences, who hold the source in mind and savour the gap, from unknowing audiences, who receive the adaptation as an original. The map's rows sort themselves by that distinction with unusual neatness. The parodies, in Mexico City in 1943 and Hong Kong in 1988, presuppose knowing audiences and fail without them; a tomb scene is funny only to spectators who know what the tomb holds. The melodramas, by contrast, play to unknowing audiences and lose nothing: no spectator needs Verona to follow a market boy and an heiress to a bus station. The frame's portability, on this evidence, does not depend on the play's fame at all in the tragic register, which is the strongest single blow the map lands on the universalist case. Where the story works hardest, it works for audiences who have never heard of it, because what they are watching is their own division, wearing the frame the way water wears a glass.

## The Blank Spaces Are Data Too

A map's blanks require the same defence as its entries, because a reader is entitled to ask whether a blank records the world or merely the survey. Turkey's blank is the survey's most deliberate. Turkish cinema is large, long-lived and well catalogued in the filmographies the survey searched, and no verifiable Turkish feature of the play presented itself; the map prints the territory as searched and not found, and notes that Turkey possesses its own doomed-lovers inheritance in the Leyla and Majnun tradition, a parallel of the Chinese kind, which may have left the niche the play fills elsewhere already occupied at home. The hypothesis is offered as a hypothesis. What the blank certifies is narrower and firmer: in the sources where such a film would be recorded, no such film stands.

Other blanks record losses rather than absences. India's 1947 feature exists as a listing because filmographies preserve titles more faithfully than prints; the survey's inability to raise a plot, a cast or a still for it measures the survival rate of the era's Bombay production, not the film's historical reality. The Philippine feature of 1972 survives as a catalogue line for the same reason. The map's reported rows, Kenya and the Indonesian golden-age entry, mark the frontier where digitised scholarship thins: both territories possess active film cultures whose records are incompletely indexed in the sources a survey of this kind can reach, and the flags on those rows are addressed to the scholars of those cinemas as much as to the reader. Finally, the map confesses its own linguistic limit. Its searches ran through English-language scholarship and the major international filmographies, the same channels through which the play's global reputation flows; a version documented only in a national-language source, in a regional archive, in a festival programme never digitised, would escape the net. The blanks are therefore graded like the entries, searched and not found in the sources consulted, and the map's last methodological word is that its silences are provisional in exact proportion as its entries are proud.

### What does a blank space on the map actually prove?

A blank proves a negative about the record, not about the territory: that in the filmographies, festival archives and scholarship the survey searched, no verifiable version stands. It cannot prove that no version was ever made, and the map's own Indian row, a 1947 feature reduced to a listing, demonstrates how much production a record can lose.

What a graded blank does prove is the state of the evidence and the shape of the archive, which industries kept their papers, which languages the scholarship reads, and which festivals wrote things down. The blank is the map's confession of its own limits, printed at the same size as its findings so that neither can be quoted without the other.

## Verdict: The Frame Is the Play's Real Export

The map's rows, grades and census converge on a single sentence. Romeo and Juliet conquered the world's screens not as a text, not as a tragedy with a fixed ending and not as a vessel of identical feeling, but as a frame of three parts, a feud of equals, a pair of lovers the feud produces, and a machinery of obstruction that each society recognises as its own. Inside that frame, the world's cinemas have built works as unlike one another as a Cantinflas parody in rhymed couplets and a South African elegy for a township, a Singaporean quarrel over rice and an Egyptian father's broken promise, a Ramallah checkpoint and a Rio police line. The cliche keeps the play Anglo-Italian and calls the rest borrowing. The map shows the rest to be the story of the play on film, numerically, geographically and artistically, with the canonical versions as its best-documented province rather than its capital. Readers weighing those canonical versions head to head will find the contest argued in [Which Film Romeo and Juliet Is Best?](/2029/05/25/which-film-version-is-best/), where the axis films receive their adjudication; the map's wider claim is that the contest itself is a provincial sport once the whole board is visible. Global cinema did not embrace the play. It did something more interesting, and the rows stand as the evidence: it took the frame apart in forty workshops and rebuilt it, every time, as a story about itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which film is the earliest documented entry on the global-cinema map?

The earliest documented entry is Spain's Julieta y Romeo of 1939, directed by José María Castellví, which the standard filmographies of the play on screen list among the direct adaptations. The entry carries a warning the map prints rather than hides: the film survives in the record chiefly as a filmography line, with little of its content documented in the sources the survey consulted, so its date is firmer than its description. The earliest fully documented feature on the map, with plot, production and scholarly record intact, is Mexico's Romeo y Julieta of 1943, the Cantinflas parody, which stands four years later and a world away in kind. The pair teaches the map's method in miniature. A census of world cinema runs on records of very unequal thickness, and an honest map dates its entries by the strongest evidence available for each, flagging the thin ones instead of either inflating or discarding them. Readers should therefore treat 1939 as the map's earliest coordinate and 1943 as its earliest certainty, and should expect the gap between those two kinds of knowledge to recur across the survey's remoter territories.

### Q: Is the Butterfly Lovers story a Chinese adaptation of the play?

No, and the map excludes it from the adaptation count for exactly that reason. The legend of Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai circulated in Chinese ballad, tale and opera for centuries before Shakespeare wrote, with textual strata reaching back toward the Song dynasty, as Wilt Idema's edition of the legend's versions documents. A story cannot descend from a source that did not yet exist in any form its tellers could reach. The resemblance between the legend and the play is real and instructive: two traditions solved the same narrative problem, a love blocked by family arrangement and ended by death, and arrived at tombs, transformations and posthumous union by independent roads. The legend's films are major works, the Shanghai colour landmark of 1954 and Li Han-hsiang's The Love Eterne of 1963 among them, and the map prints their rows with full honours. What it refuses is the annexation. Calling the Butterfly Lovers China's Romeo and Juliet mistakes a parallel for a descendant, and a map built on that mistake would credit Shakespeare with a millennium of Chinese storytelling.

### Q: Which local feud replaces the Capulet and Montague feud most often on the map?

Organised football support replaces it most often, with three documented rows: Palmeiras against Corinthians in Brazil's O Casamento de Romeu e Julieta of 2005, Persija Jakarta's Jakmania against Persib Bandung's Vikings in Indonesia's Romeo Juliet of 2009, and the fictionalized Invicta and Castelo of Portugal's Star Crossed, also 2009. The recurrence has a logic the map states plainly. Football allegiance is the modern world's closest reproduction of the Veronese house: inherited at birth, worn as a colour, territorial in its geography, policed by family and peers, and capable of turning a street, a bus or a stadium into a border. Clan and honour feuds tie football's count when Egypt's patrilineal variant is included, and class follows close behind through the Mexican and Brazilian melodramas. The distribution matters more than the winner. Read as a census, the feud column records what the world's societies are actually divided by, and the play's frame fits each entry because the frame was always a mould for division itself, waiting for local material to fill it.

### Q: Do any of the map's versions keep Shakespeare's words?

None of the graded retellings keeps the text, and only one keeps anything like its music. The world map's versions are, with near uniformity, story adaptations rather than text adaptations: they take the feud, the lovers and the machinery, and they write new dialogue in local languages for local mouths. The exception proves the rule by parodying it. Mexico's Romeo y Julieta of 1943 performs its dialogue in rhymed verse, a tumbling comic echo of the play's form that keeps the sound of poetry while discarding the poetry itself, and the joke depends on an audience that recognises the music being travestied. Hong Kong's One Husband Too Many goes further in the same direction, quoting the 1968 film's score rather than the play's lines. The axis versions that do keep the text, the Luhrmann film above all, belong to the industries this map treats as one province. The finding cuts against a natural assumption: the play's worldwide screen empire was built almost entirely without its language, which suggests the export was never the verse. The export was the frame, and the frame travels in silence.

### Q: Why does the map grade some famous titles as only title citations?

Because the title is the only thing those films took. Argentina's Romeo y Julita of 1954 is an apartment farce whose heroine's diminutive name announces the pun; India's Tamil Romeo Juliet of 2015 states in its own reference record that it is not based on the play, its lovers a gymnasium instructor and an air hostess with no feud in sight; Nigeria's Juliet Without Romeo of 2025 borrows the heroine's name for a healing drama its reviewers describe as unrelated in plot. Each film is a genuine commercial fact: the names alone carried enough market value to title a hit, and in the Tamil case a remake followed. But a census that counted titles would report the play's empire as far larger than the play's actual screen life, and the inflation would be worst exactly where industries are largest. The citation grade exists to protect the count. It also carries its own finding, which the map records with respect: a culture that can sell tickets on two names has absorbed the lovers into its vernacular as thoroughly as any adaptation could manage, by the route of the poster rather than the plot.

### Q: Which version sets the lovers among rival chicken rice stalls?

Singapore's Chicken Rice War, released in 2000 and directed by Chee Kong Cheah, known as CheeK. The Wongs and the Chans run competing chicken rice stalls side by side in the same hawker centre, and their trade war over the same dish, the same queue and the same reputation supplies the feud entire. The lovers, Fenson Wong and Audrey Chan, meet in rehearsal for their college production of the play itself, so the tragedy exists inside the comedy as a script the feud keeps interrupting. The film's elders speak Cantonese and its young people Singlish, mapping the generation gap onto the language gap, and its ending breaks the source's contract in the direction the census has learned to expect from comedy: the families reconcile, the stall war dissolves, and the couple receives the future the original pair were denied. The national library's synopsis advertises the divergence as the film's point. The map grades it a documented adaptation and files it as the reconciliation pole of the whole survey, the row where the feud turns out to have been about rice all along, and where that smallness is the film's affectionate thesis about its city.

### Q: How does the South African version rebuild the feud?

uGugu no Andile, the SABC1 mini-series adapted by Minky Schlesinger and Lodi Matsetela and first broadcast in 2008, rebuilds the feud as the political violence of Thokoza in 1993, the last year before South Africa's first democratic elections. Gugu is a Zulu-speaking girl of sixteen; Andile is an Xhosa boy of eighteen; their families stand on opposite sides of a township divided between political allegiances, hostel and street, in the period's low civil war. The adaptation keeps the play's tragic contract whole, which distinguishes it on the map: the lovers die, and their deaths begin the community's healing, the closing reconciliation of the play restated at the scale of a nation rehearsing its own. A film version followed, taking three awards at the 2009 Africa Movie Academy Awards, including Best Film in an African Language. Scholars Chandré Botha and Chris Broodryk read the work as a postcolonial re-historicising of the play, and the map concurs, adding only the institutional note that a state broadcaster commissioned the strand as nation-building, and chose the feud play for the lesson division teaches.

### Q: What stops the messenger in the Palestinian school film?

A checkpoint stops him. In Fair Palestine, the documentary drama produced by students of the Ramallah Friends Schools and premiered at the Ramallah Cultural Palace in 2008, follows the play's plot with unusual fidelity, secret wedding by a sheikh included, until the machinery reaches the undelivered message. In the play, Friar John's letter fails because plague seals the houses on his road and he is quarantined (5.2), an accident of disease and timing. In Ramallah, the messenger carrying word of the feigned death is stopped at an Israeli checkpoint, an obstruction drawn from the students' daily administration of movement. The substitution is the map's most efficient localization, a single change that converts the tragedy's abstract bad luck into the audience's lived reality without altering a line of the plot around it. The map files the production as a documented work rather than a curiosity, and draws the general lesson in the pattern section: the machinery column of any adaptation is a confession, naming what the receiving society believes actually separates people, and few confessions on the map are as precise as this one.

### Q: Is there an Iranian feature film of the play?

None that the survey could verify, and the absence is one of the map's firmest findings. Iranian searches across filmographies and scholarship produced no domestic feature adaptation or retelling. What they produced instead is a cluster of encounters. Abbas Kiarostami's three-minute Where Is My Romeo?, made for the Cannes anthology Chacun son cinéma, films Iranian women weeping at the deaths in the 1968 film, the play received as a foreign object of feeling. Tehran's Vahdat Hall mounted the play on stage in 2000 under Ali Rafii, in a run reported internationally for its scale and for the official anxieties around it. Ashkan Rahgozar's animated Juliet and the King, shown at Fantasia in 2025, stages the play as a touring performance smuggled into a Shah's court. The pattern across a quarter century is consistent: the play in Iran is watched, hosted and desired, never domestically rebuilt as a feature. The map grades the territory searched and not found at feature level and records the reason the evidence suggests, which is not indifference but permission, the regulation of romance on Iranian screens.

### Q: Why is Turkey missing from the map?

Turkey is missing because the survey searched and found nothing verifiable, and the map prints that result rather than filling the space. Turkish cinema is large, long-lived and well served by the filmographies and databases the survey used, so the blank cannot be waved away as a gap in the sources alone. No Turkish feature adaptation or retelling of the play presented itself in any of them. The map offers one hypothesis, labelled as such: Turkey possesses its own canonical doomed lovers in the Leyla and Majnun tradition, a parallel inheritance of the same kind as China's Butterfly Lovers, and a culture whose screens and stages already hold a native story of love blocked and destroyed may feel less need to import the Veronese model. The hypothesis explains the blank elegantly, which is exactly why the map flags it as unproven. What the row certifies is narrower: in the records where such a film would stand, none stands. A future researcher with a Turkish archive and a contrary document can promote the row, and the grading system is built to welcome the correction.

### Q: Which versions let the lovers marry and live?

The comedies do, without exception on the map's documented rows. Brazil's O Casamento de Romeu e Julieta of 2005 ends, as its title promises, in marriage, the Corinthians supporter absorbed into the Palmeiras family he deceived. South Korea's Clash of the Families of 2011 ends in the marriage its plot pursues from the first reel, the Jeolla groom's disguise forgiven at the family table. Singapore's Chicken Rice War of 2000 ends one step short of the altar, in reconciliation between the stallholding families and an open future for Fenson and Audrey. Behind the features stand the parodies, Mexico's of 1943 and Hong Kong's of 1988, which spare the lovers by the simpler method of laughing at the tomb. The census draws the law from the pattern: genre decides survival. Every documented comedy or parody on the map returns the lovers alive, and every documented melodrama or tragedy kills at least one of them. A director choosing the register in which to receive the play is therefore choosing the ending in advance, whether the choice is made consciously or inherited with the form.

### Q: How does Amar te duele change who dies?

It halves the death and assigns the surviving half by class. Fernando Sariñana's Mexican retelling of 2002 keeps the machinery of flight and pursuit: Ulises, the market trader's son, and Renata, the Santa Fe heiress, run for a bus station and a new life, and Francisco, the rich boyfriend who fills the Paris position, intercepts them with a gun. In the play, the tomb takes both lovers, by poison and dagger, in a symmetry the Prince's closing speech seals. In Mexico City, Francisco, inexperienced with firearms, shoots Renata by accident. She dies in Ulises's arms, and he survives her, last seen mourning at her grave. The asymmetry is the film's thesis about its city. Class violence does not fall evenly; it kills across the divide in one direction and leaves the survivor to live inside the knowledge. The map files the ending as the census's clearest proof that the distribution of death is a local decision, taken by each cinema according to its own account of where fatal power sits. Verona blamed the houses. Sariñana blames the gun in the rich boy's untrained hand.

### Q: What makes Hassan wa Nayima an analogue rather than an adaptation?

The record's own wording, and the structure beneath it. Reference sources describe Henry Barakat's Egyptian film of 1959 with unusual consistency as a love story similar to Romeo and Juliet in the Egyptian countryside, and the map takes the hint seriously. The structural difference is real: the play's tragedy needs two houses, alike in dignity and locked in mutual feud, while Barakat's film has one house and a veto. Hassan the village singer is refused Nayima's hand not by an enemy family but by her own father, who wants her married to his cousin Atwah for the land her inheritance commands. The lovers elope, the father retrieves and imprisons her, a promise is made and broken, and the tragedy tightens around property rather than enmity. Egyptian golden-age melodrama ran on exactly this stock, paternal vetoes, village honour and marriage as a land transaction, and it did not need Shakespeare to assemble Hassan and Nayima. The map grades the film a documented retelling and an analogue, prints its Berlin festival entry and its place on the Bibliotheca Alexandrina's hundred greatest Egyptian films list, and counts the resemblance as kinship rather than descent.

### Q: Which map entry is a parody of another film rather than of the play?

Hong Kong's One Husband Too Many, released in 1988 and directed by Anthony Chan. Its target is not the text but the text's most famous screen form: the costumes reproduce the doublet-and-tights designs of Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 production, the music quotes that film's love theme with new Cantonese lyrics, and the tomb scene is restaged on a makeshift stage at a night market, where Juliet wakes inside the farce. Alexa Alice Joubin's account in the MIT Global Shakespeares project preserves the film for scholarship, and the map grades it a documented parody with a distinction no other row shares. Hong Kong's Romeo arrives as a quotation of a quotation, the play filtered through its Italian film before reaching Cantonese comedy. The entry matters beyond its wit. It demonstrates that by the late twentieth century the play's global circulation ran through its films as much as through its text, so that a local cinema could engage Romeo and Juliet expertly, affectionately and at length without touching a line of Shakespeare, or a local feud, at all. The map counts that as a stage in the story's life, not a footnote to it.

### Q: Did India really film the play in 1947?

The filmographies say yes, and the map reports their testimony at exactly its strength. The standard lists of the play's screen adaptations include a Romeo and Juliet of 1947 directed by Akhtar Hussein among the direct adaptations, which places an Indian feature of the play in the year of independence, earlier than almost every other non-Western entry on the map. Beyond the listing, the survey could verify almost nothing: no plot summary, no cast list and no surviving-print history presented itself in the sources consulted. The row therefore stands at filmography grade, a date, a director and a title held up by the reference tradition alone. The map refuses both available exaggerations. It will not promote the film into a documented classic on a listing's authority, and it will not delete a record merely because the archive behind it is poor. Bombay's production of the era survives badly on paper and worse on celluloid, and the row's thinness measures that survival rate rather than the film's historical reality. If a print or a fuller record surfaces, the grade rises; the map is built to be corrected.

### Q: What does the ending census actually show?

It shows that genre, not geography, decides whether the lovers live. Counting only documented adaptations and retellings whose endings the record states, twelve rows qualify. Eight end in death and four in survival, marriage, reconciliation or farce. The four survivals are all comedies or parodies: the Brazilian wedding film, the Korean marriage comedy, the Singapore reconciliation and the Mexican parody that plays the tomb for laughs. The eight deaths are all melodramas, tragedies, a ballet and a drama of political violence. No variable on the map, not country, not century, not the feud substituted, predicts the ending as cleanly as the register in which a cinema chose to receive the story. The census also distributes the killing. The feud itself executes the lovers in the Spanish and Indian tragedies and the Soviet ballet; the state executes them in Rio; a political war surrounds their deaths in Thokoza; a suitor's stray bullet takes the heroine in Mexico City; the anime's lovers spend themselves saving their city. Each assignment of the fatal power is the adaptation's signature, and the collection of signatures is the closest thing the map offers to a world opinion about where death comes from.

### Q: Why do so many versions replace the two families with other groups?

Because the families were never the point; the division was. Verona's houses function in the play as a feud neither side can justify to an outsider, inherited, symmetrical and absolute, and any group that reproduces those properties can hold the structure up. Football firms reproduce them almost exactly, which is why Brazil, Indonesia and Portugal reach for the terraces. Classes reproduce them with an asymmetry that darkens the story into melodrama, which is why Mexico City and Rio de Janeiro reach for the gated district and the favela. Clans reproduce them with honour codes attached, sects with doctrine, regions with accents and voting patterns, and two chicken rice stalls with a completeness that exposes the mechanism by miniaturising it. The replacements also date the films: a cinema reveals its era by the division it finds most natural, clan in the Spain of 1963, class in the Mexico of 2002, political allegiance in the South Africa of 1993, occupation in the Ramallah of 2008. The map reads the feud column as a census of the world's divisions for exactly this reason. The families are interchangeable because the play built them to be replaced.

### Q: Which entry on the map is a television work rather than a cinema film?

The map's strongest African entry is one. uGugu no Andile was made for South African television, premiering on SABC1 in June 2008 as a six-part mini-series in the Shakespeare in Mzansi strand, before a film version carried it to festivals and the Africa Movie Academy Awards. The map includes it because the territory's record cannot be told honestly without it, and labels the medium in the table rather than smuggling a series into a cinema census. It is not alone in the survey's television borderland. Argentina's genuine screen life of the play runs through television, with a 1966 version directed by María Herminia Avellaneda in the filmographies and a full telenovela development in 2007, a reminder that in much of the world the play's mass audience has always been a broadcast audience. The map's rule is inclusion with labelling: television works enter where they carry a territory's record, graded and flagged, and the census counts only the rows whose medium and evidence are both stated. A map that excluded television would be tidier and falser, above all in the territories where the small screen did the work the cinema never attempted.

### Q: How was the map's evidence gathered and graded?

By a hierarchy of sources, applied uniformly and stated so that any reader can re-run the survey. Festival records and national film databases stand highest, followed by peer-reviewed scholarship and university archives, then reference filmographies, then trade and review press, with single-source claims flagged wherever they carry a row. Five grades result. A documented adaptation or retelling rests on the higher classes of evidence with the play's structure visible in the record. A title citation is documented as a film and documented as not following the play, a grade that exists because titles lie in a commercially useful direction. A reported entry rests on one lower-class source and is printed with its flag. A searched-and-not-found entry records a genuine search through the sources where a version would be listed, and states its own limits, since the survey worked chiefly through English-language scholarship and international filmographies. The grades are dated states of the evidence, not eternal verdicts. A stronger source can promote or demote any row, and the map's census counts only documented rows, so that its conclusions never rest on its thinnest evidence.

### Q: What does the worldwide spread actually prove about the play?

It proves portability, not universality, and the map insists on the distinction. A universal story would carry its meaning intact from port to port; this story arrives everywhere with its feud recast, its machinery rebuilt, its body count redistributed and, in every comedy on the map, its ending reversed. What survives the journey is a frame of three parts: two groups locked in a division neither can explain, lovers produced by those groups, and an obstruction machinery each society recognises as its own. The frame's spread is itself an industrial fact, carried by distribution networks, festival circuits, school syllabi and one very famous Italian film, as Mark Thornton Burnett's account of global Shakespeare cinema establishes. What local cinemas build inside the frame is authorship, as Alexa Alice Joubin's work on Asian adaptation demonstrates, and the builds differ more than they resemble one another. The honest conclusion flatters the play less and credits it more accurately. Shakespeare did not write a story every culture recognises as its own. He built, or inherited and perfected, a machine any culture can point at its own divisions, and the map is the record of the pointing.
