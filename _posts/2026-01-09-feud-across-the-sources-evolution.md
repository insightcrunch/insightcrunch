## How the Feud Evolved Across the Sources: The Verdict First

The feud in Romeo and Juliet has no stated cause, and the absence looks like a hole in the play until its history across the sources is restored. Traced from Dante's bare pairing of the names Montecchi and Cappelletti, through Luigi da Porto's invented novella, Matteo Bandello's courtly expansion, Pierre Boaistuau's French moralizing, and Arthur Brooke's English poem, the quarrel changes its nature at every stage. In Dante it is political faction, a symptom of an Italy abandoned by empire. In the novelle it becomes a family enmity inside one city, notorious, presumed, and already old. In the play it becomes an ancient grudge whose origin no character can supply, an inherited hatred with a body count. The verdict argued here is that Shakespeare's blank cause is a deliberate completion of a long process, not an oversight the process happened to produce. Each teller thinned the feud's explanation and thickened its machinery, and the play finishes that work: explanation falls to zero while the machinery reaches its maximum, armed servants in the first scene, public brawls, a prince's edict backed by death, and six corpses by the final tableau.

The popular account of the story takes the feud as given, a piece of scenery the lovers happen to be born behind. The source chain refuses that innocence. Dante names the two houses three centuries before the play without attaching any lover, any Verona street, or any quarrel between the houses themselves to the names. Da Porto, writing around 1524, fastens the names to Verona, invents the lovers, and gives the enmity its first narrative form as a hatred between two great households under the rule of Bartolomeo della Scala. Bandello, in the Novelle of 1554, keeps the enmity and surrounds it with the rhetoric of a court that prized magnificence and dreaded disorder. Boaistuau, in the Histoires tragiques of 1559, subordinates the quarrel to a sermon against disobedient youth. Brooke, in The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet of 1562, turns the whole inheritance into an English warning about unhonest desire and neglected authority. Shakespeare then performs the decisive subtraction and the decisive additions at once. The subtraction removes whatever residue of explanation the chain still carried. The additions give the unexplained thing its full civic anatomy: servants who fight it, citizens who club it apart, a prince who legislates against it, and a friar who schemes to dissolve it by marriage.

What each stage's feud reveals is the teller as much as the tale. Dante's pair of houses serves an imperial politics that reads private sorrow as public failure. Da Porto, a soldier of Venice crippled in Venetian service, imagines a Verona whose factions belong safely to a vanished century of signori. Bandello writes for courts that consumed stories of Italian disorder as both entertainment and instruction. Boaistuau writes for a France entering religious civil war, where a tale of domestic disobedience could be made to preach obedience. Brooke writes for a Protestant England anxious about Catholic friars, unruly servants, and children who marry without consent. The play gathers all of these investments and declines to assign the quarrel to any of them, which is why the quarrel survives translation into every later culture that inherits the story. A feud with a stated cause can be settled by argument. A feud without one can only be ended by loss, and the stations of that ending are the subject of the sections that follow.

The question that sends most readers to this strand is the simplest one the tradition generates: why do the two families hate each other? It is the question the feud explainer answers for the play on the play's own terms, and it is the question every teller from Dante onward was in a position to answer and did not. The persistence of the question across five centuries of readers is itself evidence in the case this study argues. Readers do not keep asking after accidents; a gap encountered once is a lapse, while a gap encountered at every stage of a story's transmission, by audiences in four countries and three languages, registers as the story's signature. The feud's evolution is the biography of that signature, written stage by stage in what each age added around the silence and in what each age's politics made of the feud it inherited.

The method of the study is comparative and documentary. Each telling in the chain is asked the same four questions. What form does the feud take: faction, family enmity, household premise, or civic emergency? What cause does the telling state, presume, or refuse? What public machinery attaches to the quarrel: a ruler's sentence, a statute, a crowd, a pulpit? And what toll does the quarrel exact before the story closes? Asked uniformly, the four questions produce the inverse law that the chart in this study displays. Across six tellings and nearly three centuries, the feud's explanation and the feud's apparatus never grow together. Where the cause is thickest, in Dante's imperial politics, the apparatus is absent; where the apparatus is thickest, in the play's Verona of edicts and club-wielding citizens, the cause is void. The middle tellings trade one for the other in steady proportions, shedding explanation as they accumulate machinery, until the exchange completes itself in a tragedy whose quarrel is all apparatus and no account. That law, and not any single author's whim, is what the verdict of design finally rests on.

## Dante's Feud: Two Names, No Lovers, and a Lament for Italy

The chain begins with three lines of verse and nothing else. In the sixth canto of the Purgatorio, Dante turns from the soul of Sordello to an invective against Italy, and the two houses enter literature as evidence in that indictment: "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti, / Monaldi e Filippeschi, uom sanza cura: / color già tristi, e questi con sospetti" (Purgatorio 6.106-108). In Allen Mandelbaum's translation the summons runs, "Come and see the Montecchi and the Cappelletti, the Monaldi and the Filippeschi, heedless man: the first already in sorrow, the others in fear." The address is aimed at the emperor Albert of Austria, the heedless man who has abandoned Italy to its factions. Nothing in the lines narrates a quarrel between the two houses named together. The Montecchi stand for grief already incurred, the Monaldi for the same; the Cappelletti and Filippeschi stand for fear of what comes next. The pairing is a rhetorical bracket inside a political lament, and the fuller account of [Dante's Montecchi and Cappelletti](/2025/11/28/dante-montecchi-cappelletti-feud/) sets out the documentary record behind the names, which this study treats as its prehistory and does not re-argue.

The historical houses behind the names were real, and their reality was factional rather than romantic. The Montecchi were Ghibelline lords whose strength lay at Montecchio Maggiore near Vicenza, partisans of the empire in the long contest between empire and papacy. The Cappelletti were a leading Guelph family of Cremona, aligned with the papal party in the same contest. No surviving document shows the two houses fighting each other; their pairing in Dante's line is geographic and thematic, a Vicentine house and a Cremonese house yoked as twin symptoms of a peninsula where every city had divided against itself. Dante's own city, Florence, had torn itself apart between Guelph and Ghibelline and then between White and Black Guelphs, and the poet wrote the Commedia in exile from that fracture. The feud at the chain's first stage is therefore not an event but a condition: the condition of Italy under absentee empire, where family greatness expressed itself as faction and faction expressed itself as ruin.

### Why does Dante give the houses no lovers and no Verona street?

Because Dante is not telling a story but delivering a political indictment. The two houses enter the Purgatorio as exhibits in a charge against an Italy abandoned by its emperor, their distinction spent on faction, their names useful to the poet solely as proof that great families decline into sorrow and suspicion.

The consequence for the evolution traced here is exact. Dante contributes the names, the altitude of the houses, and an atmosphere of inherited political sorrow, and he contributes nothing else: no Verona setting, since his lines belong to a survey of all Italy; no lovers, since no love story attaches to the names for another two centuries; and no quarrel between the houses, since the enmity that later tellers assume must be invented between his line and Da Porto's novella. The first stage of the feud is thus a feud in potential, two names held in a single breath by a poet whose subject was a nation's disorder. Every later teller who writes as though the Montecchi and Cappelletti had always been enemies is building on ground Dante left empty. The emptiness matters for the verdict at the end of this study, because the chain's deepest habit begins here: the feud is everywhere presumed and nowhere explained, from the first line onward. Dante presumes the houses' sorrow to be notorious enough to need no narration. Da Porto presumes the enmity notorious enough to need no origin. The play presumes the grudge ancient enough to need no cause. The presumption is the constant; only its object changes.

Dante had a remedy in theory for the condition the names emblemize, and the remedy confirms the reading. In the Monarchia, his Latin treatise on universal empire, Dante argues that a single emperor, owning everything and therefore coveting nothing, is the one power placed to hold the ring among competing cities and houses: faction is what fills the vacuum left by absent jurisdiction. The Purgatorio canto dramatizes the vacuum; the treatise proposes the fill. The Montecchi and Cappelletti thus enter literature inside a complete political philosophy, as instances of the disease for which empire is the cure, and the feud strand's long future can be stated as the story of that philosophy's disappearance. Da Porto's Scala is the cure's ghost, a single lord whose presence frames the tale and whose justice closes it; Bandello's courts admire strong princes from a distance; Boaistuau and Brooke relocate the cure inside the household, in paternal authority; the play's Escalus attempts the cure by statute and confesses its failure. Read along the strand, the feud grows exactly as fast as the ring-holder weakens, until nothing holds the ring at all and the hatred must be ended by the deaths of the heirs themselves.

The canto's own drama underlines the politics. The invective is triggered by an embrace: Virgil and Sordello, strangers who discover a shared Mantuan birth, fall into each other's arms, and Dante contrasts that spontaneous civic tenderness with the Italy of his day, whose citizens within single walls devour one another. The Montecchi and Cappelletti enter the poem as paired exhibits in that contrast, houses whose greatness has curdled into faction precisely because no emperor holds the ring. The early commentators on the Commedia gloss the lines in the same key, treating the two houses as bywords for the ruin that party hatred brings on noble families, and nothing in the commentary tradition attaches a lover, a tomb, or a private quarrel to the names. Dante's contribution to the feud is thus a frame without a picture: two surnames, a reputation for sorrow, and a theory of why Italian families destroy themselves. The picture, a Verona, a feast, a friar, a tomb, waits two hundred years for a wounded cavalry officer of Vicenza to paint it, and the frame he hangs it in is Dante's.

## The Missing Rung: Masuccio's Tale and the Feud That Is Not There

Between Dante and Da Porto stands a tale that supplies half the plot and none of the feud, and its absence of enmity is as instructive as any presence. Masuccio Salernitano, the pen name of Tommaso Guardati, published Il Novellino at Naples in 1476, and its thirty-third tale tells of Mariotto Mignanelli of Siena and his Ganozza: a secret marriage blessed by a friar, a killing that drives the husband into exile at Alexandria, a wife who takes a powder and feigns death to escape a forced second marriage, a message that fails, a tomb opened by a returning husband too late, and a double ruin, Mariotto beheaded for the tomb violation and Ganozza dead of grief in a convent. The machinery of the later tragedy is substantially present, assembled in Siena a generation before Da Porto wrote. The feud is entirely absent. Mariotto's obstacle is not an enemy house but a city's law, a father's plans, and the distance between Siena and Egypt.

Masuccio's tale therefore functions as the control case for the whole evolution. It demonstrates that the story's catastrophe does not require a feud at all: distance, law, and a failed letter suffice. What the feud adds, when Da Porto invents it, is not necessity but a different distribution of blame and a different kind of obstacle, one lodged inside the hero's own house and blood rather than in geography and courts. The comparison also fixes what the feud is for. In Masuccio, the lovers stand against circumstance. In the Verona chain, the lovers stand against their own names, an obstacle that travels with them into every room and that no voyage can outrun. The genealogy of the whole tradition, branch by branch, is mapped in [the source family tree of the tale's descent](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/); the concern here is narrower, the single strand of the quarrel as it passes along that tree. At the Siena stage the strand does not yet exist, and the tale's success without it proves that its later invention was a choice with consequences, the first of the choices this study tracks.

The setting sharpens the lesson further. Siena was no stranger to faction: the republic organized its own politics around rival monti, orders of citizens whose competition for office structured public life, and any fifteenth-century Italian reader meeting a tale of Siena brought faction to it unbidden, as Dante's readers brought faction to his names. Masuccio himself, a courtier of the Aragonese Naples writing under a Salerno mask-name, lived amid the baronial enmities of the southern kingdom and filled Il Novellino with tales of revenge, jealousy, and civic treachery. A feud was therefore available to him as a device, familiar in his world and in his book, and the Mariotto tale does without it entirely: the lovers' enemies are a sentence, a sea, and a father's second choice of husband, none of them a house. The restraint completes the control experiment. When Da Porto, two generations later, rebuilds the same plot on a feud, the addition is not the filling of a gap in the machinery, since the machinery ran without it at Siena; it is the importation of a theme, and themes are chosen.

One detail of Masuccio's tale sharpens the control. Mariotto does kill a man, striking down a fellow citizen in a street quarrel, and the killing is what exiles him; the tale therefore contains lethal violence between young men of the same city, the raw material of every feud, and still generates no enmity between houses, no retaliation, no inherited quarrel. The dead man's family does not pursue him; the law does. Siena's catastrophe is juridical and geographic, a sentence and a sea voyage, while Verona's will be genealogical, a surname and a street. The difference isolates the feud's specific gravity with experimental neatness. Law can be fled, distance can be crossed, and a sentence can expire; a name cannot be put off at the city gate, which is why the tradition, once Da Porto supplied the name-hatred, never again let it go.

## Da Porto Invents the Feud: Verona's Open Secret Under the Scala

Luigi da Porto (1485-1529) performed the invention on which everything after him depends. A gentleman of Vicenza who served Venice as a cavalry captain and was crippled by wounds received at Gradisca in 1511, he retired to Montorso Vicentino and wrote the novella known as Giulietta e Romeo around 1524; it was published at Venice in 1530, after his death, and in a fuller form in 1535. His frame presents the tale as history. The narrator is Peregrino, an archer of Da Porto's own company, who recounts what he heard in Verona: in the days when Bartolomeo della Scala ruled the city, two noble houses, the Cappelletti and the Montecchi, stood in open and bitter enmity, and Romeo Montecchi, attending a carnival ball at the Cappelletti palace in disguise, saw Giulietta and was lost. The dedication of the tale to Lucina Savorgnan wraps the whole in the conventions of courtly address, but the narrative claim is chronicle, not fable: this happened, in that city, under that lord.

The feud Da Porto invents has three properties that govern all its descendants. First, it is public knowledge inside the fiction. The enmity between the houses is presented as a standing fact of Verona that every character already knows; Giulietta's danger and Romeo's trespass at the ball register instantly with both households because the hatred needs no introduction. Second, it is unexplained. Da Porto, as Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957) makes visible by printing the novella in translation beside its descendants, gives the enmity no origin, no founding insult, no disputed inheritance or office; the houses hate as a condition of their greatness, in a city where great houses were understood to hate. Third, it is weakly mechanized. The enmity shapes the courtship's secrecy and supplies the killing of Tebaldo Cappelletti, whom Romeo stabs in a street encounter after Tebaldo strikes at him, but the novella stages no brawl, no riot, no civic disturbance. Its violence is the violence of individual gentlemen, and its authority figure, the Scala lord, acts from above the action, banishing Romeo to Mantua by sentence rather than intervening in any street.

The tale's dedication adds a private irony to its public posture. Da Porto addressed the novella to Lucina Savorgnan, and scholarship has long connected the gift with the author's own hopeless attachment to that lady, a love across the lines of rank and circumstance that the wounded officer could celebrate only by narrating a more fortunate and more fatal one. Whether or not the biography is pressed, the frame's doubleness is in the text: the feud story arrives as a courtly offering about desire, told by a soldier to a poet, inside a company of arms, and presented as Verona's history. The enmity of the houses is the offering's dark ground, the obstacle that gives the lovers' brief brightness its price, and Da Porto handles it with a soldier's economy, never wasting narrative on its origins because a soldier knows that the origins of enmities are the least operational part of them. Armies, like houses, fight the enemy in front of them; the history is for chroniclers, and Da Porto, for all his historia's dress, declines the chronicler's one essential duty at exactly the point where the feud is concerned.

Da Porto's politics can be read off that arrangement. A wounded officer of the Venetian republic, writing after the wars of the League of Cambrai had devastated the Veneto, he sets his Verona two centuries in the past, under a signore whose strong rule frames the tale and whose justice closes its public business. The factions of that Verona are safely dead, their city long absorbed into the Venetian terraferma whose peace Da Porto had bled for. His feud is accordingly elegiac rather than alarming: enmity as a relic of the age of signori, in a story whose true dangers are private, a girl's forced marriage to the Count of Lodrone, a friar's powder, a letter that never arrives. The hatred between the houses gives the lovers their secrecy and their pathos, but Da Porto's imagination of disorder stops at the threshold of the street. No crowd gathers. No servant draws. The feud has a name, a city, and a body, Romeo's killing of Tebaldo, before it has any crowd at all.

One further acquisition belongs to this stage, and the chart later in this study records it: the presumed cause. Da Porto's readers are invited to presume that great houses in a signorial city hate over precedence, honor, and the memory of injuries, the standard furniture of Italian civic life as writers of novelle understood it. The presumption is never stated as a fact of the tale; it is the silence around the enmity, filled in by any Italian reader from common knowledge of how such hatreds ran. That silent presumption is the feud's second inheritance, after Dante's names, and it travels through Bandello and Boaistuau into Brooke intact: at every stage, audiences supply from their own world the reason the text withholds. The play will break that contract by building a city in which the presumption can no longer do its quiet work, because the feud has swollen too large and too public for a private reason to be proportionate to it. Da Porto cannot be blamed for the disproportion; his feud is still sized to its presumed cause, two households' honor in one city. The disproportion is created later, by tellers who enlarge the feud's effects without enlarging its explanation, and it is completed by a playwright who makes the gap itself the subject.

The novella's central scenes show the enmity working at its original scale, intimate and social rather than civic. At the carnival ball in the Cappelletti palace, Romeo enters disguised among his friends, and the enmity announces itself as an etiquette problem: Tebaldo Cappelletti recognizes the intruder and burns to expel him, and the house's own courtesy checks the impulse, the feast's honor standing for one evening above the feud's honor. The lovers' first exchange grows out of that suspension, Giulietta taking the stranger's hand in the dance inside a room where his name would be a provocation. Da Porto's feud is thus a thing of thresholds and recognitions, deadly at the level of the duel and the intercepted life, and it never once fills a street. Even its crisis is private in form: Romeo, meeting Tebaldo with companions outside the carnival's truce, is struck at and kills in self-defense, a gentleman's catastrophe handled by a prince's sentence, banishment to Mantua, as though the city itself had not been disturbed at all.

Da Porto also plants the feud's first great alibi, its claim to be history, and the claim deserves scrutiny because the whole tradition inherits it. The novella's first edition of 1530 presents itself as a historia newly recovered, the tale of two noble lovers and their piteous death in Verona in the time of Bartolomeo della Scala, and the frame works hard for that status: a named soldier-narrator, a journey, a city the teller knows, a ruler two centuries dead. Nothing in the apparatus is evidence, yet the posture matters for the feud specifically, because a feud reported as civic history acquires the authority of record. Italian readers who might have challenged an invented enmity's missing origin accepted a chronicled enmity's missing origin as a mere gap in the archive, and the distinction governed the story's reception for eighty years. The play, which announces itself as stage traffic and claims no archive at all, will paradoxically be the first telling honest about the feud's evidentiary state: nothing known, everything enacted.

The marriage pressure that drives the catastrophe deserves the same notice, because it is not feud pressure. Giulietta's father promises her to the Count of Lodrone, a match of advantage with no enmity in it; the Count is nobody's enemy, and her refusal of him would be a family crisis in any household of Verona, feuding or not. Da Porto's plot therefore runs on two separable engines, the enmity that hides the marriage and the dynastic marriage market that forces the powder plot, and the engines only interlock. The feud's contribution to the deaths is real but partial, a contribution of secrecy and of one killing, while the letter's failure and the tomb's timing belong to fortune. Later tellers, Shakespeare above all, will weld the engines together until the feud powers every stage of the ruin; measuring Da Porto's looser coupling is what makes the play's welding visible as a choice rather than a given.

## Bandello's Feud at Court: Magnificence and the Rhetoric of Discord

Matteo Bandello (c. 1485-1561), a Dominican friar who became a courtier, diplomat, and eventually bishop of Agen in France, printed the tale at Lucca in 1554 as the ninth novella of the second part of his Novelle. His retelling, which Bullough prints in translation in the same 1957 volume, follows Da Porto's plot closely and enlarges almost everything around it: the tale grows to roughly three times the length of its model, the growth spent on dialogue, rhetoric, ceremony, and moral reflection. The feud grows with the rest, not in incident but in emphasis. Bandello's narrator returns to the enmity of the Montecchi and Cappelletti as a standing calamity of Verona, dwells on the houses' greatness and obstinacy, and frames the story within a dedicatory apparatus that presents the Novelle as a court's record of the world's instructive disorders. The quarrel in Bandello acquires the weight of a theme: two houses whose hatred the narrator treats as a specimen of the discord that ruins cities and families alike.

The additions Bandello makes to the story's machinery mostly lie outside the feud strand: the Nurse promoted toward a character, the Mantuan poison purchase slowed into a scene, the tomb sequence staged at ceremonial length. Within the strand itself, his contribution is tonal and political. Bandello spent his career in the service of great houses, the Gonzaga at Mantua among them, and finished his life pensioned in France; his Verona is accordingly a city seen from the palace, where faction is the besetting sin of the nobility and where the remedy for disorder is the wisdom of princes and the instruction of examples. His feud is magnificent, rhetorical, and backward-looking, the enmity of two princely households narrated for the warning of courts. What it never becomes in his hands is popular. The crowd of Verona does not take up the quarrel; servants do not arm for it; the street does not ring with it. Bandello enlarges the feud's language while leaving its social range exactly where Da Porto put it, confined to gentlemen and their houses.

That confinement is the fact to carry forward, because the chain's remaining tellers preserve it and the play abolishes it. In Bandello the feud's public dimension is narratorial: the narrator tells the reader that the enmity was great and damaging. In the play the feud's public dimension is enacted: the audience watches it stop traffic, draw citizens with clubs, and bring a prince down into the street to legislate. The distance between those two treatments is the distance between chronicle and theatre, and Bandello stands at the chronicle end of it, the tale's most splendid narrator and the feud's most eloquent mourner. His stage of the evolution also sharpens the presumed cause into something closer to a diagnosis: the houses hate, his rhetoric implies, because great Italian families of the old time hated, out of emulation, pride, and the competition of honor, and a reader at the court of Mantua or Agen needed no more specification than that. The diagnosis is cultural, not narrative; it explains the type and never the case. When Shakespeare's Verona arrives, the type will be embodied and the case will still be missing, and the tension between an embodied feud and a missing case is the engine the rest of this study examines.

Bandello's scale explains his emphasis. The Novelle run to 214 tales across the three parts printed at Lucca in 1554, each furnished with a dedicatory letter to a patron, a courtier, or a lady of the circles Bandello served, and the collection amounts to a vast social record of the Italian world as the courts wished to contemplate it: its wars, adulteries, revenges, and disorders, each packaged as instruction. The Romeo tale's dedication and framing place the Verona enmity inside that atlas as a known disorder of a known kind, and Bandello's narrator lingers where a courtier's interest lingers, on the bearing of great persons under the pressure of the houses' quarrel, on the splendor of the feast, on the decorum of grief. What a modern reader registers as the feud's thinness in Bandello is, from inside his project, a propriety: faction was a subject for lament and example, not for anatomy, and anatomy is precisely what the play will perform on it. Bandello hands on a feud swollen with significance and starved of mechanism, the exact inverse of the object the play finally constructs.

His biography underwrites the retrospect. Bandello was born at Castelnuovo Scrivia, professed as a Dominican, and spent the middle decades of his life in the service of the great, in Milan, Mantua, and finally in France under the protection of Cesare Fregoso, whose household at Agen he followed and whose widow's circle preserved his papers. He had watched the Italian wars dismantle the world of the independent courts from inside those courts, and the Novelle, assembled across those decades, have the temper of salvage: a civilization's stories gathered by a man who had seen its order fail. His Verona feud belongs to that salvage operation. The enmity of the Montecchi and Cappelletti is presented as belonging wholly to a finished past, to the time of the signori, dangerous now only as an example, and the safety of the distance is the point. Bandello's readers could contemplate faction with the pleasure of survivors. The play removes the distance, sets the feud in a present tense of brawls and statutes, and denies its audience the survivor's seat; that removal is the last step of the evolution and the one Bandello's example makes easiest to measure.

## Boaistuau's Feud in a Sermon: The French Moral Frame

Pierre Boaistuau (c. 1500-1566) carried the tale across the Alps in the Histoires tragiques of 1559, a collection of free adaptations from Bandello that founded a French genre. His Rhomeo et Julietta, the third tale of the collection, is the version England would inherit, and its handling of the feud marks the evolution's quietest and most paradoxical stage: the quarrel shrinks as everything around it grows louder. Boaistuau expands the tale with long moralizing addresses, sententious reflections, and rhetorical speeches, and his additions to the plot are real, most famously the apothecary of Mantua, whom source scholarship credits to the French stage of the chain. But the enmity of the houses recedes toward a premise. In Boaistuau the story's center of gravity moves from the city's discord to the children's disobedience; the feud becomes the given condition that makes the secret marriage explicable, while the narrator's energy goes into sermons on unruly desire, neglected parental authority, and the wages of haste.

The politics of that shrinkage belong to Boaistuau's France. The Histoires tragiques appeared in 1559, on the edge of the wars of religion that would divide French households against each other for a generation, and the collection's dedication and framing present tragic tales as instruments of instruction for a Christian readership. A French adapter preaching order in 1559 had every reason to make the tale argue for obedience inside the family: the household was the unit whose discipline his book existed to defend, and a feud between households, with its suggestion that the fathers themselves were the disorder, sat awkwardly inside that argument. Boaistuau's solution is the one the whole English tradition inherits. The fathers' enmity is retained as machinery and denied as subject; the moral scrutiny falls on the young, the friar, and fortune, anywhere except on the two patriarchs whose hatred built the trap. The presumed cause does its last quiet work at this stage, because a French reader of 1559, like an Italian reader of 1530, could be trusted to understand that great houses hate. No later audience would be given so little help, and no later teller would notice the withdrawal.

One inheritance from the French stage deserves separate record, since the play's opening movement depends on its absence. Boaistuau narrates the enmity in the tale's exposition, in summary, as background: the lovers' story proper begins with the feast, and the feud's violence waits for the single combat in which Romeo kills the heroine's cousin. There is no street disorder in the French version, no civic alarm, no legislation. The feud at the end of its continental journey is thus at its least theatrical and its most purely functional: a reason the marriage must be secret, and nothing more. Brooke receives it in exactly that state.

The French stage also demonstrates, in passing, how a feud can be displaced by its own scenery. Boaistuau's most durable invention, the apothecary of Mantua, belongs to the story's world of law and poverty: a poor man, forbidden on pain of death to sell poison, whom Romeo's gold persuades. The scene the Frenchman adds has nothing to do with the houses' hatred, yet it extends the tale's map of a society organized by prohibitions, edicts, and the cash that dissolves them, the same civic weather in which a feud lives. When the play later builds its own far larger machinery of prohibition around the quarrel, proclamation, penalty, and quarantine, it is developing an instinct the chain acquired in France: the story keeps generating law, and the law keeps failing, and the feud is the name the tradition eventually gives to everything the law cannot reach. Boaistuau, preaching the household's internal order, cannot see that his additions are assembling the feud's true portrait from the outside; Brooke, versifying him within three years, does not see it either.

The French collection's own afterlife measures how completely its settlement governed the story's European career. François de Belleforest took up the Histoires tragiques after Boaistuau and continued them volume by volume, and the tragic tale in French became a durable industry of exemplary catastrophe, translated, imitated, and mined by playwrights in two kingdoms. That the Verona story entered England twice within five years, in Brooke's verse and Painter's prose, both times from the French rather than from Bandello's Italian, shows the moral frame's export strength: England did not import the feud, it imported a sermon with a feud inside it. The distinction explains the play's peculiar starting position three decades later. Shakespeare's audience, insofar as it knew the story at all, knew it as a warning against disobedient love in which two great houses happened to hate. The play's first scene, a full movement of that hatred enacted in the street before the courtship has even begun, is a direct correction of the imported proportions, restoring to the feud the narrative mass the French settlement had stripped from it while keeping, pointedly, the French settlement's silence about its cause.

## The Feud in English Dress: Brooke's Warning and Painter's Prose

Arthur Brooke (died 1563, drowned in a Channel crossing) Englished Boaistuau in verse in The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet, printed by Richard Tottell in 1562, in the poulter's measure whose long lines would carry the story to English readers for decades. Brooke's feud is his French source's feud, narrated as ancient hatred between two great houses of Verona, and its English character comes from the poem's frame rather than from any change to the quarrel itself. His address to the reader announces the use to which the tale must be put: the lovers are "a couple of unfortunate lovers, thralling themselves to unhonest desire, neglecting the authority and advice of parents and friends," abetted by "drunken gossips and superstitious friars." The preface's distribution of blame is the English settlement of the story's moral account, and the feud figures in it only as scenery. The parents whose hatred made the secrecy necessary are charged with nothing worse than neglected advice; the anti-Catholic edge of the poem falls on the friar, and the anti-romance edge falls on the young. Brooke's Verona is a city whose great houses hate in the background while the foreground sermonizes the victims of that hatred, an arrangement whose unfairness the play will eventually expose by moving the background into the first scene.

Within the poem, the feud's incidents remain Da Porto's incidents at Boaistuau's remove: the ball, the secret marriage, the street fight in which Romeus kills Tybalt, the banishment pronounced by the city's prince, the forced marriage, the powder, the tomb. Brooke narrates skirmishes of the houses' followers as part of the enmity's reputation, but his poem, like the novelle, keeps the quarrel gentlemanly in rank and episodic in form. The prince of his poem is a sentencing authority, not a legislator under pressure; no edict precedes the killings, no penalty is published against future brawls, because the poem imagines no future brawls to deter. William Painter's prose version, "the goodly history of the true and constant love of Romeo and Julietta" in the second tome of The Palace of Pleasure (1567), travels the same French road with the same inheritance: the deadly enmity of the houses stated in the opening movement as the condition of the tale, then left to operate as premise while the narrative attends to the lovers. Painter's prose reached the readers Brooke's verse could not, but for the feud strand the two English versions are one witness: England received a feud that was ancient, bitter, gentlemanly, unexplained, and quiet.

Brooke's poem runs to some three thousand lines, and its treatment of the quarrel across that expanse confirms the preface's priorities. The houses' hatred is invoked at the poem's opening as the standing condition of Verona and thereafter surfaces at the story's joints, the feast, the fight, the sentence, without ever being opened up for inspection. Romeus kills Tybalt in a street encounter that the poem narrates as the feud's harvest, and the prince's banishment follows as judgment on a deed, not as policy toward a pattern. No English reader of 1562, meeting the story in Brooke or five years later in Painter, was given a single line of origin for the enmity, a single servant who fought for it, or a single law aimed at its recurrence; the English feud is an atmosphere, breathed by the characters and unquestioned by the narration. Painter's prose, for its part, domesticates the tale for the study rather than the hall, and its feud reads accordingly, less a public calamity than a social fact, the kind of enmity between great families that a reader of histories was expected to recognize as one of the world's ordinary weathers. Between them, the two English versions fixed the story's terms for the generation that included a young playwright from Warwickshire: names, houses, a friar, a feast, a fight, a tomb, and a hatred with everything except a reason.

The print record kept those terms current for three decades. Brooke's poem was reprinted in 1587, and Painter's Palace of Pleasure reached further editions across the same years, so the story's English form in the early 1590s was not a rarity but a standing item of the book trade, its feud as familiar and as unexplained to London readers as it had been to the readers of 1562. The familiarity matters for measuring the play's intervention. Shakespeare was not rescuing an obscure tale; he was revising a standard one whose proportions every reader knew, the hatred as premise, the lovers as subject, the moral as warning. A revision that opens with the premise, spends its first scene arming the premise's servants, and builds the premise a statute and a death roll announces itself against that standard as plainly as a title page could. The feud's evolution ends, therefore, not in a private act of composition but in a public act of correction, performed before an audience positioned to recognize exactly what had been changed and what, with equal deliberateness, had been left alone.

The state of the strand on the eve of the play can therefore be summarized in a single sentence. Across Dante, Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, and Painter, the feud had gained names, a city, a pair of houses, a body, and a presumption of cause, and it had never gained a servant, a riot, a statute, or an origin. Every item in the second list is the play's own contribution, and the chart that follows sets the two lists side by side, stage by stage.

The chart's shape is the study in miniature. Cause and machinery move in opposite directions down the rows: explanation drains away as enforcement accumulates, until the final row pairs a completely blank cause with a completely articulated civic machine. That inverse relation is not an accident of transmission, since transmission alone, as the Masuccio row proves, can carry the plot with no feud at all; it is the product of choices at the chain's two creative ends, Da Porto inventing an enmity with no origin and Shakespeare, three generations later, inventing the origin's conspicuous absence by surrounding the old blank with a city that ought to be able to fill it.

The toll column tells its own story when read as a sequence rather than a set of entries. Dante's toll is affective, sorrow and suspicion, damage to the inner state of houses not yet attached to a story. Masuccio, with no feud, exacts his toll through law and grief, an execution and a convent death, demonstrating that the plot's machinery of loss runs at full power on other fuel. Da Porto lights the feud and the toll becomes corporeal and specific: a cousin dead in the street, two lovers dead in a tomb. Thereafter the toll freezes for three tellings, Bandello, Boaistuau, and Brooke inheriting the same three deaths while the sermons lengthen around them, until the play doubles it at a stroke and redistributes it across the whole social order, prince's kin, count, mother, heir, heiress. A tradition's values show in what it is willing to multiply, and this tradition, offered eighty years of chances to multiply anything else about the feud, its causes, its battles, its speeches, chose in the end to multiply only its dead.

Two rows of the chart reward a second look. The Masuccio row, with its empty feud cell, guards the study against a lazy determinism: nothing about the lovers' plot compels a feud to exist, so every later presence of the feud is an author's act. And the shared Brooke and Painter row guards against a lazy nationalism: the play's additions were not latent in the English language or the English versions, both of which carried the quarrel in its most diminished continental state. The transformation happens at the last row, in the theatre, where a feud that had been narrated for three generations is suddenly required to happen in front of witnesses, in real time, with bodies. Embodiment is the final cause of the blank's exposure. A narrator can presume a reason and pass on; a stage must show men fighting, and an audience watching men fight over nothing will eventually notice the nothing.

## What the Play Adds: Armed Servants, Street Brawls, an Edict, a Body Count

The play announces its feud before it announces its lovers, and the announcement is an inventory of additions. The Prologue, a sonnet spoken by the Chorus, frames the whole action as a feud story with a love story inside it: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue, lines 1-4). The lovers enter at line 5 as the feud's offspring, "a pair of star-cross'd lovers," and the sonnet's remaining lines allot more space to the quarrel than to the courtship: "the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, nought could remove" (Prologue, lines 9-10). The full reading of [the feud as the play itself stages it, and of why the Capulets and Montagues hate](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) belongs to the play-internal account; what concerns the evolution is that every civic element in that staging is new. No source gives the feud a chorus, a prologue, or a sonnet. No source opens with it. The play's first decision about the feud is positional: it comes first, before desire, before the feast, before either lover speaks.

### What has the feud already cost Verona before the lovers meet?

Three public brawls, by the Prince's own count, bred of an airy word, have disturbed the quiet of the streets before the action opens (1.1), and the Prologue has already charged the feud with the deaths to come: the city begins the play in debt to a quarrel it cannot itemize.

The opening scene then embodies what the sonnet summarized. Sampson and Gregory, servants of the Capulet house, swagger through a public place trading boasts about the Montague men and women they would master, and Sampson bites his thumb at the approaching Montague servants, a gesture he is careful to keep deniable: "I do bite my thumb, sir," he concedes when challenged on the law of the insult, having told his fellow, "the law is on our side, if I say ay" only as a hope (1.1). The exchange is the feud's first acquisition in its theatrical life: deniable, servant-borne, obscene, and comic, a quarrel conducted by employees who did not invent it and cannot state it. Benvolio enters drawing to part the fray and is met by Tybalt's creed, the only attempt any character makes to define the feud's terms: "What, drawn, and talk of peace? I hate the word, / As I hate hell, all Montagues, and thee" (1.1). Hate of a word, hate of a category, hate of a person, in that order: Tybalt's sentence is the closest the play comes to a theory of its quarrel, and it is a theory of pure objectless escalation, which is why it satisfies. The citizens arrive with clubs crying "Down with the Capulets! Down with the Montagues!", the two old patriarchs call for their swords, Lady Capulet mocks the crutch her husband needs instead, and the feud completes its social circuit in a single scene: servants, gentlemen, citizens, patriarchs, wives, and finally the state.

### Who fights the feud's first battle on stage, and why are they servants?

Servants fight it because the feud in the play is a household infection before it is a duel of gentlemen. Sampson and Gregory open the action boasting for the Capulet house they serve, so the lowest rank carries the quarrel into the street before either patriarch appears to own it.

The state's entrance is the feud's second great acquisition, the edict. Prince Escalus, a figure the sources keep as a distant sentencing authority, descends into the street in person and legislates: "Three civil brawls, bred of an airy word, / By thee, old Capulet, and Montague, / Have thrice disturb'd the quiet of our streets" (1.1). His sentence converts the feud from a private condition into a capital public offense prospectively: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). No telling before the play arms authority in advance against the quarrel; Da Porto's Scala and Brooke's prince punish a killing already done, while Escalus attempts deterrence against a pattern, names its rhythm of recurrence, and prices its next outbreak at the patriarchs' lives. The edict also supplies the feud with a history the sources never gave it: three prior brawls, bred of an airy word. The phrase performs the blank in miniature. Verona's public record of its feud consists of a count and a shrug, disturbances thrice repeated from a cause the Prince himself describes as air.

### Why does the Friar think a secret marriage can end what edicts cannot?

Because law has already failed three times, and the Friar reasons in sacraments rather than penalties. A marriage joins the two households in a single body, converting enmity into kinship at a stroke, and Laurence wagers that blood relation can accomplish what the Prince's death-backed edict could not (2.3).

The street fight of 3.1 is where the additions converge and detonate, and its mechanics repay attention because none of them exist in the sources in this form. In Da Porto and his descendants the corresponding killing is a single encounter, Romeo against Tebaldo, combat between principals of the two houses. The play inserts a third party, a second refusal, and a fatal intervention. Romeo, newly married and therefore Tybalt's kinsman by the sacrament neither man can publish, refuses the challenge in language the feud's code can only read as shame, and Mercutio supplies the code's verdict: "O calm, dishonourable, vile submission!" (3.1). The insult is the feud thinking aloud through a man who belongs to neither house. Mercutio draws in Romeo's place, Romeo steps between the blades to stop a fight his secret has already made unintelligible, and Tybalt's sword finds Mercutio under Romeo's arm. Every element is an acquisition of the play's feud: a victim from outside the houses, a peacemaker disabled by the feud's secrecy, a death caused by the attempt to prevent one. The novelle's feud kills its principals in single combat; the play's feud kills a bystander through the machinery of mediation itself, which is the difference between a quarrel and a system.

The marriage plot is thereby reframed as feud policy. Friar Laurence agrees to marry the lovers for a stated civic reason: "For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3). The sources' friars marry the couple out of pity, confession-craft, or plot convenience; only the play's friar is a peace theorist, a subject of the Prince attempting by sacrament what the Prince attempted by statute, and the failure of his policy, through quarantine and a sealed door at a Mantua lodging, is the feud's final demonstration that neither law nor sacrament can reach it. Meanwhile the feud acquires its body count, each death a category the sources lack or hold singly. Mercutio, kinsman to the Prince and friend to Romeo, dies under Romeo's arm in the street brawl of 3.1, killed by a feud that is not his own, cursing the houses with a precision no other character achieves: "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1). Tybalt falls minutes later. Paris, the County suitor, dies at the tomb in 5.3, killed by a banished man he mistakes for a vandal. Lady Montague dies offstage of grief at her son's exile, reported in the final scene's inventory of loss (5.3). Add the lovers themselves and the feud's toll in the play is six, against the three the novelle exact. The evolution's arithmetic is stark: the play doubles the deaths while halving the explanation, and it arranges for the deaths to fall with geometric fairness, one kinsman of the Prince, one of each house's pride, one mother, and the two children whose marriage was the only peace treaty Verona produced.

Between the edict and the body count stands the feast of 1.5, the feud's middle register, where the play shows the quarrel being managed rather than fought. Tybalt recognizes the disguised Romeo and moves to expel him, and old Capulet imposes the house's hospitality over the house's hatred, declaring that the guest shall be endured and that he would not, for the wealth of all the town, do him disparagement under his own roof (1.5). The scene has a Da Porto ancestor, the checked impulse at the Cappelletti ball, but the play sharpens it into an economics: Capulet prices his courtesy against the town's whole wealth and pays it, while Tybalt, sent off with a warning, banks the insult for the street. The feud in the play is thus not a constant blaze but a system with settings, truce at table, war in the square, and the settings are switched by the elders' convenience, a detail that quietly destroys any alibi of uncontrollable passion. Men who can switch the hatred off for a banquet can be asked why they switch it on for a funeral.

The edict's fate completes the civic picture. Escalus had priced disturbance at the patriarchs' lives, yet when Romeo kills Tybalt the sentence is banishment, immediate and expressly mitigated, and the Prince's own closing audit confesses the compromise: "And I for winking at your discords too / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punish'd" (5.3). The confession is the feud's last acquisition and its most damning: the law's keeper counts himself among the feud's casualties and its enablers at once, a ruler whose edict failed because enforcement, in a city where the offenders are the two greatest houses, was always going to wink. No source gives its ruler such a line, because no source gave its ruler an edict to fail at. The play invents the statute, invents the pattern that provokes it, and invents the sovereign's admission that the whole apparatus was theatre, leaving the feud exactly where the novelle left it, in the hands of grieving fathers, but now with the difference exposed for judgment: Verona had built every institution that could have ended the quarrel, and the quarrel had outlasted them all.

## The Names of the Feud: What Each Age Called the Hatred

The feud's vocabulary migrates with its form, and the migration leaves evidence the incidents alone cannot supply. Dante has no noun for a quarrel between the houses at all, since his lines stage no quarrel: his words for the houses' condition are sorrow and suspicion, the emotional weather of faction, and the political noun that governs the canto is division, the state of a land whose parts have declared war on the whole. The Italian novelle settle on inimicizia, enmity, a formal and almost legal noun denoting a recognized state between parties, the condition that obtained between cities, factions, and great houses and that carried understood rules of injury and satisfaction. Da Porto and Bandello inherit that noun's world even where their tales soften it with odio, hatred: the feud in Italian is a status, something two houses are in, as they might be in alliance or in debt. The word presumes a legible relationship between known parties, exactly the presumption the tales themselves make about the cause.

Boaistuau's French renders the state as haine, hatred, and the shift of noun quietly assists his moral reallocation: a status can be adjudicated, its origins audited, its parties summoned, while a passion can only be preached against, and a preacher prefers passions. Brooke's English keeps the passion and adds the temperature: his narration returns to hate and its compounds as the master term for the houses, an enmity felt in the blood of the parties rather than registered between their names. Painter's prose follows the French settlement. By the time the story reaches the stage, the tradition offers the playwright a feud that has been, in sequence, a political condition, a legal status, and a passion, and the play's response is to refuse every register by trying them all.

The play's own vocabulary for the quarrel is a tour of the available registers, none of them allowed to settle. The Prologue alone spends three: grudge, a debt word, something owed and nursed and carried on the books; mutiny, a political word, the rising of subjects against order; and rage, a passion word, assigned pointedly to the parents (Prologue, lines 3 and 9). The Friar contributes rancour, a medical word, a rankling sore in the body of the city, curable in his theory by the sacrament of marriage (2.3). The Prince, closing the case, reaches for discords, a musical word, tunings that will not agree, and for hate in its plainest form, "your hate," addressed to the fathers as the scourge's target (5.3). Servants, citizens, gentlemen, priest, and prince each name the thing from inside a different discipline, accountancy, politics, medicine, music, law, and no two names agree on what kind of object the feud is. That lexical restlessness is the blank wearing synonyms. A story that knew what its feud was would call it one thing; the tradition's three centuries of renaming, completed by the play's six names in five acts, is the sound of a culture circling an object it can price, punish, mourn, and marry against, but never define.

The persons' names undergo the same reduction to pure signal. In the novelle the surnames carry geography and history, Montecchi from a Vicentine stronghold, Cappelletti from a Cremonese faction, and a reader could still hear the peninsula in them. On the London stage the names shed their etymology and become cries: the citizens take them up as slogans in 1.1, down with the Capulets, down with the Montagues, a pair of battle words emptied of everything except allegiance. Mercutio completes the process by renaming the feud's champion out of fable, calling Tybalt "the Prince of Cats" (2.4), after the cat of the Reynard tales, as though the Capulet nephew were a creature in a beast fable, all reflex and claw. The trajectory of the names is the trajectory of the feud in miniature: from places, to parties, to passwords. A password, like a blank cause, admits anyone who can repeat it and excludes anyone who cannot, and requires no knowledge whatsoever of what it stands for. Verona's servants can fight for the password in the first scene precisely because the password is all the feud has become, and the play's opening minutes are the sound of a city killing for a word it has forgotten how to parse.

## The Blank at the Centre of the Play's Feud

Against that accumulated machinery stands the fact from which this study began: nobody in the play ever says what the feud is about. The abstention is total and must be measured to be believed. The Prologue calls the grudge ancient and assigns the rage to the parents, and stops. The Prince, cataloguing three brawls, calls their seed an airy word, and stops. Old Capulet, restraining Tybalt at the feast in 1.5, treats the enmity as a custom to be managed at table rather than a case to be argued: the feud at the feast is etiquette, a young man's "saucy" intrusion to be endured because the guest bears himself humbly and the hall is full. Juliet, learning the name of the man she has kissed, frames the whole matter as a taxonomy accident: "My only love sprung from my only hate! / Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" (1.5). The names are the feud; beneath the names, nothing is ever produced. Even the final reconciliation is transacted in the language of wealth rather than reasons, Montague vowing a statue in pure gold and Capulet matching it, "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie" (5.3), so that the feud ends as it began, in the households' substance, with its account settled in gold because it was never stated in words.

### Why is the feud's oldest voice a sonnet?

Because a sonnet is the period's form for desire held inside a strict frame, and the Prologue sonnet performs that discipline on hatred: fourteen lines allot the feud its antiquity, its renewal, its bloodshed, and its end, promising an audience that even this chaos has a shape and a terminus.

The blank is therefore not an absence in the text but a pressure the text sustains at every point where an explanation would relieve it. A stated cause would give the feud handles: it could be judged proportionate or absurd, negotiated, compensated, inherited knowingly or renounced. The play forecloses every one of those transactions. Its Verona cannot argue about the feud, hold a hearing on it, or buy it off at any price below the children's lives, because there is nothing on the table to argue about, hear, or price. What the city can do is escalate and mourn, and the play's structure is the alternation of those two activities until mourning wins by exhaustion. The feud's evolution across the sources explains how the play arrived at this design. Every previous stage relied on the audience to fill the silence from the politics of their own city: Dante's readers knew faction, Da Porto's knew signorial honor, Bandello's knew court emulation, Boaistuau's and Brooke's knew the household's duty of obedience. The play's Verona is built too completely for that outsourcing to work. A feud staged at this scale, with this much law, blood, and ceremony invested in it, exposes the silence its predecessors could leave in shadow. Shakespeare's blank is the inherited silence, lit.

Juliet conducts the play's only philosophical examination of the blank, and its fate is instructive. Alone in the orchard, she reasons that the feud's whole substance is nominal: a Montague is not a hand, or foot, or arm, or face, or any part belonging to a man, and the name, being no part of the person, can be refused without loss (2.2). The argument is flawless and the play honors it by making it the lovers' operating truth; within the orchard's walls, the feud genuinely dissolves into a word. What the orchard cannot do is export its philosophy. The name that is nothing in the garden is everything in the street, where men die for it in 3.1 without ever learning that it has been refuted, and the play's bleakness lies in that asymmetry: the blank can be seen through by a girl of thirteen in a single speech and cannot be acted on by a city in five acts. Earlier tellings never stage the refutation at all, since their feuds, presumed rather than examined, generate no Juliet argument against them. The play invents both the feud's maximum presence and its complete intellectual demolition, and lets the demolition lose.

The citizens of 1.1 deserve a word in this accounting, because they are the blank's chorus. They enter with clubs and a slogan, down with both houses, the only collective judgment the city ever passes on its quarrel, and it is a judgment without a brief: the citizens do not say what the feud is about either, only that they are finished with it. Their neutrality is the audience's position written into the play. Spectators, like citizens, are given casualties to count and no case to weigh, and the play's refusal to brief its audience is the last stage of the outsourcing's collapse. Dante's readers brought faction to the names; Da Porto's brought honor; Brooke's brought a sermon. The play's audience brings nothing but attention, and attention, fixed for two hours on a hatred with no object, becomes the tragedy's instrument: the feud is finally measured not by what it claims but by what it costs, and the measuring is done by witnesses who cannot look away into a history.

## Bullough, Girard, and the Editors: Three Readings of the Blank

Three kinds of authority have confronted the feud's missing cause, and they do not agree about what the blank is. Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957) prints the chain in extenso and remains the documentary foundation of source study, treats the feud's terms as inheritance. On Bullough's showing, the play's quarrel descends from Brooke, and behind Brooke from Boaistuau and Bandello and Da Porto, and at no station of that descent does any teller state an origin for the enmity; the sources are as silent as the play. The inference his method invites is austere: the blank is not a Shakespearean device at all but a transmitted property of the story, and criticism that builds theories on the missing cause is building on a gap the playwright simply received. The documentary record assembled in this study confirms the factual half of that position entirely. No source states a cause. The blank was indeed inherited.

René Girard reads the same blank from the opposite direction and finds not a gap but the meaning of the work. In A Theater of Envy: William Shakespeare (1991), his extended study of desire and violence in the plays, Girard treats the feud as the purest available specimen of mimetic rivalry: two houses "both alike in dignity," as the Prologue insists in its first line, each the other's mirror and model, bound in a reciprocity of violence that requires no object because each side's desire to outdo the other is itself the object. On this account the cause is missing because causes are what such rivalries consume: whatever spark began the quarrel has been burned away by decades of symmetrical retaliation, and any history the houses could recite would be a partisan fiction whose function would be to license the next blow. [The reading of Girard, mimetic desire, and the feud](/2027/01/15/rene-girard-mimetic-desire-feud/) develops that framework in full; within the evolution, its power is that it alone predicts the play's additions. A rivalry of doubles cannot be fought only by principals; it must recruit servants, bystanders, and kinsmen of the Prince, because its logic is contagion, and Mercutio's death under Romeo's arm, by a quarrel not his own, is the contagion's signature event.

The editors stand between the document and the theory. Jill L. Levenson, in the introduction to her Oxford edition of the play (2000), reads the feud's unspecified origin as part of the play's civic design: the tragedy locates its cause not in an event but in a social condition, the fathers' rage as the Prologue names it, and the criticism of Verona falls on the whole order that sustains the quarrel, prince, patriarchs, and citizens together. René Weis, in the Arden Third Series edition (2012), glosses the Prologue's "ancient grudge" as a long-standing enmity and notes in his commentary that the play nowhere supplies its grounds, treating the omission as a settled feature of the text to be registered rather than repaired. The editorial tradition, in other words, concedes the inference Bullough resists: an omission this conspicuous, in a text this carefully built, functions as a choice whatever its documentary pedigree, since the playwright who added an edict, a brawl count, and a plague curse could have added a sentence of origin at no cost.

The disagreement is genuine and is adjudicated here as follows. Bullough is right about the transmission and wrong about the inference: inheritance explains where the blank came from and cannot explain what the play does with it, since the additions multiply the feud's presence at every point except the point of explanation, a pattern inheritance alone would scatter randomly rather than concentrate. Girard is right about the mechanism and extravagant about the resolution: his doubles explain why no cause can survive inside such a system, and his account of the ending, in which the dead lovers serve as the sacrifice that reconciles the community, captures the final scene's ritual shape, Capulet's "O brother Montague, give me thy hand" spoken over the bodies (5.3). What the sacrificial reading flattens is the legal and economic frame the play keeps insisting on, the edict, the fines of disorder, the golden statues, Verona settling its account in the currency of the feud's own world. Levenson's civic reading, absorbing Girard's mechanism, gets the emphasis right: the blank is a design whose function is distributive, spreading responsibility across an entire order until the Prince can close the case with the play's most judicial line, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3), and its exhausted corollary, "All are punish'd" (5.3). The feud has no cause because the play wants no single address for Verona's guilt.

Levenson's editorial work shows what the civic reading does with the feud's details once the blank is accepted as design. Her introduction and commentary dwell on the play's public world, its watches, proclamations, and civic rituals, and on the ending's character as a public settlement rather than a private reconciliation: Verona does not merely grieve, it adjudicates, with the Prince presiding over an inventory of losses and the fathers bidding against each other in gold. On that reading the statues are not sentiment but damages, the nearest thing to a judgment the feud's blank allows, since a court cannot apportion blame for a quarrel whose grounds no witness can state and can only total the costs. Weis's commentary, from the textual side, performs the complementary labor: his notes register the early texts' consistency on the point, the grudge ancient and unexplained in the authoritative second quarto of 1599 as in every derivative text, so that no compositor's error or lost passage can be blamed for the silence. Between the two editions the blank acquires its modern scholarly form, simultaneously a fact of the documents and a fact of the design, which is the adjudication this study has argued on the evidence of the chain.

A detail of the Prince's first speech ties the three readings together and supports the adjudication. Escalus describes the brawls' origin as "an airy word" (1.1), the closest any figure of authority comes to naming the feud's seed, and the phrase is a studied vacancy: a word, made of air, trivially small against three riots and a capital statute. Bullough's documentary eye registers the phrase as part of the play's inheritance of vagueness; Girard's theory predicts it, since a rivalry of doubles will always trace itself to a nothing; Levenson's civic reading explains its placement in the mouth of the law, where a real cause would have to be entered as evidence and the play's whole design requires the evidence locker to be empty. Editors have also connected the Prince's very name with the scales of justice and with the della Scala, the lords under whom Da Porto set the tale, so that Verona's judge carries the feud's entire history in his name, signorial past and judicial present, and still cannot produce its cause. The name knows everything except the one thing. The play could not have designed a neater emblem of its quarrel if it had tried, and the burden of this section is that, on the evidence of the additions, it did try.

## Flaw or Choice: The Verdict on the Missing Cause

The standing objection must be met directly, since it has shadowed the play's reputation in classrooms and common rooms alike: a feud without a cause, the objection runs, is a dramaturgical shortcut, a playwright's refusal to do the work of motivation, tolerable in a novella summary and culpable in a tragedy. The evolution documented here is the answer, and it converts the objection into the thesis. A shortcut is taken once, by one author, at the point of difficulty. The blank in this feud is taken five times, by five authors, in three languages, across eighty years, and at every taking the surrounding work grows more elaborate: Da Porto invents the enmity without an origin while inventing everything around it; Bandello triples the tale's length and leaves the origin untouched; Boaistuau adds an apothecary, speeches, and sermons and leaves it untouched; Brooke versifies, moralizes, and frames, and leaves it untouched; Shakespeare builds the feud a senate of additions, an opening brawl, a body of statute, a history of prior disturbances, a death roll, and leaves it untouched. A flaw does not survive that much deliberate company. What survives is a feature each teller recognized as load-bearing, since each teller, offered the standing invitation of a fresh retelling to repair it, declined.

The positive case completes the verdict. The blank cause does four kinds of work that no stated cause could do. It universalizes the feud: a quarrel over a named inheritance or a remembered insult belongs to its facts, while a causeless hatred belongs to every audience's city, which is why the story has been re-sited in gang territories, ethnic enclaves, and divided nations without breaking. It equalizes the houses: a cause would make one house the injured party and the other the offender, and the play's architecture of symmetry, two households alike in dignity, alike in rage, alike in loss, requires that neither be granted the dignity of a grievance. It protects the lovers: because the feud has no case, Juliet's orchard meditation, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2), can treat the enmity as a nominal accident rather than a moral inheritance, and the audience is free to agree with her without adjudicating a history. And it convicts the fathers: the Prologue assigns the rage to the parents and the removal of it to the children's end, a sentence that reads as tragedy if the rage is causeless and as mere justice if it is not. Shakespeare's version of the feud achieves, in short, the conversion of a plot premise into an instrument of judgment, and the conversion is impossible with the cause left in. The blank is the achievement.

One more feature of the blank's work deserves record, since it separates the play's feud from simple irrationality. Every participant in the play's quarrel behaves with local rationality. The servants gain status by their master's quarrel, the one arena where a Gregory can matter. Tybalt defends a code of honor whose terms his world enforces and whose breach carries real costs. Capulet husbands property, alliance, and reputation, goods a father of his rank is charged to protect. Escalus pursues public order with the instruments a prince possesses. The catastrophe is not produced by madmen but by the aggregation of these rationalities inside a structure that has no forum for their arbitration, and the missing cause is the missing forum's emblem: a stated grievance could be lodged somewhere, priced, answered, settled, while the blank can only be fed. Critics who call the feud senseless name the aggregate correctly and the agents wrongly, and the evolution explains the distinction. The novelle's feud, presumed and private, could still be imagined as somebody's policy; the play's feud, public and forumless, is nobody's policy and everybody's practice, a tragedy of coordination that required the cause's removal to become visible at all.

The strongest counterargument deserves its answer before the verdict closes. Elizabethan spectators, like Italian readers before them, knew Verona's factions as a byword and might have filled the blank from proverb as readily as Da Porto's audience filled it from honor; on that view the play presumes as much as any novella and the blank is no more designed in 1595 than in 1530. The additions answer the objection. A teller who presumes the audience's knowledge does not build redundancy around the presumption, yet the play builds nothing else: a prologue to name the rage, a first scene to embody it at every rank, an edict to date its recurrences, a friar to theorize its cure, a curse to price it, and a closing audit to total its bill. That is not the behavior of an author relying on proverb; it is the behavior of an author replacing proverb with demonstration, showing a city drowning in a hatred it cannot narrate until the inability to narrate stands revealed as the tragedy's first cause. The blank in the sources is an inheritance. The blank in the play is an exhibit, entered into evidence.

## From Chronicle to Myth: What the Feud's Evolution Explains

The chain's long arc can now be named. Dante's houses belong to chronicle: real families, a real peninsula, a poet keeping political accounts. Da Porto's novella wears chronicle's dress, a named ruler, a dated reign, a teller who heard it in Verona, while functioning as invention. Bandello publishes the tale into the great sixteenth-century market in purported histories, his Novelle framed as records of true events retold at court. Boaistuau's title promises tragedies, histoires, and Brooke's promises a history. At every stage the feud's claim to fact is part of its packaging, and at every stage the claim thins as the packaging grows, until the play completes the exchange: a work that announces itself as "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue), pure theatre, carrying a feud whose factual core has evaporated entirely and whose power has never been greater. The feud's evolution mirrors the story's migration from chronicle to myth because the two movements are one: a story becomes myth when its particulars stop needing to be true and start needing to be shapely, and the first particular the Verona story shed, long before Shakespeare inherited it, was the answer to the question of what the fighting was for.

The afterlife of the feud confirms the diagnosis from outside the chain. Every strong adaptation of the story has had to decide what to do with the blank, and the decisions are a litmus of the adapters' worlds: the feud becomes racial enmity in the New York of West Side Story, sectarian division in retellings set in Belfast or Jerusalem, gang allegiance in Los Angeles. Each substitution is an admission that the original's empty slot is the story's most portable component, the place where any culture installs its own unreasoning hatred. A feud with Da Porto's presumed honor culture intact could not travel so freely; the presumption was Italian and perishable. The blank is immortal precisely because it is blank, a shaped vacancy that each age fills with the hatred it knows best and least understands, which is as good a definition of myth as the story's long journey affords.

The vouching declines in step with the cause. Dante vouches for his houses politically, with the full authority of a poem that claims to report the afterlife. Da Porto vouches historically, with a named narrator, a journey, and a dead ruler. Bandello vouches socially, his court's collective memory offered as the record's guarantee. Boaistuau vouches doctrinally, the tale certified by the lesson it teaches. Brooke vouches homiletically, the preface's sermon standing surety for the poem. The play vouches not at all: it shows men fighting, a prince legislating, a friar scheming, and two fathers bidding in gold, and leaves the witnessing to the house. Each stage's guarantee is weaker and more theatrical than the last, until guarantee and performance merge, and the feud, believed in the end because it is seen, completes its passage from the chronicle's page to the myth's stage. A myth does not ask to be believed as fact; it asks to be recognized as form, and the feud, detached from every cause that could date it, is the most recognizable form the story owns.

What remains for a reader of the play is the discipline the evolution teaches. The feud is not background, and treating it as background repeats the error of every summarizer who hurries past the first scene to the balcony. It is the one element of the story that every teller handled, the strand along which each teller's politics can be read, and the site of the play's most considered additions and its most considered refusal. Restoring its evolution restores intention to a place where habit sees only premise, and it returns the tragedy's first question to its proper size. Before Verona asks why the lovers died, it asks, in three lines of a sonnet and one scene of servants, why the houses hate, and the whole tradition stands behind the silence that answers. The strand this study owns can now be surrendered to the tellings that own its parts: the names to Dante's, the invention to Da Porto's, the rhetoric to Bandello's, the sermon to Boaistuau's, the warning to Brooke's. The feud that passes among them, changing shape at every hand and keeping its secret through all of them, is the chain's true protagonist, older than the lovers, crueller than fortune, and still unexplained.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch feud-evolution chart, and what does each of its rows record?
The InsightCrunch feud-evolution chart is the reference table in this study that tracks the feud stage by stage along the source chain, from Dante to the play. Each row records one telling: the form the feud takes in that telling, the cause the telling states or presumes, the public machinery attached to the quarrel, and the toll the quarrel exacts. Read down the columns and the chain's pattern becomes visible at a glance. Explanation drains away as enforcement accumulates, until the final row pairs a completely blank cause with armed servants, an edict, and six deaths.

### Q: Do the Montecchi and Cappelletti ever confront each other anywhere in Dante's poem?
No. The two houses never meet, fight, or address each other anywhere in the Commedia. Their single appearance is three lines in the sixth canto of the Purgatorio (lines 106-108), where Dante summons the absent emperor Albert of Austria to come and see them: the Montecchi are named among houses already sunk in sorrow, the Cappelletti among houses living in fear. The pairing is a rhetorical bracket inside an invective against Italy's abandonment by empire. Dante supplies the names, the altitude of the houses, and an atmosphere of inherited factional sorrow, and nothing else: no Verona street, no lovers, no quarrel between the two houses themselves.

### Q: What reason does Da Porto's novella give, or refuse to give, for the hatred of the two houses?
Da Porto's novella gives no reason at all. In Giulietta e Romeo, written around 1524 and printed at Venice in 1530, the enmity of the Cappelletti and Montecchi is presented in the frame as a notorious standing fact of Bartolomeo della Scala's Verona, something every character already knows and no character explains. There is no founding insult, no disputed inheritance, no contested office. Readers are left to presume the standard machinery of Italian civic hatred, precedence, honor, and remembered injury, but the presumption is supplied by the audience's world, never by the text. The blank that the play is famous for begins here, at the feud's invention.

### Q: At which stage of the chain do armed servants first carry the feud into the street?
The play is the first stage. No telling before Shakespeare puts the feud into servants' hands: Dante has no street, Masuccio has no feud, and Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, and Painter all confine the enmity to the great houses and their gentlemen. The play's opening scene hands the quarrel to Sampson and Gregory, Capulet servingmen who bite a thumb at their Montague counterparts and brawl in a public place (1.1). The addition changes the feud's sociology at a stroke. A hatred carried by employees who cannot state its grounds is no longer a private grievance; it is a household infection, transmitted by livery rather than by memory.

### Q: What does Bandello's courtly frame do to the feud he inherits from Da Porto?
Bandello's frame enlarges the feud's language while leaving its machinery untouched. His retelling, the ninth novella of the second part of the Novelle (Lucca, 1554), expands Da Porto's tale to roughly three times its length, and the enmity of the Montecchi and Cappelletti grows in emphasis with everything else: the narrator returns to it as a standing calamity of Verona and a specimen of the discord that ruins cities. But the growth is rhetorical, not social. No crowd gathers in Bandello, no servant draws, no statute is issued. The feud stays where Da Porto put it, among gentlemen, now dressed in the moralizing magnificence of a court that consumed Italian disorder as instruction.

### Q: What does Boaistuau's moral purpose do to the feud's share of the story?
Boaistuau's moral purpose shrinks the feud to a premise. In the Histoires tragiques of 1559, the third tale adapts Bandello freely and at greater length, yet the enmity of the houses recedes as the sermons advance: the narrative's scrutiny falls on disobedient children, hasty desire, and neglected parental authority, while the fathers' hatred is retained as machinery and denied as a subject. The shift belongs to the adapter's moment, a France on the edge of religious civil war, where a tragic tale sold as instruction had reason to preach obedience inside the household rather than interrogate the household heads. England inherited the feud in exactly that diminished state.

### Q: Does Brooke's poem ever explain why the Cappellets and Montagues are enemies?
No. Arthur Brooke's Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet (1562), versified from Boaistuau, presents the hatred of the two Verona houses as ancient and bitter and offers no origin for it at any point in more than three thousand lines. His famous address to the reader distributes blame with great precision, charging the lovers with unhonest desire, the Nurse as a drunken gossip, and the Friar as superstitious, yet the parents whose enmity made the secret marriage necessary are faulted only for advice neglected. The poem's silence about the feud's grounds is total, and the play inherits that silence along with the plot.

### Q: At which stage does the feud first produce a public brawl rather than a private killing?
The play is again the first stage. Every earlier telling confines the feud's violence to individual encounters: Romeo's killing of Tebaldo in Da Porto and its descendants is a street fight between gentlemen, grave but private in scale, answered by a sentence of banishment. The play opens with a public brawl involving servants, gentlemen, armed citizens, and both patriarchs (1.1), and Prince Escalus reveals that this is the fourth such disturbance, three civil brawls having already broken the peace. A brawl differs from a duel in its politics: it implicates the whole city, demands legislation, and turns a family quarrel into a public-order crisis, which is why the play needs an edict where the novelle needed only a judge.

### Q: Which line of the Prologue says the grudge renews itself, and why does its grammar matter?
The third line of the Prologue says it: "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue, line 3). The grammar matters because the grudge is the sentence's engine: the ancient thing is the subject from which new violence breaks, in a present tense that never expires. The construction makes renewal automatic and agentless. Nobody decides to resume the feud; the grudge itself generates fresh mutiny the way a fire generates fresh flame, and the pairing of ancient with new compresses the whole evolution into five words, an old hatred forever producing young corpses. The line is the play's thesis about its quarrel stated before any character speaks.

### Q: What does the Prince's edict add to the feud that no Italian telling contains?
The Prince's edict adds prevention, and with it a public history. Da Porto's Bartolomeo della Scala and the rulers in Bandello and the French and English retellings act after the event, sentencing Romeo for a killing already done. Shakespeare's Prince Escalus legislates in advance of the next offense: having counted three civil brawls bred of an airy word (1.1), he publishes a capital penalty against future disturbance, the patriarchs' lives as forfeit of the peace. The feud thereby acquires two things it never had in Italy, a statute aimed at its future and a documented pattern of recurrence, a count of prior outbreaks that makes the quarrel a matter of public record rather than private reputation.

### Q: How does the feud's death toll grow as the story travels from Da Porto to the play?
The toll doubles. Da Porto's telling exacts three deaths from the story's machinery: Tebaldo, killed by Romeo in the street encounter, and the two lovers in the tomb, with the houses reconciled over the bodies. The intermediate tellings keep that count essentially intact. The play raises it to six by adding deaths in categories the sources lack: Mercutio, the Prince's kinsman, killed by a feud not his own in 3.1; Paris, the bridegroom chosen by Capulet, killed at the tomb in 5.3; and Lady Montague, dead offstage of grief at her son's exile, as the final scene reports (5.3). The growth is systematic rather than decorative: each added death widens the circle of the feud's victims beyond the two households.

### Q: How does Girard explain a hatred strong enough to kill that has forgotten its own cause?
René Girard explains the forgetting as the natural end of reciprocal violence. In A Theater of Envy (1991), his study of desire and violence in Shakespeare, the two houses are mimetic doubles, "both alike in dignity" as the Prologue's first line insists, each mirroring the other's pride and answering the other's blows in kind. In such a system the original object of contention is consumed by the rivalry itself: retaliation no longer needs a reason because the rival's last act is always reason enough. A cause, if one were produced, would merely be a partisan license for the next blow. The blank is therefore not missing information in Girard's reading; it is the signature of a hatred that has become purely relational.

### Q: What does Bullough's gathering of the sources reveal about the feud that no single telling shows?
Bullough's gathering reveals that the feud's blank is unanimous. Geoffrey Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957) prints the chain's tellings in sequence, in translation, beside the play, and only that juxtaposition makes the pattern legible: Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, and Brooke each enlarge the story considerably, and not one of them, at any of the natural places where an origin could be inserted, inserts one. Read singly, any telling's silence looks like an accident of compression. Read in Bullough's sequence, the silences align into a tradition, and the play's silence becomes legible as the last and most deliberate member of a series rather than an isolated lapse in motivation.

### Q: How do modern editors gloss the words ancient grudge, and what does the gloss admit?
Modern editors gloss the phrase briefly and concede the omission in doing so. René Weis, in the Arden Third Series edition (2012), treats "ancient grudge" in the Prologue as a long-standing enmity and registers that the play nowhere supplies its grounds; Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition (2000) likewise notes the unspecified origin while reading the feud as a social condition, the parents' rage, rather than an event with a history. The admissions matter because glosses exist to resolve difficulties, and here the gloss can only point at the gap. An editor's note that says, in effect, that no cause is given is the scholarly form of the play's own refusal.

### Q: Is the unexplained feud a flaw in the play or a deliberate design?
The unexplained feud is a deliberate design, and the source chain is the proof. A flaw in motivation would be a single author's lapse at a single point of difficulty. The blank instead recurs across five tellings in three languages over eighty years, and at every recurrence the surrounding workmanship grows more elaborate: Bandello triples the tale, Boaistuau adds sermons and an apothecary, Brooke adds a moralizing preface, Shakespeare adds an opening brawl, an edict, and three extra deaths. Every teller had a fresh opportunity to insert an origin, and every teller declined. What survives that much deliberate company is a feature the tradition recognized as load-bearing, not an error it failed to notice.

### Q: What work does the feud's blank cause do that a stated cause could not?
The blank does four kinds of work at once. It universalizes the quarrel, since a hatred over a named inheritance belongs to its facts while a causeless hatred can be re-sited in any divided city. It equalizes the houses, since a stated grievance would make one house the injured party and break the play's symmetry of two households alike in dignity, rage, and loss. It protects the lovers, whose enmity arrives as a bare name they can rationally dismiss. And it convicts the fathers, because a rage with no case reads as pure culpability, which is the Prologue's own verdict: the parents' rage ends only at their children's end (Prologue, lines 9-10).

### Q: What does each teller's version of the feud reveal about the teller's own city and moment?
Each version is a self-portrait. Dante's paired houses serve an imperial politics: an exile from faction-torn Florence reads family sorrow as the symptom of an Italy abandoned by its emperor. Da Porto, a Venetian officer crippled in the Cambrai wars, sets his factions safely under a vanished signore in a Verona long absorbed into Venetian peace. Bandello, a courtier of great houses, makes the feud a magnificent warning against noble discord. Boaistuau and Brooke, writing for France at the edge of civil war and for Protestant England, displace blame from the fathers onto disobedient youth, a gossip, and a friar. The play alone refuses to let any present audience outsource the guilt.

### Q: How does the feud's blankness help the story pass from chronicle into myth?
The blankness is the mechanism of the passage. The early tellings dress the tale as chronicle: Dante names real houses, Da Porto cites a real ruler and a teller who heard the story in Verona, Bandello's collection presents itself as a record of true events. A chronicle item carries particulars that must be checked; a myth carries a shape that must only be recognized. The feud's cause is the first particular the tradition sheds, because a stated cause would anchor the quarrel to one city's facts. Without it, the two households become pure form, any paired powers whose hatred outlives its reasons, and the story can be recognized in any place that has such pairs.

### Q: What do the golden statues at the play's end add to the feud's settlement that the sources lack?
The statues add a settlement conducted in wealth because it cannot be conducted in reasons. Da Porto's houses reconcile over the lovers' bodies and share a tomb, a closure transacted in grief and kinship. The play keeps the handshake, Capulet's "O brother Montague, give me thy hand" (5.3), and then escalates it into competitive expenditure: Montague vows a statue of Juliet in pure gold, and Capulet matches it for Romeo, "As rich shall Romeo's by his lady's lie" (5.3). The feud that began in the households' dignity ends in the households' substance, converted into monuments. Gold is the only language the quarrel ever spoke fluently, and the play lets it speak the last word.

### Q: How can an essay use the feud-evolution chart to argue that the blank cause was chosen, not missed?
An essay can use the feud-evolution chart as a column argument. Set out the cause column stage by stage: Dante presumes faction, Da Porto states nothing, Bandello diagnoses a type and never a case, Boaistuau and Brooke displace the blame, and the play states nothing while surrounding the silence with an edict, a brawl count, and six deaths. Then set out the machinery column beside it and show the inverse movement, explanation draining as articulation grows. The paired columns convert an impression into evidence: an omission repeated by five authors across eighty years, at rising levels of craft, is a tradition's decision. The essay's conclusion follows from the table rather than from assertion.
