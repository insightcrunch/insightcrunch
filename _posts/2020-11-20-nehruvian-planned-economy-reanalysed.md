On 15 March 1950, weeks after the Constitution of India came into force, Jawaharlal Nehru's cabinet created the Planning Commission by a single executive resolution and placed Nehru himself in the chair. The Nehruvian planned economy, the subject of this reading, was never only an economist's device. Between the Industrial Policy Resolution of 6 April 1948 and the close of the Second Five-Year Plan in 1961, the Congress government assembled a machine for deciding who could build, who could borrow, whose region received a steel plant and whose harvest the state would buy. It then defended that machine in Parliament, in party sessions and in print as the economic form of the freedom struggle's promises. This article reads those fourteen years as political architecture: five dated instruments, each of which settled an argument inside the ruling coalition before it settled anything about output.

## A Settlement in Search of an Economy, 1947 to 1950

Planning arrived in India as a Congress inheritance long before it became a method of government. The Karachi session of the Indian National Congress in March 1931 adopted a resolution on fundamental rights and economic policy that promised state ownership or control of key industries, mineral resources and public utilities. In October 1938 Subhas Chandra Bose, as Congress president, constituted a National Planning Committee and persuaded Nehru to chair it. The committee's subcommittees produced volumes of reports through the war years, and although the exercise lapsed as its members went to prison, it fixed an expectation inside the party: a free India would plan, and the argument would be about how much of the economy the state should hold, not about whether the state belonged in the economy at all.

That expectation met a hard country in August 1947. Partition cut the new dominion off from the jute mills' raw material districts and the wheat lands of West Punjab, refugees arrived in millions, and food imports consumed scarce foreign exchange. The assassination of Mahatma Gandhi on 30 January 1948 removed the one figure whose suspicion of centralised machinery might have slowed the planners. In the Constituent Assembly, the economic argument took constitutional form in the Directive Principles, above all the clauses directing the state to secure that ownership and control of material resources be distributed to subserve the common good, a text examined in the reading of [the Directive Principles against the Fundamental Rights](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/). The Assembly's members were writing instructions to a future planning state even as they limited that state's legal powers.

Nehru's own formation pushed in the same direction. His 1927 visit to the Soviet Union and his years of Fabian reading in London had convinced him that a poor country could not wait for private capital to industrialise it, a conviction he carried into the prime ministership he assumed on 15 August 1947. Other Indians reached planning from different doors. M. Visvesvaraya, the engineer-statesman of Mysore, had published Planned Economy for India in 1934, arguing for a decade-long programme of forced industrial growth administered by experts. The colonial government itself set up a Department of Planning and Development under Ardeshir Dalal in 1944 and issued a statement of industrial policy in 1945. By 1947, planning was the common vocabulary of the nationalist, the technocrat and the departing colonial administrator alike.

What distinguished the Congress version was the triple test it had to pass. A Soviet plan answered to a party congress; an Indian plan had to survive a cabinet of rivals, a parliamentary opposition armed with question hour, and an electorate of universal franchise that would deliver its first verdict in the winter of 1951 to 1952. The economic settlement of the Nehru years was therefore drafted like a coalition treaty. Every clause can be read twice: once for what it did to output, and once for which constituency it reassured, which faction it disarmed and which promise it redeemed. The five instruments that follow were built in that order of anxiety, and the reading offered here follows [the founding three-tensions framework](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) in treating their political content as their primary content.

## The First Feature: The Industrial Policy Resolution of 6 April 1948

The first instrument was a statement, not a statute. On 6 April 1948 Shyama Prasad Mukherjee, the Minister for Industry and Supply in Nehru's cabinet and no socialist, laid the Industrial Policy Resolution before the Constituent Assembly. The resolution sorted industry into four classes. Three industries were reserved as exclusive state monopolies: the manufacture of arms and ammunition, the production and control of atomic energy, and the ownership and management of railway transport. A second class of six industries, including coal, iron and steel, aircraft manufacture, shipbuilding, the manufacture of telephones and wireless apparatus, and mineral oils, was closed to new private undertakings; the state alone would establish fresh capacity there. An eighteen-industry third class, covering salt, automobiles, heavy machinery, machine tools, chemicals, fertilisers, cotton and woollen textiles, cement and paper among others, was left in private hands under state regulation and control. Everything else was open to private and cooperative enterprise.

The resolution's most consequential sentence was an assurance. Existing private undertakings in the reserved fields would not be disturbed, and the government declared that nationalisation of any existing industry would not be undertaken for ten years, after which the position would be reviewed. Indian capital had spent 1947 in a state of nerves, listening to Congress socialists speak of expropriation and watching the Congress left press for faster public ownership. The ten-year pledge was the cabinet's answer, drafted to restart investment that had stalled in the uncertainty of the transfer of power. The Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry received the statement with guarded relief, while the socialist wing of the Congress, which had seen Jayaprakash Narayan lead the Congress Socialist Party out of the parent body in March 1948, read the same document as a surrender to the mill owners.

Two further clauses reveal the coalition arithmetic. Cottage and small-scale industries received elaborate protection, with promises of reservation of spheres, cheap power and cooperative organisation, because handloom weavers and village artisans numbered in millions of voters and because Gandhi's economic ghost still walked the party. Labour received commitments on fair wages, social security and the settlement of disputes, carried forward from the Industrial Truce Resolution of December 1947, because the trade unions were a constituency the Congress intended to keep from the communists. Each clause of the 1948 resolution can be assigned its audience. The document articulated what the brief for this series calls the mixed economy, but mixed was not an economic theory in April 1948. It was the maximum programme a cabinet containing both Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel's propertied conservatism and Nehru's socialism could sign without splitting.

### Why did Bombay's leading industrialists publish a plan of their own in 1944?

The Bombay Plan of January 1944 was signed by eight leading industrialists, among them J.R.D. Tata, G.D. Birla, Lala Shri Ram and A.D. Shroff, with the economist John Mathai among its authors. It proposed ten thousand crore rupees of investment over fifteen years, accepted a large state role, and aimed to double per capita income.

Capital, in short, demanded planning before the state imposed it.

The signatories' motives were mixed, and the document has been read both as foresight and as insurance. Wartime contracts had swollen business profits while controls bit into civilian production, and the houses feared a post-war slump in which a radicalised Congress might expropriate them. By endorsing a powerful planning state that accepted private property, public borrowing and a large place for existing firms, the Bombay houses bought a voice in the design of the post-war order. The plan even accepted that the state would own basic industries and discipline capital where owners resisted the programme. When the 1948 resolution arrived with its ten-year assurance, the men of the Bombay Plan had reason to think their insurance policy had paid out. John Mathai's presence among the signatories matters for what follows, because the same man would resign from Nehru's cabinet in 1950 over the Planning Commission's powers, having discovered that a planning body chaired by the prime minister could be a different animal from a plan negotiated among gentlemen.

What the 1948 resolution settled, then, was the boundary question in principle while deferring every hard case to machinery. It announced state supremacy in arms, energy and transport, promised private capital a decade of safety, wrapped the village industries in protection and told labour the state was its patron. It created no institution capable of choosing between a steel plant in Bihar and an irrigation dam in Madras, and it fixed no totals of investment, savings or output. The Congress government had declared the mixed economy. It had still to build the engine that would mix it, and to decide who would sit at the controls.

## The Second Feature: The Planning Commission of 15 March 1950

The engine arrived by resolution rather than by law. On 15 March 1950 the Union cabinet resolved to set up a Planning Commission, charged with assessing the material, capital and human resources of the country, formulating a plan for their most effective and balanced utilisation, determining priorities and the stages in which the plan should be carried out, indicating the factors retarding economic growth, deciding the machinery for implementation at each stage, appraising progress periodically, and recommending adjustments of policy, including adjustments to the working of the central and state governments themselves. Nehru took the chair. Gulzarilal Nanda, the labour leader from Ahmedabad who had built the Indian National Trade Union Congress, became the first deputy chairman. The Commission had no constitutional basis and no statutory charter. It existed because the cabinet willed it, and it answered to the prime minister who presided over it.

That legal thinness was the design, not an oversight. A constitutional body would have required the Constituent Assembly's consent and might have collided with the Finance Commission that the Constitution itself mandated for dividing taxes between the Union and the states. A statutory body would have been debated clause by clause in Parliament, where state governments and opposition parties could have written limits into its powers. A cabinet resolution could be issued in an afternoon, expanded by practice and defended as an advisory committee of the executive. Nehru, who disliked encumbering his government with rigid forms, got a body whose authority flowed from his office rather than from any text. The Commission's appraisals of ministerial work, which its terms of reference expressly permitted, gave it leverage over every spending department in New Delhi.

The price appeared within months. John Mathai, the finance minister who had presented the budget of February 1950 and read out the Commission's prospective membership to the House, resigned from the cabinet later that year. In his resignation statement he called the Commission ill-timed and ill-conceived and warned that it was tending to become a parallel cabinet, weakening the Finance Ministry and reducing the cabinet itself to a registering authority for decisions taken elsewhere. Mathai, the Bombay Plan signatory of 1944, discovered from the inside that he had wanted planning by negotiation and had received planning by hierarchy. C.D. Deshmukh, the former Reserve Bank governor who succeeded him at finance, made his peace with the Commission and even chaired the expert panel that later blessed the Second Plan, but the Mathai episode fixed a permanent question in Indian public life: whether an unelected body of experts, however eminent, could direct elected ministers in a democracy. The question would be asked again at every plan's birth.

The Commission also had a federal gap to close. Under the Constitution, agriculture, irrigation, land and much of industry belonged to the states, yet the Commission's plans allocated investment across the whole economy. The cabinet filled the gap on 6 August 1952 by creating the National Development Council, composed of the prime minister, the Union cabinet and the chief ministers of the states, to consider and approve each plan before it went to Parliament. The council converted the chief ministers from spectators of a central design into signatories of it. Plan approval acquired a federal ceremony, and the states acquired a claim, pressed at every council meeting thereafter, that the totals offered to them were too small and the conditions attached too many. In [the Nehruvian-era institutional baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) this pair of bodies, the Commission that designed and the council that consented, forms the load-bearing wall of the settlement.

The political function of the Commission went deeper than coordination. It gave the Congress government a language in which hard choices could be presented as technical findings. When a ministry's project was deferred, the deferment could be attributed to resource constraints certified by experts rather than to a minister's defeat in cabinet. When the states demanded more, the answer could be read off a table of allocations rather than argued as favouritism. Nehru understood the uses of this insulation and also its limits, and he spent much of his energy in the early 1950s defending the Commission in Parliament against members who called it a super-cabinet and against state governments who suspected it of stealing their subjects. The institution survived its critics because the ruling party needed it: it was the one table at which the whole inheritance of 1947, the promises, the factions and the empty treasury, could be laid out in a single set of numbers.

## The Third Feature: The First Five-Year Plan, 1951 to 1956

The First Five-Year Plan was the settlement's modest first exercise. A draft outline appeared in July 1951, and the plan document was placed before Parliament in December 1952, covering the years from April 1951 to March 1956. Its architects, working with the simple growth arithmetic associated with Roy Harrod and Evsey Domar, proposed a public outlay of 2,069 crore rupees and aimed at raising national income by about 11 to 12 percent over the five years. A young economist named K.N. Raj, lately returned from the London School of Economics, worked on the plan's monetary and fiscal chapters, beginning an association between the Commission's back rooms and a generation of Indian economists who would staff the system for decades. Agriculture, irrigation and power took the largest shares of the outlay, because the plan's first task was to repair a food economy broken by Partition and war.

The plan wore its politics on its face. The great river projects, Bhakra-Nangal on the Sutlej, Hirakud on the Mahanadi and the Damodar Valley scheme, were advertised as the new temples and pilgrimages of a secular state, public works that every party could celebrate and no communal claim could divide. The Community Development Programme, launched on 2 October 1952, Gandhi's birthday, with fifty-five pilot projects, carried the plan into the village through blocks, extension officers and village-level workers, giving the Congress an administrative presence in the countryside that matched its electoral machine. Refugees from Pakistan were to be rehabilitated as a plan charge. Food imports were treated as a temporary bridge until the dams and the extension service raised the harvest. Each element answered a grievance of 1947 before it answered a target in a table.

The record, when the plan closed in March 1956, was better than the targets. National income rose by about 18 percent against the 11 to 12 percent the planners had sought, and output grew at roughly 3.6 percent a year against the 2.1 percent pace the plan frame had assumed. Foodgrain production climbed from about 52 million tonnes in 1950 to 1951 to about 67 million tonnes in 1955 to 1956, helped by kind monsoons. Prices, which had risen sharply after the Korean War boom of 1950, steadied and in some years fell. The success was real and the planners said so, but its composition deserves notice: the First Plan spent most heavily on the sector where pre-independence works were already in progress and where rainfall did much of the lifting. It proved that the machinery could spend money and count results honestly. It had not yet proved that the machinery could coerce an unwilling economy into a new shape.

Politically, the plan's quiet years were golden ones for the ruling party. The Congress fought the first general election of 1951 to 1952, chronicled in the reading of [the first general election of 1951 to 1952](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/), on the promise of planned progress, and the plan's early food successes let Nehru present the experiment as vindicated before the harder questions arrived. Inside the party, however, the success raised the stakes of the argument that the 1948 resolution had deferred. If planning worked, how far should it go? The Congress right, gathered around Patel until his death in December 1950 and thereafter around figures such as Rajendra Prasad and Morarji Desai, wanted consolidation. The party's left wanted the plan to become an instrument of redistribution in earnest, beginning with land. Nehru's answer was to raise the ambition of the exercise itself, and to give the ambition a mathematician.

## The Fourth Feature: The Mahalanobis Second Plan, 1956 to 1961

Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis was a physicist turned statistician who had founded the Indian Statistical Institute in Calcutta in 1931 and built the National Sample Survey into the largest household enquiry of its kind in the world. Nehru trusted his numbers the way he trusted few men's arguments, and the letters that passed between the prime minister and the statistician between 1949 and 1956, preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library and in the archives of the Indian Statistical Institute, show the Second Plan being argued into existence in correspondence before it was debated in any legislature. Scholars who copy such files for publication face a custody problem of their own, and the working habits collected in the guide to [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) address the handling of sensitive documentary material of exactly this kind. Mahalanobis set out his core idea in a 1953 paper on the process of growth of national income and then, in March 1955, submitted to the government a draft plan frame that became the Second Five-Year Plan's skeleton.

The frame's logic was austere. A poor country that wanted fast growth had to raise its rate of investment, and investment goods, machines that make machines, could not be imported forever. The frame therefore divided the economy into four sectors: factories producing investment goods, factories producing consumer goods, small and household industries producing consumer goods, and services. Machines were to be favoured over mill cloth; the consumer goods that a rising population needed in the meantime were to come from labour-intensive small production, which would also soak up unemployment. The plan that Parliament adopted for 1956 to 1961 carried a public outlay of 4,800 crore rupees, more than double the First Plan's, and aimed to lift national income by 25 percent over five years, raise the investment rate from about 7 percent to about 11 percent of income, and hold consumption on a tight rein while the heavy base was built. Three integrated steel plants embodied the design: Bhilai in Madhya Pradesh, built with Soviet assistance under an agreement of February 1955; Rourkela in Orissa, built with West German firms; and Durgapur in West Bengal, built with a British consortium. Each produced its first steel in 1959.

Before adoption, the frame passed through a panel of economists convened under the finance minister's chairmanship in April 1955. Twenty of the twenty-one members signed a memorandum endorsing the strategy. The endorsement mattered politically, because it let the government present the plan not as Nehru's preference but as the considered verdict of the profession, in the same way that the Planning Commission's existence let ministers present deferments as arithmetic. The one member who refused to sign, and whose refusal belongs to the critics' section below, was B.R. Shenoy. The professional consensus of 1955 was genuine, internationally echoed and, as the plan's second year would show, dangerously confident about foreign exchange.

### How much foreign aid did the Second Plan come to depend on?

The Second Plan frame of 1955 assumed about 800 crore rupees of external assistance across five years. After the foreign exchange crisis of 1957, realised assistance ran well beyond the assumption: the Aid-India Consortium, convened in August 1958, organised the donors' pledges, and aid authorised during the plan years exceeded the frame's provision by a wide margin.

The dependence grew out of the crisis of 1957. The steel plants and machinery programme drew imports at a pace the frame had not financed: foreign exchange reserves, which stood at about 746 crore rupees in 1956, fell to about 267 crore rupees by 1958, and the government imposed emergency import cuts and drew on the International Monetary Fund. World Bank president Eugene Black convened the consortium's first meeting on 25 to 27 August 1958, with the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, West Germany and Japan at the table, to cover a gap of 350 million dollars for the remainder of the financial year alone. Politically, the episode taught two lessons that recurred for a generation. Self-reliance, proclaimed as the plan's destination, had been mortgaged to the goodwill of donor governments at the plan's midpoint. And the discipline of the consortium, its periodic reviews of Indian policy as the price of its pledges, handed every later critic of planning a documented case that the plan's sovereignty was shared with its creditors.

When the Second Plan closed in March 1961, its arithmetic read as a qualified success. National income had risen at about 4.1 percent a year against the target of 4.5 percent, industrial production had grown at a pace unmatched in the colonial record, and the three steel towns stood as physical proof that the state could build what it had drawn. Food production had lagged the population, imports of grain under the American PL-480 programme, first agreed in 1956, had become a structural feature of the food economy, and the foreign exchange cupboard was bare. The plan had built the base it promised and consumed the reserves it had inherited. Both halves of that sentence shaped the politics of the decade that followed.

## The Fifth Feature: The Industrial Policy Resolution of 30 April 1956

The Second Plan needed a charter for industry to match its totals, and the charter arrived on 30 April 1956, presented to Parliament by T.T. Krishnamachari, the Minister for Commerce and Industry. The political ground had been prepared by the party: in December 1954 Parliament itself adopted the socialist pattern of society as the objective of economic policy, and in January 1955 the Congress at its Avadi session committed the organisation to the same formula, the episode read closely in the account of [the Avadi resolution and the socialist pattern of 1955](/2019/02/05/avadi-resolution-socialist-pattern-1955/). The 1956 resolution sorted industry into three schedules. Schedule A listed seventeen industries whose future growth was reserved exclusively to the state, among them arms and ammunition, atomic energy, iron and steel, heavy plant and machinery, coal and lignite, mineral oils, aircraft, shipbuilding, railway transport, and the generation and distribution of electricity. Schedule B listed twelve industries, including aluminium, machine tools, fertilisers, synthetic rubber and road and sea transport, in which the state would progressively increase its own undertakings while private enterprise was expected to supplement its efforts. Schedule C, everything else, was left to private initiative, subject to the state's power to license and direct.

The schedules converted the mixed economy from a slogan into a map of property rights. A private house could read the map and know which frontiers were closed, which were contested and which were open. The resolution's defenders, Nehru among them, described the public sector's reserved heights as the commanding heights of the economy, the phrase that fixed itself to the settlement: from steel, energy and transport, the state could steer the whole without owning the whole. The resolution also bound the state to the small producer, promising that cottage and village industries would be supported against factory competition, and to labour, repeating the pledges of fair wages and welfare that the 1948 text had carried. Eight years after Mukherjee's resolution, the Congress had moved the boundary a long way toward the state while keeping the form of an assurance to private owners in Schedule C.

The map required an enforcement machinery, and one already existed in embryo. The Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951, in force from 8 May 1952, had empowered the government to require a licence for establishing a new industrial undertaking, or substantially expanding an existing one, in any scheduled industry. Under the First Plan the licensing power had slept lightly; under the 1956 charter it became the daily instrument of the settlement. An industrialist who wished to build, expand, change his product or move his factory applied to the Ministry of Industry, whose licensing committee, advised by the Directorate General of Technical Development, weighed his proposal against the plan's capacity targets for his industry. A licence granted was an asset; a licence refused was a locked door. The state had acquired, without nationalising anything, a veto over the growth of every large factory in India.

This is the point at which the Nehruvian planned economy becomes unintelligible as economics alone. The licence system created a constituency for itself among those it licensed, who gained shelter from new competition with every refusal issued to a rival, and a grievance among those it refused, who learned that access to ministers and officials was a factor of production. The schedules told capital where it could not go; the licences told it how far it could go where it could; and the plan totals told both sides what the state intended to build in its own name beside them. The settlement of 1956 was therefore a distribution of rights as much as a programme of investment, and it would be defended and attacked on exactly that ground for the rest of the Nehru years and long after them.

## The Five-Feature Map of the Nehruvian Planned Economy

For citation, the argument of this article rests on a five-feature map of the Nehruvian planned economy, in which each feature is a dated instrument whose institutional content can be checked against the primary record. The table titled "The Five-Feature Map of the Nehruvian Planned Economy" sets out the map in full.

| Feature | Instrument and date | What it settled politically |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | Industrial Policy Resolution, 6 April 1948 | The mixed economy declared; three state monopolies named; private capital granted a ten-year assurance against nationalisation |
| Two | Planning Commission, cabinet resolution of 15 March 1950 | A planning authority created by executive will, chaired by the prime minister, outside the Constitution and above the ministries |
| Three | First Five-Year Plan, 1951 to 1956 | The machinery tested on agriculture, irrigation and river projects; consent built through the village programme and early food success |
| Four | Second Five-Year Plan on the Mahalanobis frame, 1956 to 1961 | Heavy industry chosen over consumption; three steel towns assigned to three regions; professional consensus mobilised behind the choice |
| Five | Industrial Policy Resolution, 30 April 1956 | Industry sorted into Schedule A for the state, Schedule B for the shared frontier and Schedule C for licensed private enterprise |

## The Critics Inside and Outside the Settlement

The settlement was never unopposed, and its critics divide into those who advised it, those who organised against it and those who found it too timid. The adviser's dissent came first and cut deepest. B.R. Shenoy, professor at Gujarat University and a former Reserve Bank and International Monetary Fund economist, sat on the Panel of Economists of 1955 and alone refused the memorandum his twenty colleagues signed. His note of dissent objected on three grounds: the plan was too large for the economy's savings, its gap would be filled by deficit financing that must produce inflation or a payments crisis or both, and the apparatus of controls and allocations it required would endanger a young democracy's freedoms. The payments crisis arrived in 1957, inside two years. Shenoy's reward in his lifetime was exclusion from official favour; his vindication was quoted back at the planners for decades.

A second professional dissent attacked the frame from the side of the consumer. C.N. Vakil and P.R. Brahmananda of Bombay University published Planning for an Expanding Economy in 1956, arguing that the Mahalanobis design starved the wage-goods sector, the food, cloth and simple manufactures that workers buy with their pay. A strategy that taxed the present generation's consumption to build machine-making capacity, while neglecting the goods whose shortage would drive prices up, would finance itself through inflation, the most regressive of taxes. Their wage-goods model proposed heavier weight on agriculture and light industry, where output rose quickly and employment with it. The planners answered that consumer-goods capacity without a machine base condemned India to permanent import dependence. The exchange between Bombay and Calcutta was the finest technical argument of the plan era, and it was also a political one, because each side's arithmetic implied a different answer to the question of whose consumption the state might legitimately postpone.

Foreign advice divided on the same line. Milton Friedman spent the autumn of 1955 in India under an American technical assistance programme, assigned to advise Finance Minister C.D. Deshmukh, and left a memorandum arguing that India could reach five percent growth with sound policy, that the draft frame erred in favouring heavy industry and handicrafts together at the expense of the small and medium enterprise between them, and that deficit financing and a fixed, overvalued exchange rate would breed the controls that suppress enterprise. The memorandum was filed and effectively buried in New Delhi; it circulated in print only decades later. Friedman later recorded that he had told the Planning Commission to its face that it ought to be wound up. His treatment illustrates the settlement's immune system: advice that fitted the frame was amplified through panels and commissions, and advice that challenged the frame was received politely and shelved.

Organised opposition arrived in August 1959, when C. Rajagopalachari, the former governor-general and Madras chief minister, joined with Minoo Masani and others to found the Swatantra Party. Rajaji's phrase for the system, the permit-licence-quota raj, reduced the settlement to its daily friction and entered the language permanently. A.D. Shroff, the Tata economist who had signed the Bombay Plan in 1944, had already founded the Forum of Free Enterprise in 1956 to argue the business case against multiplying controls, a sign that the insurance buyers of 1944 felt the policy had been rewritten against them. From the opposite flank, the Communist Party of India attacked the plans as a compact with landlords and monopoly houses that left the village hierarchy intact. The settlement thus faced, by the end of the 1950s, a liberal critique that it controlled too much, a communist critique that it redistributed too little, and a professional critique that its own sums did not add up. It survived all three because it held the one ground none of its critics could occupy: the state itself, won at the ballot box in 1952 and again in 1957.

### Did the public sector earn profits during the Nehru years?

The central public undertakings were not run for profit in the Nehru years. Steel prices were administered below import parity, the railways paid a fixed dividend to the exchequer, and ministers judged the plants on capacity created and regions served rather than on surplus earned. Their accounts showed modest surpluses in some years and concealed subsidies in others.

The three steel plants moved into production between 1959 and 1962, and their early accounts reflected the instruction under which the whole sector worked: break even after serving the government's price policy, and let the nation's need, not the balance sheet, set the target. Profitability was a minor test in the settlement's own ledger.

The defenders of the settlement argued that the undertakings' return should be counted in the capacity they created: machine-building at Ranchi, fertiliser plants, heavy electricals at Bhopal, and the engineering skills that migrated from public workshops into private firms across the 1960s. The critics answered that administered prices and sheltered budgets concealed the true cost of the capital locked inside the plants, and that a private owner answerable to shareholders would have sweated the same assets harder. Both readings draw on the accounts; they differ on the denominator. What the record shows beyond dispute is that the public sector was rarely judged, by the governments that owned it, on profit at all. It was judged on whether a region got its plant, a ministry its project and a party its evidence that the state could build.

## Planning as Politics: Consent, Constituency and the Congress System

To see what the settlement was for, follow the allocations. Rajni Kothari's celebrated account of [the Congress System four-component framework](/2019/11/18/kothari-congress-system-post-independence/) described a dominant party that governed by absorbing its oppositions into internal factions, and the plan gave that system its working material. Licences, plant locations, irrigation projects, steel townships and food procurement prices were all divisible goods, capable of being awarded state by state and faction by faction. A chief minister arrived at the National Development Council with a list and left with a portion of it granted, bound by the ceremony of approval into defending the total. The plan's tables were the Congress system's budget of favours, drawn on a scale no opposition party could match, since the opposition commanded no treasury.

The same machinery cut the other way. Francine Frankel, in India's Political Economy 1947-2004 (2005), argued that the Congress achieved a gradual revolution in industry while retreating in the countryside, where state legislatures dominated by landed castes diluted the land reforms that the First Amendment of 1951 had shielded from the courts, an amendment read in the account of [the first constitutional amendment of 1951](/2019/01/14/first-constitutional-amendment-1951/). Industrial policy could be centralised because its losers were few and its gains were visible; land policy could not, because its losers sat inside the ruling party's district committees. The planned economy's famous asymmetry, audacious in steel and timid in the village, was not a failure of nerve in New Delhi. It was the price of the coalition that kept the Congress in office, and the planners who drafted around that price knew its name.

### What did the commanding heights of the economy mean in practice?

The commanding heights were the seventeen industries of Schedule A in the Industrial Policy Resolution of 30 April 1956: steel, coal, mineral oils, heavy machinery, aircraft, shipbuilding, railways and electricity among them. Their exclusive reservation to the state was meant to place the economy's commanding decisions in public offices answerable to Parliament.

The phrase itself came from the era's Leninist vocabulary, borrowed in reverse: where Lenin had named the heights a revolutionary state must seize, Nehru's ministers named the heights a democratic state must hold, so that the private industry beneath them would grow inside public purposes rather than against them.

In practice the heights commanded less than the map promised and more than the critics conceded. The state held the new capacity in steel and heavy engineering, but the existing private houses retained most consumer industry, the banks, the tea gardens and the managing agency networks, and private investment across the Second Plan ran to thousands of crores alongside the public outlay. Command of the heights turned out to mean command of the increments: each year's new capacity in the reserved fields was the state's to place, and placement was power. A steel plant transformed the politics of the state that received it, as Orissa's leaders understood when Rourkela's furnaces rose on land from which adivasi villages had been displaced, a cost the settlement's ledgers recorded under compensation and its opponents under dispossession.

Consent, finally, was the settlement's scarcest resource and its true product. The Congress asked a poor electorate to accept restrained consumption, indirect taxes that fell on salt, cloth and kerosene, and the postponement of the good life to a later plan. In exchange it offered the visible liturgy of the dams and steel towns, the Bhakra dam that Nehru in 1954 called a temple of the new India, and the repeated experience of elections in which the plan itself was the manifesto. That the bargain held through two general elections is the central political fact of the Nehruvian economy, and it underwrites the wider argument of [the economic-policy-as-politics framework](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/), for which the Nehru settlement is the founding case: Nehru's successors would renegotiate the bargain, in the idiom of [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), but none could govern outside the expectation that the state owed the voter a plan.

## The Money Question: How the Plans Were Paid For

Every plan was also a budget, and the budgets expose the settlement's bargains more honestly than the speeches. The First Plan was financed in large part from the sterling balances India had accumulated in London during the war, a wartime inheritance of more than a thousand crore rupees that let the government spend without taxing at the pace the targets implied. Finance Minister C.D. Deshmukh's budgets of the early 1950s were exercises in respectability: modest deficits, courtship of foreign lenders, and a tax structure in which indirect levies on commodities carried the load that direct taxes on income and property were politically unable to bear. The settlement's founding fiscal fact was a refusal. The Congress would plan like a socialist state and tax like a cautious one, because the propertied classes inside and outside the party had been promised in 1948 that their accumulation would be disturbed only at the margins.

The Second Plan broke the refusal and disclosed the breakage. To find resources on the frame's scale, the government in 1956 invited Nicholas Kaldor, the Cambridge economist, to design a tax system for a poor economy with a narrow income-tax base. Kaldor's report of 1956 proposed an integrated code: a wealth tax, a tax on personal expenditure, a capital gains tax and a gift tax, interlocking so that what one net missed another caught. T.T. Krishnamachari, who had moved to finance, carried much of the scheme into law in 1957. The yield disappointed the planners and enraged the taxed: the expenditure tax was repealed within a few years, the wealth tax collected little from a countryside whose assets were hard to value, and evasion organised itself faster than the revenue service could follow. The episode is the fiscal twin of the licensing story. A state strong enough to reserve steel could not, or would not, reach into the incomes of the classes that staffed its own party.

What taxation could not raise, the printing press and the foreigner supplied. Deficit financing, the gap between spending and revenue filled by new money, ran well beyond the frame's provision in the Second Plan, and wholesale prices rose by roughly a third across the plan's five years, taxing every holder of cash and every buyer of food in the manner Vakil and Brahmananda had predicted. Foreign assistance filled the rest, and its diplomacy belonged to the same settlement: India took American grain and consortium credits and Soviet steel and machinery in the same plan years, converting non-alignment into a method of finance, a linkage traced in the reading of [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/). The plan's money, like its licences, was allocated by political judgement about which creditors, classes and consumers could be asked to bear which share of the cost, and in that allocation the economist's frame and the politician's instinct were the same document read in two lights.

## Land, the Village and the Limits of the Frame

The settlement's boldest language was reserved for the countryside, and its boldest language is where it achieved least. Zamindari abolition, the extinction of the intermediary landlords who stood between the state and the cultivator across much of northern and eastern India, was the one great redistribution the Congress carried through, defended in the courts by the First Amendment of 1951 and completed in most states by the middle of the decade. It abolished a class of rentiers and enriched the class beneath them: the substantial occupancy tenants and dominant peasant castes who became the village face of the Congress. Ceilings on holdings, the second stage that would have cut into that class itself, were enacted late, drafted loosely and evaded wholesale through family partitions and benami transfers. The plan documents deplored the evasion and budgeted almost nothing to defeat it.

The contradiction broke into the open at the Congress session at Nagpur in January 1959, where Nehru pushed a resolution favouring joint cooperative farming as the pattern for the future. Charan Singh, the Uttar Pradesh revenue minister and the most formidable agrarian politician in the party, resigned from the state cabinet over the resolution and wrote his case against collectivisation of the peasant holding, arguing that the Indian cultivator's attachment to his own acres was a fact no plan could repeal. Nehru retreated to assurances that cooperation would be voluntary and gradual, and the resolution was quietly shelved as policy even as it stood on the record as doctrine. A rival answer to the village question walked the country in the same years: Vinoba Bhave's Bhoodan movement, begun at Pochampally in April 1951, asked landlords to gift land to the landless and collected pledges of millions of acres, much of it unfit or entangled. The settlement absorbed Bhoodan as it absorbed so much else, praising the saint and budgeting for the dams.

The political meaning of the rural record is precise. The planned economy was radical exactly where radicalism did not threaten the ruling coalition's rural base, and cautious exactly where it did. Heavy industry had no organised losers inside the Congress; land ceilings had thousands. When later sections of this article weigh the scholarly verdicts, this asymmetry is the heaviest item on the scale, because it shows the frame's priorities being set not by the model's logic, which treated agriculture as a residual supplier of labour and food, but by the party's sociology, which knew to the village which interests could be touched. The Mahalanobis frame and the Congress machine agreed, for different reasons, to build the factories first. Their agreement, not either document alone, is the Nehruvian planned economy.

## The Statisticians' State: Numbers as Political Instruments

A planned economy must count before it can command, and the counting was itself an institution of the settlement. The Central Statistical Organisation was set up in the cabinet secretariat in 1949, the National Sample Survey began its rounds in 1950 under Mahalanobis's direction, and the Indian Statistical Institute in Calcutta functioned as a second capital of economic intelligence, its journal Sankhya publishing the papers in which the frame's mathematics appeared. Before the plans, the Indian state possessed no continuous measure of national income, no regular count of the unemployed and only fragmentary industrial statistics. After 1950 it possessed all three, produced by a statistical service whose senior figures moved between Calcutta, the Commission's offices in New Delhi and the universities with an ease that blurred any line between the academy and the state.

The political uses of the new numbers ran in both directions. For the government, the survey apparatus certified the plan's reality: targets could be announced in tonnes and crores because a machinery existed that would report tonnes and crores in return, and a missed target could be reframed as a revised estimate in the next round of the survey. For the critics, the same numbers armed opposition with official evidence: Shenoy's dissent, the wage-goods argument and the parliamentary attacks on prices all cited the state's own statistics against the state's own claims. Mahalanobis understood the dual use and guarded the survey's independence of method fiercely, since a number believed by nobody would discipline nobody. The settlement's authority rested partly on furnaces and partly on this: that its arithmetic, however contested in interpretation, was produced by institutions whose counts its enemies also quoted. Few colonial inheritances were transformed as thoroughly, or as quietly, as the census-taker's ledger became the planner's instrument panel.

## Three Readings of the Same Record: The Verdict Adjudicated

Scholarly judgement on the Nehruvian planned economy has sorted itself into three readings, and this article's brief requires a verdict among them. The first reading, associated with the development economists, scores the settlement by growth and structure. Pulapre Balakrishnan, in Economic Growth in India: History and Prospect (2010), points out that the economy grew at roughly three and a half percent a year across the Nehru decades, against near stagnation in the last half-century of British rule, and that the public investments of the 1950s created the capital-goods base, the power grid's beginnings, the irrigation stock and the technical institutes on which all later growth has stood. On this reading the plans were a foundation-laying success whose returns were collected by successors who maligned the foundation. The second reading, the planning-history reading, narrates the settlement as administrative chronicle: commissions constituted, plans published, outlays made, targets met or missed by stated percentages. It is accurate in every particular and explains almost nothing, since it cannot say why the outlays fell where they did or why the targets that mattered politically, food and foreign exchange, were the targets the frames handled worst.

The third reading is the one this article defends: the settlement as political architecture, in which each instrument settled a contest inside the Indian polity and the economic results followed from those settlements. It absorbs the first reading's evidence, because the foundation was real, and it explains the second reading's anomalies, because the missed targets were the ones whose achievement would have taxed the coalition. It also survives the strongest contrary evidence assembled by the settlement's economic prosecutors. Jagdish Bhagwati and T.N. Srinivasan, in India's Economic Reforms (1993), showed that the control system metastasised far beyond any frame's design: import licensing, improvised in the emergency of 1957, hardened into a permanent allocation of scarcity, and industrial licensing, meant to steer investment toward plan priorities, decayed into a queue in which incumbents collected permissions as property. The charge is proven on the documents. What it proves, however, is the political reading itself: a technical instrument does not metastasise by accident. The controls multiplied because each control created beneficiaries with access to the ruling party, and no cabinet that depended on those beneficiaries could bring itself to repeal what the frames had never intended.

The complication owed to the opposing case must be stated at full strength. The Mahalanobis frame was not a politician's costume over an empty box. Its two-sector logic, dividing investment between the goods that make goods and the goods that are consumed, had been worked out by the Soviet economist G.A. Feldman in 1928 and was developed independently by Mahalanobis into the four-sector version of 1955 with a rigour his critics in Bombay respected even as they rejected his weights. The wage-goods quarrel, the Shenoy dissent and the Friedman memorandum were arguments among economists about real magnitudes: savings rates, capital-output ratios, the elasticity of food supply. A reading that treated the settlement as pure politics would dissolve these magnitudes into theatre and explain nothing about why the steel plants produced steel or why the reserves ran out on schedule. The political reading defended here claims the operationalisation, not the content. The model's equations chose the destination; politics chose the road, the tolls and the passengers, and decided whose ox would be gored when the sums failed. In the longer constitutional story, the planning state also rested on a legal settlement that later decades would contest in the courts, the world of doctrine mapped in [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/): the property clauses that the First Amendment rewrote to protect land reform became, a generation on, the ground on which the state's economic power was fought over clause by clause.

What, then, did the Nehruvian planned economy operationalise? It converted the national movement's promises into a durable distribution of rights, burdens and expectations: the right of the state to reserve the commanding industries, the right of the licensed to their shelter, the burden of restraint laid on the consumer of cloth and grain, the expectation of the regions that Delhi owed them works. Those distributions outlasted the frames that announced them, which is why the dismantling attempted after 1991, chronicled in the reading of [the Rao government's reforms and the 1991 settlement](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), required a crisis of the exchequer rather than a mere change of doctrine, and why the five features of 1948 to 1956 remain the load-bearing past of every Indian economic argument since.

## The Regions' Plan: Sites, Freight and State Bargaining

The plan was national in its totals and regional in its experience. State governments submitted their own plan documents to the Commission, bargained over their sizes in the working groups and the National Development Council, and received central assistance as a mixture of grants and loans whose proportions poorer states perpetually contested. The divisible prize above all others was the large central project. A steel plant meant a township, a railway load, thousands of unionised jobs and a permanent claim on the nation's imagination, and the three Second Plan plants were placed with a geographer's care for the federation: Bhilai in Madhya Pradesh near the iron ore of Dalli-Rajhara, Rourkela in Orissa near the ore of Barsua, Durgapur in West Bengal on the Damodar coalfield. Each placement rewarded a state whose loyalty mattered and bound its politics to the settlement's prestige. When a fourth plant was promised for Bokaro in Bihar in the mid-1960s, after American financing collapsed and Soviet assistance replaced it, the announcement itself was a political event in Patna years before the furnaces existed.

Against the prizes stood the grievances, and the deepest was written into freight rates. To let factories anywhere in India buy steel and coal at uniform delivered prices, the government operated a policy of freight equalisation for these commodities, formalised in the plan years, under which distant consumers were subsidised and the mineral states' natural advantage of nearness to ore and coal was taxed away. Industrialists in Bombay and Ahmedabad received Bengal's coal and Bihar's steel at prices that erased the east's locational gift. Eastern economists and politicians complained for decades that the policy transferred the east's inheritance to the west's factories, and the complaint became a staple of the federal argument that the settlement had rigged the map. The planners defended equalisation as nation-building: industry should locate by market and skill, not by accident of geology. Both arguments were correct on their own premises, which is why the quarrel outlived the policy. The plan's geography, like its schedules, distributed advantages that its totals never mentioned.

## The Visitors: Foreign Economists in the Plan's Corridors

Delhi in the 1950s was a world capital of economic advice, and the traffic of foreign economists through the Commission's corridors formed part of the settlement's politics. Oskar Lange, the Polish economist of market socialism, advised and lectured in India repeatedly and found in the plan a vindication of rational allocation under public ownership. Michal Kalecki, his compatriot, warned in print about the financing of growth in an economy of intermediate regimes and small proprietors, a scepticism the planners quoted less often. Joan Robinson of Cambridge toured the country as an enthusiast for the experiment and, on Bhagwati's later recollection, retraced Friedman's itinerary to repair the damage his lectures had done. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology's Center for International Studies opened an India project in the 1950s, financed by American foundations, whose economists worked inside the planning establishment and carried its methods into the transatlantic argument about aid.

Among the visitors, the technicians of the big push left the deepest institutional trace. Paul Rosenstein-Rodan, the MIT economist whose wartime paper on the industrialisation of eastern Europe had supplied the theory of the coordinated investment surge, advised in India through the 1950s, and his institute's India project trained a cadre of Indian economists in the input-output and programming methods that filled the Commission's perspective planning division. The division, set up under Pitamber Pant, a physicist turned planner who became Mahalanobis's closest lieutenant in New Delhi, attempted the long view beyond the five-year frames: studies for a fifteen-year perspective, manpower budgets for engineers and doctors, and the regional balances that the annual bargaining with the states consumed. Pant's office embodied the settlement's faith that the future could be computed before it was voted on, and its papers, more than the frames themselves, show the planners thinking like the founders of a state that intended to direct its economy for a generation.

The visitors mattered because the plan was an exhibit in a world contest. India's experiment, democratic planning beside China's command economy, was watched in Washington, London and Moscow as evidence about which road a poor country should take, and aid flowed partly in payment for the exhibit's continuation. Nehru and Mahalanobis played the audience skilfully, accepting advisers from every camp while conceding the pen to none. The record of the visitors' advice, from Kaldor's tax code to Friedman's buried memorandum, shows a government that imported economic ideas the way it imported machine tools: eagerly, selectively and with a firm intention to assemble them to its own design. Where the design and the advice conflicted, the design prevailed, and the advisers who accepted that rule, Lange and Kaldor among them, left with their reputations enhanced and their schemes half-built. The settlement's confidence was not ignorance of the world's economics. It was a ruling party's certainty that it, and not the professor, would answer to the voter.

## The Election Test: Planning at the Polls

Thrice in the Nehru years the settlement faced the electorate as a governing record, in 1952, 1957 and 1962, and thrice the Congress returned with large Lok Sabha majorities, taking about 45 percent of the vote in 1952, about 48 percent in 1957 and about 45 percent in 1962. The oppositions campaigned against the plan on prices, on controls and on corruption in the allocation of permits, and the campaigns were not feeble: the Praja Socialist Party and the Jana Sangh both made the cost of living their central charge in 1957, when food prices had risen through the Second Plan's first year. The Congress answered with the plan itself, its dams opened, its steel promised, its village blocks staffed, and with Nehru's person as the plan's guarantor. The verdicts ratified the bargain this article has described: consumption restrained in the present, in exchange for a state visibly building a future and distributing the building fairly enough to be believed.

The ratification had limits that the totals concealed. The Congress vote was a plurality manufactured by the electoral system into overwhelming seats, and the parties of protest grew at its edges in the very years of the plan's triumph: the communists won Kerala in 1957 and formed a ministry that New Delhi dismissed in July 1959, and the Swatantra Party took second place in the Lok Sabha of 1962 as the licensed classes found their parliamentary voice. The settlement's electoral history is therefore double. It commanded the centre of Indian politics as no economic programme has done before or since, and it generated, in proportion to its reach, the organised forces that would eventually dismantle it.

The campaign of 1962, the last general election Nehru led, shows the settlement's opponents finding their separate voices at once. Swatantra, contesting nationally for the first time, won eighteen Lok Sabha seats on the single argument that the licence state should be dismantled; the Jana Sangh took fourteen on a programme of economic nationalism and suspicion of the Commission's centralism; the socialists, divided between their parties, attacked the plan's prices and its mercy toward the great houses. None of them threatened the Congress majority, which rested on 361 seats. Read together, however, the campaigns of 1962 drafted the indictment that Indian politics would spend the following decades arguing: that the plan had built a state too large to be honest, too centralised to be federal and too protective of its licensed clients to be the socialist instrument its manifestos advertised. The settlement had won the election. It had also, in winning, educated its executioners.

## The Small Print: Khadi, Handlooms and the Village Industries

Beneath the schedules and the steel plants ran the settlement's small print, and it was written for the voter the planners could count. Both industrial policy resolutions reserved a protected sphere for cottage and small-scale production, and the machinery of protection grew steadily: a Khadi and Village Industries Board in 1953, replaced by a statutory Khadi and Village Industries Commission under an Act of 1956, a Village and Small-Scale Industries Committee under D.G. Karve in 1955 whose report shaped the Second Plan's allocations, and a network of industrial estates that carried small workshops to district towns. The economic logic offered for the protection was employment: a rupee spent on a handloom or an ambar charkha, the improved spinning wheel the Khadi board promoted from 1956, employed more hands than a rupee spent on a mill, and the plan's capital was too scarce to waste on displacing labour it could not reabsorb. Mahalanobis's four sectors built the argument into the frame itself, assigning factory consumer goods a ceiling and household production the rest of the consumer market.

The political logic ran parallel and deeper. Khadi was the uniform of the freedom struggle, and a Congress government that taxed the mills' cloth through excise while subsidising the handloom's was paying a debt to its own mythology as much as to any model. The Gandhian economists around J.C. Kumarappa pressed the case for a decentralised economy of self-reliant villages as a moral order superior to the steel town, and although the planners rejected the order, they bought the protection as its tribute. Business complained that protection sheltered inefficiency and that the handloom's survival depended on rebates no treasury could carry forever; the weavers' constituencies, thick on the ground in Madras, Bengal and Uttar Pradesh, answered through their legislators. The settlement held both positions because it had to: the frame needed the village quiet while the factories rose, and the price of quiet was written into the excise schedule. Here again the document that wears the face of technique is a treaty among constituencies, and the charkha and the blast furnace, the two symbols the era cannot separate, were clauses of the same bargain.

## The Steel Towns: What Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur Built Besides Steel

The three steel plants were the settlement's showpieces, and their making shows the architecture at its most concrete. Hindustan Steel Limited, incorporated in 1954, was created to build and run them as a state company. Bhilai rose under a Soviet agreement of February 1955, its plant supplied and its engineers trained by the Soviet Union, its first blast furnace commissioned in February 1959. Rourkela rose under an agreement with a West German consortium led by Krupp and Demag, its first furnace also commissioned in 1959. Durgapur rose under a British consortium's contract, producing from 1959 into the following year. The three contracts were a diplomatic portfolio as much as an industrial one: the plan's steel was proof, offered to three rival camps at once, that non-alignment could be poured in concrete. In [the foreign-policy reading of these years](/2019/03/04/nehru-foreign-policy-domestic-politics/) the same contracts appear as foreign policy conducted by other means.

Each plant also built a society. The townships laid out beside the furnaces housed a new salaried working class drawn from every region of India, allotted quarters by grade, schooled in company schools and treated in company hospitals, a model citizenry of the planned order. The unions organised, the Communist and socialist federations competed for the furnaces' crews, and the plants' strikes and settlements entered national politics as tests of the state's conduct as an employer. At the fence of each township stood the older settlement that the plant had displaced: villages acquired under emergency land provisions, at Rourkela across some thirty villages whose adivasi inhabitants received cash compensation and promises that the record shows were unevenly kept. The steel towns thus carried the settlement's double truth in one landscape. They were the strongest evidence that the Nehruvian state could execute what it designed, and standing evidence, in the displaced villages at their gates, that the design's costs were assigned by the same political arithmetic that assigned its benefits, to those with the least voice in the council chamber where the map was approved.

## Food: The Plan's Weakest Ledger

No ledger of the settlement balances worse than food. The First Plan's good harvests had flattered the frames into treating agriculture as the economy's stabiliser, a sector whose output could be coaxed upward by dams, extension workers and community blocks while the investment frontier moved to industry. The Second Plan budgeted accordingly, and the countryside answered with the era's sharpest refutation. Foodgrain imports, which had fallen below a million tonnes in the mid-1950s, rose past three and a half million tonnes by 1957 as procurement faltered and prices climbed. In August 1956 India signed the first of its agreements under the American PL-480 programme, taking surplus American wheat against payment in rupees, and the shipments that were meant as a bridge became a structure: across the Second Plan's years the country imported grain in quantities that fed, in effect, the industrial workforce whose wages the wage-goods critics had warned could not be stretched. The food minister's office became a crisis ministry, passing from hand to hand as harvests dictated.

The politics of the food ledger cut against the settlement in a particular way. Steel could be inaugurated; grain had to be eaten, and its price was the one plan statistic every voter audited weekly. State governments fought the Centre over procurement prices and over the food zones that restricted the movement of grain between states, each chief minister guarding his own surplus against his neighbour's deficit, while the Centre's Food Corporation machinery for purchase and storage would not exist until 1965. The wage-goods economists had predicted the mechanism precisely: starve the sector that feeds the worker, and the plan will be financed by the price of bread. When the famines were only scarcities and the imports always arrived, the settlement survived its weakest ledger; but the dependence entered the national memory, and the agricultural turn of the later 1960s, the high-yielding varieties and the procurement state built after 1965, was the confession written by Nehru's successors that the frame had read the village wrong.

## The Balance Sheet by the Numbers

A settlement that asked to be judged by its arithmetic deserves an honest audit of the arithmetic, and the figures, read from the plan reviews and the national accounts that Balakrishnan and others have reconstructed, describe neither the triumph the Congress claimed nor the failure its later detractors advertised. Across the years from 1950 to 1951 to 1963 to 1964, national income grew at an average of about 3.9 percent a year, and per capita income at a little under 2 percent a year, against a colonial half-century in which per capita income had barely moved. Industrial production grew at about 7 percent a year through the 1950s, the fastest sustained pace in the country's recorded history to that date. The rate of investment climbed from about a tenth of national income at the start of the First Plan to about a seventh by the middle of the 1960s, financed increasingly from domestic saving rather than from the sterling balances with which the experiment had begun. Life expectancy and literacy, the plan's silent ledgers, rose faster in fourteen years than in the preceding fifty.

Set against the frames' promises, the same numbers indict. The Second Plan's own target was missed, the Third Plan's target was missed by half, and the growth that arrived was carried disproportionately by the capital-goods sector the frame had favoured, while the consumer's basket, food above all, lagged the population's growth in the plan's middle years. K.N. Raj, who had helped to draft the First Plan as a young man, looked back in 1978 and gave the quarter-century's pace its durable name: the Hindu rate of growth, a phrase he intended as an indictment of complacency about a rate that left poverty's proportions almost untouched. The fairness of the audit depends on the counterfactual chosen. Against the colonial record, the settlement accelerated the economy and built its missing foundations. Against the East Asian economies that began the same decades at similar incomes and chose export markets over import substitution, the settlement's pace was a falling behind that compounded for a generation. Both counterfactuals are documented; the choice between them is a political act disguised as an accounting one, which is the most Nehruvian conclusion an audit of Nehruvian planning can reach.

The poverty count complicates every clean verdict. When V.M. Dandekar and Nilakantha Rath published Poverty in India in 1971, measuring the Nehru years with the state's own survey data, they estimated that in 1960 to 1961 about 40 percent of the rural population and about half of the urban population lived below a minimum standard of consumption, proportions barely improved on the previous decade. The plans had raised the average; they had not reached the bottom at any pace the bottom could feel, and the finding armed every later critic who held that the settlement's priorities, heavy plant first and land reform evaded, amounted to a transfer of patience demanded from those least able to wait. The defenders answered that the same decades doubled the expectation of life at birth from about thirty-two years to about forty-one and carried literacy from under a fifth of the population to more than a quarter. A generation was being schooled, healed and fed slightly better while it was asked to finance steel cities. Which of those sentences carries the era depends, again, on the judge's constituency.

The foundations, at least, are not in dispute, and they were laid in the classrooms as much as at the furnaces. The first Indian Institute of Technology opened at Kharagpur in 1951, and four more, at Bombay, Madras, Kanpur and Delhi, followed by 1961, built with the era's characteristic portfolio of patrons: Soviet, American, British and German assistance each attached to a campus. University enrolment multiplied several times over across the plan years, and the scientific departments, the atomic energy programme under Homi Bhabha and the research laboratories of the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research, formed the technical cadre without which neither the steel plants nor their later critics could have existed. Balakrishnan's foundation-laying reading draws its strength from this ledger, in which the settlement invested in people whose output no five-year frame could schedule.

## Parliament and the Plan: The Opposition's Audit

The settlement's democratic character was tested in the chamber that could have refused it. Each plan was laid before Parliament and debated, and the debates of the 1950s show an opposition auditing the frames with a technical fluency that the bureaucracy respected. Hiren Mukherjee, the communist member for Calcutta, attacked the plans from the left for their mercy toward monopoly capital and their neglect of land redistribution, citing the government's own reports on concentration against its socialist vocabulary. Minoo Masani, who moved from the Tata boardroom to the Lok Sabha as a Swatantra member in the 1960s, made the licensing system's files his brief, reading individual cases of delay and favour into the record until the licence itself became a parliamentary byword. The Estimates Committee of the Lok Sabha examined the Planning Commission's own working and reported critically on the growth of its establishment, forcing the deputy chairman to defend the body's size and methods before the House's accountants.

The most instructive career of the decade crossed the floor. Ashok Mehta, the socialist economist who had helped to found the Praja Socialist Party and had written studies of the Congress's economic compromises, accepted Nehru's invitation to join the Planning Commission and became its deputy chairman in 1963. The absorption of the settlement's most credentialled critic into its own machinery was Kothari's Congress system operating in a single biography: the opposition's argument was not defeated but employed. By the time of Nehru's death the plan had acquired, through these audits and absorptions, a legitimacy that mere party majorities could not have given it. Parliament had never voted the experiment out of existence because Parliament, across its benches, had been given a share of the experiment's authorship, and the critics who remained outside the tent, Shenoy in the universities and Rajaji in his new party, were precisely those whose arguments the frames could not digest.

## Credit and the Countryside: The Settlement's Financial Plumbing

The schedules governed factories, but the settlement also rebuilt the plumbing through which money reached the village, and the record there is its most honest self-assessment. The Reserve Bank had been taken into public ownership on 1 January 1949, before the Commission existed, and in 1951 the Bank sponsored an All-India Rural Credit Survey whose committee of direction, chaired by A.D. Gorwala, reported in 1954. The survey measured what the planners suspected: of the cultivator's borrowing, roughly 70 percent came from moneylenders at punishing rates, about 3 percent from cooperatives, and barely 1 percent from the state. Its verdict became proverbial in the ministries: cooperation in India had failed, the report concluded, but cooperation must succeed. The settlement's answer was a state-partnered cooperative credit structure, central and state cooperative banks above primary village societies, refinanced through the Reserve Bank, an architecture of public money flowing through nominally member-owned institutions.

The same instinct rebuilt urban finance. On 1 July 1955 the Imperial Bank of India became the State Bank of India, the country's largest bank converted into the state's instrument for carrying banking to district towns the private banks had ignored; John Mathai, the finance minister who had resigned over the Planning Commission, became its first chairman, an appointment that suggests the settlement could honour its dissenters even as it outvoted them. In January 1956 the government nationalised life insurance, folding more than two hundred insurers and provident societies into the Life Insurance Corporation that began work that September, on the stated ground that policyholders' savings, spread across the whole country, should be gathered into the plan's pool of investible funds and protected from the failures that had disgraced private insurers. The corporation's annual investible surplus became, in the decades after, one of the exchequer's most reliable subscribers. Credit, banking and insurance were drawn, piece by piece, into public hands without the word nationalisation appearing in the 1948 resolution's promises, a quiet extension of the commanding heights into the economy's bloodstream, executed while Parliament and the press watched the steel plants.

The plumbing's performance matched the settlement's general pattern: the architecture was built, the flow disappointed. Cooperative credit expanded its membership and its advances across the plan years and still reached the substantial cultivator more readily than the tenant or the labourer, because the village society's committee reproduced the village's hierarchy. The moneylender's share of rural debt fell across the 1950s but remained the largest single source when the next survey counted. The planners recorded the failure in their own documents and prescribed more of the same architecture, deepened and supervised, rather than a different answer, because the cooperative form was the one rural institution the Congress could build without declaring war on the rural notables who staffed it. In finance as in industry, the settlement's limits were drawn where its coalition's interests began.

## The State as Employer: The Model Employer and Its Discontents

The settlement also made the state the country's model employer, and the doctrine carried its own politics. Both industrial policy resolutions pledged fair wages, welfare and a share in management's counsels, and the Congress built its own labour federation, the Indian National Trade Union Congress of 1947, to compete with the communist All-India Trade Union Congress for the allegiance of the organised worker. Wage boards for the cotton textile industry in 1957 and for sugar in 1960 began the practice of tripartite settlements in which the state, owning neither mill nor refinery, fixed the price of labour by award. In the public undertakings themselves, administered by managers answerable to ministries, the model employer doctrine promised security, housing and promotion by rule, and delivered a workforce whose unions learned to negotiate with the government as owner, worker politics and economic policy fused in a single ministry corridor.

The doctrine met its limit in July 1960, when the central government employees' federations struck over the merger of dearness allowance with pay. Nehru's government answered with ordinances that declared the strike illegal, arrested thousands of employees and dismissed or suspended many more, and the prime minister told Parliament that the action bordered on rebellion. The strike collapsed within days. The episode exposed the settlement's seam: a state that presented itself as labour's patron in the resolutions would break labour's strike in the secretariat, because the plan's wage bill was also the exchequer's wage bill and the model employer's benevolence ended where the deficit began. The unions remembered the ordinances when the settlement's later custodians asked for their loyalty, and the labour clauses of the five features stand in the record as the promises the architecture found hardest to keep.

## Strain and Succession: The Third Plan, the Wars and the Plan Holiday

The Third Five-Year Plan, covering 1961 to 1966, promised what the first two had prepared: a self-reliant and self-generating economy, growth at 5.6 percent a year, and a take-off into a stage where the economy would grow without aid. It opened under a sky of wars and closed in drought. The border war with China in October and November 1962, read in the account of [the 1962 China war as a domestic political event](/2019/02/16/1962-china-war-decline-nehru/), shattered the mood in which the plan had been written, drove defence spending to a new share of the budget and discredited the planner's calm. The war with Pakistan in September 1965, the food emergency of 1965 to 1966, and the suspension of American aid in 1965 after the war, followed in quick succession. Monsoon failures in 1965 and 1966 cut foodgrain output, imports of grain under the American programme reached a scale of about ten million tonnes in 1966, and the government devalued the rupee in June 1966 under consortium pressure, a decision its successors would defend as necessity and its critics as surrender. The Third Plan's growth averaged under 3 percent a year against its 5.6 percent target, the worst miss in the plan era.

Devaluation, when it came on 6 June 1966, gathered every grievance against the settlement into a single decision. The government of Indira Gandhi, pressed by the World Bank's Bell mission and by the consortium's withholding of non-project assistance, moved the rupee from 4.76 to 7.50 to the dollar and promised export promotion and freer imports in exchange for resumed aid. The aid that followed fell short of the pledges, and the freer import regime was rolled back within two years, but the political bill arrived at once. K. Kamaraj and the Congress old guard read the decision as a surrender negotiated over the party's head; the opposition benches attacked the dependence that had made the surrender necessary; and the planners who had built the frames watched a cabinet trade their instruments for a creditors' certificate. The episode belongs to the settlement's afterlife rather than to its text, yet it proved the political reading from the far side. The plan had always rested on foreign goodwill to a degree its frames admitted only in footnotes, and when the goodwill was priced, the price was paid in the currency of sovereignty.

Nehru did not live to face the reckoning. He died on 27 May 1964, in the Third Plan's fourth year, leaving behind a machinery that no successor could operate in the same way because its authority had been his personal chairmanship. Lal Bahadur Shastri's government, which negotiated the Tashkent agreement and held the food emergency with rationing and procurement, set the Fourth Plan aside in 1966 and governed the economy through three annual plans, the pause that later commentary named the plan holiday, from 1966 to 1969. D.R. Gadgil, the economist who became deputy chairman of the Commission in 1967, presided over the design of the Fourth Plan that Indira Gandhi's government eventually adopted in 1969, the same year in which the architecture of the Nehru settlement was extended into banking by the nationalisation of fourteen commercial banks in July 1969, the act read in the account of [the bank nationalisation of 1969](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/). The nationalisation showed the settlement's afterlife clearly: the state could still move dramatically, and it moved in the direction the 1956 map had marked out, toward the commanding heights, even as the plan machinery that had been the map's custodian idled through annual budgets.

### How did the 1962 war with China change the Third Plan?

The war of October and November 1962 struck the Third Plan in its second year. Defence spending doubled in the budget of 1963 to 1964, emergency rule was proclaimed, and steel, engineers and foreign exchange assigned to civilian projects were diverted to defence production. The plan's targets were quietly shelved in practice long before the plan holiday formalised the pause.

Beyond the budget, the war broke the planning consensus. The opposition charged that the machine-building strategy had left the army short of equipment on the frontier, and the government answered with a National Defence Council and an emergency that lasted years. Planning did not stop after 1962, but its unquestioned decade ended there.

What survives the reckoning is the map itself. The five features named in this article, the 1948 resolution, the Commission, the First Plan, the Mahalanobis frame and the 1956 resolution, had by 1964 become the grammar in which every later Indian government argued about the economy, whether to extend, defend or dismantle it. The 1991 reformers abolished industrial licensing in the language of the schedules they abolished, and the defenders of the public sector still invoke the steel towns as proof that the state once built what it promised. A political architecture is measured not by the permanence of its instruments, none of which survives unchanged, but by the durability of the expectations it trained. On that measure the Nehruvian planned economy, for all its missed targets and its taxes on the poor, was the most consequential economic settlement in India's history as a republic, because it taught a democratic country to ask, of any government, what it intends to build, with whose money and for whose gain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 1948 Industrial Policy Resolution?

The Industrial Policy Resolution of 6 April 1948 was the first statement of economic policy by independent India's government, laid before the Constituent Assembly by Shyama Prasad Mukherjee, the Minister for Industry and Supply. It sorted industry into four classes: arms and ammunition, atomic energy and railway transport as exclusive state monopolies; six industries, including coal, iron and steel, aircraft and shipbuilding, in which only the state would establish new undertakings; eighteen industries left private under regulation and control; and the remainder open to private and cooperative enterprise. It promised protection to cottage and small-scale industries, fair treatment to labour, and, most consequentially, assured existing private owners that their undertakings would not be nationalised for ten years. The resolution declared the mixed economy as Congress policy while deferring every hard question of totals, priorities and machinery to institutions that did not yet exist.

### Q: When was the Planning Commission established?

The Planning Commission was established on 15 March 1950, by a resolution of the Union cabinet, a few weeks after the Constitution of India came into force. Jawaharlal Nehru, the prime minister, became its first chairperson, and Gulzarilal Nanda, the Ahmedabad labour leader, its first deputy chairman. Because it rested on an executive resolution rather than on the Constitution or on an Act of Parliament, the Commission was an advisory body in law whose authority flowed from the prime minister's chairmanship and from its terms of reference, which charged it with assessing the country's resources, formulating a plan for their balanced use, fixing priorities and stages, appraising progress and recommending changes of policy to the central and state governments. Its extra-constitutional birth was deliberate: it avoided a constitutional amendment, escaped the Finance Commission's separate domain, and could be expanded by practice without fresh legislation.

### Q: What was the First Five-Year Plan 1951-1956?

The First Five-Year Plan covered April 1951 to March 1956 and was the Nehruvian settlement's first exercise of its new machinery. Drawn with the growth arithmetic associated with Harrod and Domar and published in full in December 1952, it proposed a public outlay of 2,069 crore rupees, concentrated on agriculture, irrigation and power, and aimed to raise national income by about 11 to 12 percent over five years. It carried forward the great river projects, Bhakra-Nangal, Hirakud and the Damodar Valley, launched the Community Development Programme in October 1952, and treated food imports and refugee rehabilitation as charges of the plan. Its closing record exceeded its modest promises: national income rose by about 18 percent, output grew at about 3.6 percent a year against a 2.1 percent assumption, foodgrain production rose from about 52 million tonnes to about 67 million tonnes, and prices steadied after the Korean War boom.

### Q: What is the Mahalanobis Plan?

The Mahalanobis Plan is the name given to the strategy of India's Second Five-Year Plan of 1956 to 1961, built on the draft plan frame that the statistician P.C. Mahalanobis submitted to the government in March 1955. Its core proposition was that long-run growth required a country to produce its own investment goods, the machines that make machines, rather than import them indefinitely. The frame divided the economy into four sectors: factories making investment goods, factories making consumer goods, small and household industries making consumer goods, and services. Heavy investment was directed to the first sector, while the household sector was assigned the task of supplying consumer goods and employment in the interval. Carried into the adopted plan, the strategy more than doubled public outlay to 4,800 crore rupees, sought a 25 percent rise in national income over five years, and embodied itself in the three steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur.

### Q: What was the 1956 Industrial Policy Resolution?

The Industrial Policy Resolution of 30 April 1956, presented to Parliament by T.T. Krishnamachari, was the charter of industry under the Second Plan and the fullest statement of the Nehruvian settlement. It sorted industries into three schedules. Schedule A reserved seventeen industries to the state alone, including arms, atomic energy, iron and steel, heavy machinery, coal, mineral oils, aircraft, shipbuilding, railways and electricity generation. Schedule B named twelve industries, among them aluminium, machine tools, fertilisers and sea transport, where the state would take an expanding role alongside private enterprise. Schedule C left all remaining industries to private initiative, directed by the government's licensing power under the Industries Act of 1951. The resolution followed Parliament's adoption of the socialist pattern of society in December 1954 and the Congress party's Avadi resolution of January 1955, and its reserved heights were defended as the commanding heights of the economy.

### Q: How was the Nehruvian planned economy a political project?

The Nehruvian planned economy was a political project because each of its instruments settled a contest inside the Indian polity before it settled any question of output. The 1948 resolution's ten-year assurance was written to calm private capital and hold the Congress coalition's right wing; the Planning Commission's birth by cabinet resolution in 1950 spared the government a parliamentary and federal fight over its powers; the First Plan's dams and village programmes built consent for the experiment among voters; the Mahalanobis frame mobilised a professional consensus that let the government present a contested choice, heavy industry before consumption, as arithmetic; and the 1956 schedules distributed rights to build, region by region and house by house, through the licence. Land reform's evasion and licensing's beneficiaries mark the same truth from the other side: the frame's priorities bent wherever the ruling party's coalition would have broken.

### Q: Who was P.C. Mahalanobis?

Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis (1893 to 1972) was the statistician who gave the Nehruvian planned economy its strategy and much of its machinery of knowledge. Trained as a physicist at Cambridge, he founded the Indian Statistical Institute in Calcutta in 1931, built the National Sample Survey from 1950 into the largest continuing household enquiry of its kind, and served as honorary statistical adviser to Nehru's cabinet. His 1953 paper on the process of growth of national income and his draft plan frame of March 1955 supplied the Second Five-Year Plan's heavy-industry strategy, with its four-sector division of the economy and its wager that investment goods must be made at home. Nehru trusted his counsel across the plan era, and their correspondence, preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library and in the institute's archives in Calcutta, records the Second Plan being argued into existence between the two men.

### Q: Why was the Planning Commission created by a cabinet resolution rather than by law?

The cabinet resolution route was chosen in March 1950 because the alternatives carried political costs the government refused to pay. A constitutional body would have required an amendment and risked collision with the Finance Commission, which the Constitution itself created to divide tax revenues between the Union and the states. A statutory body would have been debated clause by clause in Parliament, where opposition parties and state governments could have written limits into its charter. A resolution of the cabinet could be issued at once, gave the Commission only advisory status in form, and let its real authority grow from the prime minister's chairmanship and from practice. The form also kept the body flexible: its functions, membership and procedures could be altered by the executive without returning to the legislature. Critics, beginning with Finance Minister John Mathai in 1950, read the same thinness as the design's vice, a super-cabinet answerable to no text.

### Q: Why did John Mathai resign from Nehru's cabinet in 1950?

John Mathai resigned as finance minister in 1950, months after presenting the budget in which he himself had announced the Planning Commission's creation and named its prospective members. An economist who had been among the eight signatories of the business community's Bombay Plan of 1944, Mathai had favoured planning by consultation; in office he concluded that the Commission as designed would override that consultation. His resignation statement called the body ill-timed and ill-conceived, warned that it was tending to become a parallel cabinet, and objected that it would weaken the authority of the Finance Ministry and reduce the cabinet to a registering authority for decisions taken elsewhere. Nehru accepted the resignation, C.D. Deshmukh succeeded him, and the Commission survived its first crisis. Mathai's later appointment as the first chairman of the State Bank of India in 1955 suggests the quarrel was about the distribution of power, not about persons.

### Q: Who was K.N. Raj, and what did he bring to the First Plan?

Kakkadan Nandanath Raj (1926 to 2010) was a young economist from Kerala, trained at the London School of Economics, who joined the small circle of economists who drafted the First Five-Year Plan and worked on its monetary and fiscal chapters, bringing to the exercise the new Keynesian arithmetic of savings, investment and the capital-output ratio that the plan frame employed. He was twenty-five when the plan outline appeared, and his presence in the Commission's back rooms marked the entry of a professional generation that would staff Indian economic institutions for decades: Raj himself went on to found the Centre for Development Studies at Trivandrum in 1970 and to advise governments at home and abroad. His most quoted judgement on the era he helped to open came in 1978, when he named the economy's long-run pace of about three and a half percent a year the Hindu rate of growth, a label intended to shame complacency about how slowly poverty was retreating.

### Q: What was the National Development Council, and what did it do to the plans?

The National Development Council was created on 6 August 1952 to give the states a formal share in approving the plans that the Planning Commission drafted. Constituted, like the Commission itself, by executive resolution rather than by the Constitution or by statute, it brought together the prime minister as chairman, the Union cabinet, the chief ministers of all the states and the members of the Planning Commission. Its function was to consider and approve each five-year plan before the plan went to Parliament, to review the plan's working, and to recommend measures for achieving its targets. In practice the council was the settlement's federal clearing house. Chief ministers arrived with their states' demands for larger outlays, more projects and softer loan terms, and left as signatories of the total they had criticised, bound into defending a document their signatures had authorised. The ceremony converted central design into shared ownership, which was its political purpose.

### Q: What was the draft Plan Frame of 1955, and who wrote it?

The draft Plan Frame was the document that P.C. Mahalanobis, the government's honorary statistical adviser, submitted in March 1955 as the proposed skeleton of the Second Five-Year Plan. It set out the frame's arithmetic and strategy: a sharp rise in the investment rate, priority to the industries that produce investment goods, reliance on small and household production to supply consumer goods and employment, and targets for national income growth across the plan period. Before adoption the frame was examined by a Panel of Economists of twenty-one members, convened under Finance Minister C.D. Deshmukh, whose memorandum of April 1955 endorsed the strategy, with B.R. Shenoy alone dissenting, and by the National Development Council, which accepted it as the basis of the plan. The Second Five-Year Plan document of 1956 followed the frame's structure closely, which is why the strategy of those years carries its author's name in Indian public memory as the Mahalanobis Plan.

### Q: Who was B.R. Shenoy, and what did his note of dissent argue?

Bellikoth Raghunath Shenoy (1905 to 1978) was a classical liberal economist, trained at the London School of Economics, who had worked at the Reserve Bank of India and the International Monetary Fund and held the chair of economics at Gujarat University. In April 1955, serving on the Panel of Economists asked to review the draft plan frame for the Second Five-Year Plan, he alone among twenty-one members refused to sign the majority memorandum and appended a note of dissent. His objections ran on three lines: the plan was too large for the resources the economy could save; the gap would be filled by deficit financing, which would produce inflation or a balance-of-payments crisis, or both; and the apparatus of controls and physical allocations the plan required would endanger the freedoms of a young democracy. The payments crisis arrived in 1957. Shenoy's dissent was quoted against the planners for decades and made him a founding figure of India's liberal economic tradition.

### Q: What did C.N. Vakil and P.R. Brahmananda argue in their wage-goods critique?

C.N. Vakil and P.R. Brahmananda, economists at the University of Bombay, published Planning for an Expanding Economy in 1956 as a systematic alternative to the Mahalanobis frame. Their argument turned on wage goods, the food, cloth and simple manufactures that workers buy out of wages. The Second Plan, they held, concentrated investment in heavy industry whose output would mature slowly, while neglecting the wage-goods sector whose supply governed prices in the present. The resulting gap would be closed by inflation, a tax falling hardest on wage earners and the poor, and the plan would finance itself by quietly confiscating the consumption it claimed to raise. Their wage-goods model proposed heavier weight on agriculture and light industry, where output and employment rose quickly together. The planners replied that consumer-goods capacity without a domestic machine base condemned India to permanent dependence on imports. The exchange between Bombay and Calcutta remained the plan era's central technical controversy.

### Q: What did Milton Friedman tell the Indian government in 1955?

Milton Friedman, the Chicago economist, spent the autumn of 1955 in India under an American technical cooperation programme, assigned to advise Finance Minister C.D. Deshmukh while the Second Five-Year Plan was being framed. In the memorandum he left with the government he argued that a growth rate of five percent a year was entirely feasible for India with sound policy, and that the draft frame's errors lay in its mechanical concentration on the volume of investment, its twin favouring of heavy industry and of handicrafts at the expense of the small and medium enterprises between them, its reliance on deficit financing, and its defence of a fixed and overvalued exchange rate. He warned that the controls used to suppress the resulting strains would stifle the private energy on which Indian growth depended. The memorandum was filed away in New Delhi and reached print only decades later. Jagdish Bhagwati later recalled Friedman telling the Planning Commission directly that the body ought to be wound up.

### Q: What did an industrialist have to obtain before building a factory under the Industries Act of 1951?

Under the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951, in force from 8 May 1952, an industrialist who proposed to establish a new undertaking in any industry listed in the Act's schedule, to expand an existing undertaking substantially, to change its location, or later to manufacture a new article, first had to obtain a licence from the central government. The application went to the Ministry of Industry, where a licensing committee, advised by the Directorate General of Technical Development, tested the proposal against the capacity targets the current plan had fixed for that industry, the availability of raw materials and foreign exchange, and the government's locational preferences. A licence carried conditions on capacity, product and sometimes place, and substantial expansion without one was an offence. Before 1956 the power was lightly used; under the Second Plan and the 1956 Industrial Policy Resolution it became the settlement's daily instrument, the document without which no large factory could lawfully be born.

### Q: Why did the Second Plan run into a foreign exchange crisis in 1957?

The crisis of 1957 arose because the Second Plan's construction programme consumed imports faster than the frame had financed them. The steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur, the machinery for new factories and the equipment for the river projects were bought abroad, while India's export earnings, concentrated in jute, tea and cotton textiles, stagnated. The frame had assumed external assistance of about 800 crore rupees and a slower draw on reserves; the realised import bill ran far ahead of both. Foreign exchange reserves, which stood at about 746 crore rupees in 1956, fell to about 267 crore rupees by 1958, forcing emergency cuts in imports, a tightening of import licensing that never afterwards relaxed, and a drawing on the International Monetary Fund. B.R. Shenoy's dissent of 1955 had forecast precisely this sequence. The episode fixed a pattern in Indian economic policy: each plan's foreign exchange arithmetic would thereafter be audited first, because 1957 had shown where the frame's optimism hid.

### Q: What was the Aid-India Consortium, and why was it formed in 1958?

The Aid-India Consortium was the standing conference of India's creditor governments, convened by the World Bank after the foreign exchange crisis of 1957 to 1958 exposed the Second Plan's financing gap. Its first meeting sat in Washington on 25 to 27 August 1958 under the chairmanship of Eugene Black, the Bank's president, with the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, West Germany and Japan attending and the International Monetary Fund observing. The immediate business was a gap of about 350 million dollars for the remainder of the financial year to March 1959; the durable business was a system under which the donors reviewed India's economic policies and plan progress periodically and pledged assistance against that review. India gained a predictable flow of aid with which to complete the Second Plan and finance the Third. It also accepted, as the price of the flow, a continuing external audit of the settlement's management, a fact its critics cited whenever sovereignty over the plan was claimed in Parliament.

### Q: What did the Third Five-Year Plan promise, and what broke it?

The Third Five-Year Plan, covering 1961 to 1966, promised to carry India to a self-reliant and self-generating stage of growth. It targeted an increase of national income at 5.6 percent a year, a further rise in the investment rate, food self-sufficiency within the plan period and the completion of the basic industries' frame that the Second Plan had begun. What broke it was a sequence no frame had budgeted: the border war with China in October and November 1962, which doubled defence spending and consumed steel, engineers and foreign exchange assigned to civilian industry; the war with Pakistan in September 1965, after which American aid was suspended; and successive monsoon failures in 1965 and 1966 that cut the harvest, drove grain imports to about ten million tonnes in 1966 and forced the rupee's devaluation that June. Growth across the plan averaged under 3 percent a year against the 5.6 percent target, the plan era's widest miss, and the Fourth Plan was postponed into the annual-plan pause of 1966 to 1969.

### Q: What were the plan holidays of 1966 to 1969?

The plan holidays were the three years from 1966 to 1969 in which India governed its economy without a five-year plan, through three successive annual plans, after the Third Plan closed amid war, drought and devaluation. The Fourth Five-Year Plan, due in 1966, was postponed because the resources, the foreign exchange and the political calm that a five-year frame required did not exist; Lal Bahadur Shastri's government first deferred it, and Indira Gandhi's government, which took office in January 1966, continued the pause while it stabilised prices and the food supply. The Planning Commission, under Deputy Chairman D.R. Gadgil from 1967, used the interval to redesign the Fourth Plan, which was finally adopted in 1969. The phrase plan holiday, which later commentary attached to the pause, is misleading in one respect: investment, licensing and procurement continued under the annual plans, and the state's grip on the economy did not relax. What was suspended was the five-year promise itself, the settlement's habit of announcing a destination half a decade ahead.
