Almost every account of Marx on class begins in the wrong place. It begins with two names, bourgeoisie and proletariat, arranged opposite each other like chess pieces, and it presents that arrangement as the theory itself. A reader who starts there learns a slogan and misses an argument. The honest place to begin is a gap. Near the very end of the third volume of Capital, in a short chapter titled Classes, Marx set out to answer the question his whole system presupposes: what makes a class? He named three great classes, posed the question, raised a difficulty against his own first answer, wrote a little more, and stopped. The manuscript breaks off after roughly a page and a half, and Friedrich Engels, preparing the volume for publication after Marx's death, let the fragment stand with an editorial note recording that the text ends there. The definition of the central category of Marxist theory was never finished by its author.

![Class in Marxist theory, the relational definition, the unfinished chapter and the historical classes - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That single fact reorganises everything a careful reader should say about class in Marx. If the definition is a fragment, then every confident summary of what Marx meant by class is partly a reconstruction, built by joining the fragment to the criterion Marx applies consistently across his economics and his historical writing. The criterion is recoverable. He uses it for forty years, in works of very different kinds, and it rarely wavers: a class is defined by its relation to the means of production, by whether its members own productive property and live from the labour of others, or own little beyond their capacity to work and must sell that capacity to live. What is not recoverable is a finished, systematic definition that settles the boundary cases, ranks the criteria when they conflict, or explains how a position in production becomes a collective actor in politics. Marx left those tasks undone, and the history of Marxist class analysis is largely the history of successors attempting them, disagreeing, and each claiming the fragment as authority.

This guide is built on the gap rather than on the slogan. It reads the unfinished chapter closely, extracts the criterion Marx actually uses, distinguishes the relational conception of class from the gradational conception that dominates official statistics and everyday speech, and then sets the abstract model beside the crowded class maps of Marx's own historical writings, where finance aristocrats, industrialists, shopkeepers, peasants, and the stratum he called the lumpenproletariat all do analytical work that a two-class scheme cannot perform. It gives the strongest objections their full force, including the objection that a theory whose founder never defined its central term is built on sand, and it ends with the practical consequences for students, teachers, and anyone who writes about society for a living. One rule governs the whole treatment, and it deserves a name because named rules survive where paragraphs fade. The unfinished chapter rule holds that, because Marx's definition of class survives only as a fragment, every confident statement about what Marx meant by class is a reconstruction, and the honest procedure is to state the criterion he consistently applies rather than the definition he never completed.

## The chapter that breaks off

Chapter Fifty-Two of Capital Volume Three is the final chapter of the work, and its brevity is startling against the scale of what surrounds it. The third volume as Engels assembled it runs to hundreds of pages on profit, interest, rent, and the division of surplus value among claimants. Then comes the chapter on classes, the topic toward which the entire analysis of distribution might seem to have been travelling, and it occupies barely more than a page before the text gives out. Marx begins by naming the owners of the three great revenue sources. The owners of mere labour power, the owners of capital, and the owners of land, whose respective sources of income are wages, profit, and ground rent, are wage-labourers, capitalists, and landowners, and these form the three great classes of modern society based on the capitalist mode of production. He adds that in England, the most developed case, the class structure appears in its purest form, though even there middle and intermediate strata blur the boundaries, a qualification his summarizers routinely drop.

He then asks the question directly: what makes a class? His first move is toward identity of revenues and revenue sources, and he immediately sees the trap inside that answer. If sameness of income source constituted a class, then physicians and officials would each form a class, since their incomes flow from distinguishable sources, and the same logic would shatter every large class into indefinitely many fragments based on divisions of labour and specialisation of function. The fragment ends inside that difficulty. Marx had glimpsed that a definition by revenue source alone multiplies classes without limit, and he had not yet supplied the principle that would stop the multiplication. Engels's note tells the reader plainly that the manuscript breaks off at that point. There is no lost continuation to hope for, no drawer manuscript that completes the argument. The chapter is a door opened onto a room Marx never built.

The placement matters as much as the content. Marx did not fail to define class in an early, immature work and then repair the omission later. The fragment stands at the end of his most systematic economic project, written in draft in the middle eighteen sixties and left unrevised through the remaining two decades of his life, decades in which he revised the first volume repeatedly, learned new languages, studied Russian land tenure, and filled notebooks on mathematics and anthropology. He had time, in other words, and he spent it elsewhere. Interpreters divide over what to make of that priority. One reading treats the omission as accidental, the product of illness, journalism, politics, and the sheer scale of the Capital project. Another treats it as structural: a thinker who believed concepts had to be developed through the analysis of a whole social formation may have distrusted a free-standing definition of class, detachable from the history that gives classes their changing content. The texts support elements of both readings, and this guide does not pretend otherwise. What the texts do not support is the pretence that no gap exists.

The gap explains a puzzle that otherwise looks like inconsistency. Readers who move from the Communist Manifesto to the historical writings to Capital often feel that Marx operates with two theories of class, a simple one and a complicated one, and they ask which is the real Marx. The fragment suggests a better framing. Marx operated with one criterion, applied at different levels of abstraction for different purposes, and he never wrote the mediating chapter that would have explained, in his own authoritative voice, how the levels connect. His successors wrote that chapter for him, in incompatible ways, and the incompatibility is not a scandal to be hidden but the actual state of the tradition a student inherits.

### How does Marx define class?

By relation to the means of production. Those who own productive property and live from others' labour form one class; those who must sell their labour power form another. Marx began a formal definition in Capital Volume Three but the chapter breaks off after roughly a page.

That short answer carries the whole weight of this guide, and its two clauses deserve separation. The first clause gives the criterion: relation to the means of production. The second clause gives the warning: the formal definition was begun and abandoned. A definition of Marxist class that omits the criterion is wrong. A definition that omits the warning is overconfident. Examination answers, textbooks, and journalism fail in both directions constantly, stating the two-class slogan as though it were a definition, or dismissing the whole concept because the definition was never completed, as though a criterion consistently applied for forty years counted for nothing. Holding both clauses together is the discipline the unfinished chapter rule names.

## The criterion Marx actually applies

Strip away the missing definition and a stable test remains, visible wherever Marx analyses a concrete society. Ask of any group three questions. Do its members own means of production, the land, tools, machines, premises, and finance without which production cannot proceed? Do they live by working with means of production owned by others, selling their capacity to work for a wage or salary? Or do they stand in some intermediate or dependent position, owning small productive property of their own yet working it themselves, neither hiring many others nor hired by anyone? The answers sort the population into positions inside a relation. No position is intelligible alone. A capitalist is a capitalist only because others lack productive property and must present themselves as sellers of labour power. A wage-labourer is a wage-labourer only because productive property is concentrated in other hands. Each term of the relation defines the other, which is why this conception is called relational, and why it differs so sharply from schemes that sort individuals by personal attributes.

The criterion also explains why Marx treats class as an economic position before it is anything else. Culture, accent, education, taste, and self-image all matter to his historical writing, and he deploys them brilliantly when describing, for example, the political theatre of French society at mid century. But they describe how a class lives and represents itself, not what constitutes it. A landowner who loses his estates and takes salaried employment has changed class even if his manners, opinions, and dinner table remain aristocratic for a generation. A manual worker who wins a lottery and buys rental property and a small firm has changed class even if every habit of speech and dress stays as it was. Position first, attributes after: that ordering is the signature of the theory, and reversing it produces most of the popular confusion about who counts as working class.

Ownership in this criterion needs care, because legal title and effective control can separate. Marx wrote before the full flowering of the joint stock company, yet he already saw, in the third volume's discussion of credit and joint stock capital, that ownership of capital could detach from the function of managing it, producing a class of money owners confronting a stratum of functioning managers who work, in a sense, for wages, however princely. Later analysts made much of that seam. For the criterion itself, the question is not whose name sits on a document but who stands in which relation to productive assets and to the surplus those assets help produce: who appropriates it, who performs the labour that creates it, and who directs the process under whose authority. Those three roles can coincide in one person, as with a self-employed craftsman, or split across owners, managers, and workers, and the sorting follows the roles rather than the job titles.

Exploitation is the hinge between the criterion and the rest of the system, and readers who want the mechanism in full should work through the account of [Marxist theory of exploitation](/2010/12/01/marxist-theory-of-exploitation/), where the production of surplus value is laid out step by step. For class analysis the point is compact. The relation between owner and non-owner is not a neutral difference of function, like the difference between drivers and passengers. It is, on Marx's account, a channel through which unpaid labour time flows systematically from one position to the other. That is why class positions carry opposed interests built into them, why class struggle is not an optional extra in the theory, and why Marx thought class, rather than any attribute of individuals, was the central category around which a science of society should be organised.


## Relational and gradational: the distinction that does the work

Two families of class schemes compete in modern discussion, and nearly every public argument about class goes wrong by sliding between them. A gradational scheme ranks people along a continuum. Income bands, occupational prestige scores, years of education, and the lettered grades of market research, A, B, C1, C2, D, E, are all gradational instruments. They place each person higher or lower than others on some scale of advantage. Boundaries inside such schemes are conveniences. Nothing in the world changes when a statistical office moves a threshold, and the groups on either side of a line need stand in no particular relation to each other. The richest tenth and the poorest tenth, in a gradational table, are simply distant points on a distribution. Neither exists because of the other.

A relational scheme works in the opposite way. It defines positions by their connections, so that each position owes its existence to the others in the set. Landlord and tenant, creditor and debtor, master and servant, are relational pairs: abolish one side and the other vanishes with it, not gradually but by definition. Marx's classes are positions of that kind. The bourgeoisie is not the set of people above an income line. It is the position of owning productive property and appropriating surplus labour, a position that presupposes the opposed position of propertyless sellers of labour power, just as that position presupposes it. This is why translation between the two families of scheme is treacherous. A gradational table can show wages rising across the whole distribution for decades while the relational structure underneath, who owns, who must sell, who appropriates, remains untouched. A headline announcing that the working class has shrunk because fewer people hold manual jobs has usually measured a gradational shift, occupational composition, and reported it as a relational change. The two claims differ entirely, and only one of them touches Marx's category.

The practical test is simple to state and hard to keep. When a writer calls a group a class, ask whether the claim survives rephrasing in ownership terms. Surgeons form a gradational elite by income and training. In relational terms most surgeons are sellers of labour power, employed by hospitals or health systems, owning no productive property beyond perhaps a share portfolio whose returns are a claim on surplus produced elsewhere. That verdict surprises people raised on gradational usage, which is precisely its value: it exposes which scheme is in play. Conversely, the owner of a modest workshop employing six people sits low on most gradational scales beneath senior executives and consultants, yet occupies the capitalist position in the relational scheme, buying labour power and appropriating the surplus it produces. Marx's map and the prestige map are both coherent. They are maps of different things, and the unfinished chapter rule asks only that a writer say which map is being used and not borrow authority from one to decorate the other.

### How do relational and gradational schemes differ in practice?

A gradational view ranks people on a scale of income, status, or occupation, so classes are layers with arbitrary boundaries. A relational view defines each class by its connection to others through ownership of productive property, so each position exists only because its opposite exists too.

Official statistics overwhelmingly speak the gradational language, for respectable reasons. States need categories that can be counted from census forms and tax records, and relations to productive property are awkward to count, since ownership hides behind companies, trusts, pensions, and spouses, while a wage relation hides behind contracts labelled self-employment. Scholars who need numbers therefore build proxies, and the proxies quietly become the concept in public debate. Part of the service a guide like this one can perform is custodial: keeping the relational definition visible underneath its proxies, so that when a dataset says class, a reader knows to ask which of the two conceptions the dataset could possibly have measured.

## The abstract model: three great classes, not two

Return to the fragment with the criterion in hand and its opening move looks different. Marx names three great classes, not two: wage-labourers, capitalists, and landowners, corresponding to the three revenue forms wages, profit, and ground rent. The triad is not a concession or an afterthought. It follows from the structure of the third volume, which spends its final parts tracing how surplus value, produced as a single pool, is divided among industrial profit, commercial profit, interest, and ground rent, each portion sustaining a distinct position. Landowners are not capitalists in this scheme. They own a condition of production, land, that they do not themselves operate, and they draw rent by permitting capital to use it. Their interest can collide with industrial capital's interest, over food prices, over land values, over taxation, and nineteenth-century politics, from the Corn Law struggles onward, is unintelligible if the two are merged into one undifferentiated owning class.

The three-class opening also disciplines a lazy habit in summaries of Marx: treating feudal leftovers as the only reason landowners appear. In the fragment they appear inside modern society based on the capitalist mode of production, as one of its constitutive positions, because ground rent is one of the forms in which surplus value is distributed under capitalism itself. Urban landlords, mineral owners, and agricultural proprietors all occupy versions of the position. Later Marxist analysis often folded landowners into a broader bloc of property owners for political purposes, and sometimes had good strategic reasons for doing so, but the fold is a later editorial decision, not Marx's text. That rule applies here with special force: the one classificatory statement Marx made in his systematic work names three classes, and any presentation of his theory that names two without comment has silently replaced his text with the Manifesto's polemic.

Beneath the three positions sits the deeper two-term relation, and keeping the levels distinct dissolves the apparent contradiction. At the level of the production relation itself, capital confronts wage labour: surplus value arises from that meeting and no other. At the level of distribution, the surplus is shared out among several claimant positions, which is where landowners and financiers enter. At the level of a concrete society, the positions multiply further into fractions, strata, and transitional groups, as the historical writings show. Two, three, and seven are all numbers that appear in Marx, each correct at its own level of abstraction. The error is never the number. The error is quoting a number from one level as though it answered a question posed at another.

## The Manifesto and the two-camp simplification

The famous two-class picture does exist in Marx, and its home is the Communist Manifesto of eighteen forty-eight, where society is described as splitting more and more into two great hostile camps, bourgeoisie and proletariat, directly facing each other. Three cautions govern the use of that passage. First, the Manifesto is a political pamphlet written for a small league of revolutionaries, a genre in which simplification is a weapon, and its authors knew the difference between a mobilising text and a scientific one. Second, it predates the mature economics by two decades: when it was written, Marx had not yet worked out the theory of surplus value, the analysis of rent, or the distribution scheme of the third volume, and the class language of eighteen forty-eight is correspondingly blunt. Third, even the Manifesto itself refuses to stay at two for long. Its first section names freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild master and journeyman as the opposed pairs of earlier epochs, sketches intermediate strata in the present, and predicts the simplification as a tendency of capitalist development rather than reporting it as an accomplished fact. The two camps are a direction of travel in that text, not a census.

Why, then, did the simplification conquer the world's picture of Marx? Partly because the Manifesto conquered the world: it was translated, reprinted, and memorised while the third volume of Capital, assembled posthumously, was read by specialists. Partly because movements need banners, and two camps fit a banner as seven fractions never will. And partly because the tendency claim contains a real insight that experience kept half confirming. Capitalist development does dissolve many intermediate positions, does proletarianise ruined craftsmen and peasants, does concentrate ownership; the error lies only in mistaking a tendency operating against counter-tendencies for a finished state. Marx himself charted the counter-tendencies, the growth of middle strata of managers, technicians, and clerks, in the same third volume whose last chapter names three classes. A reader armed with the unfinished chapter rule will treat the two-camp passage as what it is, a polemical compression of a tendency, written early, and will refuse to let it overrule the analytical texts.

The companion treatment of the pamphlet itself, its writing, structure, and afterlife, sits in the [Communist Manifesto readers guide](/2011/10/15/communist-manifesto-readers-guide/), and students meeting the two-camp language there should pair it with this pillar before quoting either. The pairing matters because examination questions love the gap: quote the Manifesto's simplification, then ask whether it matches Marx's own analyses, and the candidates who have only memorised the pamphlet discover, mid answer, that they have been set up.

### Why does the count of Marx's classes keep changing between his books?

Both numbers appear, at different levels. The Manifesto's two camps compress a tendency into a polemic. The systematic work names three great classes, wage-labourers, capitalists, and landowners, and the historical writings deploy six or seven positions, fractions included, as each concrete analysis requires.

Holding the levels apart also protects against a symmetrical blunder from the theory's critics. A critic who refutes the two-class census, pointing at the obvious multiplicity of modern occupations, has refuted the pamphlet's rhetoric and left the relational criterion untouched, since the criterion never predicted that only two occupational groups would exist. It predicts that positions in production stand in definite relations, that those relations generate opposed interests over the surplus, and that political conflict will repeatedly, though not mechanically, organise itself around them. Refuting that requires different ammunition, and the strongest available kinds are examined later in this guide, at the length they deserve.


## The historical writings: class analysis at street level

Marx's reputation as a class analyst was made not by Capital but by his political journalism on France, three works written in the heat of the revolutions and counter-revolutions of the middle nineteenth century: The Class Struggles in France, covering eighteen forty-eight to eighteen fifty; The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, on the coup that destroyed the French republic; and The Civil War in France, on the Paris Commune of eighteen seventy-one. Read together, they contain the most intricate class map Marx ever drew, and no reader who has absorbed them can again describe his theory as a two-box diagram. France in these pages contains a finance aristocracy of bankers, railway kings, and speculators who batten on state debt; an industrial bourgeoisie that wants order, markets, and a voice in government; a petty bourgeoisie of shopkeepers and small masters, radical in phrase and terrified in property; a peasantry forming the largest mass of the population, holding small plots under crushing mortgages; a proletariat concentrated in Paris and the industrial towns; and beneath and beside these, the lumpenproletariat, the stratum of the dispossessed and demoralised that Louis Bonaparte recruited into his Society of December the Tenth and used as street muscle for reaction.

The analytical point of the crowded map is not decoration. Each fraction is introduced because it explains an event that the simpler scheme cannot. The finance aristocracy explains why the monarchy of Louis Philippe served bankers while industrialists chafed in opposition. The conflict between fractions of the bourgeoisie explains the republic's strange career, in which the possessing classes repeatedly surrendered political power to an adventurer rather than risk arming the classes below them. The peasantry explains the coup's social base, the millions of smallholders whose votes and acquiescence made Bonaparte possible. The petty bourgeoisie explains the revolution's rhetoric, democratic and socialist in vocabulary, and its collapse, since a stratum that owns a little property will march against the great owners until the moment workers demand the abolition of property as such, at which point it flees to order. Class analysis in these texts is an explanatory instrument applied to events, tested against them, and refined when events surprise it. That is a very different activity from reciting a definition, and it is the strongest internal evidence that Marx's criterion, whatever its unfinished formal state, did real work in his hands.

The vocabulary of fractions deserves emphasis because later debate depends on it. A fraction is a subdivision of a class position distinguished by a different relation to the surplus or a different place in the circuit of capital: industrial, commercial, and financial capitalists all occupy the capitalist position, yet their revenues arrive as profit of enterprise, commercial profit, and interest, and their political appetites diverge accordingly. A stratum, in looser usage, names a group marked off by income, status, or function without a distinct position of its own, such as the stratum of senior managers examined below. Marx moves between class, fraction, and stratum without a terminological police force, another consequence of the missing chapter, and his successors' attempts to legislate the terms have generated more heat than the underlying distinctions warrant. What matters analytically is the question each label answers: does this group occupy a distinct position in production, a distinct share in distribution, or merely a distinct style of life? The map stays honest as long as the question is asked.

### How crowded is the class map of the French writings?

Beyond bourgeoisie and proletariat, the French writings deploy a finance aristocracy, an industrial bourgeoisie, a petty bourgeoisie of small owners, a vast smallholding peasantry, and a lumpenproletariat outside stable production, each fraction introduced because events require it to explain alliances, votes, and defeats.

Two further features of the historical writings anchor major themes of this cluster. The first is the treatment of the state. In the Eighteenth Brumaire the French state appears not as a simple instrument in the hands of a united ruling class but as an enormous bureaucratic and military machine with weight of its own, capable, under Bonaparte, of rising above a divided bourgeoisie and ruling in its long-term interest precisely by excluding it from direct power. That analysis plants the seed of every later Marxist debate about the relative autonomy of the state. The second feature is the Commune. In The Civil War in France, Marx hailed the Parisian workers' government as the political form at last discovered for working-class emancipation, and the text supplies the tradition's central case of a class moving from position to power, with all the improvisation and catastrophe that movement involved. Both themes presuppose the class map of the French writings; neither makes sense inside a two-box scheme.

## The peasantry and the sack of potatoes

One passage from the Eighteenth Brumaire has shaped class theory more than any definition Marx failed to finish. Writing about the French smallholding peasants, he observed that insofar as millions of families live under conditions that separate their mode of life and interests from those of other classes, they form a class; insofar as the bond between them is merely local, and the identity of their interests produces no community, no national organisation, and no political voice of their own, they do not form a class. He compared their aggregation to potatoes in a sack constituting a sack of potatoes: a mass formed by simple addition, each unit alike and unconnected. The famous sentence about such groups, that they cannot represent themselves and must be represented, supplied the epitaph for Bonapartism's rural base and one of the most quoted fragments in all social theory, kept short here as quotation discipline requires: "a sack of potatoes" is the image, and it has never been bettered as a picture of numerical weight without organisation.

Inside that passage sits the distinction later generations formalised as class in itself and class for itself. A class in itself exists as a shared position: the peasants really did occupy the same relation to land, mortgage, tax, and market, whether or not any peasant knew it. A class for itself exists when occupants of a position recognise their shared interest, organise around it, and act in politics as a collective force. The peasants of the Brumaire pass the first test and fail the second, blocked by the very structure of smallholding production, which isolates families on scattered plots, makes each nearly self-sufficient, and starves the communications from which organisation grows. Their representative, Bonaparte, could therefore present himself as their champion while ruling over them, representation substituting for self-activity. The distinction converts class from a label into a two-stage question, position and then consciousness and organisation, and the second stage is the subject of the companion article on [class consciousness explained](/2011/09/15/class-consciousness-explained/), where the subjective dimension gets the extended treatment it needs.

The peasant case also breaks a naive assumption baked into popular versions of the theory: that the largest class in a society will dominate its politics. The French peasantry outnumbered every other group combined and yet functioned as the ballast of a regime headed by an impostor, precisely because numbers without connection are weight without leverage. Marx's analysis honours demography and then subordinates it to organisation, communications, and the material structure of production. Later analysts of rural politics, of dispersed gig workers, and of every fragmented workforce have reached for the sack of potatoes ever since, which is some evidence that a concept embedded in a failed definition can still earn its keep across a century and a half of changed circumstances.

## Landowners, rent, and the position that refuses to vanish

Because the two-camp slogan has no room for them, landowners are the most frequently dropped class in summaries of Marx, and restoring them is a useful test of whether a summary has read the third volume at all. In Marx's scheme the landowner owns a monopolised condition of production. Land is not produced by labour, yet its ownership lets its holder charge capital a toll, ground rent, for access to soil, sites, mines, and waters. Rent in this analysis is a portion of surplus value, diverted to the landowner because capital must bargain for the ground on which production stands, and its forms, differential rent from superior fertility or location and the further forms Marx develops in the rent chapters, occupy some of the most intricate pages of the third volume. The landowner therefore stands inside the capitalist distribution system while standing outside the capital and labour relation that generates the surplus. Parasitic is a word polemicists reach for; Marx's analytical point is cooler, that a whole class can live, generation after generation, from a claim on value it does not produce, secured by nothing more than legal title to a gift of nature.

History kept supplying the position with new costumes after Marx's death. Urbanisation turned ground rent into the economics of city land and housing, where the toll falls on workers' wages and firms' costs alike. Mineral and energy wealth made rent the organising fact of entire national economies, whose rulers distribute oil income rather than taxing production. Intellectual property, though not land, behaves in recognisably rent-like ways, a legal monopoly over a condition of production or circulation, charged for access. Analysts who fold all owners into one bourgeoisie cannot see these divergences clearly, because the rentier's interest in scarcity and high asset values often opposes the industrial capitalist's interest in cheap inputs and expanding markets. The three-class fragment saw the divergence coming. Dropping landowners was a simplification made by the tradition, not by the text, and the unfinished chapter rule, which privileges the criterion and the text over the tradition's habits, instructs the reader to put them back.

### Why do summaries of Marx keep dropping the landowners?

As one of the three great classes of the unfinished chapter, distinct from capitalists. Landowners own a condition of production they do not operate, and they draw ground rent, a portion of surplus value, in return for permitting capital access to land.

A further subtlety completes the picture. Marx expected capitalist development to subordinate landed property progressively, turning land into a traded asset and rent into a deduction capital grudgingly pays, and in agriculture that expectation largely held. What he did not foresee at full scale was finance's eventual dominance over both industrial capital and land, a development that reorganises the fractions without abolishing the underlying positions. The map's durability lies in its questions rather than its nineteenth-century dramatis personae: who owns the conditions of production, who works them, who appropriates the surplus, and in which of its divided forms, wages excluded, does each claimant's revenue arrive?


## Class struggle without caricature

No phrase in the tradition is more quoted or more flattened than class struggle. The Manifesto opens its first section by declaring the history of all hitherto existing society to be the history of class struggles, a claim Engels later footnoted to restrict to written history. Flattened versions picture struggle as personal hostility: workers hating employers, employers despising workers, politics as a brawl between two crowds. Marx's structural meaning is different and more demanding. Struggle names the built-in opposition of interests between positions. Because the working day divides into paid and unpaid portions, its length and intensity are contested by definition, whatever the personal feelings of the parties; because wages and profits both draw on newly produced value, their shares are inversely linked, whatever courtesies pass across the negotiating table. Two exceptionally civilised negotiators, each genuinely fond of the other, still enact the struggle when one argues for a longer day or a faster line and the other resists. Antagonism in this theory is a property of the relation, not of temperaments.

That structural reading explains the range of forms struggle takes across Marx's writing. At its most visible it is strikes, insurrections, and revolutions, the Commune above all. At its most routine it is factory inspection law, the political fight over the length of the working day that fills the tenth chapter of the first volume of Capital, where Marx treats statute as frozen struggle, the outcome of a protracted civil war between classes conducted through parliament and the courts. Trade unions, parties, newspapers, and legal campaigns are all, in this vocabulary, organs of struggle, and so, on the other side, are employers' associations, lockouts, blacklists, and the quiet discipline of unemployment, which keeps the sellers of labour power aware of their replaceability. Struggle also operates through the state, whose rules on combination, migration, welfare, and property tilt the terrain on which the two positions meet. To call class struggle the motor of history, as the tradition does, is shorthand for claiming that these structured conflicts, rather than ideas alone or great persons alone, drive the great transformations of social form. The claim is contestable, and historians contest it, but it should be contested in its strong form, not against a cartoon of shouting crowds.

### What does it mean to call class struggle the motor of history?

Marx's claim, from the Manifesto onward, is that conflict between classes over the surplus and the conditions of production drives the great transformations of society. It names a structural antagonism of positions, not personal hatred between employers and employees.

Two disciplines keep the concept honest. The first is that opposed interests do not dictate single outcomes. The same structure of conflict produced the factory acts in one country, revolution in another, and long quiescence in a third, because organisation, law, religion, nationalism, and state form mediate between position and event. Marx's French writings are masterclasses in exactly those mediations, which is why they remain the best antidote to mechanical readings of his own slogans. The second discipline is symmetry: class struggle in the theory is waged from above as energetically as from below. The enclosures that created a propertyless class, the statutes that criminalised combination, and the managed scarcity of labour in tight markets are all moves in the same long contest. A guide that presents struggle as something only subordinate classes do has quietly abandoned the theory it claims to explain.

## Classes before and beyond capitalism

A theory of class built around wage labour risks appearing to start history in the factory age, and part of the criterion's strength is that it travels. In every mode of production Marx distinguishes, classes are sorted by relation to that mode's means of production and by the specific form in which surplus labour is extracted. Under slavery, the master owns the producer's person as well as the instruments, and surplus extraction is direct and visible: the slave's whole labour belongs to another, and the division between necessary and surplus time is hidden only by being total. Under feudalism, the serf owns or possesses tools and a plot, works part of the week on the lord's demesne or pays dues in kind or money, and the division of the week makes the surplus portion stand out in daylight, which is why Marx treats labour rent as the most transparent form of exploitation. Under capitalism the extraction becomes formally invisible, folded into the wage, requiring the whole apparatus of the value theory to expose. Same criterion, three mechanisms of extraction, three class structures: slave owners and slaves, lords and serfs, capitalists and wage-labourers, with priests, officials, and intermediate strata arranged around each pair according to their real relation to production and its surplus.

Estates and castes mark the boundary of the concept, and Marx's usage respects it. An estate is a legally defined rank, with rights and duties fixed by law and birth, as in the orders of feudal Europe. A caste is a hereditary, ritually sanctioned division, enforced by rules of marriage, occupation, and purity. Classes, by contrast, are constituted by economic position and are in principle open in the grim sense that ruin and enrichment move individuals between them without any change in law or ritual. The categories can overlap in real societies, and caste in India posed a famous analytical challenge to Marxist historians, since a division that is at once ritual and occupational refuses to collapse into either term. The honest treatment acknowledges the friction: relation to production explains much about caste's material base and little about its religious durability, and a class analysis that claims otherwise overreaches. Marx himself wrote on India with more confidence than his evidence warranted, and the later tradition has had to correct him in detail while retaining his question, which is the pattern this whole cluster repeats.

Transitional and dependent positions fill the spaces the great pairs leave. The peasant who owns a plot but must labour for others in the off season, the artisan being undercut by factory goods, the domestic servant paid from revenue rather than capital, the colonial populations subjected to forms of extraction that mix slavery, tribute, and wages: Marx's historical writing is populated by such figures, and his analyses treat them as real positions with real political consequences, not as noise around a pure model. The model supplies the coordinates. The concrete analysis plots the actual inhabitants, most of whom live at some angle to the axes.

## The objection at full strength: a theory built on sand

Every serious reader eventually meets the objection, and this guide states it in its strongest form before answering. The objection runs as follows. Class is not one concept among many in Marx's system; it is the load-bearing category. The theory of exploitation needs it, the theory of the state needs it, the philosophy of history is written in its terms, and the political project addresses itself to a class as its chosen agent. Yet the founder never defined it. The single chapter devoted to the task stops after a page and a half, mid difficulty, and the difficulty it stops on is not trivial: it is the discovery that his own provisional criterion, identity of revenue source, fragments classes indefinitely. A system whose central term is undefined, and whose one attempt at definition collapses on inspection, is not a theory but a vocabulary, a set of evocative labels, bourgeois and proletarian, that borrow the prestige of science while doing the work of rhetoric. The forty years of consistent usage prove nothing against this charge. Astrologers used their categories consistently for centuries. Consistency shows habit, not truth, and a criterion applied everywhere can be a prejudice applied everywhere. Nor can the successors rescue the founder: the reconstructions disagree with each other fundamentally, over whether managers, professionals, and the self-employed are inside or outside the working class, over whether class is binary or graduated, over whether it survives the spread of shareholding and pensions, and a concept that can be reconstructed in incompatible ways by competent scholars is functioning as an empty vessel into which each generation pours its politics. The conclusion pressed is severe. Marxism does not have a theory of class. It has a promissory note, issued in eighteen forty-eight, never redeemed, and the unfinished chapter is the documentary proof.

That objection deserves its force. It correctly identifies that the missing definition is not a bibliographic curiosity but a structural feature of the tradition, visible in the endless boundary disputes that no appeal to the texts can settle, because the texts break off exactly where settlement would begin. It also correctly refuses the devotional reply that Marx must have had the definition in mind, since arguments are judged by what they contain, not by what their authors are presumed to have thought in private.

The reply begins with a distinction between a definition and a criterion. A definition states necessary and sufficient conditions in a closed form. A criterion supplies a test that sorts cases, admits borderline disputes, and connects to a body of theory that gives the sorting its point. Many working concepts in successful sciences are criteria before they are definitions, and some remain criteria permanently: biology operated with a fertile, contested notion of species through its greatest century of progress, and still argues the boundary. Marx's criterion, relation to the means of production, together with the mechanism of surplus extraction that makes the relation matter, sorts the central cases unambiguously, explains why the sorted positions have opposed interests, and generates the concrete analyses whose explanatory power even hostile historians concede in the French writings. The fragment's unfinished state blocks final settlement of the borderlands, supervisors, professionals, small owners, and the reply concedes that blockage as permanent and real. What it denies is the inference from an unsettled border to an empty centre. Maps are not useless because coastlines are fractal.

The second strand of reply turns the objection's own evidence. The reconstructions disagree, yes, but they disagree about a shared object, within a shared criterion, and their disagreements are instructive rather than chaotic: nearly all of them are attempts to handle positions Marx himself flagged as seams, the manager between owner and worker, the small owner between capital and labour, the official paid from revenue. A vessel into which anything can be poured would not generate such specific, recurring, well-defined problems. It generates, instead, this pattern: a stable core, a known set of hard cases, and structured argument about the hard cases. That is the normal condition of a living research programme, and the rule exists precisely to keep the tradition honest inside it, forcing every writer to present reconstructions as reconstructions, with the fragment's limits stated rather than concealed by false confidence.

### Why does one category carry the weight of the whole system?

Because relation to the means of production determines who must work, who appropriates the surplus, and whose interests conflict by structure rather than by opinion, class organises Marx's economics, his theory of the state, and his account of historical change around one axis.

A final concession completes the reply, and it should not be softened. The gap between position and politics, between a class existing as a location in production and a class acting as a historical force, is the standing problem of Marxist class analysis, acknowledged inside the tradition from the sack of potatoes onward, and no amount of definitional repair closes it, because it is not a definitional gap. It is the distance between a structure and the consciousness, organisation, culture, and circumstance through which structures become events. The next article in this cluster takes up that distance directly, and readers who keep the two problems separate, the unfinished definition and the unfinished journey from position to action, will find the whole debate clearer than partisans on either side usually leave it.

## Contradictory locations and the successors' repairs

The repair industry began early and produced one indispensable tool: the notion of contradictory class locations, developed most systematically by the sociologist Erik Olin Wright in the later twentieth century. Wright began from the embarrassing cases. Managers command labour like capitalists yet sell their labour power like workers. Supervisors exercise authority they do not own. Highly credentialed professionals trade on scarce skills that function almost like assets, bargaining from strength no ordinary wage-labourer commands. Small employers work alongside their few employees while appropriating their surplus. Forcing each case into one box or the other destroyed information, so Wright placed such positions between the great poles, sharing features of both, contradictory locations inside the class structure whose occupants could be expected, on the theory, to waver politically in patterned ways. His later work rebuilt the scheme around exploitation more rigorously still, distinguishing assets in means of production, in organisation, and in skills, and testing the result against survey data on income and attitudes, an unusual marriage of Marxist categories with empirical sociology.

Other repairs used different instruments. Nicos Poulantzas, writing from a structuralist reading of Marx, redrew the boundary of the working class to exclude large strata of salaried employees, grouping commercial, supervisory, and service workers into a new petty bourgeoisie defined by political and ideological as well as economic criteria, a move that shrank the proletariat drastically and provoked ferocious debate. Harry Braverman, in his study of labour and monopoly capital, moved in the opposite direction, charting the degradation and expansion of clerical and service labour as capital rationalised offices and shops, and treating the growing salaried strata as part of a transformed working class. The Webers and their heirs offered a rival framework rather than a repair, sorting society by class, status, and party as independent dimensions, and the systematic comparison belongs to the later article on [Marx vs Weber on class](/2011/12/15/marx-vs-weber-on-class/). What unifies the Marxist repairs, beneath their quarrels, is fidelity to the fragment's method: all of them keep relation to production as the sorting principle and fight over its application to positions Marx saw only in embryo. None of them can cite a finished definition against the others. That rule predicts exactly this landscape, a stable criterion, a flowering of incompatible completions, and no court of final appeal, which is either the tradition's disgrace or its normal science, depending on the standards a reader brings.

The middle positions get a full hearing in the companion article on the [petty bourgeoisie and middle class](/2011/12/01/petty-bourgeoisie-and-middle-class/), where the old petty bourgeoisie of shopkeepers and artisans meets the newer salaried strata and the argument between those who see one dissolving and those who see the other swelling is staged in detail. This pillar confines itself to the moral that matters here: the existence of hard intermediate cases refutes only the slogan version of the theory. The criterion version expects intermediates, locates them, and explains why their politics oscillate, which turns the critic's favourite counterexamples into the theory's own subject matter.

## The misreadings that survive every correction

Five errors recur so reliably that a reader can use them as a checklist against textbooks, broadcasts, and examination answers alike. The first is the strict two-class census, already dismantled above: Marx predicted a tendency toward polarisation while naming three great classes in his systematic work and deploying more in his histories. The second error defines class by income. Income is a flow that a position helps determine, not the position itself, and identical incomes conceal opposed relations: a senior executive and a successful small contractor may earn the same sum while one sells labour power at a magnificent price and the other buys it at a modest one. Policies, loyalties, and risks differ accordingly, which the income definition cannot see. The third error defines class by manual work, so that the call-centre operator, the warehouse picker managed by algorithm, and the care worker are promoted out of the working class in commentary because their labour wears no overalls, while a self-employed plumber with two apprentices is demoted into it because his does. Marx's criterion sorts by ownership and the sale of labour power, and on that test the service proletariat is vast and the artisan employer is a small capitalist, whatever the costumes suggest. The running argument over these cases animates the later article asking [does the working class still exist](/2011/11/01/does-the-working-class-still-exist/), where the empirical evidence about occupational change receives its full airing.

The fourth error treats the Manifesto as the mature theory, dating the system to a pamphlet written before its author had produced the analysis of value, rent, or the circuits of capital. The fifth error shrinks class struggle into personal conflict, quarrels between grasping bosses and resentful workers, and then dismisses the theory as a psychology of resentment. The structural reading, opposed interests built into positions, survives the discovery that many employers are courteous and many workers conservative, since the theory predicted interests, not feelings, and repeatedly warns, through the peasant case, that interests without organisation and consciousness may never become action at all. Each error is a way of flattening a levelled theory into a single plane. Each is also, not coincidentally, the version of the theory easiest to refute, which is why polemic keeps them in circulation long after scholarship has filed them.

A sixth error belongs to sympathetic writers, and honesty requires naming it: treating every inequality as class. Gender, race, caste, citizenship, and region all distribute advantage and suffering through mechanisms that interact with class without reducing to it, and Marxist analysis has repeatedly damaged itself by annexation, announcing that some other axis of domination is class in disguise and thereby explaining neither. The criterion is a precision instrument with a defined jurisdiction, relation to the means of production and the extraction of surplus, and its credibility depends on declining cases outside that jurisdiction while studying how the jurisdictions intersect. The fragment's unfinished state tempts both enemies and friends to make the concept mean whatever the argument needs. The unfinished chapter rule is the refusal of that temptation.

## Keeping notes on an unfinished theory

Writers who return to this material across a course of study face a practical problem the theory itself creates: the evidence for any claim about Marx on class is scattered across a pamphlet, three volumes of economics, a shelf of historical journalism, and notebooks published long after his death, and the relations among those texts, which level of abstraction, which decade, which genre, matter to every judgement. Serious students solve the scattering with a single organised set of notes in which each text keeps its date, level, and exact class vocabulary beside the claims drawn from it, so that a Manifesto slogan can never again impersonate a third-volume analysis in their own work. The study notes at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are built for precisely that discipline, and the habit they support, one claim, one source, one level of abstraction recorded, is the everyday machinery by which the unfinished chapter rule stops being a motto and becomes a practice.

## Sorting real lives: the criterion at work

Abstract distinctions earn their keep only when they sort actual cases, and the cases that trouble students are predictable enough to work through in a row. A hospital consultant earns a salary that places her in the top gradational band, works with her hands and her judgement, and owns no share of the hospital. Relationally she sells labour power, at a price strengthened by scarce credentials, and belongs to the broad class of wage-labourers, distinguished within it by a skill asset that Wright's scheme treats as a contradictory advantage. A warehouse operative on a fraction of her income shares her relational position exactly. The criterion's indifference to their income gap is not a bug smuggled past the reader; it is the theory's central assertion, that the fundamental line in a capitalist society runs not between high and low earners but between those who must sell their capacity to work and those who live by buying it or by owning assets. A reader who finds that assertion false should reject it openly, at the level of the assertion, rather than quietly substituting an income definition and refuting the substitute.

The call-centre worker employed through an agency, the delivery rider engaged as a contractor, the cleaner whose employer is a facilities company interposed between her and the building she cleans: all three sell labour power under contractual costumes designed, in part, to obscure the sale. The criterion undresses the costumes by asking who controls the work process, who bears no real entrepreneurial risk, who cannot set prices or accumulate productive assets from the engagement. Where control and dependence are present and independence is fictitious, the relational verdict is wage labour whatever the contract calls it, a judgement labour courts applying quite different legal tests have repeatedly reached in parallel. The genuinely self-employed tradesperson sits on the other side of the line in a more interesting way: owning tools and a van, selling finished services rather than labour power by the hour, hiring an apprentice in busy seasons, she occupies the petty bourgeois position, and her politics, hostile to the tax office and to the union alike, follow its logic with a fidelity Marx would have recognised from the Paris of the Brumaire.

The small employer with six staff, the founder with six hundred, and the chief executive with sixty thousand trace one position at three scales, and the differences among them are differences of fraction and stratum rather than of class. The founder owns and directs. The chief executive directs without owning more than a sliver, yet is remunerated partly in equity precisely so that her revenue mimics the owner's form, a deliberate blurring the third volume's discussion of joint stock capital anticipates when it separates the function of management from the ownership of capital and predicts a class of mere money capitalists confronting a hierarchy of working functionaries. Where the executive's pay is a salary, however swollen, she is formally a seller of labour power; where her wealth is equity, she is an owner. Most real executives are both, in proportions that vary, and the analysis follows the proportions rather than the title on the door. Farmers reproduce the whole map in miniature: the agribusiness proprietor, the tenant farmer, the family smallholder, and the seasonal wage hand stand in four distinct relations to land and labour on a single road of fields, which is why rural politics so rarely moves as one bloc and why Marx's French analysis, written for a countryside of smallholders, still instructs analysts of countrysides that have since been transformed.

Pensioners and students test the scheme's temporal edges. A retired machinist lives from a pension that is, in substance, deferred wages and a share of returns on funds accumulated from wages; her class biography is proletarian and her present revenue is a claim mediated by finance. A student owns little and sells little, suspended in a passage between a class of origin and a class of destination that education is meant to determine. The criterion handles both by tense: class position in Marx is a place in a structure that lives occupy over time, entering, crossing, and leaving positions, and the snapshot fallacy, demanding that every person be sortable at every instant by present revenue alone, belongs to the gradational imagination. The fragment's unfinished state leaves the temporal rules unwritten, and lifecourse analysis in the successors' hands is another reconstruction, faithful when it keeps the criterion and honest when it says the criterion needed help.

### Does a high salary lift someone out of the working class?

Ownership decides, and the consultant and the operative prove it from opposite ends. Their incomes differ by an order of magnitude while their relation to the means of production is the same: neither owns the workplace, both sell labour power. Income measures how well a position is rewarded, not which position it is.

## What the tradition did with the fragment

The century after Marx's death turned the fragment into systems, and a rapid survey of the major completions shows the range of what fidelity has been taken to mean. Vladimir Lenin, writing for agitation and organisation, worked with a definition assembled in nineteen nineteen that lists large groups distinguished by their place in a historically determined system of production, their relation to the means of production, their role in the social organisation of labour, and the dimensions and mode of acquiring their share of social wealth. The list is recognisably built from Marx's criterion, widened with role and revenue clauses that Marx's fragment had raised and left unresolved, and it served a party that needed to identify allies and enemies across a peasant empire. Its breadth is its practicality and its weakness: a definition with four clauses can be aimed at almost any target, and the history of its use includes both brilliant concrete analysis and the classification of whole peasantries as enemies by administrative decree, a fate that illustrates why the unfinished chapter rule insists on stating criteria rather than wielding labels.

Georg Lukacs, in the essays collected as History and Class Consciousness in nineteen twenty-three, shifted the emphasis from position to standpoint, arguing that the proletariat's location in production gives it the possibility of grasping society as a totality, since the commodity form that veils every relation is, for the worker, the form of his own life. The argument re-philosophised class at the moment of its greatest political hopes, and its critics, friendly and hostile, have charged it with substituting an imputed consciousness, what the class ought to think given its position, for the empirical consciousness workers actually hold, thereby licensing a party to override the class in the class's own name. The sack of potatoes problem returns in sophisticated dress: who certifies the standpoint when the occupants decline it? Lukacs himself later repudiated parts of the book, and the question outlived his repudiation, resurfacing wherever a movement claims to speak for a class that has not authorised the speech. The fragment cannot settle the dispute, since it never reaches consciousness at all, and that silence is itself one of the tradition's formative facts.

The later twentieth century professionalised the question. Sociologists built class schemas for mobility research, most famously the Goldthorpe schema in Britain, which sorts occupations by employment relations, service contracts against labour contracts, in a scheme with a family resemblance to the relational idea and no debt to Marxist politics. Historians fought the linguistic turn's challenge, in which Gareth Stedman Jones argued that the Chartists' language constituted their politics rather than expressing a prior class interest, inverting the causal arrow between position and consciousness. Anthropologists carried class into villages and plantations where wage labour braided with kinship and tribute. Each discipline kept the criterion it could use and discarded the rest, and the cumulative result is a concept simultaneously everywhere and owned by no one, invoked in parliaments and seminar rooms with half a dozen incompatible meanings in play. It is offered, in part, as hygiene for exactly that condition: whatever else a writer's class means, a reader is owed the information of whether it means Marx's.

A word belongs here to the states that ruled in Marx's name, since their practice forms part of the concept's real history and this guide's neutrality rules require keeping theory and state record distinct rather than fused. Twentieth-century socialist states classified their populations officially, privileged and persecuted by class label and class origin, and in several cases generated new strata of administrators and managers whose relation to the means of production, formally socialised, actually rested on political control of access and allocation. Analysts inside those societies, Milovan Djilas most famously with his account of a new class of party bureaucrats, turned Marx's own criterion against the regimes claiming his authority, asking who effectively controlled the surplus and finding an answer the official ideology forbade. Whatever verdict a reader reaches on those states, the episode proves the criterion's independence from its founder's movement: a test that can be applied against the test-maker's heirs is a real test, not a catechism. It also completes the objection's strongest evidence and the reply's: the same fragment has served emancipation and administration, analysis and denunciation, and only the discipline of stated criteria and open reconstruction keeps its analytical use separable from its career as a badge.

## Why the unfinished definition still organises argument

Stand back from the detail and the pillar's claim can be stated plainly. Class survives as an organising category not because a definition protects it but because the question it asks refuses to go away: in any society, who owns the conditions under which everyone must work, who must therefore work for others, and who pockets the difference between what labour produces and what labour is paid? Societies that abolish the vocabulary do not abolish the relations, and the relations keep generating the phenomena the vocabulary was built to explain: opposed interests at the point of production, wealth that arrives as a claim on others' work, political conflicts that align, roughly and repeatedly, with the lines of ownership. Gradational statistics describe the resulting landscape with increasing precision while declining to name its engine. The Marxist criterion names the engine and leaves its own formal definition unfinished, a combination that exasperates tidy minds and sustains a research tradition. Both reactions are data. The exasperation records the genuine cost of the fragment. The tradition records the genuine yield of the criterion.

That is why this cluster places the pillar where it does, before the articles on consciousness, on the petty bourgeoisie and the middle strata, on whether the working class still exists in recognisable form, and on the Weberian rival. Each of those treatments presupposes the map drawn here and the warning stamped across it. Proceed with the criterion in one hand and the fragment in the other, and the rest of the debate becomes legible: a century and a half of brilliant, quarrelsome reconstruction, built on a page and a half that ends mid sentence.

## The cast of the concrete map, figure by figure

The concrete panel of the artifact table compresses figures who each deserve a slower portrait, because examinations and arguments alike turn on handling them individually. Begin with the bourgeoisie itself, which Marx never treats as a monolith. Its industrial fraction organises production directly and wants cheap food, cheap labour, open markets, and a state that keeps order without taxing accumulation. Its financial fraction lives from state debt, speculation, and the issue of shares and loans, and wants a state that borrows lavishly, guarantees its paper, and leaves the machinery of credit alone; under Louis Philippe this fraction ruled France, in Marx's account, as an aristocracy of money that treated the country as its estate. Its commercial fraction earns the merchant's share of surplus by realising value in sale. Fractions share the capitalist position and fight like rivals over policy, personnel, and the spoils of the state, and a class analysis that cannot register the fighting will misread every crisis in which the possessing class appears divided against itself, which is to say most of them.

The petty bourgeoisie is the map's most psychologically vivid figure: the shopkeeper, artisan, small master, and small farmer who own their means of production and work them, often with family labour and an apprentice or two. Marx's portrait is double-edged. This stratum is squeezed from both directions, ruined by large capital's competition and terrified of falling into the proletariat below, and its politics oscillate accordingly, radical against the great owners in the morning, desperate for order against the workers by nightfall. Its representatives in the French assemblies spoke, in Marx's acid analysis, a language far grander than the stratum's real weight, mistaking their phrases for the world's movement. The figure matters beyond its century because the position recurs wherever small property survives inside developed capitalism, and its modern descendants, the contractor, the franchisee, the platform driver classified as an independent business, inherit the same structural oscillation between resenting the giants above and fearing the collective below. The companion article on the petty bourgeoisie and the middle class develops the modern salaried strata at length; here the point is the position's internal logic, ownership enough to fear expropriation, labour enough to know exhaustion.

The lumpenproletariat is the map's most notorious figure and its most abused term. Marx uses it for the stratum of the dispossessed who live outside stable production: beggars, discharged soldiers and prisoners, swindlers, ragpickers, the whole sediment he lists in the Brumaire with a novelist's relish, organised by Bonaparte into a praetorian mob. His contempt for the stratum is real and has drawn justified criticism, since poverty's casualties deserve better than a slur, and since the category gathers people whose only shared trait is exclusion. The analytical core beneath the contempt is worth salvaging: a group with no position in production has no structural leverage and no collective interest generated by a shared workplace, and is therefore available for hire by any paymaster, a vulnerability Marx watched Bonaparte exploit. Used as an insult, the term is dead polemic. Used as the question whether a given population's exclusion from production deprives it of independent political capacity, it remains uncomfortably alive, and the honest analyst will state the question without the sneer.

The proletariat of the historical writings differs from its Manifesto silhouette in instructive ways. In the Class Struggles it is a class learning politics by defeat, rising in June eighteen forty-eight and being shot down in an insurrection whose isolation Marx anatomised without withdrawing his allegiance. In the Civil War it governs Paris, improvises institutions, replaces the standing army with the armed people, makes officials elective and revocable at working wages, and is drowned in blood in the final week of May. Marx's prose in the latter work is the most exalted he ever wrote, and it is addressed to a class acting, not a class diagrammed. Between those texts sits the gulf the whole cluster studies: position, consciousness, organisation, power, and the abyss that can open at every joint. Readers who meet only the abstraction often imagine the theory promises an automatic ascent. Marx's own history writing records something closer to a tragedy with structural causes, in which the class with the future in its hands repeatedly arrives at the decisive moment divided, unprepared, or betrayed by its representatives, exactly as the sack of potatoes warned.

Landowners in the historical writings appear less often as actors than as a weight: the legitimist grandees of the French Party of Order, fused with their Orleanist rivals not by love but by the shared need to defend property against the republic's democratic logic. Marx's account of that fusion, two dynasties' partisans united by the material identity of large property in its landed and moneyed forms, is a small masterpiece of the relational method, showing class interest dissolving a political quarrel that ideology alone would have kept burning. It also previews a recurring modern phenomenon, the closing of ranks among fractions of ownership whenever the property relation itself is challenged, which analysts who model fractions as permanent rivals chronically underpredict. The map's figures, in short, are drawn in motion, with alliances and betrayals tracked as carefully as positions, and that dynamism, not the static list, is what a student should carry away.

## The proletariat as universal class: the philosophical root

Beneath the economic criterion lies an earlier, philosophical stratum that explains why this class, of all classes, carries the theory's hopes. In the introduction to his critique of Hegel's philosophy of right, written in eighteen forty-three, the young Marx cast the proletariat as a class with radical chains, a sphere of society whose suffering is universal because its wrong is not a particular wrong but wrong in general, and which can free itself only by freeing every sphere. The argument is Hegelian in form and incendiary in content: previous rising classes freed themselves by installing a new particular domination, whereas the propertyless class owns nothing whose preservation requires domination, so its emancipation logically contains the emancipation of all. The mature economics replaced the philosophy's machinery with the analysis of surplus value, but the selection of the protagonist survived the replacement, and the theory's critics have always noticed the seam. Is the proletariat's centrality an economic finding or a philosophical inheritance wearing economic dress?

The honest answer, following the unfinished chapter rule, is that it is both, in layers a reader should distinguish. The economic layer is solid on its own terms: wage-labourers are the class whose labour produces the surplus, whose numbers grow with the system's expansion, whose concentration in production gives them a leverage no earlier subordinate class possessed. The philosophical layer adds a claim about universality that the economics alone does not prove, since producing the surplus does not by itself guarantee that a class will act for all rather than for itself alone, a gap the peasant case exposes brutally. Marx's wager was that position, concentration, and the stripping away of all property would school the proletariat into universality through its own struggles. The wager's historical record is the most argued-over inheritance in the entire tradition, claimed as vindicated by every labour movement that ever generalised its demands and as refuted by every nationalism that ever split a picket line. A guide can do no more, and should do no less, than mark exactly where the economics ends and the wager begins, because the tradition's worst confusions start where the two are blurred.

## Ideology: how positions acquire worldviews

Class in Marx is never only a position; positions secrete interpretations of the world, and the theory of ideology names that secretion. The ruling ideas of each age, as the German Ideology manuscript of the eighteen forties puts it, are the ideas of its ruling class, not because owners censor every sentence but because the class that controls material production also controls the means of mental production, the presses, pulpits, universities, and platforms through which a society explains itself. Workers, correspondingly, develop interpretations from their own experience, and the friction between lived experience and received explanation is one engine of class consciousness. Between the poles lies the contested terrain on which parties, churches, schools, and media fight, and on which the outcome, whether a position becomes a self-understanding or remains a statistical fact, is decided in practice.

Two errors about ideology mirror the errors about class itself. The crude version treats ideas as reflexes, stamped on minds by positions without remainder, and is refuted by every devout worker, every renegade aristocrat, and Marx himself, a bourgeois-educated lawyer's son theorising the proletariat. The sophisticated corrective, developed by Antonio Gramsci under the name of hegemony, treats the dominance of a class's worldview as a continual achievement, built through institutions and concessions, always incomplete, always contestable in the trenches of civil society. Gramsci's prison notebooks, written under fascism's censorship, asked why the western working classes had not followed the Russian script and answered with the density of the institutions standing between position and consciousness. That answer extends the sack of potatoes into the age of mass media: connection and representation are achievements, not givens, and the side that builds them wins the interval between structure and event. The companion article on class consciousness takes up the subjective side in full; this pillar simply registers that the class map is incomplete until the worldviews drawn on it, and the institutions that carry those worldviews, are part of the analysis.

## The Weberian shadow

No map of this territory is complete without the rival whose presence disciplines every comparison, Max Weber, whose systematic contrast with Marx is reserved for its own later article. Weber accepted class as an economic category and then pluralised the field: class names a shared market situation, the chances a person's assets and skills command in exchange; status names honour, prestige, and lifestyle, distributed by a logic of their own; party names organised power-seeking, cutting across both. The scheme's power lies in explaining what the single axis strains to explain, the prestige hierarchies inside one class, the closure of professions by credential and manners, the political formations that unite owners and workers of one nation against another. Its weakness, from the Marxist side, is that market situation describes the surface on which positions contend while declining to ask why the contenders arrived with such unequal assets, or how the surplus that funds the whole market is produced and appropriated. The two frameworks are best read as instruments aimed at different depths, gradational and multidimensional in Weber's hands, relational and production-centred in Marx's, and the student who can state each in its strong form, and locate the exact question on which they diverge, owns this topic. That question, finally, is whether inequality is fundamentally a distribution of chances among individuals or a relation between positions in production. Everything else is commentary.

## Class inside the architecture of Capital

The unfinished chapter sits at the end of the third volume, but class operates on every level of the work that precedes it, and tracing its appearances shows how much of the system the fragment was meant to crown. The first volume begins with commodities and money and arrives, in the chapter on the buying and selling of labour power, at the moment the two great positions meet in the market: the owner of money confronting the owner of labour power, formally free and equal, bound by a contract both are compelled by their positions to seek. The analysis then follows them into production, where the formal equality of exchange gives way to the real asymmetry of the working day, and through the struggles over its length, the machinery chapters, and the long account of primitive accumulation, where Marx narrates the violent historical process that created the two positions in England: enclosures separating producers from the land, statutes disciplining the separated into wage labour, colonial plunder feeding the accumulation that employed them. Class in the first volume is therefore both a structural pair and a historical product, made by identifiable acts over identifiable centuries, which is why the theory can never be a static taxonomy. The positions had a birthday, and what was made can in principle be unmade.

The second volume, following the circuits of capital through production and circulation, adds the commercial and money-dealing functions whose performers will complicate the class map, and the third volume completes the distributive picture: the single pool of surplus value divided among industrial profit, commercial profit, interest, and rent, each revenue sustaining a position, the fragment's three great classes among them. Read in that sequence, the missing definition looks less like an oversight and more like the final stone of an arch that Marx spent three volumes building toward, the point where the economic categories were to be gathered into the social groups that bear them. That the stone was never set is the fact this cluster of articles keeps returning to, because every later class analysis is, whether it knows it or not, an attempt to set it.

Engels's role deserves its own paragraph, since the fragment reaches readers only through his hands. Editing the third volume from manuscripts of very uneven finish, he assembled chapters, supplied transitions, and, at the Classes chapter, stopped where the manuscript stopped and said so. That honesty is a scholarly virtue with large consequences: a less scrupulous editor might have stitched the chapter into plausibility from Marx's other writings, and the tradition would then have argued over a definition its supposed author never wrote. The 1894 reader received the gap as a gap. The footnote Engels added to the Manifesto's opening line in the eighteen eighty-eight English edition, restricting the history of class struggles to written history in light of later knowledge of primitive communal societies, shows the same editorial conscience at work on the tradition's most famous class sentence, and it models the unfinished chapter rule in practice: state what the text says, state what later knowledge changes, and never confuse the two.

## Class formation: how a position becomes a people

Between the criterion and the historical actor stands the process historians call class formation, and the most influential account of it written inside the Marxist tradition, Edward Thompson's study of the making of the English working class, begins by refusing to treat class as a thing. Class, for Thompson, is a relationship and a historical phenomenon, something that happens when people, out of common experiences largely determined by the productive relations into which they are born or enter, come to feel and articulate an identity of interests against others whose interests oppose theirs. The definition keeps Marx's anchor, productive relations determine the experiences, while relocating class from a box in a diagram to an event in time. English artisans, weavers, and factory operatives were not a class in eighteen hundred merely because they sold labour power; they became one across three decades of Luddism, illegal unions, radical journalism, Owenite experiment, and Chartist mobilisation, forging traditions, institutions, and a language in which the position could recognise itself.

Thompson's account corrects two opposite reductions at once. Against the slogan version, it shows that position does not secrete consciousness automatically, like heat from a fire; the same objective location can coexist for generations with deference, religion, and localism, as the sack of potatoes taught. Against purely cultural accounts, it insists that the experiences from which consciousness is made are not free-floating: they are the experiences of exploitation at work, of the working day, of the overseer and the wage packet, and they recur with a pattern imposed by the relations of production. Class formation is therefore the bridge concept the unfinished chapter never supplied, naming the historical work, organisational, intellectual, and emotional, by which a class in itself becomes a class for itself, or fails to. Later historians applied the same lens to bourgeoisies, asking when merchants, manufacturers, and professionals acquired a shared identity and programme, and found the process slower, more regional, and more dependent on the state than the heroic version of bourgeois revolution suggests. The concept travels in both directions, which is the mark of a good one.

Formation also has its failures and reversals, and a complete guide should name them. Classes can decompose: the English handloom weavers were destroyed as a class position by the power loom, their skills devalued, their communities scattered, their politics absorbed or extinguished. Classes can be made by states as well as against them, through conscription, schooling, welfare categories, and housing policy that sort populations into lived identities. Classes can divide along lines of craft, skill, religion, and nation that the criterion does not predict and that organisers must overcome rather than deny. None of this refutes the criterion. It marks the distance, once again, between a map of positions and the history that plays out across the map, a distance Marx's French writings navigate intuitively and his systematic work never theorised. The successors who theorised it were completing the fragment by other means.

### What happens when one life straddles two class positions?

In lived reality, people straddle positions constantly: the nurse with a buy-to-let flat, the professor whose pension rides on equities, the tradesperson employing an apprentice while subcontracting to a large firm. The criterion sorts each strand of such a life separately, ownership here, sale of labour power there, and treats the mixture as a fact to be analysed rather than an embarrassment to be hidden.

The straddle cases matter politically because allegiance often follows the strand a person experiences as most threatened or most promising, which no structural map can read off in advance. A homeowner worker may defend property values against her wage interest; a indebted small employer may march with employees against the banks that hold his loans. Marx's French analyses are full of such cross-pressured figures, and his treatment of them is the tradition's best lesson in refusing to deduce behaviour from position mechanically while refusing, equally, to abandon position as the starting point of the analysis.

## Parties, representation, and the state

If classes do not act directly but through organisations, then political parties occupy a peculiar place in the theory: they claim to represent classes, and the claim is partly constitutive, since a party that successfully organises a scattered position helps bring the class for itself into being. Marx watched this machinery from inside, in the International Working Men's Association, whose inaugural address and rules he drafted, and whose internal struggles between trade unionists, Proudhonists, and Bakuninists taught him how contested the representation of a single class could be among people who all invoked its name. The Communist Manifesto had already assigned communists a strange non-party status, not a separate party opposed to other working-class parties but the most resolute section of them, distinguished by clarity about the movement's overall direction. That self-image, a vanguard defined by understanding rather than by separate interest, runs through the later tradition and its catastrophes, and it begins as an answer to a genuine problem the class map creates: if the proletariat is the majority class and its interest is universal emancipation, why does it need representation by a minority that claims to see further? Every answer the tradition has given, from educational to organisational to coercive, is an attempt to close the gap the sack of potatoes opened, between a class that exists and a class that can speak.

The state concentrates the same problem. The Manifesto's famous compression describes the executive of the modern state as a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie, and generations of polemic have treated the sentence as the theory of the state complete. Marx's own Bonaparte analysis complicates it beyond recognition: a state machine of hundreds of thousands of officials and soldiers, a parasitic body in his phrase, achieving real independence from the very class whose social dominance it secures, because that class was too divided and too frightened to rule directly. Later Marxists formalised the two poles as instrumentalism, the state as a tool wielded by class members, and structuralism, the state as a structure whose form secures class dominance regardless of who staffs it, and their celebrated debate of the nineteen seventies rehearsed, at a higher level of abstraction, the tension between Marx's pamphlet and Marx's history. The unfinished chapter rule dissolves part of the quarrel: a founder who never finished defining class was never going to finish a theory of the state that presupposes the definition, and the rival schools are reconstructions contending over a genuinely open inheritance.

Class and the state also meet in the quieter territory of law and policy. Factory legislation appears in Capital as the product of class struggle conducted through the state, a limit imposed on capital's appetite by organised workers using parliamentary leverage, against the resistance of the class that formally held power. Social insurance, public education, and health provision have all been read inside the tradition both as conquests of the working class and as stabilising reforms that reconcile workers to the relations that exploit them, and the evidence usually supports a compound verdict. The point for this pillar is narrower: none of these debates can even be stated without a prior account of which classes exist, what they want, and how their positions generate those wants, which is why the definition Marx left unfinished keeps taxing everyone who builds on his foundations.

## Measuring class without betraying the concept

Empirical researchers who take the relational criterion seriously face a measurement problem their gradational colleagues avoid. A survey can record income exactly; it cannot so easily record whether a respondent owns productive property, controls the labour of others, or sells labour power under compulsion of propertylessness, because modern ownership is intermediated. Shares sit inside pension funds whose beneficiaries exercise no control. Founders own firms while drawing salaries. Contractors sell labour while invoicing as businesses. Wright's empirical programme met the problem head-on by building survey instruments around the criterion's components: ownership of productive assets, authority over others at work, possession of scarce skills and credentials. Respondents were sorted into class locations by their answers, and the resulting maps of countries such as the United States and Sweden showed large contradictory strata between the poles, small classical proletariats by strict measures, and systematic relationships between location, income, and attitudes that neither a pure gradational scheme nor a two-box scheme reproduced. Whatever a reader concludes about Wright's conclusions, the attempt itself proved something the built-on-sand objection denies: the criterion is operationalisable, at the price of hard decisions openly declared.

The openly declared part is where measurement and honesty meet. Every operational scheme must decide whether supervisors count as controllers of labour or as labour controlled, whether credentials are assets or merely labour power's polish, whether a pension beneficiary owns capital in any sense that matters. Different decisions produce different class structures from the same society, and there is no appeal to Marx's text that settles them, because the text breaks off. The gradational tradition hides its equivalent decisions inside conventions so old they look like facts. The Marxist tradition, condemned to rebuild its instrument for each study, at least knows its instrument is built. A researcher following the unfinished chapter rule will publish the decisions with the results, invite rivals to rebuild differently, and treat the spread of outcomes across schemes as information about the concept's open texture rather than as a scandal.

Households complicate measurement in ways the nineteenth-century texts barely anticipate. Class position attaches to individuals in the criterion's logic, yet people live in units that pool income, property, and risk across positions: the executive married to a teacher, the apprentice living on parental property, the retired worker whose former position predicts little about present resources. Feminist critics inside and outside the tradition pressed this point hard in the nineteen seventies and eighties, arguing that sorting households by the occupation of a presumed male head rendered women's class position invisible and treated marriage as a class destiny. The debate improved the research practice permanently: individuals are now sorted individually, households analysed as sites where positions combine and sometimes collide. Marx's texts, focused on a working class in formation and a bourgeoisie of family firms, supply little direct guidance, and the gap is a genuine one, best handled by acknowledging that domestic labour, the unpaid work of reproducing labour power, sits largely outside the fragment's field of vision and has had to be theorised by successors working, once again, as reconstructors.

## Class and the other axes of domination

No honest modern treatment can stop at the concession, made earlier, that class does not exhaust inequality. The intersections are where the theory either matures or degenerates into slogan. Marx himself wrote about Ireland in terms that cut across the class map: English workers' hostility to Irish immigrants, he argued in a letter of eighteen seventy, was the secret of the English working class's impotence, since the antagonism split the class, enriched both nations' rulers, and was artificially sustained by the press and the pulpit. The analysis is striking because it grants a national and cultural division real causal force inside class politics without dissolving the class structure underneath: the Irish were disproportionately proletarian in England and doubly burdened in Ireland, and the division operated through wages, housing, and organisation. Race in the United States posed the same structure at larger scale, and the tradition's record there is mixed, containing both profound analyses of slavery as a labour system, from Marx's own Civil War journalism, which treated the struggle over slavery as the pivot of American class politics, and later evasions that subordinated racial domination to class in ways its victims had reason to resent.

Gender cuts deeper still, because the reproduction of labour power itself, the daily and generational work of feeding, caring, cleaning, and raising, is performed largely unpaid and largely by women, inside households that the wage relation presupposes and does not pay for. Engels attempted the connection in his work on the origin of the family, tying women's subordination to the rise of private property, and the argument's broad strokes, historicising the family, linking its forms to property and production, remain generative even where later scholarship has dismantled its anthropology. The durable point for class analysis is that the boundary of the working class does not run neatly around the waged: the unwaged labour that makes wage labour possible belongs inside any complete account of how the class lives and reproduces, and analyses that begin at the factory gate have started the story in the middle. A pillar article need not resolve these debates to respect them. It needs to mark the joints accurately, class explaining the production and appropriation of surplus, other axes explaining divisions and dominations that interact with that process, sometimes reinforcing it, sometimes splitting its actors, never reducible to decoration upon it.

Global scale redraws the map once more. The criterion sorts positions inside a world economy in which the owner of a garment brand, the contractor running a factory on another continent, and the machinist sewing the garments occupy three different relations to the same product's surplus, and in which entire national workforces can stand in quasi-proletarian relation to capitals headquartered elsewhere. Marx anticipated fragments of this in his writings on colonialism and world trade, though his judgements on British rule in India, at once an engine of destruction and an unconscious tool of transformation in his notorious phrase, remain among the most contested pages he wrote, admired for their materialism and condemned for their Eurocentric confidence. The unfinished chapter rule applies with undiminished force at world scale: no finished Marxist theory of global class exists, because no finished theory of class exists, and the analyst's obligation is the same, criterion stated, reconstructions labelled, hard cases faced rather than defined away.



## For students and exam candidates

The mark-carrying move across this whole cluster is the relational and gradational distinction, stated early and used visibly. When a question asks how Marx defines class, open with the criterion, relation to the means of production, then immediately show that you know its textual condition: the formal definition was begun in the final chapter of the third volume of Capital and breaks off unfinished, so the answer reconstructs from consistent usage. That pairing, criterion plus warning, separates strong scripts from slogan scripts in the first paragraph. Deploy the levels deliberately: two camps for the Manifesto's polemic, three great classes for the systematic fragment, the crowded map for the French writings, and name the level each time so the examiner watches you control the material rather than contradict yourself. Keep one hard intermediate case in reserve, a manager or a small employer, and use it wherever evaluation is requested, since the treatment of borderline positions is where this topic's judgement marks live. Never define class by income or by manual work in your own voice; if a question's wording does, correct it politely and proceed with the criterion.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The two-class misconception is best dismantled by juxtaposition rather than assertion: set the Manifesto's two-camp paragraph beside the opening of Chapter Fifty-Two and the class dramatis personae of the Eighteenth Brumaire in a single seminar, and let students discover that one author wrote all three. Follow with the sack of potatoes passage and ask the group to formulate the in-itself and for-itself distinction before naming it, since the peasant case teaches it more durably than any glossary. A productive exercise assigns each student one figure from the historical map, a finance aristocrat, a shopkeeper, a smallholder, a supervisor in a modern firm, and requires a defence of its placement using only the ownership criterion, with the class as a whole voting on the placements and the tutor exposing which arguments quietly smuggled in income or prestige. Close by confronting the built-on-sand objection directly and requiring students to argue both the charge and the criterion-versus-definition reply; the topic's intellectual honesty is itself the lesson.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the fragment is a methodological instruction: any operationalisation of class for Marxist purposes is a reconstruction and should be published as one, with the criterion stated, the seams named, and the treatment of managers, professionals, and the self-employed defended rather than assumed. Wright's contradictory locations and the Poulantzas boundary dispute remain the two poles between which empirical work must locate itself, and survey or archival findings should be reported with the scheme that produced them attached, since class statistics are scheme-relative in a way income statistics are not. For journalists, the discipline is simpler and stricter. Calling a group working class in a Marxist sense is a claim about ownership and the sale of labour power, not about accent, schooling, income, region, or the work of hands. Before using the label, check whether the group in question owns productive property, whether its members must sell their capacity to work, and whether the story's actual subject is a gradational fact, pay, housing costs, schooling, wearing Marxist vocabulary as costume. Where the subject is income or lifestyle, say income or lifestyle; the relational terms are reserved, by their own theory, for positions in production, and borrowing them for colour misleads readers about both the group and the theory.

## Before Marx: the word he inherited

Class arrived in Marx's hands with a history, and the history explains some of the concept's permanent tensions. The word descends from the Latin classis, the divisions into which the Roman census sorted citizens by property for taxation and military service, so that classification by wealth for the purposes of the state is inscribed in the term's ancestry. Early modern Europe preferred the language of ranks, orders, and estates, vocabularies of legal and honourable standing, and the modern economic sense of class crystallised only when the classical political economists, Adam Smith above all, began sorting society by revenue source into those who live by rent, those who live by profit, and those who live by wages. Marx's three great classes are Smith's triad, taken over almost intact, and the fragment's opening is best read as his attempt to rebuild the economists' common sense on his own foundations: the revenue sources Smith listed become, in the third volume, forms of surplus value, and the classes defined by them become positions in a mechanism of exploitation rather than entries in a fiscal register. The inheritance explains the fragment's odd double character, at once a break with political economy and a completion of it.

The French historians of the Restoration, Guizot and Thierry among them, had already made class struggle the key to modern history before Marx wrote a line on the subject, narrating the rise of the Third Estate against the aristocracy as the plot of a thousand years. Marx acknowledged the debt explicitly in a letter of eighteen fifty-two, listing as his own contributions not the discovery of classes or of their struggle, which bourgeois historians and economists had achieved before him, but the proof that classes are bound to particular phases of production, that their struggle leads to a transitional dictatorship of the proletariat, and that the destination is a classless society. The letter is the nearest thing to a self-assessment the tradition possesses, and it quietly confirms this guide's framing: Marx's originality in class theory was never the concept itself but what he welded to it, a mechanism of exploitation beneath it and a terminus beyond it. A definition of the concept alone, detached from the welding, may have struck even its author as the least original part of the enterprise, one more speculation about why the chapter could wait while the volumes on value, accumulation, and rent could not.

The destination deserves its sentence here because it colours every use of the category. Classes, for Marx, are not eternal furniture of society but products of a determinate stage, preceded by long eras of communal organisation without them and destined, in his expectation, to be superseded when productive property ceases to be privately held. The theory of class is thus written throughout in a historical tense that gradational schemes do not share: an income distribution has no built-in expiry date, while a class structure, in Marx's telling, carries the date of its own abolition inside its analysis. Critics regard the expectation as the theory's messianic residue. Defenders regard it as the theory's honesty about its own historical limits. The fragment, characteristically, says nothing about it at all, breaking off in the driest analytical territory, as if the definition refused to be hurried by the eschatology waiting outside the door.

## Crises, tendencies, and the changing shape of the map

A relational map is not a still photograph, because the relations themselves move under the pressure of accumulation, and Marx's crisis theory is unintelligible without class positions shifting inside it. Concentration and centralisation of capital, the growth of firms and the swallowing of the small by the large, steadily transfer population from the petty bourgeois position into the wage-labouring one, while simultaneously swelling a stratum of managers, engineers, and administrators inside the enlarged enterprises. Competition presses capitals to cheapen labour, labour organisation presses back, and the reserve army of unemployed, the subject of an earlier pillar in this series, disciplines the employed from beneath. The map's poles therefore grow and shrink and grow internal layers across the business cycle and across decades, and predictions drawn from it fail when they assume the proportions of one decade are the theory's prediction for all decades. Marx himself, watching the joint stock company and the cooperative factory, described forms in which the capitalist becomes superfluous as a functionary while ownership persists as pure claim, and treated them as transitional forms inside the old society, moments when the map redraws its own middle.

Technology redraws it further, in ways the contemporary debate about automation rehearses almost line for line from the machinery chapter. Each wave of mechanisation expels labour from one occupation and summons it in another, degrades one skill and premiums the next, and enlarges the relative surplus population whose existence the theory treats as both a product and a lever of accumulation. The classes persist through these redrawings because positions persist: someone still owns the automated plant, someone still tends it for a wage, someone still draws the rent of the land it stands on and the interest on the loans that built it. What changes is the staffing and the costumes. Analysts who announce the end of class with each technological wave have confused the costumes with the positions, and analysts who announce that nothing has changed have confused the positions with the costumes. The criterion permits, and this guide insists on, the double refusal.

Long-run tendencies in the distribution of wealth add a final layer. Where the return on accumulated assets outruns the growth of the whole economy, inherited ownership compounds across generations and a rentier stratum thickens at the map's owning pole, a dynamic recent economic history has documented with a mass of fiscal data and that sits comfortably with the fragment's three-class opening, landowners generalised into asset owners living from claims rather than from enterprise. Where growth is rapid and labour scarce, wages can rise for decades and gradational measures announce a universal middle class while the relational structure underneath, the division between those who live from assets and those who live from selling themselves by the month, persists intact beneath the prosperity. Both patterns have occurred inside living memory, sometimes in the same country in successive decades. A class analysis worth its name must be able to describe both without discarding its categories at each turn of the data, and the test of the criterion, applied with the fragment's humility, is that it can: the positions endure, their populations and fortunes churn, and the churn is the history the categories exist to organise.

## The notebooks: classes before capital

The manuscripts published in the twentieth century as the Grundrisse contain a celebrated section on forms preceding capitalist production, and it is the fullest statement Marx ever produced of how the class criterion operates across history. There he examines communal forms, the ancient city, and the feudal order as arrangements in which the individual's relation to the land and to the community precedes and conditions any relation to an employer, since capital and free wage labour do not yet exist. The free labourer himself appears in that analysis as a historical result: the dissolution of the older bonds, separation from the land, from the guild, from the instruments held as communal or feudal property, is the precondition for a person who owns nothing but labour power and confronts capital as its seller. Classes in the precapitalist chapters are therefore sorted by their mode of access to the conditions of production through the community or the lord, a criterion recognisably ancestral to the fragment's test yet adapted to worlds where the market in labour power is absent or marginal. The section matters for this pillar because it blocks an anachronistic reading of the criterion as a timeless checklist. Marx adjusts the test to each formation's actual relations, which is what a criterion, as distinct from a definition, is for.

The ethnological notebooks of his final years, in which Marx excerpted and annotated studies of communal and kin-based societies, extend the lesson into his last decade. They show the founder of class theory poring over societies organised without classes in his sense, without private ownership of productive land, and revising, in private, the unilinear confidence of his earlier sketches of history's stages. Editors and scholars who published those notebooks forced a permanent widening of the tradition's picture of its founder: the man who never finished defining class also never stopped complicating the world to which the definition would apply. There is a fitting symmetry in that. The fragment breaks off, and the notebooks sprawl on, and between the broken page and the sprawling notebooks sits the honest summary of Marx on class: a criterion of genuine power, a lifetime of application, and no finished system where the criterion should have been crowned.

## Alliances: the politics of the in-between

Because concrete societies contain more positions than the great poles, their politics is a politics of alliances, and Marx's historical writing doubles as the tradition's first manual of alliance analysis. The possessing classes of the French republic combined landed and moneyed property in the Party of Order to defend the social order while quarrelling over dynasty; the democratic petty bourgeoisie allied with the workers in the movement's rhetoric and deserted to order when property was threatened; Bonaparte built his regime by posing as the representative of the largest and least organised class, the peasantry, against all the organised ones. Each configuration follows from the map when the map is read for interests and capacities rather than recited as a roster. The general lesson is that no class in a concrete society, not even the largest, makes history alone: outcomes are decided by which fractions combine, which split, which are neutralised, and which find a representative who can speak a common language over their real divisions. Class analysis earns its keep in political explanation precisely by predicting the seams along which alliances will form and fracture, the tax question splitting smallholders from financiers, the food question splitting landowners from industrialists, the wages question splitting every employer bloc from every labour bloc.

The alliance lens also disciplines the theory's friends. Movements that imagine their class as a solitary protagonist, needing no allies and making no concessions, are refuted by Marx's own narratives before any opponent speaks, since his heroes, the Parisian workers of the Commune, fell in part through the isolation the peasantry's separate world imposed. Movements that imagine alliances as costless mergers of goodwill are refuted by the same pages, in which every partner pursues its position's interest and the alliance holds only while those interests run parallel. Between solitude and fusion lies the actual terrain: negotiated, temporary, interest-based combination among positions that remain distinct. A pillar on class can leave the strategy to others. It should not leave the structural fact, because readers who grasp it will never again be satisfied with accounts of politics as a duel between two camps, however stirring the duel sounds in a pamphlet written for another century's barricades.

## A reader's protocol for class claims

This pillar ends its analytical work with a protocol, because the series trades on usable method rather than on assent. When any writer, speaker, syllabus, or headline deploys the word class in Marx's vicinity, five questions sort the sound uses from the decorative ones. Which scheme is in play, relational or gradational, and does the writer know? Which level is being quoted, the pamphlet's two camps, the fragment's three classes, or a concrete analysis with its fractions, and is the level named? What happens to the claim's central cases, the manager, the small owner, the credentialed professional, the rentier, and are they handled or hidden? Is the claim about position, about consciousness, or about organised action, and does it keep the sack of potatoes between the first and the others? And is the definition being used attributed honestly, as Marx's criterion, as a named successor's reconstruction, or as the writer's own instrument, with no borrowed fragment dressed as a finished text? A claim that survives the five questions may still be wrong, but it is wrong in the open, where argument can reach it. A claim that fails them is not yet an argument at all, only the word class doing ceremonial work, and the unfinished chapter, that page and a half of magnificent incompleteness, stands as the permanent rebuke to ceremony.

## One industry under the lens

Abstract criteria convince slowly; a single industry, followed patiently, can teach the map faster than another chapter of theory. Take the railway industry of the nineteenth century, the sector Marx watched transform his own continent. At its owning pole stand the shareholders and bondholders of the railway companies, drawing dividends and interest from an enterprise most never visited, the joint stock form already separating their claim from any function. Beside them, in the landowner's position generalised, stand the owners of the ground the lines cross, whose wayleaves and station sites command rents from a scarcity the railway itself created. Inside the enterprise, a hierarchy of labour unfolds that the criterion sorts without hesitation and gradational instinct sorts very differently: the locomotive engineer, aristocrat of labour with a scarce and dangerous skill, the driver and fireman, the guard, the porter, the navvies who built the permanent way and vanished from the payrolls, the clerks in the booking offices whose clean cuffs marked them, in their own eyes, as a different order of being from the men on the footplate. All sell labour power. Their incomes, prestige, uniforms, and politics diverge so widely that a contemporary observer could be forgiven for seeing four classes where the criterion sees one class internally stratified by skill, scarcity, and function, confronting owners whose unity is guaranteed by the dividend list. The industry's history then supplies the test: when strikes came, they came along the relational seam, footplate and shed and office discovering common cause against the company in proportions no prestige theory predicted, while in quiet times the internal strata reasserted themselves in separate unions, separate chapels, separate pubs. Position explained the seam; stratification explained the quiet. A theory that keeps both levels earns the right to be called an analysis.

Run the same lens over a modern hospital and the pedagogical yield repeats. The trust that owns the buildings may be public, which moves the owning pole into the state and changes the politics without changing the structure of the working day. Private finance initiatives reintroduce the rentier in modern dress, drawing decades of payments from a building whose construction risk was fleeting. Consultants, nurses, porters, cleaners whose employer is an outsourced contractor, agency staff whose labour is sold twice over, managers who command rotas while answering for budgets they do not own: the map sorts them, the hard cases cluster exactly where the fragment's silence predicts, around supervision, credentials, and contracted intermediaries, and the conflicts that erupt, over pay bands, staffing ratios, and the contracting out of the cleaners, align along the seams the criterion draws while baffling any commentator who sorted the cast by salary on the morning of the dispute. The exercise generalises: pick an industry, draw its positions before reading its headlines, and the headlines become more intelligible. That, in the end, is the empirical cash value of a definition its author never finished.

## Criticisms beyond the sand objection

The built-on-sand objection received this guide's fullest attention because the brief assigns it, but a complete pillar should register the other standing criticisms in their strong forms, since students meet them in the wild. The first is the charge of economic reductionism: that sorting human beings by production relations demotes every other loyalty and identity to a shadow, and that the historical record, in which workers have repeatedly chosen nation, faith, or race over class solidarity, refutes the priority the theory assigns. The tradition's considered reply distinguishes the claim that class position structures interests from the caricature that it scripts behaviour, and points to its own French masterpieces, in which religion, region, and dynasty do enormous explanatory work. The charge lands against mechanical Marxisms. Against the criterion itself it mostly misses, since a map of positions never promised to be a psychology.

The second criticism is empirical and concerns the embourgeoisement thesis in its successive forms: that rising living standards, home ownership, and shareholding dissolve the proletariat into a general property-owning middle, so that the relational map describes a vanishing world. Each generation's version has captured something real about consumption and aspiration, and each has stumbled on the same rock: ownership of a house one lives in and a pension whose assets one does not control does not place a household on the owning side of the production relation, and the wage relation's compulsion, the need to sell next month's labour power to meet next month's obligations, survives affluence intact. The third criticism is normative and cuts from the opposite side: that the category itself, however accurate, organises politics around enmity and has licensed, in the hands of states and parties speaking its name, persecutions that no analytical yield can excuse. This guide has kept the theory and the record of its political uses in separate columns, as the series rules require, and it leaves the moral ledger there open, noting only that a criterion which has been turned against its own tradition's regimes, as Djilas turned it, cannot be dismissed as a mere instrument of apologetics. Readers will weigh these matters with their own judgement informed, which is the most a pillar article may properly ask.

## The vocabulary in the wild: a field guide to slippage

Because the word class does heavy ceremonial work in public speech, readers benefit from a short catalogue of the slippages they will meet weekly. Political speech uses middle class to mean the broad band of households a speaker wishes to flatter, a gradational embrace so wide it can include both the salaried professional and the small employer, whose positions the criterion separates, and working class to mean a cultural heartland, defined by region, accent, and memory, whose present inhabitants may include pensioners, publicans, and contractors in any mixture. Advertising uses class as a synonym for elegance, a usage descended from status rather than from economics. Bureaucratic usage sorts claimants by household income and calls the sort a class analysis because the form has boxes. None of these usages is wicked; all of them become misleading the moment they borrow Marx's authority, claiming a scientific warrant for categories he would not have recognised. The discipline this series recommends is attribution: middle class in the pollster's sense is a respectable instrument for measuring opinion among income bands, and it becomes an error only when a commentator slides, mid paragraph, from the pollster's band to Marx's relation and draws conclusions about exploitation or solidarity that the band cannot support.

The slippage runs in the other direction as well. Writers inside the tradition sometimes use class where the evidence offered is purely gradational, citing wage statistics to prove a relational thesis, or invoke the proletariat for audiences whose composition, self-employed, retired, studying, has never been examined against the criterion. The unfinished chapter rule, impartially applied, rebukes both directions, because its demand is not loyalty to a vocabulary but honesty about instruments: say which scheme, say which level, say whose reconstruction. A reader trained by this cluster to ask those three things of every class claim, friendly or hostile, has acquired the skill the pillar exists to teach, and can then read the tradition's masters and its critics with the same calm instrument in hand.

## What would change the picture

A theory earns its place by knowing its own defeaters, and class analysis has three developments that would genuinely wound it, as distinct from the costume changes it absorbs. If ownership of productive assets became so universally and evenly dispersed that no distinct position of living from others' labour could be identified, the map's owning pole would empty and the criterion would describe a past formation, as it already describes slave and feudal formations. If the link between production position and life chances dissolved, so that where a person stood in relation to the means of production predicted nothing about income security, health, political behaviour, or children's prospects, the category would lose explanatory point even where its boxes could still be filled. And if organised politics durably and everywhere ceased to align, even roughly and intermittently, with the seams of ownership, the tradition's claim that class is the central axis would shrink into a specialty of economic historians. None of those developments describes the world the evidence presents: ownership remains concentrated, position remains predictive, and the seams keep reappearing in conflicts whose participants often disavow the vocabulary while enacting its logic. Stating the defeaters plainly is not a concession extracted under pressure; it is the final application of this pillar's method, which trusts a criterion more, not less, when its holders can say what would make them put it down.

## The fragment as an invitation

There is a final way to read the broken chapter, kinder than the objection and sterner than devotion. A finished definition, delivered with the full authority of the system's founder, would have frozen class analysis at the knowledge of the eighteen sixties: before the joint stock company's maturity, before the salaried professions swelled, before welfare states, mass education, pension funds, and platforms rearranged the surfaces of ownership and work. Every generation since has had to rebuild the category for its own social formation, and the rebuilds, Wright's locations, Thompson's formations, the feminist corrections, the global extensions, are not corruptions of a pure original but the normal life of a concept whose author left it, perhaps mercifully, in working order rather than in a sealed case. The price of that life is permanent argument about the boundaries. The yield is a category that has survived a century and a half of social forms its inventor never saw, because its criterion asks a question every one of those forms still answers. Honesty about the fragment and confidence in the criterion are not enemies. In the best of the tradition they have always been the same virtue, practised under two names.

## Teaching the map to read itself

One last discipline belongs in a pillar that students will actually study from. The class map repays being drawn, literally, for any society a reader is examining: the positions placed, the revenues named beside each, the fractions sketched inside the poles, the strata that fit nowhere set honestly in the margin rather than forced into a box for tidiness. Drawn maps expose errors that prose conceals. A map of a modern economy that omits the rentier drawing income from housing and intellectual property has forgotten the fragment's third class in its modern costume. A map whose working class contains only the waged has forgotten the unwaged labour that reproduces them. A map with no margin for the straddlers has forgotten Wright. The drawing exercise is humble, almost schoolroom, and it operationalises the whole argument of this guide: criterion first, levels distinguished, hard cases visible, reconstructions labelled with their authors' names and dates. Readers who perform it once for a society they know well will find the protocol of the previous section has become reflex, and reflex, in the handling of a word as abused as class, is worth more than another shelf of denunciations of other people's sloppiness.

## Conclusion

Marx never finished defining class, and this guide has treated that unfinished state not as an embarrassment to be footnoted but as the organising fact of the topic. From the fragment in the final chapter of the third volume, one stable criterion can be recovered: class is a relation to the means of production, sorting those who own productive property and appropriate the labour of others from those who must sell their labour power, with landowners drawing rent as a third great position and a penumbra of fractions, strata, and transitional figures filling every concrete society. The two-camp picture belongs to a youthful pamphlet and names a tendency, not a census. The historical writings show the criterion at work on societies of six or seven significant positions, explaining alliances and defeats that no simpler scheme reaches. The objection that an undefined centre voids the whole theory has been given its full strength and answered with the distinction between a definition and a criterion, plus the concession that borderlands stay permanently contested and that the distance from position to political action is a real, unsolved problem rather than a verbal one. The unfinished chapter rule asks only for honesty about which of those elements any given claim rests on. A reconstruction, openly labelled, built on a criterion consistently applied, is a respectable inheritance. A slogan wearing the fragment's authority is not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does Marx define class?

By relation to the means of production. Those who own productive property, land, machinery, premises, finance, and live from the labour of others form the owning class; those who own little beyond their capacity to work and must sell that capacity for a wage form the labouring class. Marx began a formal definition in the final chapter of the third volume of Capital but never completed it, so this criterion is reconstructed from his consistent usage across the economics and the historical writings rather than quoted from a finished definition.

### Q: Did Marx ever finish his definition of class?

No. Chapter Fifty-Two of Capital Volume Three, titled Classes, opens by naming wage-labourers, capitalists, and landowners as the three great classes, asks what constitutes a class, notes that identity of revenue source would multiply classes indefinitely, and then stops after roughly a page and a half. Engels, editing the volume after Marx's death, printed the fragment with a note that the manuscript breaks off there. No continuation exists. Every later definition offered in Marx's name is therefore a reconstruction built on the criterion he applies elsewhere.

### Q: Are there two classes or more in Marx?

More, at every level except one polemical compression. The Communist Manifesto speaks of society splitting into two great camps, bourgeoisie and proletariat, as a tendency and in a mobilising genre, two decades before the mature economics. The systematic fragment names three great classes, adding landowners. The historical writings on France deploy six or seven positions, including a finance aristocracy, an industrial bourgeoisie, a petty bourgeoisie, a peasantry, and a lumpenproletariat. The numbers answer questions posed at different levels of abstraction, and quoting one level against another manufactures a contradiction Marx did not commit.

### Q: Is class about income or about ownership?

Ownership, decisively. Income is a flow that flows partly from class position, but identical incomes can sit on opposed positions: a highly paid executive sells labour power, however magnificent the price, while a modest workshop owner buys it and appropriates surplus. Income bands belong to gradational schemes that rank people on a scale. Marx's scheme is relational, sorting people by their connection to productive property and to each other through it. Confusing the two schemes is the single most common source of error in popular discussion of who counts as working class.

### Q: What is the difference between a relational and a gradational view of class?

A gradational view ranks individuals along a continuum of income, education, or occupational prestige, so its classes are layers separated by boundaries that statisticians choose and can move without changing the world. A relational view defines positions by their connections: capitalist and wage-labourer, landlord and tenant, exist only in relation to each other, and abolishing one position abolishes its opposite. Marx's conception is relational throughout. Gradational data can describe the terrain on which relational positions sit, but it cannot substitute for the relations themselves.

### Q: Which classes appear in Marx's historical writings?

The French writings deploy a full political sociology. A finance aristocracy of bankers and speculators enriched by state debt dominates under the July Monarchy. An industrial bourgeoisie presses against it. A petty bourgeoisie of shopkeepers and small masters supplies democratic rhetoric and then flees to order. A vast smallholding peasantry provides the social base for Louis Bonaparte. A proletariat concentrated in the cities rises in eighteen forty-eight and again in the Commune. A lumpenproletariat of the dispossessed is recruited as reaction's street force. Each fraction appears because events require it for explanation.

### Q: Where do landowners fit in Marx's scheme?

As one of the three great classes of the unfinished chapter, coordinate with capitalists rather than folded inside them. Landowners own land, a condition of production they do not themselves operate, and they charge capital ground rent for access to it. Rent is a portion of surplus value diverted by legal title to a gift of nature. The position survives translation into modern forms, urban site owners, mineral holders, and other rentiers, and its interests in scarcity and high asset values regularly oppose industrial capital's interest in cheap inputs, a divergence invisible in any two-class summary.

### Q: Is class struggle the motor of history?

That is the tradition's shorthand for Marx's claim that conflicts between classes, rooted in opposed positions within production, drive the great transformations of society. Struggle here is structural before it is personal: the division of the working day into paid and unpaid portions, and the inverse relation of wages and profits, build contest into the relation itself, among parties who may feel no hostility at all. Its forms run from statute and inspection law through strikes to revolution. Whether the claim explains all major change is contested by historians, but it should be contested in that strong form.

### Q: Can someone belong to two classes at once?

Strictly, a person occupies a position, but real people can straddle positions, and Marx's own cases generate the straddles: the smallholder who hires out for wages in the off season, the manager who sells labour power while commanding the labour of others, the worker whose pension fund holds shares. Successors formalised the difficulty as contradictory class locations, positions sharing features of two poles. The criterion still sorts the components of such a life; what it refuses is a single tidy label where the underlying relations genuinely point both ways. Dual occupancy is a datum about the world, not a defect of the test.

### Q: What makes class the central category for Marx?

No other category connects his economics, his politics, and his philosophy of history. Relation to the means of production determines who must work for others, who appropriates the surplus that work creates, and whose interests therefore conflict by structure rather than by temperament. From that one axis Marx derives the dynamics of accumulation, the form of the state, the content of ideology, and the agency expected to transform society. Categories such as income, status, and occupation describe distributions and styles. Class, in his usage, names the machinery that produces the distributions, which is why it carries the system's explanatory weight.

### Q: Why does Marx's unfinished class chapter matter for every later definition?

Because it removes the possibility of appeal to a finished authority. Every school of Marxist class analysis, binary, threefold, Wright's contradictory locations, Poulantzas's redrawing of boundaries, is a completion of a fragment, and completions can be compared only by their fidelity to the criterion, their handling of the hard cases, and their explanatory yield, never by quotation of a definition that does not exist. Writers who know the chapter is unfinished argue honestly about reconstructions. Writers who do not know it mistake one reconstruction for Marx's own settled doctrine and defend it with a confidence the texts cannot support.

### Q: How does class differ from a caste or an estate in Marx's usage?

Estates are legal ranks, fixed by law and birth, with prescribed rights and duties, as in feudal orders. Castes are hereditary divisions sanctioned by ritual, governing marriage, occupation, and social contact. Classes are constituted by economic position alone: no law assigns a person to one, and ruin or enrichment moves individuals between classes without any legal or ritual act. The categories overlap in real societies, and caste's fusion of ritual and occupational division resists clean translation into class terms, a friction Marxist historians of India have had to respect rather than dissolve.

### Q: What should a journalist check before describing an income group as a class in Marx's sense?

Three checks. First, ownership: does the group own productive property, or must its members sell their labour power to live? Second, relation: against which opposed position is the group defined, since a Marxist class exists only in relation to another? Third, subject: is the story actually about income, housing, schooling, or accent, which are gradational facts, rather than about position in production? If the third check shows a gradational subject, the accurate vocabulary is income group, occupation, or neighbourhood, and class in Marx's sense should be left out of the headline.

### Q: How do slaves and serfs fit a theory built around wage labour?

The criterion precedes the wage form. Under slavery the master owns the producer's person, and all the slave's labour is appropriated directly. Under feudalism the serf, possessing tools and a plot, works part of the week on the lord's land or renders dues, so the surplus portion stands visibly separate from the labour that sustains the serf's household. Capitalism hides the same division inside the wage. Slave owners and slaves, lords and serfs, capitalists and wage-labourers are the class pairs of three modes of production, sorted by one criterion and distinguished by the mechanism through which surplus labour is extracted in each.

### Q: What makes a definition of class relational rather than a description of lifestyle?

A relational definition sorts by position within a structure of production: who owns the means of production, who must sell labour power, who appropriates the surplus, who draws rent. Its groups exist only in connection, as landlord implies tenant. A lifestyle description sorts by consumption, taste, accent, schooling, and self-image, attributes that correlate with position unevenly and change without any change in position, as when an impoverished landowner keeps aristocratic manners in salaried work. Marx treats lifestyle as evidence about how a class lives and represents itself, never as the thing that constitutes it.
