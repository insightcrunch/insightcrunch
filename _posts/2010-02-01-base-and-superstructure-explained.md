Ask a room of students to draw the base and superstructure model and almost all of them will produce a building: a solid rectangle at the bottom labelled the economy, a taller rectangle balanced on top of it labelled law, politics, religion, education, and culture, and an upward arrow. Ask them what the diagram claims and the answers become vague. Ask them whether the law that defines who owns a factory belongs in the box above or the box below, and the room divides, because that question has no comfortable answer and has generated a century of theoretical repair work.

The base and superstructure model is the most drawn, most quoted, and least examined thing in Marxism. Its imagery is architectural, which invites a picture of a passive upper storey resting inertly on a foundation, and that picture is wrong on both halves. Its boundary is genuinely unstable rather than merely blurry, because the terms in which the base is ordinarily specified are terms that belong to the superstructure. And its most influential modern statements are not restatements of Marx but revisions of him, produced by people who thought the original formulation could not stand as written.

![The base and superstructure model in Marxism explained, what belongs to each and the boundary problem - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-20.webp)

This guide draws the boundary as precisely as it can be drawn, names the point at which it stops being drawable, and sets out the repairs. The central claim it advances is what can be called the circularity repair: any usable statement of base and superstructure must either define the base without legal vocabulary or abandon the claim that the base explains the superstructure, and every serious version of the model since Marx has been an attempt at one or the other. A reader who understands that dilemma understands why the diagram on the board is not a description but an unfinished argument.

## What the model actually claims

The model asserts a relation of explanatory priority between two levels of a social formation. The base, or foundation, is the economic structure of society, meaning the totality of the relations of production. The superstructure comprises the legal and political institutions of that society, and, in a formulation the textbooks usually merge into the same category, the forms of social consciousness that correspond to the economic structure.

Three features of the claim deserve isolating at once, because almost everything that goes wrong with the model goes wrong by losing one of them.

The claim is about explanation, not about location. It does not say that law sits physically or temporally on top of production, nor that the base comes first in time. It says that if you want to explain why a society has the legal and political institutions it has, and why the forms of consciousness available in it take the shape they take, the productive relations are where the explanation is to be sought. Reverse the direction, and you get the idealist historiography Marx was writing against in the 1840s, in which the development of legal and philosophical ideas drives the development of institutions.

The claim concerns durable structures rather than individual events or beliefs. It is not the proposition that a given judge decides a given case because of their class position, and it is not the proposition that any particular person believes what their economic situation dictates. It concerns why a legal system of a certain type persists, why a political form is stable, why a range of ideas circulates as common sense. Applying it at the level of individual belief produces the crude version that critics attack and that no serious defender holds.

The claim admits of degrees, and the tradition's history is largely a record of arguing about which degree. Determination can mean that the base fixes the superstructural forms, that it selects among a limited range of possible forms, or that it sets outer limits and exerts pressures without fixing anything. All three readings have textual support and named holders, and the [complete guide to historical materialism](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/) sets out the full ladder of positions on which this pair of terms is one rung's vocabulary.

### What does the model claim in one sentence?

The base is how a society organises production: who owns the productive resources, who works for whom, and how the surplus is extracted. The superstructure is the legal system, the state, and the prevailing ideas and institutions of consciousness. The claim is that the base explains the superstructure rather than the reverse.

## The textual source, and how thin it is

The single most useful fact about this model is one that almost no competing page states: Marx sets it out compactly in one place, in a preface, and never develops it into a theory.

That place is the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, published in 1859. In a passage of a few sentences, Marx says that the totality of the relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, describes this as the real foundation on which a legal and political superstructure rises, and adds that definite forms of social consciousness correspond to it. The passage is autobiographical in purpose; Marx is describing the guiding thread he arrived at through his studies, not presenting a doctrine. He then moves on. There is no chapter on the base and superstructure anywhere in his published work, no extended treatment of the relation, and no worked example in which he applies the pair of terms systematically to a historical case.

The surrounding argument is developed elsewhere, principally in the manuscripts written with Engels between 1845 and 1846 and published long after both were dead. Those manuscripts argue that consciousness cannot be treated as an independent starting point, that people must be understood first as producers of their means of subsistence, and that the ruling ideas of an epoch stand in a relation to the material dominance of a class. That is the substantive argument. The architectural metaphor is a compression of it into two words, offered once, in a preface.

What follows from this for a reader is a matter of proportion. A model that occupies a paragraph in Marx occupies a chapter in every textbook, which means the textbook version is not primarily Marx's. It is the accumulated product of the Second International's systematising, of Soviet codification, and of the twentieth-century revisions that were written to fix problems the compressed original left open. Treating the diagram as a summary of Marx's position, rather than as a later construction resting on a few sentences of his, is the first and largest error.

A note on caution is worth entering here. It is sometimes claimed that Marx used the metaphor only once, and sometimes that he used it constantly. Neither claim should be repeated without a verified count, since Marx's corpus includes journalism, correspondence, and manuscripts in several languages, and architectural imagery appears in various forms across it. What can be said safely, and is enough for the argument, is that the compact canonical statement occurs in the 1859 preface, that no developed treatment of the pair exists anywhere in his published work, and that the model's prominence in the tradition vastly exceeds its prominence in the sources.

### Did Marx develop the metaphor into a theory?

No. The compact statement appears in the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, and Marx never wrote an extended treatment of the pair, never applied it systematically to a historical case, and never resolved the boundary questions it raises. The developed model belongs to the later tradition.

## The German words and what they carry

The original terms are Basis and Uberbau, and the second of these deserves a sentence of its own because it explains a good deal of the confusion in English.

Uberbau is ordinary German architectural and engineering vocabulary. It is what sits above a foundation: the superstructure of a bridge, the part of a building above ground level. It is not a technical coinage invented for social theory, and it carries no implication of flimsiness or of passivity. A superstructure in the ordinary building sense is not decoration; it is the functioning part of the structure, the part the building exists for, and it bears loads of its own.

English usage has drifted from this. The word superstructure in general English has acquired a faintly derivative flavour, something erected on top of a more real thing, and readers import that flavour into the model without noticing. The result is the passive-reflection reading, in which the superstructure is a shadow cast upward by the base. That reading is not licensed by the German, and it is contradicted by Marx's own statement in the same preface that the ideological forms are where people become conscious of the conflict and fight it out, which requires those forms to be causally active rather than reflective.

There is a further translation point worth a sentence, because it affects how the model reads in the languages where much of the twentieth-century theory was written. French renders the pair as infrastructure and superstructure, and the first of those words carries associations in English that the German does not, suggesting roads, cables, and physical plant rather than social relations. A reader moving between anglophone and French-influenced literature will meet the same model under vocabulary that pulls the base towards the technical and away from the relational, which compounds the confusion the English word superstructure already creates. Where a source uses infrastructure, check whether it means the relations of production or the material equipment, because the two readings license different arguments.

The word Basis carries its own trap in the opposite direction. It suggests something simple and given, a floor to stand on, when what it names is the ensemble of relations of production, which is an abstraction of considerable complexity and is precisely the thing the model has most trouble specifying. A reader who pictures the base as the solid part and the superstructure as the complicated part has the difficulty exactly backwards.

## What exactly belongs to the base

The base is the ensemble of relations of production. That formula is precise and it is also the point at which most explanations stop, leaving the reader no better placed to sort an actual institution into one box or the other. Unpacking it properly takes three steps.

The relations of production are the social relations through which production is carried on, and in a class society they are relations of appropriation: they determine who performs surplus labour and who lives on it. A wage relation is a relation of production. A relation between a lord and a serf performing labour services is a relation of production. A relation between a landowner and a sharecropping tenant is a relation of production. What all three have in common is that they specify who works, who commands the work, and how the surplus above subsistence is transferred.

The base therefore includes the pattern of effective control over productive resources, the mechanism by which surplus labour is extracted, and the division of labour insofar as it structures those relations. It includes the technical organisation of the workplace where that organisation is part of how control is exercised, which is why the analysis of the labour process belongs in the base rather than in a separate technical category.

The base does not include the economy in the ordinary journalistic sense. This is the single most common misplacement. Market prices, interest rates, trade volumes, the level of unemployment, the exchange rate, and the business cycle are not the base. They are phenomena occurring within an economy organised by particular relations of production, and treating them as the base collapses the distinction the model exists to draw. A society can have wildly different price levels, trade patterns, and monetary arrangements while its relations of production remain constant, and it is the relations that are supposed to do the explaining. When a summary says the base is the economy, ask which relations it means, and watch how often no answer is available.

Whether the productive forces belong to the base is a genuine disagreement rather than a settled matter. The 1859 preface identifies the base with the relations of production specifically, and on a strict reading the forces, meaning instruments, techniques, and labour power, sit outside the base as the thing the base organises. Many expositions include the forces in the base, which is defensible if the base is being understood as the whole mode of production rather than as its relational component. The distinction matters for the technology question addressed below, and the [guide to the mode of production](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/) is where the relationship between forces, relations, and the unit as a whole is worked out in full.

## What belongs to the superstructure

The 1859 passage names two things and most summaries name one, which is why the superstructure is usually described more loosely than it should be.

The first is the legal and political superstructure: the state apparatus, the legal system, the courts, the constitutional order, the machinery of administration and coercion. Marx says this rises on the real foundation. It is the institutional layer.

The second is the forms of social consciousness, which Marx says correspond to the foundation rather than rising on it. This covers religion, philosophy, morality, art, and the general categories through which people understand their situation. Whether the difference between rising and corresponding is deliberate is arguable, but it is in the text, and merging the two into a single undifferentiated upper storey loses a distinction the original draws.

The distinction is not merely textual pedantry. Institutions and forms of consciousness relate to the base differently and change differently. A legal regime can be altered by legislation in a session; a prevailing moral outlook cannot. An institution has personnel, budgets, and enforcement powers that give it interests of its own; a body of ideas does not, though the institutions that transmit it do. The twentieth-century revisions of the model, particularly Althusser's, are substantially attempts to handle this difference by relocating the transmission institutions, meaning schools, churches, media, and the family, into a category that is neither purely institutional nor purely ideational.

What the superstructure emphatically does not mean, on any serious reading, is a passive reflection. The preface itself locates the ideological forms as the medium in which the conflict between forces and relations is fought out, which requires them to be active. Engels, writing to Joseph Bloch in 1890, states that the various elements of the superstructure exercise their influence on the course of historical struggles and in many cases preponderate in determining their form. That is a strong statement of superstructural efficacy from the surviving author of the theory, and any exposition that omits it presents a version its own authors disowned.

### Can the upper level act back on the foundation?

Yes, and the theory's authors said so explicitly. Engels's letter to Joseph Bloch in 1890 describes superstructural elements as influencing the course of historical struggles and often determining their form. The claim of the model is explanatory priority in the last analysis, not one-way causation.

## The boundary problem and the circularity repair

Everything above is the standard account, drawn as carefully as it can be drawn. This section is where the model stops working as stated, and it is the content that separates a serious treatment of these terms from a definitional one.

The relations of production are ordinarily specified in terms of ownership. The capitalist owns the means of production; the worker owns nothing but labour power; the feudal lord holds the land. But ownership is a legal relation. It is constituted by a legal system that defines what may be owned, by whom, with what powers of exclusion and transfer, enforced by courts and by the coercive apparatus of a state. And the legal system is superstructural: it is the thing the base is supposed to explain.

The model therefore appears to define its explanans in terms of its explanandum. The base explains the superstructure, but the base can only be described using the vocabulary of the superstructure, which means either the description is circular or the explanation is. This is not a clever objection invented by hostile critics. Marx puts his finger on it himself in the fetters clause of the same preface, where he writes that the productive forces come into conflict with the existing relations of production or, as he immediately adds, with the property relations that express those relations in legal terms. The parenthesis concedes the point: the relations are ordinarily encountered in their legal expression.

Two repairs are available, and the claim of this guide is that every serious version of the model since has been one or the other.

The first repair defines the base without legal vocabulary. Its most rigorous statement is G. A. Cohen's, in his reconstruction of 1978. Cohen redescribes relations of production as relations of effective power rather than of legal right: what matters is who actually has the capacity to use, to withhold, to direct, and to exclude, whether or not any legal system recognises the capacity. On this account a slaveholder's relation to a slave, a manager's relation to a workforce, and a bureaucrat's relation to an enterprise can all be specified as de facto powers, and the legal system is then explained as the codification and stabilisation of powers that exist independently of it. The circularity is broken because the base is now described in non-legal terms.

The repair is elegant and it has costs that must be stated. The first cost is that de facto powers in complex societies are extremely difficult to specify without reference to the legal framework that constitutes them; the power of a shareholder over an enterprise is not a physical capacity but an entitlement that exists only within a legal order. The second is that once the base is defined as a distribution of effective powers, the claim that it explains the legal system becomes considerably less surprising, since a legal system that codifies existing powers is doing something close to what the definition already says it does. The third is that the repair makes the base an object nobody can observe directly, which weakens the model as a research instrument.

The second repair abandons the strong explanatory claim. It concedes that base and superstructure are mutually constitutive, that the legal form is not external to the relations of production but part of what makes them what they are, and that determination should be understood as the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures rather than as the fixing of outcomes. This is Raymond Williams's move in his essay of 1973 on base and superstructure in Marxist cultural theory, and it is the position underlying most of what cultural studies did with the model thereafter. The cost here is symmetrical to the first repair's: what is bought in accuracy is paid for in bite, since a model asserting that economic arrangements limit and pressure without determining is one that few historians of any persuasion would contest.

The circularity repair, stated as a rule: any usable statement of base and superstructure must either define the base without legal vocabulary or abandon the claim that the base explains the superstructure. A version that does neither is asserting a circle, and a reader can test any exposition they encounter against this rule in about a minute.

### Where does the state belong on this model?

Formally the superstructure, since the 1859 passage names the legal and political apparatus as what rises on the foundation. In practice the placement is contested, because in many social formations the state is directly involved in surplus extraction through taxation, tribute, or the direct organisation of production, which is a base function on the model's own criterion.

Two things are visible from the table as a whole and neither is visible from the diagram on the board.

The first is that the difficult rows are difficult for the same reason. Property law, the state, the family, and education all present the pattern where an institution classified as superstructural turns out to be doing constitutive or reproductive work for the base. That recurring pattern is what generated the twentieth-century revisions, and it is why relative autonomy and reproduction became the central concepts rather than determination.

The second is that several rows are institution-specific rather than category-specific. A newspaper is a site of ideological production and a capitalist enterprise with wage labour, and it is both at once rather than one at a time. A model that requires each institution to occupy exactly one box will misdescribe any institution of that kind, which is most of them.

## The wrong definitions in circulation

Five versions of this model circulate widely and each is wrong in a way that a reader can learn to spot.

The first is the passive-reflection version, in which the superstructure is a mirror held up to the economy and has no causal power of its own. This is the version most criticisms attack, and it is the version Engels spent the 1890s writing letters against. Its practical signature is an account in which a legal change or a political outcome is explained as expressing an economic interest, with no account of the process by which that interest reached the statute book. If a reading offers no political actors, no conflict, and no institutional mechanism, it is running the passive version.

The second is the economy-as-base version, in which the base is markets, money, and commerce. This is the version that survives in general journalism and in a good deal of sociology teaching, and it is wrong because it substitutes phenomena for relations. A society can change its price system, its monetary regime, and its trade patterns without changing its relations of production, and the model claims explanatory power for the relations, not for the phenomena.

The third is the architectural-literal version, in which the diagram is treated as a description of how a society is built rather than as a claim about explanatory priority. Its signature is the question of what happens to the superstructure if the base is removed, asked as though the model made a prediction about collapse. It makes no such prediction, and the German word for the upper level carries no implication of dependence in the relevant sense.

The fourth is the false consciousness version, in which the superstructure's function is to deceive a subordinate class into accepting arrangements against their interest, and in which this deception is treated as Marx's own account. The phrase false consciousness does not appear in Marx. It appears in a letter Engels wrote to Franz Mehring in 1893, describing ideologists as working with thought material they take as given without examining its real origins. Its later career is largely independent of both authors, and citing it to Marx is one of the most reliable indicators that a source has not checked its own references. The [dedicated treatment of the Marxist theory of ideology](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/) sets out the several distinct accounts of ideology that this version flattens into one.

The fifth is the consensus version, in which the model is presented as the settled Marxist position on the relation between economy and culture. It is not settled and it never was. Its most influential twentieth-century statements, by Gramsci, by Williams, and by Althusser, are revisions written because their authors judged the original formulation unusable as it stood. A page that presents the model without saying that its leading modern exponents were repairing it has withheld the most important fact about it.

### What is the standard criticism of the model?

Because the strong version makes cultural and political forms derivative of economic arrangements, which fits the historical record badly: societies with similar production relations have produced very different legal and cultural orders. Defenders answer with relative autonomy and reciprocal action, and critics then reply that these concessions leave the model asserting very little.

## The near-neighbour terms and the exact difference in each case

Five terms sit close enough to this pair to be confused with it routinely, and in each case the difference can be stated in a sentence.

Base and mode of production are not synonyms. The mode of production is the larger unit: forces and relations of production taken together, the whole way a society produces. The base is the relational component of that unit, meaning the relations of production considered as the foundation on which the legal and political order rests. A reader who substitutes one for the other will misplace technology, which sits inside the mode of production without straightforwardly sitting inside the base. The full specification of the larger unit belongs to the [guide to the mode of production](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/).

Superstructure and ideology are not synonyms, though the confusion is nearly universal. The superstructure is a level of a social formation and it includes institutions with buildings, budgets, and enforcement powers: courts, legislatures, police, schools. Ideology is a category of content and of practice within that level, concerning the forms in which people understand their situation. A court is superstructural and is not an ideology. A body of legal doctrine is both superstructural and ideological. The relation is that of a container to one of its contents, and treating them as identical loses the institutional half of the superstructure entirely.

Superstructure and culture are not synonyms either, and the drift towards treating them as such is what produced the version of the model used in cultural studies. Culture in the anthropological sense includes practices of production themselves, which are base phenomena, and culture in the artistic sense is a subset of the forms of social consciousness. The superstructure includes the state, which nobody would call culture.

Determination and causation are not synonyms in this literature, and the difference carries most of the weight of the twentieth-century argument. Causation, in the ordinary sense, is a relation between events. Determination, in the sense used here, is a relation between a structure and the range of forms it permits or presses towards. Williams's reformulation makes this explicit by glossing determination as the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures, which is a claim about constraint on possibility rather than about the production of particular outcomes.

Relative autonomy and autonomy are not the same claim, and the qualifier is doing real work that summaries drop. Autonomy would mean that the superstructural level operates by its own laws with no systematic relation to the base. Relative autonomy means that it has its own effectivity, its own institutional interests, and its own tempo of change, within limits set elsewhere. The whole difficulty of the concept lies in specifying the limits, and its critics argue that no specification has ever been supplied.

### How do these terms differ from ideology?

The superstructure is a level of society that includes institutions such as courts, the state apparatus, and schools. Ideology is a category of content and practice occurring within that level. Courts are superstructural without being ideologies; legal doctrine is both. Treating the terms as identical drops the institutional half of the superstructure.

## How later Marxists rewrote the model

The model's history after Marx is a history of repair, and knowing the sequence lets a reader place almost any modern use of the terms.

Engels began the repairs while the ink was still wet. His correspondence of the 1890s, principally the letters to Joseph Bloch in 1890, to Conrad Schmidt in the same year, to Franz Mehring in 1893, and to Heinz Starkenburg in 1894, insists that the economic element is ultimately determining but not the only determining one, that the superstructural forms exert real influence and often determine the form that struggles take, and that treating the materialist conception as a formula for constructing history rather than as a guide to studying it is an abuse of it. He offers the image of history as a resultant of innumerable intersecting wills, a parallelogram of forces producing an outcome nobody intended. Whether this is a retreat from the founding statement or a clarification of what it always meant is disputed, and the better-supported reading is the second, since the 1859 passage itself locates the ideological forms as the arena in which the conflict is fought out.

Antonio Gramsci, writing in prison notebooks in the late 1920s and 1930s under conditions of censorship that shape his vocabulary, made the largest structural change. His concept of the historical bloc treats base and superstructure not as two levels in a causal relation but as a unity in which the superstructural forms are the way the relations of production are lived and secured. Hegemony, his central contribution, describes the organisation of consent through the institutions of civil society, which gives the superstructural level a decisive rather than a derivative role in maintaining a social order. Gramsci's motivation was concrete: the transformations the theory expected in Western Europe had not occurred, and the explanation appeared to lie in the density of civil society in the west, which absorbed shocks that a thinner social structure in the east had not.

Raymond Williams, in an essay of 1973 on base and superstructure in Marxist cultural theory, performed the most careful conceptual work anyone has done on the pair. His argument has three moves. He rejects the reduction of the base to a fixed economic state, insisting it be understood as a dynamic set of activities and relations. He rejects the treatment of the superstructure as a reflected content, replacing it with a range of cultural practices. And he redefines determination as the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures rather than as prefiguration and control, drawing on an older sense of the word that means to fix a boundary. Williams also supplies the vocabulary of dominant, residual, and emergent cultural formations, which allows an analysis to describe cultural elements that do not correspond to the current base at all, either because they survive from an earlier one or because they anticipate a possible one.

Louis Althusser, from the early 1960s and in his essay of 1970 on ideology and ideological state apparatuses, produced the most influential structural revision. His model retains determination while granting the political and ideological levels their own effectivity, through the concepts of overdetermination, structural causality, and relative autonomy. The economy determines in the last instance which level is dominant in a given social formation, so that in one formation politics may be dominant and in another religion, while the economic remains determinant. His remark in For Marx that "the lonely hour of the last instance never comes" was written as a caution against expecting the determination to appear in pure form, and it has been received, not unreasonably, as an admission that a determination which never arrives is doing no work. The ideological state apparatuses essay makes the further move of relocating schools, churches, the family, and the media into a category defined by function rather than by level, on the argument that what they do is reproduce the relations of production. The [dedicated treatment of that essay](/2012/03/01/althusser-ideology-state-apparatuses/) covers the argument and its difficulties in full.

E. P. Thompson attacked the structuralist revision in a book-length polemic published in 1978, and his objection is the most important one made from inside the tradition. His argument is that the structural model eliminates human agency and experience, that it converts history into the working out of a system, and that the actual historical record shows people making choices within conditions rather than positions being occupied by a structure. His own historical practice, particularly the study of the formation of the English working class, treats class as something that happens in the course of struggle rather than as a position generated by the base, which is a different model of the whole relation.

The cumulative result is that the model in serious use today bears limited resemblance to the diagram. What survives is a commitment to explanatory priority stated with heavy qualification, a set of concepts for describing partial autonomy, and a research practice of looking for the material reproduction of institutional orders. What has largely gone is the two-storey picture.

### Who repaired the model after Marx?

Gramsci recast the levels as a unity secured through consent in civil society. Williams redefined determination as the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures. Althusser introduced relative autonomy and determination in the last instance. Each rewrite grants the superstructure more independent effectivity than the original formulation appears to allow.

## The strongest objection to the model

Stated at full strength, in the terms its own proponents use, the objection is this.

Once the model has conceded reciprocal action, so that the superstructure influences the base; once it has conceded relative autonomy, so that superstructural levels operate by their own logics within unspecified limits; once it has conceded that determination means the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures rather than the fixing of forms; and once it has conceded that the last instance never arrives in pure form, then the model asserts nothing that a non-Marxist historian would deny. Every competent historian believes that how a society produces its livelihood constrains its politics, that legal institutions are shaped by the interests of those with resources, and that ideas do not float free of the conditions in which they circulate. If that is the claim, the model is a truism with a diagram attached. If it is more than that, the additional content has never been specified, and the concessions have been made precisely in order to avoid specifying it, each one entered after the previous version proved indefensible.

The objection has a sharper second edge. The concessions were not made in response to new evidence. They were made in response to political disappointment: Gramsci's revision follows the failure of revolution in Western Europe, Althusser's follows the crisis of orthodoxy in the postwar communist parties, and Williams's follows the recognition that the reflection theory produced unusable literary criticism. A research programme whose central concepts are revised each time its expectations fail, in the direction of asserting less, exhibits the pattern the philosophy of science calls degeneration.

Three things can be said in reply, and they mitigate the objection rather than dissolving it.

The model still forbids something specific, and the prohibition is violated regularly by real historical writing. It forbids the explanation of a durable institutional order by reference to ideas, values, or preferences alone, with no account of how that order is materially reproduced and by whom. An account of the persistence of a legal regime that never asks who benefits, who pays, who enforces, and out of whose surplus the enforcement is funded, is ruled out. That is not a truism, and a great deal of intellectual history and of institutional analysis proceeds without meeting it.

The concessions were not all defensive, and some generated independent research. Gramsci's hegemony is not a smaller claim than the original; it is a different and more demanding one, requiring an account of how consent is organised, through which institutions, with what internal contradictions, and it produced a large body of work in political sociology and cultural analysis with findings of its own. Williams's dominant, residual, and emergent triad is a genuine analytical addition that lets an account describe cultural material the base does not explain, which is more than the original could do. An auxiliary that opens new questions is doing something different from one that merely absorbs an anomaly.

The strongest reply is also the most uncomfortable for defenders: the weak version buys its safety at the price of much of its bite, and that trade should be acknowledged rather than concealed. The version of the model that almost no historian would deny is also a version that does little work. The version that does substantial work, in which the character of the legal and political order is explained by the relations of production, faces the comparative evidence badly, since social formations with similar relations of production have sustained markedly different legal and political orders over long periods. A defender who moves between these two versions in the course of an argument is performing the manoeuvre the objection describes, and the honest position is to pick one and pay its costs. The full form of this dispute, and the related argument about how much determination the economic level can bear, belongs to the [debate over economic determination](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/).

## Working the model on real cases

A definitional article earns its place by making the terms usable, so three short applications show what the model does and where it stops.

Take factory legislation limiting the working day. The passive-reflection version predicts that legislation will serve the interests of the owning class, and it does not obviously do so, since the legislation restricts what employers may do. The model handles this better than the caricature suggests, and Marx's own treatment in the first volume of Capital is the demonstration: the legislation is explained through the conflict between the interest of individual capitals in extending hours and the interest of capital in general in the continued reproduction of the labour force, mediated by working-class organisation and by the political capacity of a landed interest with reasons of its own to constrain manufacturers. The base does not dictate the statute. It supplies the interests, the conflict, and the constraint within which a political process produced a particular outcome. Note what the account requires: named agents, an institutional channel, a mechanism. That is the model used properly.

Take the abolition of slavery in a plantation economy. The idealist account credits moral argument and religious conviction. The strong base account credits the declining profitability of the slave relation relative to wage labour. Neither survives on its own: profitability estimates are contested and in several cases point the wrong way, while a purely moral account cannot explain why the argument prevailed when it did rather than a century earlier. The model's serious use here is to insist that the moral movement be located, meaning that one asks which classes supplied its organisational base, what their relation to the plantation economy was, and through what political mechanism their pressure told. That is a research programme rather than a conclusion, which is what a framework should generate rather than a verdict to be recited.

Take a change in the way a broadcasting system is financed, a case chosen because it sits awkwardly on the boundary rather than comfortably inside either level. Here the model shows its limits usefully. Broadcasting is superstructural in the classical placement and is simultaneously a capitalist industry with its own wage relations, its own capital requirements, and its own competitive pressures. The interesting analysis is not about which box it goes in but about how the two aspects interact: how the conditions of production of cultural goods shape their content, which is a base-level question about a superstructural institution. Any use of the model that spends its energy on the classification rather than on that interaction has mistaken the taxonomy for the analysis.

### What is a real-world example of base and superstructure?

Factory legislation limiting working hours is the standard worked case. The relations of production supply the conflicting interests, between individual employers wanting longer hours and capital in general needing a reproducible workforce, while the outcome is produced through political organisation, parliamentary process, and the interests of other propertied groups. The base sets the terms; it does not write the statute.

## The diagram problem

The two-box diagram is the most efficient teaching device in the subject and it installs three errors simultaneously, which is why it deserves separate treatment.

It installs verticality. Two boxes stacked with an arrow pointing up encode a spatial relation, and the spatial relation carries an implication of support and dependence that the explanatory claim does not require. Nothing in the model says the superstructure would fall if the base changed; the model says a different base would be associated with a different superstructure, which is a claim about correspondence over historical time rather than about structural load-bearing.

It installs discreteness. Two boxes with a line between them encode a clean partition, and the boundary table above shows that the partition is not clean for the institutions people most want to classify. Property law, the state, education, and the family all straddle. A diagram that cannot represent straddling will produce students who believe the difficulty is theirs.

It installs stasis. A diagram shows a state, and the model is about a relation over time: relations of production developing, coming into conflict, and generating transformation, with the superstructural forms as the arena in which the conflict is fought. Williams's insistence that the base be understood as a set of activities rather than as a fixed economic state is a direct response to exactly this defect of the picture.

A better teaching device, if one is wanted, is a question rather than a shape. Ask what has to keep happening, materially, for this institution to persist, and who does it. That question generates the model's actual analytical content, forces the identification of agents and mechanisms, and has no picture to be misread.

## Where the model is used now, and under what names

The terms base and superstructure appear less often in current scholarship than the ideas do, and a reader tracking the influence needs to know the aliases.

In sociology the model survives most visibly in the teaching of theory, where it is presented as the Marxist position and contrasted with functionalist and Weberian alternatives. Its research use is narrower and runs mainly through the sociology of law and the sociology of education, where the questions of whose interests a legal regime secures and how educational institutions reproduce social positions are directly descended from it, usually with the vocabulary of reproduction rather than of superstructure.

In cultural and media studies the influence is deep and the terminology is Williams's rather than Marx's. Dominant, residual, and emergent formations; structures of feeling; the analysis of cultural production as a material practice with its own conditions: all of these descend from the essay that revised the model, and they are used by people who would not describe their work as Marxist. The proposition that a text stands in an explicable relation to the conditions of its production is the model's most widely held descendant and its origin is now largely invisible.

In legal theory the descendant is the body of work asking what interests a doctrinal structure secures and how legal form relates to commodity exchange, a line running from early Soviet legal theory through critical legal studies. In political science and international relations the descendant is the analysis of the structural constraints that an economy places on state action regardless of who governs, which is the model's strongest surviving empirical claim and one with a substantial quantitative literature.

Where the model has lost ground entirely is in the strong reflective form. Almost nobody now defends the proposition that cultural or legal forms can be read off from economic arrangements, and the disappearance of that position is the largest single change in the model's standing. What has replaced it is a family of weaker claims about reproduction, constraint, and conditions of production, and whether those claims are still the model or are its dissolution is a fair question with no agreed answer.

### Is the base and superstructure model outdated?

The strong version has few defenders and the two-box diagram misleads more than it teaches. The weaker claims descended from it, about material reproduction of institutions and about the conditions under which cultural forms are produced, remain in active use across sociology, legal theory, and cultural studies, usually under other names.

## Where technology sits

The question of whether technology belongs to the base is asked constantly and answered badly, and the reason is that it has two correct answers depending on how the base is being defined.

On the strict reading of the 1859 passage, the base is the economic structure constituted by the relations of production, and technology belongs to the productive forces, which are a different category. On that reading technology is not in the base at all; it is what the base organises, and the relation between the two is the subject of the fetters argument rather than of the base and superstructure pair. This is the reading Cohen defends, and it has the virtue of keeping the two distinctions separate rather than collapsing four terms into two.

On the looser reading, in which the base means the whole material foundation of a society, technology is obviously included, and this is how most textbooks present it. The looser reading is not wrong so much as imprecise, and it costs something: it makes the base a mixture of technical and social elements, which obscures exactly the question a Marxist analysis is supposed to press, namely whether the technical arrangement or the social relation is doing the explaining.

There is a third consideration that neither reading captures and that is more interesting than the classification. Marx's own analysis of machinery in the first volume of Capital treats the adoption of techniques as driven by the class relation, since machinery is introduced to raise relative surplus value and to break the resistance of skilled workers. On that account, technology is neither straightforwardly base nor straightforwardly superstructure; it is a terrain on which the relations of production are contested, and its specific forms are partly explained by that contest. A large empirical literature in the sociology of work, much of it produced by researchers with no relation to the tradition, supports this and is the strongest available evidence for a materialist account of technical change.

The practical rule for a writer is to say which reading is in use before deploying the term, and to avoid the sentence that technology determines the superstructure, which conflates the two distinctions and produces the technological determinism that the theory's critics rightly attack.

## Where culture sits, and how much autonomy it has

Culture is nominally the easiest placement in the model and is in practice the one that generated the largest body of subsequent theory, because the classical placement produces conclusions that nobody who works on culture can accept.

On the classical placement, artistic and intellectual production belongs to the forms of social consciousness and therefore corresponds to the base. Applied strictly, this yields the reflection theory: a work expresses the outlook of a class, and criticism consists in identifying which. The theory produced a great deal of unusable criticism in the middle of the twentieth century, and its failure is the reason the field moved.

Three considerations broke it. The first is Marx's own observation, in the introduction to the Grundrisse, that the artistic achievement of a society may bear no proportion to its material development, with Greek art as his example, and that the difficulty is not explaining that art in relation to its society but explaining why it continues to give pleasure long after that society has gone. That is a problem the reflection theory cannot address and Marx raises it himself.

The second is the persistence of cultural material that does not correspond to the current base at all. Williams's triad of dominant, residual, and emergent formations was constructed precisely to describe this: residual elements survive from earlier social arrangements and remain active, emergent elements anticipate arrangements that do not yet exist, and only the dominant corresponds in the way the classical model expects. A framework that can only recognise the dominant will misdescribe most of any actual cultural field.

The third is that cultural production is itself production. A publishing industry, a film industry, and a broadcasting system have wage relations, capital requirements, and competitive dynamics of their own, which means the conditions of production of cultural goods are a base-level matter concerning superstructural institutions. Analysing those conditions has proved far more productive than classifying the outputs, and it is where most serious work in the area now sits.

The autonomy question therefore has a specific answer rather than a vague one. Cultural forms are not autonomous in the sense of being unconditioned, since their conditions of production are material and describable. They are autonomous in the sense that their content is not derivable from those conditions, and any account that attempts the derivation will fail on the evidence. That formulation is Williams's in substance, and it is the position most current work operates with whether or not it cites him.

## How the model hardened into a doctrine

The distance between a few sentences in a preface and the diagram on a classroom board was covered in stages, and knowing the stages explains why the taught version is so much more rigid than the source.

The first stage was Engels's popularising work of the 1870s and 1880s, which presented the materialist conception with a systematic completeness the primary statements do not have, and which extended materialism into a general account of nature that Marx never wrote. Those works reached far more readers in the nineteenth century than anything Marx published, and they supplied the movement with a doctrine where the sources had supplied a research programme.

The second stage was the Second International's codification between the 1880s and 1914, in which the theory became a party platform and acquired the evolutionary confidence that its later critics attack. The third was the Soviet codification, in which historical materialism was taught as an application of a prior philosophical system, with the base and superstructure relation stated as a law and the boundary questions closed by authority rather than by argument. The strong reflective reading of culture, in which artistic works express class positions and are assessed accordingly, is a product of this stage and of the cultural policy that accompanied it.

The fourth stage is the one most readers encounter: the version that entered textbooks and school curricula, usually via sociology, presented as the Marxist theory of the relation between economy and society, contrasted with functionalism and Weberian analysis, and illustrated with the two-box diagram. That version descends from the third stage rather than from the first, which is why students taught it are surprised to discover that its leading twentieth-century exponents spent their careers repairing it.

The practical consequence for a reader is a habit of provenance. When encountering a confident statement of what the base and superstructure model claims, ask which stage it came from. If it presents a law-like determination with clean boundaries and a passive upper level, it descends from the codification rather than from the preface, and the primary text does not support it.

## What the model forbids, and how to use it as a test

A framework that forbids nothing explains nothing, so the most useful thing a reader can carry from this article is a statement of what the model rules out and a procedure for applying it.

The model forbids the explanation of a durable institutional order by reference to ideas, values, preferences, or intentions alone, with no account of how that order is materially reproduced and at whose expense. It does not forbid ideas from playing a causal role; it forbids an explanation that stops at them. An account of why a legal regime persisted for two centuries that never identifies who benefited, who bore the cost, who staffed and funded the enforcement, and out of whose surplus the funding came, is ruled out by the model, and a great deal of intellectual and institutional history proceeds without meeting that requirement.

The model also forbids treating a legal or political form as its own foundation. The proposition that property exists because the law recognises it, offered as a terminus, is precisely the move the model was constructed against, since it leaves unexplained why the law recognises what it recognises and why it changes when it changes.

The test that follows has three questions and can be applied to any explanation in a few minutes. First, does the account identify the relations of production it depends on, concretely enough that you can say who works for whom on what terms? Second, does it supply a mechanism connecting those relations to the institutional outcome, meaning identifiable agents acting through identifiable channels, rather than asserting a functional fit? Third, is the account comparative, so that it can say what different relations would have produced and be checked against a case where they obtained?

An explanation that passes all three is doing what the model is for. An explanation that fails the second is offering a functional correlation dressed as a cause, which is the failure mode the model's critics identify most often and which its better practitioners guard against explicitly. An explanation that fails the third has not been tested against any alternative and cannot claim to have established anything. Applying that test is the reader success condition for this article: someone who can run it on a piece of historical or sociological writing has got the analytical content of the model rather than its picture.

## The rival accounts of the same relation

The model is normally taught alongside two competitors, and knowing precisely where each disagrees sharpens what the Marxist claim actually is.

The functionalist account explains institutions by the needs of the social system: a legal order exists because societies require regulation of conflict, an educational system because they require the transmission of skills and norms. The formal similarity to the Marxist model is close enough to cause confusion, since both explain institutions by what they do rather than by what their participants intend. The substantive difference is whose needs are being served. The functionalist account attributes needs to the system as a whole and treats the institution as integrative; the Marxist account attributes interests to particular classes and treats the institution as securing the conditions of one group's appropriation from another. That difference is not cosmetic: it produces opposite predictions about whose interests will prevail when the requirements of integration and the requirements of appropriation diverge, which is precisely what happens during a period of open class conflict.

The Weberian account denies that any single class of causes can be assigned general priority. Weber's own image of ideas functioning as switchmen, determining the tracks along which the dynamic of interest pushes action, concedes the interests and disputes the direction of explanation. His analysis of bureaucratic rationalisation supplies a further rival: a process of institutional development with its own logic, driven by the technical superiority of calculable administration, which is neither derived from class interest nor reducible to it. Where the Weberian account is strongest is on the observation that legal and administrative forms have proved remarkably portable across very different economic arrangements, which is difficult for a model claiming the base explains the superstructure. Where it is weakest is that a plurality of causes with no ranking generates no research programme, since it forbids nothing.

The model's distinctive contribution against both rivals is the insistence on material reproduction. It asks a question neither competitor asks systematically: what has to keep happening, and who has to keep doing it, for this institution to persist year after year, and out of whose surplus is that activity funded. A functionalist account can say an institution meets a need without ever specifying who pays; a Weberian account can trace a process without asking whose labour sustains it. That question is the model's surviving analytical core, and it is worth more than the two-box picture that displaced it.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This is the diagram every class draws and roughly half of them draw it wrong, so the teaching problem is unusually well defined: the misconception is not vague confusion but a specific, confident, and inherited picture.

The misconception that dominates is the passive-reflection reading. Students arrive believing that the superstructure is a mirror, that the model says culture is caused by the economy, and, in the sharper versions, that Marxism claims people believe whatever their bank balance dictates. They arrive with this because it is what the diagram encodes and what most revision material says. Correcting it by assertion produces compliance without conviction, since the assertion competes with every other source they have consulted.

The question that surfaces it is a classification problem with no comfortable answer. Ask the class where the law of property belongs. Most will place it in the superstructure, correctly on the letter of the text. Then ask them to define the base without using the word ownership. The room will stall, and the stall is the lesson: the base is ordinarily specified in legal terms, and the legal order is what the base is supposed to explain. Students discovering the circularity themselves will hold it, and the discovery takes about four minutes.

The extract that resolves it is Engels's letter to Joseph Bloch of 1890, which is short, direct, and comes from the surviving author of the theory. Engels states that the economic element is ultimately determining but that anyone who twists this into the claim that it is the only determining element turns the proposition into a meaningless phrase, and he describes superstructural forms as often preponderating in determining the form that historical struggles take. Handing a class a primary text in which one of the authors attacks the version they were taught changes the discussion permanently, and it models the checking habit the course should install.

For a politically mixed room, the balance move is to teach the model as a contested and repeatedly revised construction rather than as the Marxist position. A session that presents Gramsci, Williams, and Althusser as people who thought the original formulation unusable, and asks students to judge whether their repairs succeed, gives the sceptical students the objection at full strength and the sympathetic ones the best available versions. Nobody leaves feeling preached at, and the assessment writes itself, since asking a student to place a contested institution and defend the placement tests exactly the understanding that matters.

## For students and exam candidates

The two-sentence definition that earns the mark is this: the base is the totality of the relations of production, meaning the arrangements determining who controls productive resources and how surplus labour is appropriated, while the superstructure comprises the legal and political institutions and the forms of social consciousness that correspond to it; the model claims explanatory priority for the base rather than one-way causation. Writing that, and specifically including the words explanatory priority rather than causes, signals in one clause that the candidate knows the difference between the model and its caricature.

The evaluative point that lifts the answer is the boundary problem. Stating that the relations of production are ordinarily specified in terms of ownership, that ownership is a legal relation, that law is superstructural, and that the model therefore appears to explain the superstructure by something described in superstructural terms, is a genuine analytical objection rather than a recited criticism. Adding that Cohen's redescription of production relations as de facto powers is the standard repair, and that it costs the model some of its observability, demonstrates that the candidate knows the literature rather than the summary.

Two traps recur. The first is writing that the superstructure has no effect on the base, which is refuted by the primary sources and will be marked as an error rather than as a simplification. The second is attributing false consciousness to Marx, which is a factual mistake and a costly one in any philosophy or theory paper. The answer structures for questions on this topic, and the mark logic behind each move, belong to the exam guidance in this cluster rather than to a definitional article, and candidates assembling revision material across the cluster can [save these guides and build a citation-linked set of concept notes free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), which keeps the textual anchor attached to the claim it supports rather than leaving quotations floating in a notebook without their sources. Candidates who build that habit early find the difference at revision time, because a claim without its citation cannot be used under examination conditions and a citation without its claim is dead weight.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three checks prevent the errors that appear most often in professional writing on this pair of terms.

Never cite false consciousness to Marx. The phrase belongs to Engels's letter to Franz Mehring of 1893 and appears nowhere in Marx's own writing. It is the single most reliable indicator of an unchecked source, and a writer who avoids it will be trusted by readers who know.

Check the dates and recipients of the Engels correspondence, which are misreported constantly. The relevant letters are to Joseph Bloch in 1890, to Conrad Schmidt in 1890, to Franz Mehring in 1893, and to Heinz Starkenburg in 1894, the last sometimes catalogued under a different addressee name. Getting these right takes a minute and is the difference between a piece a specialist will finish and one they will not.

Do not assert a count of how many times Marx used the metaphor. Both the claim that he used it once and the claim that he used it constantly circulate, and neither is safely verifiable across a corpus that includes journalism, correspondence, and manuscripts in several languages. The defensible statement is that the compact canonical formulation occurs in the 1859 preface, that no developed treatment exists in the published work, and that the model's prominence in the tradition greatly exceeds its prominence in the sources. That formulation makes the point without staking a number.

A fourth check applies to anyone quoting the twentieth-century revisers. Althusser's remark about the last instance, Williams's formula about limits and pressures, and Gramsci's vocabulary all circulate in paraphrases that have drifted from the originals, and the prison notebooks in particular exist in manuscript-based and edited versions that differ. Verify the wording against a scholarly edition before putting it in quotation marks.

## Using the terms correctly: a summary

Correct usage of this pair can be compressed into six rules.

Use base to mean the relations of production, not the economy in the journalistic sense of markets, prices, and commerce. If you cannot say who works for whom on what terms, you have not identified a base.

Use superstructure to mean both the legal and political institutions and the forms of social consciousness, and keep the two components distinguishable, since they change differently and relate to the base differently.

State the relation as explanatory priority rather than as causation, and if you mean the strong version, say so and accept that the comparative evidence is against you. If you mean the weak version, say so and accept that it claims relatively little.

Expect the boundary to fail on property law, the state, the family, and education, and treat that failure as the interesting feature rather than as a defect in your understanding. Those four are where the theoretical work has been done for a century, and an answer that treats them as settled will be marked down by anyone who knows the literature. The productive move on encountering a straddling institution is to specify in which respect it belongs to each level rather than to force a single placement, since the double aspect is usually the analytically interesting thing about it.

Attribute the repairs to their authors. Relative autonomy is Althusser's, limits and pressures is Williams's, the historical bloc and hegemony are Gramsci's, de facto powers is Cohen's, and the reciprocal action qualification is Engels's. Presenting any of these as Marx's own is the commonest sourcing error in writing on this subject.

Do not draw the two-box diagram without immediately identifying what it gets wrong and why the picture cannot be trusted, since the picture installs verticality, discreteness, and stasis, none of which the model requires. A reader who follows those six rules can use these terms in an essay, a seminar, an article, or a lecture without being caught out, which is the whole purpose of a definitional article. The natural next step is the [guide to the mode of production](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/), which specifies what the base actually consists of in each historical form, followed by the [debate over economic determination](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/), which takes the strong and weak readings of this relation and argues them out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the base and superstructure in simple terms?

The base is how a society organises production: who controls the productive resources, who works for whom, and how the surplus produced above subsistence is transferred. The superstructure is everything built in relation to that: the legal system, the state and its apparatus, and the prevailing forms of consciousness such as religion, morality, philosophy, and art. The model claims that the base has explanatory priority, meaning that if you want to understand why a society has the legal and political order it has, you should look to the productive relations rather than treating law and politics as self-explaining. The claim concerns durable structures rather than individual beliefs, and it does not assert one-way causation, since the surviving authors of the theory stated explicitly that superstructural forms act back on the base and often determine the form that historical conflicts take.

### Q: What exactly belongs to the base and what belongs to the superstructure?

The base contains the relations of production: the pattern of effective control over productive resources, the mechanism by which surplus labour is extracted, and the organisation of the labour process insofar as it constitutes that control. It does not contain markets, prices, interest rates, or trade patterns, which are phenomena occurring within an economy rather than the relations organising it. The superstructure contains two components the original passage distinguishes: the legal and political institutions, which are said to rise on the foundation, and the forms of social consciousness, which are said to correspond to it. Several institutions resist clean placement, notably property law, the state, education, and the family, and that resistance is the model's most theoretically productive feature rather than a failure of the reader's understanding.

### Q: Does the superstructure ever influence the base?

Yes, and this is stated directly by the theory's surviving author rather than being a later concession. Engels, writing to Joseph Bloch in 1890, says that anyone who twists the materialist conception into the claim that the economic element is the only determining one turns the proposition into a meaningless phrase, and describes superstructural elements as exercising influence over historical struggles and in many cases determining the form those struggles take. Concrete instances are abundant: a legal regime that secures property rights makes particular relations of production viable, a state that enforces contracts constitutes the conditions of the wage relation, and an educational system reproduces the distribution of skills the productive order requires. The model's claim is explanatory priority in the last analysis, not unidirectional causation, and expositions that present it as one-way are presenting a version its authors rejected.

### Q: What does relative autonomy of the superstructure mean?

It means that the superstructural levels operate according to their own logics, at their own tempo, with their own institutional interests, within limits set by the economic level rather than being determined by it directly. The term belongs to Louis Althusser and to the structuralist current of the 1960s and 1970s, and it was introduced to preserve the claim of determination while accommodating the historical evidence that legal, political, and cultural forms do not track economic arrangements closely. The qualifier does the crucial work: autonomy without qualification would mean no systematic relation at all, which is not a Marxist position. The concept's persistent difficulty is that nobody has specified the limits within which the autonomy operates, which is why critics argue that in practice relative autonomy functions as autonomy with a reassuring adjective attached.

### Q: Why do critics say the base and superstructure model is too rigid?

Because the strong version implies that legal, political, and cultural forms can be explained by reference to economic arrangements, and the comparative historical record does not support it: societies with closely similar relations of production have sustained markedly different legal systems, political constitutions, religious settlements, and cultural traditions over long periods. Defenders reply with reciprocal action, relative autonomy, and determination understood as the setting of limits, and critics then advance the sharper objection that these concessions leave the model asserting nothing a non-Marxist historian would dispute. That second objection is the more serious one, and the honest reply is that the model still forbids explaining a durable institutional order by ideas alone with no account of its material reproduction, while conceding that the weak version buys its safety at real cost.

### Q: How have later Marxists rewritten the base and superstructure model?

Engels began the revisions in his correspondence of the 1890s, insisting on reciprocal action and warning against using the conception as a formula. Antonio Gramsci, writing in prison in the late 1920s and 1930s, recast the two levels as a historical bloc in which superstructural institutions organise the consent that secures a social order, which gives them a decisive rather than a derivative role. Raymond Williams, in an essay of 1973, redefined the base as a set of activities rather than a fixed state and redefined determination as the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures. Louis Althusser introduced overdetermination, structural causality, relative autonomy, and determination in the last instance, and relocated schools, churches, and media into a functional category defined by their role in reproduction. Each of these is a repair rather than a restatement.

### Q: Is the state part of the base or the superstructure?

On the letter of the 1859 passage it is superstructural, since the legal and political apparatus is named as what rises on the foundation. In practice the placement is one of the model's hardest problems. In many social formations the state extracts surplus directly through taxation, tribute, corvee labour, or the direct organisation of production, which is a base function by the model's own criterion that the base is where surplus appropriation occurs. This is precisely why the Asiatic mode of production generated such difficulty, and why analyses of state socialist economies had to decide whether a planning apparatus was performing a base role. The defensible position is that the state is superstructural in its constitutive definition and base-performing in specific formations, and that the model requires the exception to be stated rather than smoothed over.

### Q: Where does culture sit in the base and superstructure model?

In the superstructure, among the forms of social consciousness, on the classical placement. Applied strictly this produces the reflection theory, in which cultural works express the outlook of a class, and that theory produced criticism nobody now finds usable. Three considerations broke it. Marx himself observes in the introduction to the Grundrisse that artistic achievement bears no proportion to material development and that the real puzzle is why old art still gives pleasure. Much cultural material does not correspond to the current base at all, which is why Williams introduced residual and emergent formations alongside the dominant. And cultural production is itself production, with its own wage relations and capital requirements, so the conditions under which cultural goods are made are a base-level question about superstructural institutions.

### Q: What is the difference between the superstructure and ideology?

The superstructure is a level of a social formation and it contains institutions with premises, personnel, budgets, and coercive powers: courts, legislatures, police forces, schools, established churches. Ideology is a category of content and of practice occurring within that level, concerning the forms in which people understand their situation and the practices that install those forms. A courtroom is superstructural and is not an ideology; a body of legal doctrine is both superstructural and ideological. The relation is that of a container to one of its contents. Treating the terms as interchangeable, which is common, loses the institutional half of the superstructure entirely and produces an account in which the model concerns only ideas, which then makes the state and the legal system disappear from a theory that named them first.

### Q: Did Marx use the base and superstructure metaphor often?

The compact canonical statement occurs in the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, and no developed treatment of the pair exists anywhere in his published work. He never wrote a chapter on it, never applied the terms systematically to a historical case, and never resolved the boundary questions the formulation raises. Claims that he used it exactly once and claims that he used it constantly both circulate, and neither should be repeated without a verified count across a corpus that includes journalism, correspondence, and manuscripts in several languages. What can be said safely is that the model's prominence in the tradition vastly exceeds its prominence in the sources, and that the developed version taught in textbooks belongs to the later tradition rather than to Marx.

### Q: Is technology part of the base?

It depends on how the base is defined, and both answers are defensible. On the strict reading of the canonical passage, the base is the economic structure constituted by the relations of production, while technology belongs to the productive forces, which is a separate category and the one the fetters argument concerns. On the looser reading, where the base means the whole material foundation, technology is included, which is how most textbooks present it. The looser reading costs precision, because it mixes technical and social elements and obscures the question of which is doing the explaining. A third consideration cuts across both: Marx's own analysis of machinery treats technique as adopted in order to raise relative surplus value and to weaken skilled workers' resistance, which makes technology a terrain of class conflict rather than a category to be filed.

### Q: Where does the family fit in the model?

It is the sharpest of the disputed placements. The classical placement is superstructural, since the family is a legal and ideological institution transmitting values across generations, and Althusser explicitly lists it among the ideological state apparatuses. The countervailing argument, developed at length in the literature on social reproduction, is that the domestic labour producing and maintaining labour power is production without which no wage relation could persist for a single generation, and that a model excluding it from the base has defined production so as to exclude most of the work that sustains it. Engels's work on the family, private property, and the state complicates matters further by treating the family form itself as varying with property relations. The dispute is unresolved and it is one of the model's most consequential open questions.

### Q: What is the base and superstructure in sociology?

In sociological teaching it appears as the Marxist account of the relation between economy and social institutions, usually contrasted with functionalist explanations that treat institutions as meeting system needs and with Weberian accounts that deny any single class of causes general priority. Its research use in the discipline is narrower than its teaching presence and runs mainly through the sociology of law and the sociology of education, where the questions of whose interests a legal regime secures and how educational institutions reproduce social positions descend directly from it, generally under the vocabulary of reproduction. Students should be aware that the version presented in introductory theory courses is usually the strong reflective one, which few current scholars defend, and that examiners rewarding evaluation expect awareness of the revisions.

### Q: How should the diagram be drawn?

Preferably not at all, or drawn and then immediately criticised. The two-box picture installs three errors: verticality, which implies structural support the model does not claim; discreteness, which implies a clean partition that fails for property law, the state, education, and the family; and stasis, which hides that the model concerns a relation developing over historical time. If a visual is required, a better one shows the contested institutions straddling the boundary, with arrows running in both directions and a note that the direction of explanation is a claim about priority rather than about traffic. The best substitute for a diagram is a question: ask what has to keep happening, materially, for a given institution to persist, and who does it.

### Q: Which repair of the model should a student cite in an essay?

Whichever one the question invites, named and attributed, since the marks come from knowing that repairs exist and who made them. For a question about rigidity or determinism, cite Williams on determination as the setting of limits and the exertion of pressures, which directly answers the charge. For a question about the state or about culture retaining independence, cite Althusser on relative autonomy, with the caveat that the limits have never been specified. For a question about the circularity of the base, cite Cohen's redescription of production relations as de facto powers rather than legal rights. Citing all three without distinguishing what each was fixing reads as a list; citing one and saying what problem it solves reads as command of the literature.

### Q: Did Engels contradict Marx on the superstructure?

The better-supported reading is that he clarified rather than contradicted. His letters of the 1890s insist on reciprocal action and on the real efficacy of superstructural forms, and the case for calling this a retreat rests on the assumption that the founding statement asserted a rigid one-way determination. That assumption is hard to sustain against the canonical passage itself, which locates the ideological forms as the arena in which the conflict is fought out and which distinguishes institutions that rise on the foundation from forms of consciousness that correspond to it. What is fairer to say is that Engels's own popularising works of the 1870s and 1880s presented the theory with a systematic confidence that encouraged the rigid reading, and that the letters of the 1890s were partly correcting an orthodoxy he had helped to create.

### Q: Does the model apply to societies without capitalism?

Yes, and it was formulated as a general claim about class societies rather than as an account of capitalism specifically. Applied to feudal Europe, the base is the relation between lord and peasant through which rent in labour, kind, or money is extracted, and the superstructure includes the manorial courts, the legal doctrines of tenure, and the religious institutions that legitimated the order. Applied to slave economies, the base is the relation between owner and enslaved worker, and the superstructure includes the legal machinery defining property in persons. The difficulties multiply in formations where a state apparatus is the direct extractor, since the institution named as superstructural is then performing a base function, and this is exactly the problem the Asiatic mode controversy turned on and the reason it was politically awkward in the tradition.

### Q: What are the German words for base and superstructure?

Basis and Uberbau. The second matters more than the first for an English reader, because Uberbau is ordinary German architectural and engineering vocabulary meaning what sits above a foundation, such as the superstructure of a bridge or the part of a building above ground level. It is not a coinage invented for social theory and it carries no implication of flimsiness, dependence, or passivity: a superstructure in the ordinary building sense is the functioning part of the structure and bears loads of its own. English usage has drifted, giving the word a derivative flavour that readers then import into the model, which is one source of the passive-reflection misreading. Basis carries the opposite trap, suggesting something simple and given when it names the ensemble of production relations, which is the harder of the two levels to specify.
