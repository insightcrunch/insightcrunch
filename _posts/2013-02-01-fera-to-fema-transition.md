The FERA to FEMA transition is usually told as a story about freedom, and it did set people free of something. In legal fact it began, on 1 June 2000, as a change of consequences: from that morning, a person who broke India's foreign exchange rules stopped being a criminal. The transaction itself did not change. Holding a bank account abroad without permission, paying a person outside India through an unauthorised channel, failing to bring export earnings home: each of these was forbidden on 31 May 2000 and remained forbidden on 1 June 2000. What changed overnight was the legal character of the act and the consequence of being caught. The day before, the act was an offence that could end in a prison term imposed by a criminal court. The day after, it was a contravention that could end in a money penalty imposed by an adjudicating officer. The FERA to FEMA transition, the replacement of the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1973 by the Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999, is remembered in business folklore as the moment India deregulated foreign exchange. The statute book tells a narrower and more interesting story, and this article tells it from the texts.

The claim defended through the article is a single one. The replacement changed the character of liability rather than the extent of regulation. Parliament kept the architecture of control almost intact: the channel through which foreign exchange must move, the duty to bring earnings home, the prohibition on holding assets abroad except as permitted, and the state's grip on capital movements across the border all survived the repeal. What Parliament discarded was the criminal machinery wrapped around that architecture, the prison sentence, the presumption of a guilty mind, the power of arrest, and the reach of the statute over citizens wherever on earth they lived. A reader who assumes that foreign exchange became unregulated in 2000 because it stopped being criminal has drawn exactly the wrong conclusion, and the error is not academic. The civil regime reaches into ordinary remittances, student accounts, inherited property abroad and the savings of returning migrants, and it is administered by the same enforcement agency that runs the criminal money laundering statute, so that a single set of facts can still produce two proceedings of entirely different characters.

That is why the audience for this history is not only the historian of liberalisation. It is the non-resident Indian who holds accounts in two countries and must know which legal identity the statute assigns, the chartered accountant who must explain to a client why a penalty proceeding is not a prosecution and why that distinction decides the burden of proof, the exporter who discovers that a duty imposed in 1973 is owed in identical words under the successor statute, and the family that finds a flat purchased abroad decades earlier sitting inside a seizure provision inserted into the civil Act in 2015. Each of them is governed by the residue of a repealed law and by the design choices of its replacement, and the design choices are invisible unless the two statutes are read together.

## The scarcity state and the statute it built

Exchange control in India was born as an emergency rationing system, and the rationing mentality never entirely left it. A country that earns less foreign currency than it needs for essential imports treats every dollar held by a private person as a dollar withheld from a collective pool, and a statute written in that spirit does not ask whether a transaction is harmful. It asks whether the transaction was permitted. The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1973, Act 46 of 1973, was the fullest expression of that spirit: a prohibitory code under which dealings in foreign exchange were unlawful unless affirmatively allowed, enforced by an agency with powers of search, seizure and arrest, and remembered a generation later with a shudder by the business families and the diaspora who lived under it.

### When did exchange control first become law in India?

Exchange control began as a wartime measure in 1939, when the Defence of India Rules gave the Reserve Bank power over foreign exchange dealings. Parliament put the control on a statutory footing through the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947, and an amendment in 1957 made that statute permanent rather than temporary.

The 1947 Act was a modest instrument by later standards, framed for an economy in which the overriding anxiety was the sterling balances and the import bill of a new state. It was renewed and continued rather than rethought, and it was made permanent in 1957 on the reasoning that the shortage it policed showed no sign of ending. The shortage indeed deepened. Through the nineteen sixties India financed food imports and industrialisation from a thin reserve, fought wars that consumed foreign exchange, and devalued the rupee in 1966 under external pressure. By the early nineteen seventies the policy consensus across parties held that foreign exchange was a national asset to be conserved, and the 1973 Act, passed in September of that year and brought into force on 1 January 1974, wrote that consensus into a comprehensive code. Its long title announced the purpose without embarrassment: the conservation of the foreign exchange resources of the country and their proper utilisation in the interests of economic development. Conservation first, utilisation second, and the individual holder of foreign currency nowhere in the sentence.

The Act of 1973 also arrived at the high-water mark of a particular style of Indian economic legislation, in which the state's suspicion of private foreign dealings was treated as a premise rather than a problem. The same Parliament that enacted FERA worked within a framework of industrial licensing, import licensing and monopoly control, and exchange control was simply the external wall of that structure. A manufacturer needed a licence to expand, an importer needed a licence to buy abroad, and anyone at all needed permission to hold, pay or receive foreign exchange outside the authorised channel. The first oil shock of 1973, which struck within weeks of the Act's commencement, quadrupled the price of India's largest import and converted a chronic shortage into a standing emergency, and a statute drafted for conservation found itself perfectly matched to its decade.

Section 29 of the Act carried the conservation logic into corporate ownership. A company incorporated abroad, and an Indian company with substantial non-resident ownership, could establish or carry on a place of business in India only with the permission of the Reserve Bank, and the Reserve Bank used that gate to impose the policy known as dilution: foreign shareholding in most Indian operations was to be brought down, in the general run of cases to forty per cent, with higher levels tolerated only in priority industries, export-oriented units and a few favoured sectors. The direction was administered case by case, and by the end of the decade the Reserve Bank had processed the entire population of foreign-controlled companies through it. Most diluted, issuing shares to Indian investors rather than selling down. A minority refused, and two refusals became the era's defining business story.

### Why did IBM and Coca-Cola leave India in 1977?

Section 29 of FERA made foreign companies hold Reserve Bank permission to do business in India, and the Bank directed most to dilute foreign shareholding, commonly to forty per cent. Coca-Cola refused to dilute or reveal its concentrate formula, and IBM refused to give up majority control, so both wound up their Indian operations and left in 1977.

The departures were read abroad as proof that India was closed for business, and read at home, by the Janata government that enforced the directions, as proof that sovereignty over the economy was real. Both readings were available because the episode genuinely supported them. What the episode demonstrated about the statute, beneath the politics, was the depth of its reach: a law about the conservation of currency had become a law about who could own an Indian business, because in a scarce-exchange economy every question of ownership is also a question about dividends, royalties and fees flowing outward. That fusion of exchange control with industrial policy is the first thing to grasp about FERA, because its successor deliberately separated the two. FEMA has almost nothing to say about who owns an Indian company; ownership policy migrated into the foreign investment rules and press notes, leaving the exchange statute to police the movement of value rather than the nationality of owners.

The Act's reputation, however, was made less by its corporate provisions than by its criminal ones, and by the breadth of the population they touched. Because India has one of the world's largest diasporas, a statute keyed partly to citizenship and administered through arrest powers reached into the lives of emigrants, students, sailors, air crew, visiting relatives and the families who received money from them. The unauthorised channels through which remittances moved, collectively called hawala, existed because the authorised channel was slow, priced at an official rate divorced from any market, and entangled in permission. Every family that accepted money through an informal broker, every traveller who carried currency beyond the permitted limit, every student who kept a foreign account open after returning home stood in technical breach of a criminal statute. Most were never touched. The point is that they were touchable, and a law that makes ordinary conduct criminal at the discretion of an enforcement agency acquires a reputation no amendment can launder. That reputation is the political fact that made the transition of 2000 a liberation in public memory, whatever the texts say about continuity.

## What the repealed statute actually prohibited

Read as a machine, FERA had four working parts. The first was a channel rule: foreign exchange could be dealt in only through persons the Reserve Bank authorised for the purpose, principally the authorised dealers, and Section 8 forbade any person to buy, borrow, sell or transfer foreign exchange outside that channel except as permitted. The second was a payments rule: Section 9 restricted payments to persons resident outside India, payments on behalf of such persons, and the placing of sums to their credit, again except as permitted by the Act or by the Reserve Bank. The third was a set of asset rules: restrictions on the issue of bearer securities and on the transfer of securities to non-residents, a duty cast on persons entitled to receive foreign exchange to realise it and offer it as directed, rules governing the export of goods and the realisation of their proceeds under Section 18, and, under Section 31, a prohibition on acquiring or holding immovable property outside India except with permission. The fourth part was an import and export control on currency itself under Section 13, policing the physical movement of notes, bullion and securities across the border.

Two features of this scheme deserve emphasis because both were reversed in 2000 and both are routinely forgotten. The first is the default. FERA's prohibitions were general and the permissions were exceptions carved out by the Reserve Bank, so the legal question in any transaction was whether a permission existed that covered it. Silence in the rulebook meant prohibition. The second is the personal reach. By Section 1 the Act applied to all citizens of India outside India and to branches and agencies outside India of Indian companies, so citizenship itself carried the statute across borders. A citizen who had settled abroad permanently remained, in the Act's design, a person whose foreign dealings the Indian state could in principle regulate, a claim of jurisdiction that sat naturally with an era of closed capital accounts and uneasily with everything that followed.

Around these provisions sat the apparatus of permissions that structured ordinary life: permission to open and keep a foreign currency account, permission to hold shares in a foreign company, permission to take up employment abroad and retain its earnings in specified ways, Reserve Bank approval for a foreign company to open a branch, for an Indian firm to appoint an agent abroad, for a resident to invest in a joint venture overseas. The Reserve Bank published manuals and guidelines that softened the statute's edges, and from the nineteen eighties onward a growing body of general permissions replaced case-by-case approval in routine matters. The statute's text, though, remained prohibitory, and the general permissions were revocable grace rather than right. Contemporary criticism of the Act from within the system made exactly this distinction: the administration of FERA had liberalised considerably by the nineteen nineties, but the law on the page still described a regime in which the citizen held foreign exchange on sufferance.

The securities and agency provisions extended the net over the quieter forms of cross-border life. The issue of bearer securities, instruments payable to whoever holds them and therefore invisible to any register, was restricted outright, a provision aimed at the anonymous movement of value. Transfers of shares and securities in Indian companies to persons resident outside India required the Reserve Bank's permission, so that the share register of an Indian company was, in effect, a supervised border. A person in India could not, without permission, act as the agent, technical adviser or representative of a foreign principal in a range of transactions, and a resident could not take up or continue employment arrangements and directorships abroad in defiance of the permission structure. None of these provisions punished dramatic conduct; they made the ordinary connective tissue of an international business career, the agency agreement, the foreign directorship, the share transfer to a partner abroad, into licensed acts. The cumulative picture is of a statute that did not distinguish in kind between the smuggler's courier and the consultant's retainer, because its unit of concern was never the morality of the act but the movement of value across the line.

The export provisions show the same architecture from the trade side. Section 18 required the exporter to furnish a declaration that the full export value would be realised, and cast the duty to do all that was necessary to ensure the proceeds came home within the prescribed period. Behind that duty stood the balance-of-payments logic of the whole code: an export was not a private sale but a national earning event, and the exporter's freedom to leave the price abroad, to under-invoice, or to delay repatriation was a leak in the collective pool. Under-invoicing of exports and over-invoicing of imports were accordingly the classic FERA offences of the trading class, and the enforcement directorate's most important cases of the era were built on valuation, on proving that the paper price and the real price of a consignment had parted company. The successor statute kept the realisation duty in nearly identical substance, which is one of the cleanest proofs of this article's claim: the obligation that generated the era's largest prosecutions survived the repeal word for word in spirit, and only the consequence of breaching it changed.

The daily texture of the regime was set less by the Act than by the Reserve Bank's Exchange Control Manual, the consolidated book of directions through which the bank administered its permissions, and by the general permissions and circulars that accumulated within it. The manual is the forgotten artefact of the FERA decades: a working text, revised as policy shifted, that told the authorised dealer at the counter whether a student's fees, a patient's treatment abroad, a business traveller's expenses or a deceased emigrant's repatriation could be released, and on what proof. Its existence refined the citizen's experience in a particular way. The statute's prohibition was absolute in form, but life was lived at the level of the manual, where the operative question was documentary: which form, which certificate, which chartered accountant's statement would unlock the permission. Critics of the era who described FERA as a licence raj were describing this layer, the conversion of rights into applications. It is equally the layer at which the regime quietly liberalised across the nineteen eighties, as general permissions replaced individual approvals category by category, so that the FERA of 1995 was a considerably more porous system than the FERA of 1975 even before the 1993 amendments rewrote parts of the text. The transition of 2000 must be measured against that moving baseline, and one of this article's recurring points is that the baseline's movement was administrative long before it was legislative.

## Adjudication, prosecution and the presumption: the FERA enforcement machine

FERA's severity did not lie only in what it prohibited. It lay in a double enforcement track that allowed the state to proceed against the same act twice, in two forums, under two standards, with imprisonment waiting at the end of one of them. The first track was departmental adjudication. Under Sections 50 and 51, an adjudicating officer of the enforcement directorate could hold an inquiry and impose a penalty of up to five times the amount or value involved in the contravention, or five thousand rupees, whichever was greater. The second track was criminal prosecution under Section 56, and the section opened with the words that made the design unmistakable: the award of a penalty by the adjudicating officer was no bar, because prosecution lay without prejudice to it. For an offence involving more than one lakh of rupees, conviction carried imprisonment of not less than six months and up to seven years, with a fine; for smaller offences, imprisonment of up to three years, or fine, or both. A person who failed to pay a penalty or obey an order of the adjudicating officer, the Appellate Board or the High Court faced a further offence under Section 57, punishable with imprisonment of up to two years.

The prosecution track was then tilted by Section 59, the presumption of culpable mental state. Where an offence required a guilty mind, the court was directed to presume that the accused had it, and the accused carried the burden of proving its absence. The section added a refinement that a defence lawyer of the era could recite from memory: for the purposes of that proof, a fact was to be treated as proved only when the court believed it to exist beyond reasonable doubt, and not merely on a preponderance of probability. The consequence was an inversion that startles lawyers trained in other systems. The prosecution enjoyed a presumption on the central question of intent, while the accused, in displacing it, was held to the criminal standard ordinarily reserved for the prosecution itself. Section 59 applied, with the necessary changes, to adjudication proceedings as well, so the tilt operated on both tracks. Litigation under the Act circled repeatedly around the material generated by this machinery: statements recorded by enforcement officers from persons summoned before them, and the evidentiary worth of such statements when retracted. In K.T.M.S. Mohd. v Union of India the Supreme Court examined the status of a person summoned to give evidence under the Act and the distinction between the adjudication and the prosecution that might follow, and in Vinod Solanki v Union of India the Court held that a retracted confessional statement could not, without corroboration, carry the weight the prosecution asked it to bear. Both decisions moderated the machinery at its edges; neither disturbed its design.

Corporate accused received no shelter either. In Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement, decided in 2005 on prosecutions launched in the FERA years, the Supreme Court held that a company could be prosecuted for an offence carrying a mandatory term of imprisonment, the court imposing fine in place of the imprisonment the company could not serve. The judgment belongs to the long tail of FERA litigation that outlived the Act itself, and it illustrates a point the transition section of this article develops: the repeal of 2000 stopped new criminality from arising, but the criminal past of the statute took years of litigation to exhaust.

Alongside the two tracks stood a third instrument that belonged to neither, and its existence completes the picture of the era's enforcement philosophy. The Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act 1974 empowered the state to detain a person, without charge or trial, with a view to preventing that person from acting in any manner prejudicial to the conservation or augmentation of foreign exchange resources. Preventive detention is the state's most uncompromising power, reserved in the constitutional scheme for threats to security and public order, and its routine availability for exchange offences placed the hawala dealer and the offending exporter, in the eyes of the law, in the company of the smuggler and the saboteur. Detention orders under that statute were challenged throughout the FERA decades and the power was upheld, and it is telling that when the Supreme Court came to describe the post-2000 order in Dropti Devi v Union of India, a case about such a detention, it did so by contrasting the detention statute's survival with the decriminalisation of the exchange law itself: the exchange offence had become a civil contravention, while the detention power built for the exchange offence remained on the book.

Around the two tracks stood the coercive powers that gave the directorate its fearsome name: powers to summon and examine persons, to call for documents, to search premises and conveyances, to seize currency, documents and property connected with a contravention, and to arrest a person believed to have committed an offence under the Act, with the arrested person produced before a magistrate without delay. The Act also contained its own modest correctives, including a provision punishing vexatious search and arrest by enforcement officers, a provision whose existence in the text concedes what the era's critics alleged about the practice. The honest summary of the machine is that it was designed, deliberately and coherently, for an economy in which foreign exchange offences were ranked with smuggling as offences against the state itself. Smuggling law and exchange law shared officers, procedures and presumptions, and the citizen who kept an unauthorised account abroad was processed by a system built for the gold smuggler. Whether that ranking was ever proportionate is the controversy the verdict section of this article takes up; that it was the design is not in doubt.

## The extraterritorial claim: citizens, branches and the statute that travelled

FERA's application to citizens abroad deserves a fuller examination than it usually receives, because it marks the outer limit of the conservation philosophy and its abandonment marks the philosophical core of the replacement. A state asserts jurisdiction over its nationals abroad for many purposes, taxation and treason among them, but the regulation of a settled emigrant's ordinary banking life is a stronger claim: it treats emigration as a change of address rather than a change of legal allegiance for economic purposes. The claim had a fiscal logic in the era of scarce reserves, when the emigrant's earnings were a resource the home state hoped eventually to attract home, and when the line between the emigrant and the resident agent of an Indian business was genuinely difficult to police. It also produced the statute's most resented encounters, the returning emigrant interrogated about accounts lawfully opened and lawfully taxed in the country of residence, the student treated as a conduit, the seafarer and the air crew member whose working lives were spent in foreign currency. The branches and agencies limb carried the same philosophy into corporate life: the Dubai office of an Indian trading house was, for exchange purposes, an outpost of the resident economy, its accounts and dealings answerable to the home code.

FEMA's redesign is therefore more than a jurisdictional tidy-up. By keying the regime to residence, and by defining residence through the intention-sensitive test examined later in this article, the new Act accepts that the emigrant's economic centre of gravity can genuinely move, and confines its extraterritorial reach to the establishments through which residents act abroad. The acceptance is principled and it is also practical: a residence-based regime can be administered through the banking system of the territory, while a citizenship-based regime requires the state to police persons it cannot see, through agencies it does not control, over accounts it cannot inspect. The 2015 seizure provision is best understood against this background. Having surrendered the citizenship claim in 2000, the state found in 2015 that it needed a way to reach the resident who abused the residence-based system by parking assets abroad, and it built the reach not out of personal jurisdiction over the foreign asset, which it lacked, but out of the resident's Indian assets, which it had. Section 37A is the conservation philosophy returning through the property door after being shown out of the personal one, and the litigation over its reach is, at bottom, litigation over how far that return may go.

## The hawala economy and the diaspora: the human material of FERA

No account of why FERA was feared is complete without the parallel economy it created. Where a state fixes the price of foreign currency below its market level, rations access to it, and criminalises the alternatives, an unofficial market is not a pathology but a prediction. The hawala network, built on brokers who settled balances across borders without moving money through the banking system at all, moved remittances for emigrant workers, lubricated under-invoiced trade, and served every customer the official channel priced out, delayed or refused. Its customers were not a criminal class. They were the Gulf worker whose family needed money faster than the authorised channel delivered it, the small importer whose licence covered part of his order, the family paying a foreign university through an intermediary because the paperwork had failed them. FERA's criminal provisions fell on this population with the same text they applied to the professional smuggler, and the political memory of the statute, in the diaspora above all, is the memory of that indiscrimination. The Indian state's own conduct conceded the channel's failure in advance: when reserves ran short, the state repeatedly went courting the emigrant's dollars through special instruments, from the India Development Bonds issued in the crisis of 1991 to the Resurgent India Bonds of 1998, offering the diaspora premium terms through the front door while the statute threatened the diaspora's informal arrangements at the back.

The hawala economy also produced the FERA era's most consequential prosecution failure. The diaries seized from the Jain brothers in 1991 recorded payments, attributed by initials, to a roll of senior politicians and officials, and the ensuing prosecutions under FERA became a national scandal about the corruption of the political class. The Supreme Court's intervention in Vineet Narain v Union of India restructured the supervision of the investigating agencies in response, a lasting constitutional legacy. The FERA charges themselves collapsed in the trial court for want of admissible evidence connecting the diaries to the payments alleged. The episode deserves its place in this history because it displayed, at the highest level of public attention, the two truths about the statute that its defenders and critics each held separately. The reach was real: the exchange law could be, and was, aimed at the most powerful people in the country. And the machinery was brittle: for all its presumptions and powers, the criminal track demanded proof of transactions designed to leave no trace, and when the proof failed, the criminal case failed with it, while the adjudication machinery that might have absorbed such conduct at a civil standard had spent decades as the prosecution's junior partner, used for penalties but rarely trusted as the regime's centre of gravity. FEMA's design, in which the civil track is the whole of the law for the generality of conduct, can be read as the institutional lesson of that collapse.

## The decade that made repeal unavoidable

Two events converted the case for a new statute from respectable to irresistible. The first was the balance-of-payments crisis of 1991, when India's reserves fell to a level covering only a few weeks of imports, gold was pledged abroad to secure emergency credit, and the government of Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao, with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister, began the liberalisation programme that dismantled industrial licensing and opened the economy to trade and investment. A state that invites foreign capital cannot plausibly maintain a statute premised on the criminality of unauthorised foreign dealings, and the contradiction was visible to every investor the new policy courted. The second event was quieter: in August 1994 India accepted the obligations of Article VIII of the International Monetary Fund's Articles of Agreement, undertaking not to restrict payments for current international transactions. Current account convertibility, as the commitment was called, could be honoured in practice while FERA stood, but only through the fiction that a prohibitory statute administered liberally equalled a permissive one. The gap between the international commitment and the domestic text became the technicians' argument for repeal, complementing the politicians' argument from reputation.

The first repair was attempted inside the old statute. The Foreign Exchange Regulation (Amendment) Act 1993, brought into force on 8 January 1993, cut back a series of FERA's restrictions, liberalised the rules on residents holding foreign exchange and foreign assets in defined situations, eased the position of foreign companies and non-resident Indians, and aligned the text with the first phase of reform. The amendments were substantial enough that a reader comparing the 1974 text with the 1999 text would see two different regulatory climates. What the amendments could not change was the statute's grammar. The scheme remained prohibition first, the offences remained criminal, Section 59 remained on the page, and the enforcement directorate remained an agency of arrest. Amendment could soften a criminal code; it could not convert one into a management framework, and the government's own reformers said so in the language of the time. The committee appointed under S. S. Tarapore to chart a phased move towards capital account convertibility reported in 1997 on the assumption that the exchange law itself would be recast, and the Finance Ministry's drafting of a replacement began from that premise.

The sequencing within the decade carries a further lesson that the repeal's celebrants usually omit. The Asian financial crisis of 1997 struck economies that had opened their capital accounts faster than their banking systems could bear, and India's escape from serious contagion was credited, by the Reserve Bank and by outside observers alike, in part to the very controls that reform had marked for demolition. The crisis did not stop the replacement of FERA, which proceeded on schedule to enactment two years later, but it shaped the replacement's deepest design decision. Section 6's retention of capital account control was not an oversight or a failure of nerve left over from the old regime; it was a deliberate hedge, priced by the experience of 1997, and the Tarapore framework's own conditionalities, on inflation, fiscal deficit, banking soundness and reserve adequacy, supplied the official vocabulary for keeping the gate while removing the prison. Any account of the transition that treats the surviving controls as mere residue misunderstands the document: the drafters of FEMA had watched open capital accounts fail in the neighbourhood and chose, with their eyes open, a statute that could be opened by regulation in good times and closed by regulation in bad ones.

The passage record of the Bill shows the two anxieties that any decriminalisation of exchange offences was bound to attract, and the enacted text answers both, which is worth noticing because the answers became permanent features of the regime. The first anxiety was indulgence: that converting offences into contraventions would be read, by the hawala networks and by the class of offenders the old Act had pursued through the nineteen nineties, as the state's surrender. The answer built into the Bill was quantum and machinery, a penalty ceiling of three times the sum involved, confiscation powers over the property involved in a contravention, and a directorate carried across the repeal intact, so that the audience for the change of label would find the enforcement institution unchanged and its money remedies enlarged. The second anxiety was the opposite one, pressed from the side of the citizen: that a penalty of three times an unquantifiable sum is no ceiling at all, and that civil process without criminal safeguards would let the department convert weak cases into heavy settlements. The answer to that anxiety is visible in the two-lakh ceiling for sums that cannot be quantified, in the daily penalty's modest figure, in the compounding route for the small case, and in the two-tier appeal structure with the High Court at its head on questions of law. Neither anxiety was irrational, and the statute's durability owes something to the fact that its drafters answered both in the text rather than dismissing either in debate.

The drafting produced a bill in 1998, which was examined by the Standing Committee on Finance and returned with the benefit of that scrutiny, and the replacement was enacted in December 1999. The interval between the 1993 softening and the 1999 replacement is instructive about how Indian economic statutes actually change: the practice liberalised first, the amending Act ratified the practice, and the replacement statute arrived last to give the new practice a coherent legal form. Anyone assessing the claim that 2000 was a rupture must reckon with the fact that by 2000 the lived regime had been migrating for a decade. The rupture was real, but it was a rupture in legal character, executed at the end of a long drift in administration, and that is precisely why its effects were concentrated in liability rather than in the day-to-day permissions of trade.

## Writing the replacement: management in place of regulation

The Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999, Act 42 of 1999, received the assent of the President on 29 December 1999 and was brought into force on 1 June 2000. The five-month interval was used to build the regulatory understructure, because the new Act was drafted as a skeleton: its operative content was to live in rules made by the Central Government and regulations made by the Reserve Bank under Sections 46 and 47, notified in time for the commencement date. The long title announced the programme in a single sentence that repays attention, since every word of it reverses a word of its predecessor. FEMA is an Act to consolidate and amend the law relating to foreign exchange with the objective of facilitating external trade and payments and for promoting the orderly development and maintenance of the foreign exchange market in India. FERA's long title had spoken of conservation and proper utilisation; the successor speaks of facilitation and of a market to be developed and maintained. The change of title from regulation to management was criticised at the time as cosmetic, and the criticism is worth taking seriously rather than repeating, because it identifies exactly the question the rest of the Act has to answer: whether the machinery beneath the gentler vocabulary had genuinely changed.

In its central design choices, it had. The new Act divided the universe of transactions into two classes and treated them in opposite ways. Current account transactions, defined in Section 2(j) to cover payments due in the ordinary course of trade and business, interest and investment income, remittances for living expenses of family abroad, and expenditure on travel, education and medical care, were set free by Section 5: any person may sell or draw foreign exchange from an authorised person if the transaction is a current account transaction, subject to reasonable restrictions which the Central Government may impose in the public interest and in consultation with the [Reserve Bank](/2012/12/01/rbi-act-1934-explained/), whose own governing statute assigns it the management of the currency and the external value of the rupee. The default for the transactions of ordinary life was reversed: permitted unless restricted, in place of forbidden unless permitted. Capital account transactions, defined in Section 2(e) as transactions that alter the assets or liabilities, including contingent liabilities, of residents outside India or of non-residents in India, were kept under control by Section 6, under which the Reserve Bank, in consultation with the Central Government, specifies the classes that are permissible and the limits up to which they may be undertaken. The conservatism of the old regime survived, but it was relocated to the category where the state genuinely feared instability, and stated openly as control rather than disguised as a general prohibition from which capital movements were one exception among many.

The liability provisions then did the work the title promised. Section 13 made contravention a civil matter, punishable on adjudication with a penalty of up to three times the sum involved where the amount is quantifiable, up to two lakh rupees where it is not, and a further penalty of up to five thousand rupees for each day a continuing contravention persists. Section 14 attached the statute's only general imprisonment, and attached it indirectly: a person who fails to pay the penalty in full within ninety days of the demand is liable to civil imprisonment, the coercive process of a debtor rather than the punishment of an offender. Section 15 introduced compounding, under which most contraventions could be settled by payment of a sum to the compounding authority, closing the matter without adjudication. Sections 16 to 19 built the forum: an Adjudicating Authority appointed by the Central Government, an appeal to the Special Director (Appeals), a further appeal to the Appellate Tribunal for Foreign Exchange, and, under Section 35, an appeal to the High Court on a question of law. Section 36 established a Directorate of Enforcement under the new Act, and Section 37 armed it with investigative powers modelled on those of the income tax authorities, including search and seizure, but not with the old power to arrest for the contravention itself. The vocabulary of offence, accused and conviction was replaced throughout by the vocabulary of contravention, adjudication and penalty, and the replacement was carried into the burden of proof, as a later section explains.

The regulations notified for the commencement date show the skeleton being clothed, and their titles are a map of the new order's priorities. The Reserve Bank issued regulations on permissible capital account transactions, which performed Section 6's sorting of capital movements into allowed classes; on the acquisition and transfer of immovable property in and outside India; on deposits; on the transfer and issue of securities; on borrowing and lending; on the export of goods and services; and on the realisation and repatriation of foreign exchange. The Central Government's Current Account Transactions Rules carried the Section 5 restrictions in their three schedules. None of this material is in the Act, and all of it is the law. A citizen who read only FEMA in June 2000 would have known the constitution of the new regime and almost nothing of its content, which is the sharpest formal difference between the two statutes: FERA was a code that aspired to contain its subject, and FEMA is a framework that deliberately does not. Whether that is a vice depends on the value placed on parliamentary visibility against administrative speed, and the argument recurs in the verdict; the fact itself is the necessary starting point for any reader who proposes to use the new law rather than merely admire its preamble.

## What was kept, what was discarded, what was added

A repeal and replacement is best audited as three separate transactions, because the political argument usually merges them. Begin with what FEMA kept, since it is the largest of the three categories and the least acknowledged. It kept the authorised channel: Section 3 forbids dealing in foreign exchange except through an authorised person, and Section 4 preserves the core asset prohibition in modern dress, providing that no person resident in India shall acquire, hold, own, possess or transfer any foreign exchange, foreign security or immovable property situated outside India except as provided under the Act. It kept the export machinery: Section 7 requires the exporter to furnish a declaration of the full value of the goods or services, and Section 8 imposes the duty to realise and repatriate foreign exchange due, the lineal descendant of FERA's Section 18 discipline. It kept the enforcement agency, re-founded under Section 36 but continuous in personnel, method and institutional memory with the directorate that had policed FERA. It kept, above all, the state's discretion over capital movements, exercised through regulations that can be rewritten without returning to Parliament, so that the effective law of foreign investment, borrowing and overseas acquisition sits in subordinate legislation under both regimes.

### What is an authorised dealer under FEMA?

An authorised dealer is a bank or other person authorised by the Reserve Bank under Section 10 of FEMA to deal in foreign exchange or foreign securities. Almost every lawful transaction with an overseas element must pass through an authorised dealer, which checks the transaction against the regulations, reports it and keeps the records.

What FEMA discarded was the criminal skeleton. Gone was the prosecution track for the generality of contraventions and with it the six-month minimum sentence of FERA's Section 56. Gone was the presumption of culpable mental state, and with it the accused's burden of disproving intent beyond reasonable doubt. Gone was the power to arrest for a contravention, and gone the offence of disobeying a penalty order that FERA's Section 57 had created. Gone, too, was the citizenship keying of the old Act: FEMA extends to the whole of India and to branches, offices and agencies outside India owned or controlled by persons resident in India, so its personal reach follows residence and control rather than the passport, and the emigrant who has genuinely settled abroad passes out of the regime altogether. The adjudication penalty was even reduced in quantum, from five times the amount involved under FERA to three times under Section 13, a small numerical confession that the old multiple had been set by the logic of deterrence of crime rather than by the logic of regulatory penalty.

One element of continuity deserves separate notice because it reverses the usual direction of the reform story. Both statutes had to solve the problem of the corporate contravener, and both solved it the same way. FERA's Section 68 deemed the persons in charge of a company's business liable for its offences unless they proved absence of knowledge or due diligence, alongside the company itself. FEMA's Section 42 reproduces the structure for contraventions: the company and every person who was in charge of and responsible for the conduct of its business at the time are deemed liable to the penalty, with the same defence of no knowledge or all due diligence, and with liability extended to directors, managers and officers whose consent, connivance or neglect contributed. Decriminalisation changed what such persons risk: prosecution and prison under the old section, penalty under the new. It did not change the attribution of responsibility within the firm, and the boardroom lesson of the transition is that the personal exposure of officers survived in financial form, which is why FEMA compliance sits on the agenda of audit committees in a way that the folklore of deregulation would never predict.

What FEMA added was mostly machinery for making a civil regime workable, together with one provision of genuine novelty in its consequences. The machinery included compounding, which converted the settlement of contraventions from an exercise of prosecutorial grace into a statutory route with published procedure; the two-tier appeal structure with a specialist tribunal; and the classification of every transaction as current or capital account, which gave the system a sorting rule FERA had never needed because it prohibited first and classified later. The novelty was in the replacement's treatment of time. FEMA did not merely repeal FERA prospectively. In Section 49 it reached back and set a fuse under the entire criminal past of the old Act, and the working of that fuse is the most under-reported part of the transition. The claims made for the transition as a whole are tested against the comparison table in a later section; the wind-down deserves its own examination first, because it is where the new Parliament stated, in operative words, how seriously it meant the decriminalisation.

## The FEMA machine in daily operation

A framework statute is only as real as the machinery that runs it, and FEMA's machinery deserves description because it explains how a permissive Act produces a compliance-heavy daily experience. The system's front line is not the enforcement directorate but the authorised dealer, the bank through which virtually every lawful transaction with an overseas element must pass. The authorised dealer is the statute's chosen instrument of distributed enforcement: it examines the transaction against the regulations before executing it, collects the declarations the regulations require, allots the purpose codes under which the Reserve Bank classifies cross-border flows, reports the transaction into the Reserve Bank's monitoring systems, and retains the documents against future inquiry. A remittance that the regulations prohibit does not reach an enforcement officer; it stops at a bank counter, which is precisely the design. The criminal law of FERA policed transactions after they happened, with the apparatus of prosecution. The civil law of FEMA polices them before they happen, with the apparatus of banking compliance, and the second apparatus touches immeasurably more transactions than the first ever did.

The dealer's own position under the two regimes marks the change as sharply as the citizen's. Under FERA the authorised dealer was a licensee inside a rationing system, dispensing permissions the Reserve Bank had framed and sheltering behind them; the dealer's legal safety lay in literal compliance with the manual, and its commercial instinct was refusal wherever the manual was silent. Under FEMA the dealer is a regulated gatekeeper with judgement to exercise: the regulations assign it responsibility for ensuring that a transaction is permissible before executing it, for the know-your-customer diligence that identifies the customer and the purpose, and for reporting that feeds the Reserve Bank's electronic monitoring of cross-border flows. A dealer that executes a prohibited transaction faces action against its authorisation and penalties of its own, which converts the bank from the citizen's counterparty into the state's first examiner. The consequence for the citizen is a distinctive feature of the modern regime that no reading of the Act discloses: the effective law is applied, in the first instance, by a bank's compliance department, whose institutional caution frequently makes the operative rule stricter than the written one, and whose refusal is, in practice, unappealable except by taking the business elsewhere or seeking the Reserve Bank's clarification. FERA's citizen queued before an officer of the state. FEMA's citizen negotiates with an officer of a bank exercising the state's delegated suspicion, and the second encounter, though gentler in every formal respect, is not obviously the more accountable one.

Above the dealers sits the Reserve Bank's regulatory stack, and its texture matters for anyone trying to state what the law is. The Act supplies the frame. The Central Government's rules govern current account transactions and a handful of structural matters. The Reserve Bank's regulations, notified in the Gazette and numbered in series, govern the substance: acquisition of property, deposits, borrowing and lending, export of goods and services, overseas investment, and the rest of the working catalogue. Beneath the regulations sit the Master Directions, consolidated statements of the operative instructions, and beneath those the circulars, styled A.P. (DIR Series), by which the Reserve Bank adjusts the system between consolidations, raising a ceiling here, adding a reporting requirement there, extending a deadline in a crisis. The stack is amended scores of times in a busy year. Its virtue is responsiveness, the quality the 1997 crisis taught the drafters to prize. Its cost is the one recorded earlier in this article: the law a citizen must obey is no longer fully visible in any single document, and the distance between the Act's permissive vocabulary and the system's operative strictness is filled entirely by instruments most citizens will never read. Compounding sits at the end of the machine as its safety valve, and the volume of compounding applications, from multinational companies regularising investment reporting delays to individuals regularising an inherited account, is the standing measure of the gap between the system's strictness on paper and the innocence of most of the conduct it catches.

## The wind-down: Section 49 and the two-year sunset

Section 49 of FEMA is a repeal and savings provision of unusual aggression. Its first sub-section repeals the 1973 Act outright. Its fourth sub-section performs the conventional saving: subject to what follows, anything done under the repealed Act continues to be governed by it, so that offences committed before 1 June 2000 remained punishable under FERA and investigations and proceedings for them could continue as though the repeal had not happened. Standing alone, that saving would have guaranteed the old criminal regime a long afterlife, with prosecutions for the hawala era winding through the courts for decades, as tax and customs prosecutions routinely do. The third sub-section cut the afterlife short. It provided that, notwithstanding anything in any other law, no court shall take cognizance of an offence under the repealed Act, and no adjudicating officer shall take notice of a contravention under Section 51 of the repealed Act, after the expiry of two years from the commencement of FEMA. The window therefore closed on 31 May 2002. A FERA prosecution that had been filed by then could run its course, and many did, some for years. A FERA prosecution that had not been filed by then could never be filed at all. The section closed with a drafting precaution that reveals the care taken over the extinction: a final sub-section preserved the general application of Section 6 of the General Clauses Act 1897 to the effects of the repeal, so that the specific savings in the section were not to be read as narrowing the background law on accrued rights and liabilities beyond what the sunset itself ordained. The relationship between the two instruments was litigated in the transition's early cases, and the working reconciliation that emerged respects both texts: the General Clauses Act saving governs the consequences of the repeal in general, while Section 49(3) imposes its own overriding deadline on the initiation of punitive proceedings, a deadline that operates notwithstanding anything in any other law.

The device deserves a moment's analysis, because it is a rare example of a legislature using a savings clause to impose mortality on its own predecessor's enforcement. An amnesty would have forgiven the past offences; Parliament did not forgive them. A simple repeal with a full General Clauses Act saving would have preserved them indefinitely; Parliament did not preserve them. Instead it gave the enforcement agency a deadline measured in months to convert its files into complaints or lose them, and it coupled the deadline with the continuity provisions of Section 49(5), under which things done under the old Act were deemed done under the new where consistent with it, and appeals pending before FERA's Appellate Board stood transferred to the new Appellate Tribunal. The combined effect was a managed extinction: the regulatory content carried forward seamlessly, the appellate content carried forward institutionally, and the punitive content was given two years to live. In Dropti Devi v Union of India, the Supreme Court, reciting the structure of the new Act in the course of upholding a preventive detention, quoted the sunset clause as part of its account of a legislature that had made foreign exchange violations civil and compoundable. The description is accurate, and the sunset is the strongest textual evidence for it, because a Parliament that merely wished to relabel the regime would not have bothered to bury its prosecutions.

For individuals caught in the machinery, the sunset produced outcomes that turned on dates rather than merits, and any honest account must record that as a cost of the device. The trader whose file the directorate completed in 2001 faced prosecution under a criminal code with a statutory presumption against him; the trader whose identical file was completed in 2003 faced nothing at all under the old law, whatever the new law said about the future. Limitation always draws such lines, and the two-year fuse was defensible as the price of finality for an entire class of disputes arising from a disowned economic era. But the device also created a filing rush in its closing months and a generation of litigation over whether particular complaints had been taken cognizance of in time, litigation that turned on the precise meaning of cognizance and on the dates stamped on complaints in magistrates' courts across the country. The criminal regime thus ended as it had operated, in procedural contest, and the last FERA arguments heard in Indian courts were not about whether the accused had broken the rules of a closed economy but about whether the state had beaten a clock of its own legislature's making.

## What actually changed: reading the comparison

The full side-by-side treatment of the two statutes, provision by provision, belongs to [the FERA and FEMA comparison](/2013/04/15/fera-vs-fema-comparison/), which owns that head-to-head. The table set out here answers the narrower question this article has pursued: across the dimensions that decide a person's practical position, which of the celebrated changes of 2000 were substantive and which were formal, in the sense that the operative constraint on conduct survived the change of label. The count of formal rows is the argument in miniature.

| Dimension | FERA 1973 | FEMA 1999 | Substantive or formal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scheme of the statute | Prohibitory: dealings forbidden except as permitted | Permissive for current account, regulated for capital account | Formal for most transactions, substantive at the default |
| Nature of liability | Criminal offence, with a parallel civil penalty | Civil contravention attracting penalty | Substantive |
| Standard of proof | Beyond reasonable doubt, aided by a presumption of culpable mental state | Preponderance of probabilities before an adjudicating authority | Substantive |
| Forum | Criminal court, alongside departmental adjudication | Adjudicating Authority, Special Director (Appeals), Appellate Tribunal, High Court on law | Substantive |
| Sanction | Imprisonment of six months to seven years for larger offences, plus penalty of up to five times the sum | Penalty of up to three times the sum; civil imprisonment only for default in payment | Substantive |
| Applicability test | Residence, and citizenship for Indians abroad | Residence, with intention built into the test | Substantive at the margin |
| Current account transactions | Permitted case by case under a prohibitory code | Free unless reasonably restricted | Substantive in default, modest in daily practice |
| Capital account transactions | Controlled through permission | Controlled through specified permissible classes and limits | Formal: control retained in full |

Two rows reward a slower reading than the table allows. The forum row conceals a change in the sociology of the dispute. A FERA prosecution placed the citizen before a magistrate, in a criminal court, in public, with the moral vocabulary of guilt and the procedural armour of a trial, and the state bore the costs of delay that armour imposes. A FEMA adjudication places the citizen before an officer of the enforcing department's own cadre, in a proceeding the department initiates and documents, with appeal outside the department only at the second stage. Defenders of the design call this specialisation and speed, and the tribunal ladder that follows, ending in the High Court, supplies the judicial supervision the first stage lacks. Critics answer that the first stage decides most cases in practice, since appeals are costly, and that a system in which the investigator's institution also adjudicates must work harder to demonstrate its fairness, through reasoned orders, hearings genuinely granted, and the proportionality doctrine the appellate courts have built. Both readings are available on the record of two decades, and the design's legitimacy in any given case is earned at the hearing, not inherited from the statute.

The sanction row conceals a change in who pays for enforcement's mistakes. Under FERA, a prosecution that failed after years of trial left the accused with costs the system never repaid, in money, reputation and liberty of movement, and the state's own failure was free. Under FEMA, an adjudication that fails on appeal fails faster and cheaper for both sides, and the penalty, if collected and then set aside, is refundable in a way that years of a criminal trial are not. Decriminalisation is often discussed as a transfer of advantage from the state to the citizen, and in the matter of sanctions it was. In the matter of process it was nearer a trade: the citizen surrendered the trial's armour and received in exchange speed, privacy, proportion and a money ceiling, and the fairness of the trade in the individual case depends on how the adjudicating authority uses a discretion the criminal courts used to supervise from the first day.

Read the table by its final column. On every dimension that concerns the consequence of error, the forum, the burden, the sanction and the reach of the law over the person, the change was substantive and large. On the dimensions that concern the coverage of control, the permissions governing the movement of capital and the routing of transactions through an authorised channel, the change was formal: the same transactions are controlled, by the same institutions, under powers that in FEMA are if anything more flexibly drafted, since a regulation can be amended overnight while FERA's permissions at least required the discipline of a published code. This distribution is not a defect in the reform, and the verdict section resists the cynical reading of it. A state may rationally decide that capital movements must be managed while deciding at the same time that mismanaging them should not be a crime. The Indian reform of 2000 made exactly that pair of decisions. The error lies only in the folklore, which noticed the second decision and hallucinated the repeal of the first.

## The standard of proof: the difference that decides cases

Ask practitioners who worked on both sides of the 2000 line what the transition meant in the hearing room and the answer, with a consistency that polling rarely produces, is the burden of proof. Under FEMA, a contravention is established before the Adjudicating Authority on the preponderance of probabilities, the civil standard: the authority asks whether the department's account of the transaction is more probable than the noticee's, on the material each side has produced, and imposes a money penalty if it is. There is no presumption of a culpable mental state to displace, no liberty at stake, and no criminal record at the end. The Supreme Court's general adjudication jurisprudence, developed in the customs context in cases such as Collector of Customs v D. Bhoormull, had long treated departmental adjudication as a proceeding of this civil character, in which the strict rules of criminal evidence do not apply and the deciding authority acts on the probabilities disclosed by the record. FEMA aligned exchange law with that model expressly and completely.

Under FERA the same facts could be, and routinely were, run through the criminal track, where the formal standard was proof beyond reasonable doubt but the machinery around it was anything but neutral. The prosecution had first to be launched on the complaint of an authorised officer, which filtered the caseload without softening it. Once launched, Section 59 supplied the presumption of the accused's culpable mental state, and the accused's statutory defence of innocence had itself to be established to the criminal standard, a burden the section spelled out in terms. The evidence in such trials was characteristically documentary and testimonial in a particular way: statements recorded by enforcement officers during the investigation, retractions of those statements, and arguments about the circumstances in which each was made, of the kind the Supreme Court disciplined in Vinod Solanki by insisting on corroboration for a retracted confession. A defence under FERA was therefore a defence conducted uphill, against a presumption, on evidence largely created inside the investigating agency, in a trial whose outcome was imprisonment. A defence under FEMA is a contest about documents and probabilities in which the noticee's explanation, if more plausible than the department's, wins. The statutes differ in many clauses; this difference in the lived experience of being proceeded against is the one that justifies calling the transition a change in the character of the law rather than in its stationery.

The hearing room mechanics differ in ways that follow from the standard. In a FEMA adjudication the department opens with the material its investigation has assembled, bank records, dealer reports, the noticee's own filings and correspondence, and the noticee answers from the transaction's documents. There is no charge framed by a court, no plea, and no accused in the dock; the proceeding more closely resembles a tax assessment with a penal edge than a trial. The noticee who cannot produce the paper trail of a transaction carries a practical disadvantage that functions as an evidential burden without being named one, since an authority deciding on probabilities will treat an unexplained gap in the documents as counting against the side that should have filled it. Practitioners accordingly describe FEMA defence as a documentary discipline: the case is won in the file, in the completeness of the remittance records, the dealer's advices, the contracts and invoices and the correspondence that shows what the parties believed they were doing. Set against the FERA trial, in which the decisive material was so often a contested oral statement, the shift is from a jurisprudence of confession to a jurisprudence of records, and it explains a professional transformation the transition produced, the migration of exchange law work from criminal chambers to the offices of chartered accountants and regulatory lawyers.

A connected point, often missed in accounts that equate decriminalisation with the removal of fault, is the place of intention inside the civil regime. A penalty under Section 13 is a civil liability, and the Supreme Court's jurisprudence on regulatory penalties, stated for the securities laws in Securities and Exchange Board of India v Shriram Mutual Fund, treats such a penalty as a civil obligation for a breach of a civil duty, for which a guilty mind is not an essential ingredient in the way it is for a crime. Adjudicating authorities under FEMA have worked on the same understanding: the department must establish the contravention, the doing of the prohibited act or the omission of the required one, and the noticee's state of mind becomes relevant principally to the quantum of penalty and to the exercise of discretion, where good faith, voluntary disclosure and cooperation do real work, rather than to liability itself. The reversal from FERA is complete and slightly paradoxical. The criminal statute made intention the centre of the case and then presumed it; the civil statute makes intention peripheral to liability while allowing it to dominate the argument about how much the established contravention should cost. Practitioners who understood that inversion early, and built their defences around documentation, disclosure and quantum rather than around the theatre of innocence, were the ones who served their clients best in the new regime.

Two qualifications keep the contrast honest, and both matter in practice. The first is that the criminal standard under FERA coexisted with the civil track throughout: adjudication under the old Act was already a probabilities proceeding in substance, and many FERA matters never left it. Decriminalisation did not invent the civil proceeding; it abolished its criminal twin and left the civil proceeding standing alone. The second qualification runs the other way. A FEMA penalty of up to three times the sum involved can exceed, in money terms, the fines that criminal courts typically imposed under FERA, and the adjudicating authority may in addition direct confiscation of the currency, security or property in respect of which the contravention occurred and order the repatriation of foreign exchange holdings. The civil regime is gentler in kind and capable of being heavier in amount, and a noticee who treats a FEMA proceeding as a formality because it is not a prosecution misunderstands the instrument. What decriminalisation removed was the prison, the presumption and the stigma; it did not remove the state's capacity to make a contravention very expensive.

One structural gap in the civil design should be recorded, because the courts have had to fill it. FEMA prescribes no limitation period within which adjudication proceedings must be commenced, a silence that sits oddly in a statute enacted to close an era: the repealed Act's legacy was given a two-year fuse, while the new Act's own proceedings were given none. In practice, notices have been issued many years after the transactions they examine, sometimes after the authorised dealer's records have thinned and the personnel who handled the matter have scattered, and the noticee's complaint of prejudice is real even where the transaction is documented. The appellate courts, confronted with stale proceedings, have disciplined them case by case, treating the absence of a statutory period not as a licence for indefinite delay but as an invitation to test the delay for reasonableness and for the prejudice it causes. The position is workable and untidy in equal measure, and a limitation provision for the civil regime remains the most obvious piece of unfinished drafting in the Act, a quarter century of case law having mapped the hole that a single clause could have filled at enactment.

## Residence: the test that decides who is inside the regime

Every exchange statute must first answer a jurisdictional question: whose transactions does it govern? FERA answered with a mixture of residence and citizenship, extending itself to Indian citizens wherever they lived. FEMA answers with residence alone, and then defines residence in a way that surprises almost everyone who encounters it for the first time. Under Section 2(v), a person resident in India is, in the individual case, a person residing in India for more than one hundred and eighty-two days during the course of the preceding financial year. The definition then admits two intention-based exceptions that operate immediately and in both directions. A person who has gone out of India for employment, for carrying on a business or vocation abroad, or in circumstances indicating an intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period, is not resident, whatever the day count says. A person who has come to India for employment, for business, or in circumstances indicating an intention to stay for an uncertain period, is resident, again whatever the day count says. Residence under FEMA is therefore a compound of arithmetic and intention, with intention senior to arithmetic at the moment of movement.

The practical consequence is that FEMA status changes on the day of the journey, not at the end of a counting period. The software professional who leaves Hyderabad in October to take up employment in Toronto becomes a person resident outside India on departure, entitled from that day to operate the accounts and hold the assets the regulations allow a non-resident, and obliged from that day to redesignate the resident accounts left behind. The same professional, returning for good some years later, becomes resident on arrival with the intention to stay, and the foreign accounts that were lawful yesterday become holdings that must be fitted within the rules for residents, which are channelled through facilities designed for the returning emigrant. Students occupy the designed edge of the test: a person who goes abroad for studies has ordinarily gone for a purpose that indicates an intention to stay for an uncertain period only in some readings, and the Reserve Bank's clarifications treat students as non-residents for the period of study in the general run of cases, a position that generations of families have had to discover rather than read off the section.

### Can a person who returns to India keep a bank account opened while living abroad?

Yes, in a converted form. A person who returns to India for good may keep foreign currency balances in a Resident Foreign Currency account and may retain assets acquired while resident abroad, but accounts operated as a non-resident must be redesignated once the person becomes resident under FEMA again.

The definition's remaining limbs extend the residence idea from persons to their establishments, and they matter to every business with a footprint on both sides of the border. An office, branch or agency in India owned or controlled by a person resident outside India is itself treated as a person resident in India, and an office, branch or agency outside India owned or controlled by a person resident in India is treated the same way. The statute thus reaches the Indian branch of the foreign company and the foreign branch of the Indian company through the same concept, and the compliance obligations that follow, on routing transactions, holding accounts and reporting, attach to the establishment as a resident actor in its own right. Around the definition the regulations build the visible furniture of non-resident finance: the non-resident external and non-resident ordinary accounts through which persons resident outside India bank in India, the foreign currency accounts of returning Indians, and the deposit regulations that specify which accounts each status may open, fund and repatriate from. A change of status is therefore not an abstraction. It is an instruction to one's bankers, and the person who crosses the residency line without issuing it carries accounts whose names no longer match their legal character, which is the seed from which a surprising share of individual FEMA defaults grow.

The confusion that costs money, identified in the brief for this article as the single costliest error in the field, is the assumption that residence means the same thing across Indian law. It does not. Section 6 of the Income-tax Act 1961 tests residence by presence in India during the previous year itself, one hundred and eighty-two days or, in the alternative combination, sixty days in that year with three hundred and sixty-five days across the four preceding years, and it asks nothing about the traveller's purpose or intention. FEMA tests presence during the preceding financial year and then overrides the count with intention at the moment of movement. The two statutes therefore produce different answers for the same person in the same year as a matter of routine: the executive who returns to India in November to settle is resident under FEMA from November and, depending on the day count, may be non-resident under the Income-tax Act for that entire financial year, with bank accounts, holding permissions and disclosure duties pulling in opposite directions under the two codes. Neither answer is an error. Each statute drafted its test for its own purpose, the tax statute to locate the right to tax worldwide income, the exchange statute to locate the duty to route transactions through the authorised channel, and the person caught between them must simply run both tests, separately, every time status matters.

## The reach of the civil regime: what FEMA still forbids

The liberation narrative survives contact with Section 4 of FEMA for about one paragraph. A resident of India may not acquire, hold, own, possess or transfer foreign exchange, a foreign security or immovable property outside India except as the Act and its regulations provide. Everything a resident lawfully holds abroad is held through a permission structure, and the structure is detailed. The Foreign Exchange Management (Current Account Transactions) Rules, made by the Central Government, divide current account dealings into a prohibited schedule, covering items such as remittances for the purchase of lottery tickets and the proceeds of lotteries and racing, a schedule of transactions requiring the approval of the Central Government, and a schedule requiring the approval of the Reserve Bank beyond stated limits. The capital side is governed by an accumulating library of regulations and rules on foreign investment into India, on overseas investment by residents, on borrowing and lending in foreign currency and on the acquisition of property abroad. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme, introduced in 2004 as an experiment in trusting the resident individual, allows a resident to remit up to two hundred and fifty thousand US dollars per financial year for permitted current and capital purposes, including opening accounts abroad, buying shares and acquiring property, and its ceiling was raised in stages from twenty-five thousand dollars to the present figure as the reserves position strengthened. The scheme is the clearest single exhibit of the post-2000 philosophy: a general permission, generous by the standards of the FERA era, bounded by a ceiling, routed through an authorised dealer, and revocable in design if the external position deteriorates.

The corporate side of the capital account shows the same pattern at larger scale. An Indian company that wishes to invest in a joint venture or wholly owned subsidiary abroad does so under the overseas investment framework, which allows financial commitment up to limits expressed against the company's net worth under the automatic route, requires approval beyond them, and attaches reporting duties to each step, from the initial remittance to the annual performance reporting on the foreign entity. Borrowing from abroad runs through the external commercial borrowing framework, with its eligible borrower and lender categories, maturity floors, all-in-cost ceilings and end-use restrictions, again administered substantially by regulation and master direction rather than by the Act. In each area the operative answer to the citizen's question, may I do this and how much, is found not in FEMA but in instruments made under it, and the instruments in the borrowing and investment fields have been rewritten wholesale more than once since 2000 as policy has moved between encouragement and restraint. The statute's contribution is to insist that there shall be a framework and that the Reserve Bank shall keep it; the politics of the decade decide what the framework says.

The remittance ceiling itself has already served once as a crisis instrument, in an episode that vindicates the drafters' preference for adjustable controls. In the currency turbulence of 2013, when the rupee fell sharply in the months of the taper tantrum, the Reserve Bank cut the Liberalised Remittance Scheme ceiling from two hundred thousand dollars to seventy-five thousand dollars and tightened the overseas investment limits for companies, using the delegated machinery to close the gate within days and without Parliament. As reserves recovered the ceiling was restored in stages, to one hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars and then to two hundred and fifty thousand, where it has since remained. A FERA Parliament would have had to amend the Act, in session, in public, to achieve the same movement. The episode is the strongest practical evidence for the proposition that the transition replaced a fixed code with an adjustable instrument, and it cuts both ways in the argument: the adjustability that protects the external position in a crisis is the same adjustability that leaves the resident's permissions permanently provisional.

Two features of this landscape deserve the reader's emphasis. The first is that the operative law lives below the statute. FERA, for all its faults, printed its prohibitions in an Act of Parliament that a citizen could read; FEMA prints a framework and delegates the detail to rules, regulations, directions and circulars, which the Reserve Bank amends frequently as policy moves. The lawyer's skill in this field is the skill of tracking subordinate legislation, and the citizen's risk is that a transaction lawful when planned is reclassified before it is completed. The second feature is the sheer ordinariness of the conduct inside the net. The FEMA net catches the parent who pays a child's fees abroad through the wrong route, the freelancer who leaves export-of-services earnings sitting in a foreign payment account beyond the permitted course, the heir who inherits a parent's flat in another country and does nothing about it, the returning emigrant who forgets an account, and the investor who buys foreign shares outside the remittance scheme's route. None of these people resembles the hawala operator of the FERA imagination, and the enforcement system's encounter with them is the subject on which the civil character of the regime is most severely tested, because a civil penalty machinery applied without proportion to the small and the inadvertent reproduces, in money, the disproportionality that the criminal regime achieved in liberty. Compounding exists to absorb exactly these cases, and its extensive use is the best evidence that the system understands the point.

## The hardening of 2015: equivalent value seizure and the return of imprisonment

The story of FEMA after 2000 is not a straight line towards gentleness, and the most important amendment to the Act pulled in the opposite direction. The Finance Act 2015 inserted Section 37A, brought into force in September of that year, to solve a jurisdictional embarrassment: the assets whose holding FEMA prohibits sit abroad, outside the physical reach of Indian process, so a resident could hold an unauthorised foreign account or a foreign property in defiance of Section 4 while the enforcement agency stood at the border with an unenforceable finding. Section 37A answers by substituting the asset. Where an authorised officer has reason to believe, recorded in writing, that foreign exchange, a foreign security or an immovable property situated outside India is held in contravention of Section 4, the officer may seize value equivalent, situated within India, of the foreign holding. Seizure is barred where the aggregate value of the foreign holding falls below the prescribed threshold of one crore rupees. The seizure order must be placed before a Competent Authority for confirmation within thirty days, the Competent Authority must decide within one hundred and eighty days, and an appeal lies from its order to the Appellate Tribunal constituted under the Smugglers and Foreign Exchange Manipulators (Forfeiture of Property) Act 1976, the forum whose very name announces the lineage of the power. If the person discloses the foreign holding and brings the equivalent value into India, the seizure can be set aside, a proviso the Appellate Tribunal applied in the case of Devendra N. Desai to lift a seizure after the foreign exchange had been repatriated, on the reasoning that the provision's object had been fulfilled. In the matter arising from the estate of Anil Salgaocar, the same tribunal held that assets acquired abroad before Section 37A existed could still found a seizure if they continued to be held abroad after its commencement, rejecting the charge of retrospectivity. The provision is litigated, its temporal reach contested, and its constitutionality argued; any account that presents it as settled law misdescribes a live front of exchange jurisprudence.

The mechanics reward precision, because the provision is two instruments wearing one section number. The seizure under Section 37A is interim and securing in character: it freezes the Indian asset so that the value remains available while the question whether the foreign holding contravenes Section 4 is adjudicated, and it is hedged with the threshold, the thirty-day reference to the Competent Authority, the one hundred and eighty day decision period, and the release that follows disclosure and repatriation. Confiscation, by contrast, is a final consequence that arrives only through adjudication under Section 13, after the contravention has been established on the civil standard. Critics of the provision direct their fire at the interim instrument, arguing that a seizure founded on an officer's reason to believe, in a regime whose premise is that these are civil matters, reproduces the coercive sequence of the criminal law, asset frozen first and liability determined afterwards, and that the one crore threshold is low enough to catch the merely careless alongside the deliberate concealer. Defenders answer that without an interim instrument the adjudication would be pointless in the paradigm case, since the whole difficulty is that the real asset is beyond reach and the Indian asset can be sold in an afternoon, and that the safeguards added, recorded reasons, a confirming authority drawn from a different departmental lineage, a statutory clock and a repatriation exit, are exactly the safeguards a civil system should substitute for the criminal trial it has renounced. Both positions are live in the tribunal litigation described here, and the contest between them is the second act of the drama that began in 2000: the first act decided that exchange violations are not crimes, and the second is deciding how much of the criminal law's toolkit a civil regime may borrow before the distinction stops doing work.

The same amending package reached into Section 13. New sub-sections imposed, for the class of contraventions consisting of acquiring foreign exchange, foreign securities or immovable property abroad beyond the Section 37A threshold, a penalty of up to three times the sum involved together with confiscation of the equivalent value situated in India, and made the acquisition punishable, on prosecution, with imprisonment of up to five years and fine. The prosecution route is filtered: the Adjudicating Authority must first recommend prosecution in writing, the Director of Enforcement must be satisfied and record reasons, and a court may take cognizance only on the written complaint of an officer of the prescribed rank. Filtered or not, the provision is a criminal offence inside the civil statute, aimed at precisely the subject matter, undisclosed foreign assets, that the era's politics had made salient; the same holdings also attract the separate and heavier regime of [the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act 2015](/2012/09/01/black-money-act-2015-explained/), enacted in the same season and aimed at the same taxpayer. The accurate summary of the 2015 turn is that the Indian state, having spent fifteen years congratulating itself on decriminalising foreign exchange, rebuilt a criminal edge for the one class of conduct it cared most about, while keeping the civil machinery for everything else. The lesson generalises beyond this statute: decriminalisation in Indian economic law is characteristically a sorting exercise, in which the generality of conduct is moved into civil process and a narrowing core of aggravated conduct is re-criminalised with modern drafting, and the citizen's task is to discover which side of the sort a given act falls on.

## One agency, two statutes: the parallel proceedings problem

The Directorate of Enforcement that Section 36 of FEMA establishes is the same directorate that administers the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act 2002, which took effect on 1 July 2005, and the overlap is the most practically important feature of the whole landscape. A FEMA contravention, without more, is not money laundering: the laundering offence requires proceeds of a scheduled criminal offence, projected as untainted. But the factual patterns that attract the directorate's attention characteristically involve both worlds at once. The unauthorised outward remittance routed through a hawala broker may be a FEMA contravention in itself and, if the money being moved is the proceeds of a scheduled offence, the raw material of a laundering prosecution; the undisclosed foreign account may attract FEMA adjudication and a tax prosecution and, depending on the source of the funds, laundering proceedings as well. The reader who needs the architecture of that second statute should go to [the complete guide to the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act 2002](/2013/03/01/pmla-2002-complete-guide/), which owns it; what belongs here is the interaction. The same facts are then examined in parallel proceedings under different statutes, in different forums, under different standards of proof: the adjudicating authority on probabilities, the criminal court beyond reasonable doubt, with the directorate's own powers, including its power of arrest under the laundering statute as upheld in the Supreme Court's ruling examined in [the analysis of the PMLA judgment](/2013/03/15/pmla-supreme-court-judgment/), available in the second proceeding and unavailable in the first.

### Can the Enforcement Directorate arrest a person under FEMA?

No. FEMA gives the Directorate powers of investigation, search and seizure, but no power of arrest; the only imprisonment inside FEMA is civil imprisonment of a person who defaults on a penalty, and prosecution for the narrow class of foreign asset contraventions added in 2015. The Directorate's arrest power comes from the money laundering statute.

Parallel proceedings of this kind generate the characteristic anxieties of the field, and they should be named rather than gestured at. Statements made to the directorate in the civil investigation can acquire a second life in the criminal one, and the protections available differ by proceeding. Attachment under the laundering statute can freeze the same assets that a FEMA adjudication is valuing. Timelines diverge: a compounding application may be pending while a prosecution complaint is drafted. The professional management of such a matter is, in large part, document management over years, and anyone who has lived through an adjudication that outlasted the transactions it examined learns the value of keeping the whole record, every notice, statement, order and proof of remittance, in a single private file assembled before memory fades, which is the use [an encrypted workspace built for keeping sensitive files privately, of the kind VaultBook provides](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), is designed to serve. The deeper point is structural. The transition of 2000 separated liability from criminality inside the exchange statute, and the enactment of the laundering statute five years later reconnected them at the level of the enforcing institution. A citizen's practical exposure is set not by either statute alone but by the pair of them, administered by officers who move between the two files, and assessments of the post-2000 order that ignore the second statute systematically understate how much coercive law a foreign exchange irregularity can still attract.

## The transition litigated: how the courts received the new order

The meaning of a replacement statute is settled as much in litigation as in drafting, and the transition generated four bodies of case law worth distinguishing. The first concerned the sunset itself: whether particular FERA complaints had been taken cognizance of within the two-year window, what stage of a proceeding counted as cognizance, and whether adjudication notices issued in time survived the deadline for their completion. These were arid questions with liberty riding on them, and the courts answered them by holding the state to the legislature's text, quashing proceedings begun too late and allowing those begun in time to run. The second body concerned the continuing vitality of FERA jurisprudence inside FEMA adjudication. Because the two statutes share concepts, the authorised channel, the realisation duty, the definition-rich border between residents and non-residents, courts and tribunals carried a body of learning across the repeal, citing FERA-era reasoning on the meaning of the shared vocabulary while refusing to import FERA-era presumptions into a statute that had deliberately omitted them. The discipline of that distinction, honouring continuity of concepts while refusing continuity of coercion, is the judiciary's own version of the audit this article has performed.

The third body concerned the new machinery's fairness: challenges to adjudication for delay, for want of a hearing, for penalties disproportionate to the gravity and gain of the contravention, and for the treatment of technical breaches as though they were substantive ones. Out of these challenges the tribunal and the High Courts built a working doctrine of proportion in penalty, in which the quantum provisions of Section 13 are read as ceilings for the worst cases rather than tariffs for all cases, and in which voluntary disclosure, cooperation and the absence of loss press the figure downward. The fourth and most consequential body is the litigation over the 2015 additions, the equivalent value seizure and the revived prosecution for foreign asset holdings, already described in the section devoted to them, where the contested questions are temporal reach and the adequacy of the safeguards around a seizure that precedes adjudication. Taken together, the four bodies of litigation describe a judiciary performing quality control on each layer of the reform in turn: enforcing the extinction of the old offences, policing the fairness of the new penalties, and testing the new coercion against the constitutional standards the old regime had strained. That the courts were given useful work at every layer is itself a measure of how much the transition left genuinely undecided.

## Three encounters: the same facts under the two regimes

Abstractions about liability acquire their meaning in individual files, and three recurrent encounters show the transition at ground level. Consider first the student who kept a foreign account. Under FERA, the student who remained abroad after completing studies, or who returned to India leaving an account open and funded abroad without the permissions the regime required, held an unauthorised asset inside a criminal statute whose reach followed citizenship; the exposure was prosecution, and the practical advice of the era was regularisation through the Reserve Bank's permission machinery, sought with some anxiety. Under FEMA the same facts present as a status problem: the account's lawfulness turns on the holder's residence at each stage, on the retention permissions for assets acquired while non-resident, and on the remittance scheme's route for anything acquired after returning, and a default is met by adjudication or compounding, by money rather than by charge sheet. The conduct required of the citizen has not lightened; the paperwork is as demanding as it ever was. The catastrophe has been removed from the paperwork's price list.

Consider next the exporter who realised proceeds late and in part, the staple case of both regimes. Under FERA the file could travel both tracks: a penalty of up to five times the value in adjudication, and a prosecution in which the state had to prove its case with Section 59 tilting the question of intent, the trial measured in years, the proprietor's liberty and the firm's bank lines hostage to the outcome. Under FEMA the file travels one track: adjudication on probabilities, a penalty calibrated, in the doctrine the tribunals have built, to the gravity and gain of the default, and compounding available for the cases that are really about delay and documentation. The exporter's duty to bring the money home is identical in the two worlds, and this article has insisted on the identity. What the exporter experiences as the transition is the conversion of an existential legal risk into a financial and reputational one, and for a trading business that conversion is the difference between a legal system that can be complied with at a price and one that can destroy the enterprise for a documentation failure.

Consider finally the heir. A parent who worked abroad for thirty years dies holding accounts and a small flat in the country of employment, and the children, resident in India, inherit. Under FERA the inheritance placed the heirs inside a permission regime for holding foreign assets that was built for traders and companies, and the family's instinct to do nothing, to leave the accounts as they stood while grief and paperwork sorted themselves out, accumulated criminal exposure with time. Under FEMA the position is governed by the retention and acquisition permissions for inherited assets, which are real but conditional, and inaction produces a contravention to be compounded or adjudicated, with the 2015 additions waiting if the holding is large and undisclosed and the family's silence begins to look like concealment. The pattern across the three encounters is the article's thesis in biographical form. The questions the state asks have barely changed since 1974. The answers it accepts are more numerous, the routes to compliance more various, and the price of a wrong answer has been converted from liberty to money. That conversion is the whole of the liberation, and it is not a small thing.

## An honest assessment: liberation, continuity, and what the repeal proves

The strongest version of the liberation account is easily stated and largely true. A statute that presumed the guilt of the accused and put prison behind routine paperwork errors was an instrument of fear out of proportion to the reserves it protected, and its criminal provisions fell with special weight on emigrants, small traders and families whose offence was to participate in an economy the official channels served badly. Removing the presumption, the arrest power and the prison sentence was a genuine civil liberties gain, of the same order as the repeal of any offence that criminalises ordinary economic life, and the diaspora's memory of the change as emancipation is evidence about the old law, not confusion about the new one. The strongest version of the continuity account is also easily stated and largely true. The state surrendered no control it valued: capital movements remain licensed in substance, the authorised channel remains compulsory, the realisation duties remain, the detail migrated into regulations that change faster than any Act, and in 2015 the state bolted a seizure power and a criminal offence back onto the civil frame for the assets it most wanted to reach. Both accounts are correct because they describe different layers of the same reform, and the work of this article has been to keep the layers separate.

Between the two accounts sits the question of judgment: was the bargain a good one? The evidence supports a verdict, and the verdict is yes, with a named reservation. Decoupling the consequence from the control was the intellectually honest resolution of a real dilemma. India in 2000 was not ready, on any view of its reserves, its banking system or its fiscal position, to surrender the capital account, and the Tarapore roadmap itself had made convertibility conditional and phased; a reform that had pretended otherwise would have been reversed at the first external shock, taking the decriminalisation down with it. By contrast, nothing in the external position ever required the imprisonment of exporters who realised their proceeds late or the presumption against a traveller's state of mind. The reform took the part of the old regime that necessity defended and discarded the part that only habit defended, which is what successful repeal and replacement looks like. The reservation concerns delegation and the 2015 additions. A framework statute whose operative content sits in regulations invites governance by circular, and the equivalent value power, administered through seizure first and adjudication afterwards, reproduces within the civil law the sequence, coercion before adjudication, that the reform of 2000 was supposed to have retired. These are live faults, argued in live litigation, and they mark the frontier on which the transition's meaning will be settled.

The transition also remains, in one declared respect, unfinished, and honesty requires the point to be recorded before the verdict hardens into myth. The stated destination of the reform era was a phased movement towards fuller capital account convertibility, assessed against the Tarapore conditionalities and revisited by the second Tarapore committee in 2006, which again proposed a phased roadmap. The destination has been approached repeatedly and deferred repeatedly, and FEMA's text accommodates the deferral without strain, because a statute that delegates the capital account to regulation never has to announce whether the journey is continuing or has quietly ended. Defenders of the deferral point to the crises the controls have weathered, the Asian crisis before enactment, the global financial crisis, the taper tantrum, and argue that the option value of the gate has paid for itself several times over. Critics answer that a permanent emergency power, permanently delegated, is a standing invitation to govern the external sector by circular and to treat the resident's economic liberty as a policy variable. FEMA does not resolve that argument; it institutionalises it, and the argument will be conducted inside the statute's machinery for as long as the machinery lasts. A reader who understands that has understood the deepest sense in which the transition of 2000 was a change of management rather than a change of regime.

The pattern has since become a template, which is a further reason to read this transition carefully. The decriminalisation programme that the Indian state pursued across its economic statutes in later years, culminating in omnibus exercises such as the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act 2023, which replaced imprisonment with monetary penalties for minor offences across dozens of central laws, proceeds on the logic FEMA first embodied at full scale: that the criminal sanction is a scarce instrument to be reserved for conduct involving fraud, harm or defiance, and that regulatory defaults are better priced than prosecuted. The FERA to FEMA experience supplies both the encouragement and the caution for that programme. The encouragement is that a feared criminal code can be converted without the sky falling on enforcement, because the civil machinery, properly built with adjudication, compounding and proportionate penalties, collects more compliance than the prosecution machine ever did. The caution is the 2015 sequel: a state that decriminalises in general while re-criminalising at the point of maximum political salience will produce a law whose severity is a function of headlines, and the citizen's protection against that drift is not the statute's preamble but the discipline of asking, for each new offence inserted into a civil code, which side of the 2000 settlement it belongs on.

One general lesson for readers of other statutes remains. The FERA to FEMA transition is the cleanest modern Indian demonstration that repeal is an ambiguous event: the same ceremony can bury a liability regime and preserve a control regime, and the two must be audited separately before the word reform is earned. What was kept, what was discarded and what was added is not a rhetorical triad but an audit protocol, and applied to this pair of statutes it returns a precise answer. Kept: the channel, the duties, the capital controls and the agency. Discarded: the crime, the presumption, the arrest and the citizenship reach. Added: compounding, a tribunal ladder, a residency test of arithmetic and intention, a sunset that executed the criminal past, and, a decade and a half later, a criminal edge for foreign assets that qualifies the whole settlement. The general position described in this article is information about a body of law, not advice on any individual matter, and it is no substitute for advice on a specific case, in a field where limitation periods and appeal windows are short and unforgiving.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was FERA and why was it so feared?

The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1973 was India's exchange control code, in force from 1 January 1974, built on the premise that foreign exchange was a scarce national asset to be conserved. It prohibited dealings in foreign exchange except through authorised dealers and as permitted, regulated payments to non-residents, exports, securities and property abroad, and applied to Indian citizens even outside India. It was feared because breach was a criminal offence carrying imprisonment, enforcement officers held powers of search, seizure and arrest, and Section 59 directed courts to presume the accused's culpable mental state, leaving the accused to prove innocence. Ordinary conduct, including informal family remittances, sat inside its reach.

### Q: Why was FERA replaced?

FERA was replaced because the economic policy it served had been abandoned. It was drafted for a closed economy with chronically scarce reserves, in which every private foreign transaction was a drain on a collective pool. The 1991 balance of payments crisis and the liberalisation that followed committed India to courting foreign trade and investment, and the acceptance of current account convertibility in 1994 made a prohibitory criminal code untenable in principle as well as in reputation. Amendments in 1993 softened FERA considerably, but they could not alter its grammar of prohibition, offence and presumption. A new statute was needed that kept capital controls while converting liability from criminal to civil, and FEMA was drafted to do exactly that.

### Q: When did FEMA come into force?

The Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999, Act 42 of 1999, received the assent of the President on 29 December 1999 and came into force on 1 June 2000. The interval between assent and commencement was deliberate. FEMA is a framework statute whose operative detail sits in rules made by the Central Government and regulations made by the Reserve Bank, and those instruments, including the current account transaction rules and the first set of Reserve Bank regulations, had to be notified and the authorised dealer system prepared before the new regime could begin. On commencement, the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1973 stood repealed by Section 49 of the new Act.

### Q: What happened to FERA prosecutions after the repeal?

Section 49 of FEMA both saved and time-limited them. Offences committed under FERA before 1 June 2000 remained governed by the repealed Act, so investigations and prosecutions for the FERA years could continue as if the repeal had not occurred. But Section 49(3) imposed a hard sunset: no court could take cognizance of an offence under the repealed Act, and no adjudicating officer could take notice of a contravention under its Section 51, after the expiry of two years from FEMA's commencement. Prosecutions filed by 31 May 2002 ran their course, some for years afterwards. Prosecutions not filed by that date could never be filed, whatever the underlying conduct.

### Q: Does FEMA still restrict capital account transactions?

Yes, comprehensively. Section 6 of FEMA preserves state control over capital account transactions, defined as transactions that alter the assets or liabilities, including contingent liabilities, of persons resident in India outside India or of persons resident outside India within India. The Reserve Bank, in consultation with the Central Government, specifies which classes of capital account transaction are permissible and the limits up to which they may be undertaken, and the detail is carried in regulations on foreign investment, overseas investment, borrowing, lending and guarantees. The rupee is convertible on the current account, subject to specified restrictions, but it has never been made freely convertible on the capital account, and the repeal of FERA changed the liability for breach, not the existence of the control.

### Q: What is the residency test under FEMA?

Section 2(v) of FEMA defines a person resident in India, for an individual, as a person residing in India for more than one hundred and eighty-two days during the course of the preceding financial year, but the definition is then qualified by intention. A person who has gone out of India for employment, for carrying on a business or vocation abroad, or in circumstances indicating an intention to stay abroad for an uncertain period, is not resident even if the day count is met. A person who has come to India for employment, business, or with the intention of staying for an uncertain period, is resident even before any count is satisfied. Status can therefore change on the day of travel, and bodies corporate incorporated in India are resident regardless of day counts.

### Q: Can assets in India be seized for a foreign exchange contravention?

Yes, in a defined class of case. Section 37A of FEMA, inserted by the Finance Act 2015 and in force from September of that year, empowers an authorised officer who has reason to believe that foreign exchange, a foreign security or immovable property situated outside India is held in contravention of Section 4 to seize value equivalent situated within India. Seizure is unavailable where the aggregate value of the foreign holding is below the prescribed threshold of one crore rupees. The seizure must be placed before a Competent Authority within thirty days and confirmed or set aside within one hundred and eighty days, with an appeal to the Appellate Tribunal under the SAFEMA framework. Disclosure and repatriation of the foreign holding can lead to the seizure being set aside.

### Q: Who enforces FEMA?

Enforcement is divided between regulation and investigation. The Reserve Bank of India administers the regulatory side: it makes the regulations that carry FEMA's operative detail, authorises the dealers and money changers through whom foreign exchange must move, issues directions to authorised persons and compounds most contraventions. The Directorate of Enforcement, established under Section 36 of the Act within the Ministry of Finance, investigates suspected contraventions using powers modelled on the income tax code, including summons, search and seizure, and places cases before the Adjudicating Authorities appointed under Section 16, which impose penalties under Section 13. Appeals run to the Special Director (Appeals), then to the Appellate Tribunal for Foreign Exchange, and then to the High Court on questions of law. The same Directorate also administers the money laundering statute.

### Q: What penalty could be imposed under FERA without a criminal trial?

FERA ran a departmental adjudication track alongside prosecution. Under Sections 50 and 51, an adjudicating officer of the enforcement directorate could inquire into a contravention and impose a penalty of up to five times the amount or value involved, or five thousand rupees, whichever was greater. Appeals lay to the Appellate Board and then to the High Court. Crucially, adjudication was no shield: Section 56 provided that prosecution lay without prejudice to any penalty awarded, so the same act could attract the fivefold penalty in the departmental proceeding and a prison sentence in the criminal one. Failure to pay a penalty or obey an order was itself an offence under Section 57, punishable with imprisonment.

### Q: Can a contravention under FEMA be compounded?

Yes. Section 15 of FEMA empowers the Reserve Bank, and for specified contraventions the Directorate of Enforcement, to compound a contravention on application, which means the matter is settled on payment of a sum determined by the compounding authority, without adjudication and without any criminal consequence. Compounding closes the case: once the amount is paid, no further proceeding lies for that contravention, and the order records the contravention as compounded. The mechanism was designed for the large population of technical and inadvertent breaches that a regulatory statute inevitably generates, and it is the route by which most ordinary FEMA defaults, particularly reporting delays and procedural lapses by individuals and companies, are in practice resolved.

### Q: What happens if a person fails to pay a penalty imposed under FEMA?

Section 14 of FEMA makes the defaulting person liable to civil imprisonment. If the penalty is not paid in full within ninety days from the date on which the notice of demand is served, the Adjudicating Authority may proceed to recover it by ordering the defaulter's arrest and detention in a civil prison, after giving the defaulter an opportunity to show cause why the order should not be made. A warrant may issue without that hearing where the authority is satisfied the defaulter is likely to abscond or leave the jurisdiction to delay execution. This is the only imprisonment the Act generally deploys, and it is coercive rather than punitive: it enforces a money demand, in the manner of a decree, rather than punishing an offence.

### Q: What did the 1993 amendments change in FERA before its repeal?

The Foreign Exchange Regulation (Amendment) Act 1993, in force from 8 January 1993, was the liberalisation era's repair of the old code. It cut back a series of substantive restrictions, widened the situations in which residents could lawfully hold foreign exchange and foreign assets, eased the regime for foreign companies and for non-resident Indians, and raised certain monetary limits and penalties in the enforcement provisions. What it did not touch was the architecture: dealings remained prohibited unless permitted, contraventions remained criminal offences, the presumption in Section 59 remained, and the enforcement directorate kept its powers of arrest. The amendments proved that a criminal exchange code could be administered more gently; the government's conclusion was that gentleness by amendment was not a substitute for a differently designed statute.

### Q: How does FEMA define a current account transaction?

Section 2(j) defines a current account transaction negatively and then by illustration: it is a transaction other than a capital account transaction, and it expressly includes payments due in connection with foreign trade, other current business and services, and short-term banking and credit facilities in the ordinary course of business; payments due as interest on loans and as net income from investments; remittances for the living expenses of parents, spouse and children residing abroad; and expenses in connection with foreign travel, education and medical care of parents, spouse and children. Section 5 then makes such transactions freely permissible through an authorised person, subject only to reasonable restrictions the Central Government may impose in the public interest in consultation with the Reserve Bank, which are carried in the Current Account Transactions Rules.

### Q: Did FERA apply to Indian citizens living outside India?

Yes. Section 1 of FERA extended the Act to all citizens of India outside India and to branches and agencies outside India of companies and bodies corporate registered or incorporated in India. Citizenship, not residence alone, carried the statute across the border, so an emigrant who had settled permanently abroad remained within the class of persons the Act addressed, and dealings that a resident could not undertake without permission were, in the Act's design, controlled for the citizen abroad as well. The practical enforcement of that claim was uneven, but its existence shaped the diaspora's experience of the statute and its reputation. FEMA abandoned the citizenship limb and keys its application to residence, supplemented by the branches and agencies of residents.

### Q: How does residence under FEMA differ from residence under the Income-tax Act?

The two statutes test different things over different periods. FEMA, under Section 2(v), asks whether the person resided in India for more than one hundred and eighty-two days during the preceding financial year, and then overrides the arithmetic with intention: leaving India for employment or an uncertain stay abroad makes a person non-resident at once, and arriving with the intention of staying makes a person resident at once. The Income-tax Act, under its Section 6, counts days of presence in the previous year itself, one hundred and eighty-two days, or sixty days combined with three hundred and sixty-five days over the four preceding years, and asks nothing about purpose. A person can therefore be resident under one statute and non-resident under the other in the same year, and must run both tests separately.

### Q: What must an exporter do about foreign exchange earnings under FEMA?

Sections 7 and 8 carry the exporter's core duties. The exporter must furnish to the Reserve Bank, or to the prescribed authority, a declaration of the full export value of the goods or services, and must do all that is within the exporter's power to realise and repatriate to India the full value, within the period and in the manner specified in the Reserve Bank's regulations. The duty is one of substance, not mere invoicing: value must actually come home, through an authorised dealer, within the stipulated period, and realisation is monitored through the banking channel. The discipline descends directly from FERA's Section 18 regime and survived the repeal intact, although breach is a civil contravention attracting penalty rather than the criminal offence it was before 2000.

### Q: How does adjudication of a FEMA contravention proceed?

Adjudication begins under Section 16, under which the Central Government appoints Adjudicating Authorities to hold inquiries into contraventions. The authority issues a notice requiring the person to show cause why a penalty should not be imposed, fixes a hearing at which the person may appear in person or through a legal practitioner or chartered accountant, and decides on the material produced, applying the civil standard of proof. If the contravention is established, the authority imposes penalty under Section 13 by a reasoned order, and may also direct confiscation of the property involved and the repatriation of foreign exchange holdings. An appeal lies to the Special Director (Appeals) under Section 17, then to the Appellate Tribunal for Foreign Exchange under Section 19, and then to the High Court on a question of law under Section 35.

### Q: Can a resident Indian buy immovable property abroad under FEMA?

Section 4 of FEMA starts from prohibition: a person resident in India may not acquire or hold immovable property outside India except as the Act and its regulations provide. The permissions are specific. A resident individual may acquire property abroad using funds remitted under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, within its annual ceiling, or hold property acquired while resident outside India, or inherited from a person who was resident outside India. Joint acquisition with a relative who is resident outside India is permitted on defined terms, and persons who were non-resident and return are governed by the retention rules for assets acquired abroad. Each route is a permission inside a prohibition, which is why an unexamined purchase, however honestly funded, can still be a contravention.

### Q: What happened to appeals pending under FERA when FEMA commenced?

Section 49(5) managed the institutional handover. Permissions, licences and authorisations granted under the repealed Act continued in force so far as they were not inconsistent with FEMA, and acts done under the old Act were treated as done under the corresponding provisions of the new one. Appeals that had been preferred to the Appellate Board constituted under FERA's Section 52 but not disposed of before 1 June 2000 stood transferred to the Appellate Tribunal for Foreign Exchange created by the new Act, which disposed of them. Any appeal that lay from a decision of the old Board to the High Court could be filed there within sixty days of FEMA's commencement, with the High Court empowered to condone delay for sufficient cause. The design preserved appellate rights while abolishing the forum that was hearing them.

### Q: What is the Liberalised Remittance Scheme?

The Liberalised Remittance Scheme is a standing general permission, introduced by the Reserve Bank in 2004, under which a resident individual may remit up to two hundred and fifty thousand US dollars per financial year through an authorised dealer for permitted current and capital account purposes. The permitted purposes include private travel, education, medical treatment, gifts and donations, maintenance of relatives abroad, opening a foreign currency account abroad, buying shares and other securities, and acquiring immovable property abroad. The scheme began with a ceiling of twenty-five thousand dollars and was raised in stages as India's reserves strengthened, which makes it a compact record of the post-FERA philosophy: trust the resident with a generous general permission, bound it with a ceiling, and police the boundary rather than the transaction.
