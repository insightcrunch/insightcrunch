The Forest (Conservation) Act 1980 is the shortest statute in Indian environmental law and, hectare for hectare, the most consequential. As enacted it ran to five sections, of which only one does operative work, yet that single section placed a lock on every forest in the country: no state government, and no authority acting under one, may de-reserve a reserved forest, use forest land for a non-forest purpose, lease forest land outside government control, or clear naturally grown trees even for reafforestation, unless the Central Government has approved the order in advance. A mine, a dam, a highway, a transmission line, a cement plant, an iron ore beneficiation unit, a township: anything that needs forest land must pass through that gate in New Delhi, and the gate has shaped the geography of Indian development for over four decades.

![Forest (Conservation) Act 1980 and the prior approval gate, showing how one section and a court-supplied definition decide which land needs central clearance - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-20.webp)

The paradox at the centre of the Act explains both its power and its controversy. Parliament wrote a brief brake on state governments and omitted to define the statute's central word. The Act nowhere says what a forest is. For sixteen years that omission mattered less than it might, because administrators read the Act against the forest classifications of the Indian Forest Act 1927 and state records. In December 1996 the Supreme Court, in proceedings begun by a landowner from the Nilgiris named T. N. Godavarman Thirumulpad, held that forest bears its dictionary meaning, that the Act reaches every tract answering that description whatever its ownership or classification, and that any area recorded as forest in government records is forest land for the Act whether or not a tree stands on it. One interpretive sentence converted a statute about notified forests into a statute about every wooded tract in India, produced the category administrators call deemed forest, and drew private estates, community forests of the North-East, revenue land with tree cover, and plantations into a clearance regime designed for government forest departments.

Everything else in the forest clearance system hangs from those two facts, a prior approval rule and a missing definition. The two-stage clearance, the Forest Advisory Committee, the compensatory afforestation obligation, the net present value charge, the accumulated fund that Parliament placed on a statutory footing in 2016, the gram sabha consent question that arrives from the Forest Rights Act 2006, and the argument that ran for three decades about whether a court should supervise forest policy through a single writ petition: each is a consequence of the gate, or of the judicial answer to the question Parliament left open. This article takes the statute apart in that order, provision by provision, order by order, and levy by levy, and gives the strongest form of the case on each side of the arguments the Act generates before reaching a verdict on what the record supports.

## The world the Act was made to end

Forest administration in India was built by the Indian Forest Act 1927, a colonial statute that organised government control over forests through classification. Land could be notified as a reserved forest, in which nearly every act was prohibited unless permitted; declared a protected forest, in which the state reserved specified trees and activities; or left as village forest or unclassed forest under looser regimes. The 1927 Act was a management and revenue code. It vested enormous discretion in state governments and their forest departments, and it treated the forest estate as an asset the state administered, not as an ecosystem the law insulated from the state's own development decisions.

That discretion is what failed. After Independence, state governments diverted forest land on a large scale for river valley projects, industries, plantations, resettlement and the extension of cultivation, acting entirely within their legal powers. Government records reconciled by the Forest Survey of India put the diversion of forest land between 1951 and 1980 at 42,380 square kilometres, an area larger than Kerala. Ministry compilations commonly cited in Parliament put the annual rate in the decades before 1980 at roughly one and a half lakh hectares a year. The diversion was lawful. That was the problem the legislation of 1980 was designed to solve: the decision to convert forest land was being taken by the same governments that wanted the land for other purposes, with no external check, no ecological accounting, and no obligation to replace what was taken.

The National Commission on Agriculture, reporting in 1976, recorded the shrinkage of the forest estate and pressed for stronger central oversight, and the constitutional ground for central intervention had just been laid. Forests had stood in the State List since the Constitution came into force, which meant Parliament could not legislate a general forest code for the states. The Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act 1976 changed the allocation, deleting forests from the State List and inserting Entry 17A, "Forests", in the Concurrent List, alongside Entry 17B for the protection of wild animals and birds. From 1976 both Parliament and state legislatures could legislate on forests, with central law prevailing in case of conflict. The 1980 Act is unintelligible without that amendment: it is Parliament using a four-year-old concurrent competence to restrain the very governments that had held exclusive power before.

### What changed when forests moved to the Concurrent List in 1976?

The Forty-second Amendment moved forests from the State List to Entry 17A of the Concurrent List, so Parliament gained power to legislate on forests for the whole country and central law prevailed over inconsistent state law. The Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, restraining state governments themselves, was possible only because of that transfer.

The transfer did not make forests a central subject, and the distinction matters for understanding the Act's design. States retained legislative competence, retained ownership and administration of the forest estate, and retained the forest departments that manage it. What the 1980 Act did was narrower and more surgical than a takeover. It did not transfer a single hectare to the Union. It attached a condition to the exercise of state power: four specified kinds of order, each of which reduces or converts the forest estate, would have no effect unless the Central Government approved them beforehand. The states kept the forest; the Centre took the key to the gate. Critics of the Act, including state governments across the political spectrum over four decades, have described this as centralisation by condition, a concurrent entry used to subordinate. Defenders reply that the pre-1980 record is the answer: exclusive state control produced 42,380 square kilometres of lawful diversion in three decades, and a check located anywhere else would have been a check the diverting government imposed on itself.

The figure deserves one further context. India's recorded forest area, the land legally recorded as forest in government records, stands at roughly 23.6 per cent of the country's geographical area in Forest Survey of India reporting, a category that includes land with few or no trees. The National Forest Policy of 1952 had set a goal of one third of the land area under forest. The estate was therefore already smaller than the policy target when the diversions of the 1950s, 1960s and 1970s proceeded, and each diversion was effectively irreversible on any human timescale, because a natural forest cleared for a mine or a reservoir is not recreated by planting trees somewhere else. The case for a prior approval rule, made at its strongest, is that a decision of that character should never rest with a single level of government acting alone, and should be forced into the open with conditions attached. The case against, also made at its strongest, is that the rule froze the boundary between forest and development at the moment of maximum ignorance about which land was actually forest, and that the costs of that freeze fall on the poorest states and districts, which hold the most forest and need the most infrastructure. Both cases recur throughout this article, because both are true of different parts of the record.

## An ordinance, five sections, and a single operative rule

The Act began as an ordinance. The Forest (Conservation) Ordinance 1980 was promulgated on 25 October 1980, and Parliament replaced it within the same year with the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, Act 69 of 1980, whose first section deems it to have come into force on 25 October 1980, the ordinance date. The ordinance route is itself part of the story. A government that believed diversion was proceeding faster than parliamentary time allowed chose the instrument that stopped the clock immediately, and the retrospective commencement ensured that no diversion ordered in the interval between ordinance and Act escaped the new rule. Ordinance-making for environmental restraint, rather than for executive convenience, set the tone for the statute: speed on the conservation side, procedure on the development side.

The rationale Parliament recorded for the measure was the one the Supreme Court later quoted back to the country: the Act was enacted with a view to checking further deforestation, which results in ecological imbalance. The objects of the legislation dwelt on the rate of diversion and on the inability of the existing law, under which the states decided, to arrest it, and the remedy chosen was characteristic of the period's centralising temper and unusual in its restraint: not a central forest code, not a takeover of the estate, but a veto. The government of 1980, returned to office that year with deforestation high in the environmental politics the Stockholm decade had created, legislated the smallest instrument that would change the decision structure, and the instrument's smallness is why it survived every subsequent change of government without repeal or serious dilution for over forty years. Statutes that do one thing are hard to repeal, because repeal requires a government to say, in terms, that the one thing should stop being done.

The institutional moment matched the legal one. The ordinance and the Act arrived in the year the Union government created a Department of Environment, in 1980, the first administrative home for environmental subjects at the Centre, which grew into the Ministry of Environment and Forests in 1985. The approval power the Act created therefore had, from its early years, a department whose own standing depended on exercising it: the conservation ministry's authority within the government of India was built substantially on the gate, since no other environmental statute of the period gave the department a veto over the decisions of states and sister ministries. Institutional self-interest is not a disreputable foundation for a regulatory regime, and in this case it aligned the bureaucracy's incentives with the statute's purpose for four decades: a ministry that exists to hold a gate will hold it.

The enacted text is remarkably spare. Section 1 gives the short title, the extent across the whole of India, and the deemed commencement. Section 2 contains the restriction that is the Act's entire operative content. Section 3 empowers the Central Government to constitute an advisory committee. Section 4 confers rule-making power. Section 5 repealed the ordinance and saved action taken under it. That is the whole statute as it left Parliament in 1980. There is no definition section. There is no schedule of exempt activities, no appellate structure, no penalty clause in the original text, and no definition of forest, forest land, or tree. The brevity was not carelessness about drafting in general; brevity was the design. The Act is a prohibition with a permission mechanism, and its authors evidently believed that the classifications of the Indian Forest Act and state forest laws supplied the vocabulary the prohibition needed. The litigation of the following decades is the record of that belief being tested.

Two later insertions completed the skeleton. The Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act 1988, Act 69 of 1988, widened Section 2, added the penal provisions as Sections 3A and 3B, and expanded the statutory explanation of non-forest purpose; those insertions took effect in stages, with the penal sections commencing on 15 March 1989. Much later, Section 2A, inserted by the National Green Tribunal Act 2010 with effect from 18 October 2010, gave any person aggrieved by an order or decision under Section 2 an appeal to the National Green Tribunal. The statute book entry therefore grew from five sections to a still small family, and every addition after 1980 was a patch on the original frame rather than a redesign. A genuine redesign arrived only in 2023, and it is treated at the end of this article and in its own.

## Section 2 of the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980: the four prohibitions

Section 2 opens with a non-obstante clause and a prohibition addressed to governments. Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law in force in a state, no state government or other authority shall make, except with the prior approval of the Central Government, any order directing one of four things. The first is that any reserved forest, within the meaning of that expression in the law in force in the state, or any portion of it, shall cease to be reserved. The second is that any forest land or any portion of it may be used for any non-forest purpose. The third is that any forest land or portion of it may be assigned by way of lease or otherwise to any private person, or to any authority, corporation, agency or other organisation not owned, managed or controlled by government. The fourth is that any forest land or portion of it may be cleared of trees which have grown naturally in it, for the purpose of using it for reafforestation. Each of the four limbs answers a documented route by which the forest estate had been shrinking, and each repays separate attention.

The first limb, de-reservation, targets the legal act of unclassifying a reserved forest. Reservation under the Indian Forest Act was the strongest protection the old law knew, and a state that wished to free reserved land for a project had first to strip the classification. Section 2 makes that step impossible without central approval, which means a state cannot do indirectly, by reclassification, what the second limb forbids it to do directly. The second limb, use for a non-forest purpose, is the workhorse of the Act and the limb under which nearly every project clearance is sought. Its reach depends on two undefined or semi-defined terms, forest land and non-forest purpose, and the statute addresses only the second, through an explanation examined in the next section. The third limb, assignment by lease or otherwise, was inserted by the 1988 amendment to catch a route the original text had left open: a state could leave the classification and the nominal use untouched while handing the land to a private party by lease, and the land would pass out of public control as effectively as by diversion. Mining leases, plantation leases and leases to corporations are the standard cases; the limb reaches any private person and any body outside government ownership, management or control.

The fourth limb is the subtlest and the one lay readers most often miss. It forbids, without prior approval, the clearing of naturally grown trees on forest land for the purpose of reafforestation. A natural mixed forest could otherwise be felled and replaced with a commercial plantation of teak, eucalyptus or pine, and the transaction recorded in official statistics as forest remaining forest, while the ecosystem was converted from a diverse natural stand into a single-species crop. The limb treats that conversion as a loss the Act cares about, which is an ecologically literate judgment embedded in a 1980 statute: canopy cover is not the same thing as a forest. Plantation activity that does not involve clearing natural growth stands outside the limb, and the distinction between improving a degraded forest by planting and replacing a natural forest by planting runs through the compensatory afforestation debate examined later in this article.

Three features of the section's construction govern everything that follows. First, the prohibition is on the making of an order. The Act does not itself criminalise felling or occupation in the first instance; it disables governments from authorising the four outcomes, and a diversion carried out on a state's own authority is a diversion without legal authority. Second, the approval must be prior. The architecture assumes a sequence: proposal, central examination, approval, then state order, then work. Approvals sought after work has begun, the regularisation and ex post facto questions that recur in the Act's history, sit uneasily with that sequence, and the courts and the Ministry have wrestled with them for decades. Third, the approval is the Central Government's, exercised in practice by the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change on the advice of the committee Section 3 authorises. The state proposes; the Centre decides; and the state that owns and manages the forest cannot lawfully convert it alone. That division of labour, proposer and approver separated by level of government, is the institutional heart of the Act.

## What counts as a non-forest purpose

The Act's only definitional provision sits in the Explanation to Section 2, and it defines non-forest purpose rather than forest. For the purposes of the section, non-forest purpose means the breaking up or clearing of any forest land or portion of it for the cultivation of tea, coffee, spices, rubber, palms, oil-bearing plants, horticultural crops or medicinal plants, or for any purpose other than reafforestation. The 1988 amendment expanded the Explanation by naming the plantation crops expressly, closing an argument that commercial plantation agriculture was a forest activity because it kept land under tree cover. A tea garden, a coffee estate, a rubber plantation: each is a non-forest purpose, however green it looks from the air, because the Explanation asks what the land is used for and not what grows on it. Beyond the named crops, the general limb of the definition catches any breaking up or clearing for any purpose other than reafforestation, which is why mining, industry, roads, railways, canals, reservoirs, townships, defence installations, transmission lines and wind farms all pass through the clearance gate.

The same Explanation carries an exception of equal practical importance. Non-forest purpose does not include any work relating or ancillary to the conservation, development and management of forests and wildlife, and the text instances the establishment of check-posts and fire lines, wireless communications, and the construction of fencing, bridges and culverts, dams, waterholes, trench marks, boundary marks, pipelines or other like purposes. The exception explains itself. A forest department cannot protect a forest without entering it, and the works that protection requires, a guard's check-post at a forest boundary, a fire line cut before the dry season, a waterhole for wildlife in a drought-prone sanctuary, a boundary pillar demarcating the estate, are forest purposes even though they involve construction on forest land. The exception is functional rather than categorical: it covers work whose purpose is the conservation, development and management of the forest itself, and it is construed by reference to that purpose. A pipeline instance in the list is ancillary only where it serves the forest establishment; a commercial pipeline crossing a forest is a diversion like any other.

The definition's breadth catches activity that does not look like destruction. Mineral prospecting that breaks up forest land, including exploratory drilling, falls within it, because the Explanation turns on the breaking up or clearing of the land and not on the permanence of the project that follows, and the treatment of early-stage exploration has been a standing negotiation between the mining and environment ministries ever since: exploration is how a mining pipeline begins, and a definition that exempted it would let the first and most consequential step of a mining diversion proceed ungated. Survey and investigation of the kind that does not break up or clear the land stand outside the definition, and the line between a survey party walking a ridge with instruments and a drill rig opening a pad in the same forest is the line the Explanation draws, between looking at a forest and using it.

The drafting choice to define the prohibited use and leave the protected object undefined deserves to be seen clearly, because it determined the next forty years. Parliament evidently assumed that forest land identified itself: the reserved and protected forests of the 1927 Act, the forests recorded in state records, the areas everyone in a forest department could point to. Definitions of the trigger concepts were left to the older statute book and to administration. For notified forests that assumption held. For the large estate of unclassed forests, community forests, revenue forests and tree-covered private land, it did not, and the question of what the word forest means was waiting for the first court forced to answer it.

## The amendment of 1988: Parliament patches the gate

Eight years of operation taught the system's administrators where the original text leaked, and the Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act 1988, Act 69 of 1988, is the record of those lessons. The two new limbs of Section 2 answer evasions that practice had revealed. Assignment by lease had let states hand forest land to private plantation and mining interests while the classification and the nominal forest use stood undisturbed, so the third limb brought every assignment to a person or body outside government control inside the approval requirement. The clearing of natural forest for reafforestation had let departments and lessees convert mixed natural stands into commercial plantations while reporting the area as forest throughout, so the fourth limb brought that conversion inside the requirement too. The expanded Explanation, naming tea, coffee, spices, rubber, palms, oil-bearing plants, horticultural crops and medicinal plants, ended the argument that a plantation crop was a forest because it was perennial and green. And Sections 3A and 3B gave the Act its first offences, aimed not at the trespasser but at the contravention of the approval discipline itself, with the deeming provisions for departmental heads and persons in charge drawn from the standard template of Indian regulatory statutes.

The amendment's silences are as instructive as its insertions. Parliament in 1988 had the Act open on the table, had eight years of administrative experience to draw on, and still wrote no definition of forest and no definition of forest land. The omission can no longer be read as inadvertence. The recorded-forest understanding, under which the Act governed the forests of the records and the departments, was working smoothly for the governments that operated it, and a statutory definition would have forced the question the records evaded: the status of the unrecorded, the unclassed and the private. Legislatures leave undefined the terms whose definition would cost them a decision, and the cost was transferred, as undefined costs usually are, to the courts. When the Supreme Court supplied the dictionary meaning eight years later, the surprise registered in state capitals was the surprise of institutions that had treated their own convenience as the statute's meaning. The 1988 amendment also fixed the Act's penal character at the modest level examined later in this article, a choice consistent with the same institutional settlement: the Act would discipline governments through permission and price, not through prosecution, and its authors were content that it should.

## The definition Parliament never wrote

Nothing in the Act defines forest, and nothing defines forest land. Section 2 borrows the expression reserved forest from the law in force in each state, which ties the first limb to the classifications of the Indian Forest Act and its state counterparts, but the second, third and fourth limbs speak of forest land without qualification, and the Act supplies no test for identifying it. In the Act's first sixteen years the gap was papered over by practice: clearances were sought for recorded and notified forests, and land outside the records was generally treated as outside the Act. States decided for themselves which of their tracts the Act governed, and a state that classified a tract as revenue land could treat it as free of the clearance obligation whatever grew on it.

The consequences of the omission were not symmetrical. A definition written by Parliament would have fixed the Act's reach in 1980, for good and ill, and could have been revised by amendment as knowledge improved. The absence of a definition meant the reach would be fixed by whoever authoritatively interpreted the statute, and in Indian law that means the courts. It also meant the fixing would happen inside litigation about something else: not a considered mapping exercise but a writ petition about felling in one district of Tamil Nadu. The Godavarman order of December 1996, examined in detail in its own section below, is therefore not an accident that befell the Act from outside. It is the predictable product of the Act's own structure, a statute that regulated a noun it never defined. The series thesis that a statute's operative meaning often lies outside its text finds here its purest instance: the most important sentence in Indian forest law is a sentence of statutory interpretation, and everything since 1996, the deemed forest category, the expert committees, the state identification exercises, the 2023 amendment itself, is an argument about who should supply the missing definition and what it should say.

### How do states decide whether an unrecorded tract is a deemed forest?

Acting on the Supreme Court's 1996 direction, each state constituted an expert committee to identify tracts answering the dictionary meaning of forest, applying criteria the state itself fixed: minimum area, canopy density, and the proportion of naturally grown trees. Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, for instance, adopted a threshold of ten hectares with two hundred or more trees per hectare.

The variation in those criteria is the practical core of the deemed forest problem. A ten-hectare threshold excludes the small wooded patches, sacred groves, roadside and canal-side strips, and fragmented village forests that are ecologically significant in aggregate and worthless to no one who lives beside them; a density test counts trees and not ecosystems, so a dense plantation can qualify while an open natural woodland, a grassland-forest mosaic or a scrub jungle full of wildlife fails. The Ministry's own presentation of the state expert committee reports describes the collated identification data as incomplete, and the reports were compiled state by state over different periods with different methods, so the national map of deemed forest has never existed as a single settled document. For a landowner, the consequence is concrete: whether a particular parcel attracts the Act can depend on an identification exercise conducted in the district years earlier, on criteria chosen by a committee the owner never heard of, recorded in a report the owner cannot easily obtain. For a state government, the same incompleteness is an administrative embarrassment and, occasionally, a convenience. The conservation answer to this critique is that the imperfection of identification is an argument for completing the exercise, not for narrowing the protection: the tracts at issue are forests in fact, and a record-keeping failure by the state is a strange ground for stripping them of the law's cover. That exchange, between administrative certainty and ecological fact, is the deemed forest debate in miniature, and Parliament's 2023 attempt to settle it by tying the Act to the records is the point at which this article's successor takes up the story.

## The machinery: an advisory committee and a ministry

Section 3 provides that the Central Government may constitute a committee of such number of persons as it deems fit to advise it on the grant of approval under Section 2 and on any other matter connected with the conservation of forests referred to it. The committee the section authorises is the Forest Advisory Committee, the body around which the entire clearance process is organised. Its composition, drawn from the Ministry's forest bureaucracy and outside experts in forestry, wildlife, soil conservation and allied fields, is a matter of executive constitution rather than statutory specification, and its advice, while formally advisory, is in practice the decision point of the system: approvals and rejections track its recommendations, and a proposal that cannot survive the committee does not survive at all.

The committee does not work alone, and its workload is distributed by size and category under the rules and guidelines the Ministry maintains. Proposals are examined first within the state, by the forest department and the state government, and reach the Centre through the Ministry's regional offices. Larger proposals, in the standard arrangement those above forty hectares, are appraised by the Forest Advisory Committee itself; proposals below that scale are appraised by Regional Empowered Committees attached to the regional offices; and specified small or linear categories have at various points been placed under general approvals, where a standing permission subject to conditions replaces case-by-case appraisal. The thresholds and category lists have been revised repeatedly by guideline and rule amendment, and the durable structural point is the filtration: tens of thousands of proposals cannot all be decided in one room in New Delhi, so the statute's single committee power has grown into a tiered appraisal system, with the committee at its apex and the regional machinery doing the bulk of the examination.

Between the committee and the forest stand the Ministry's regional offices, the layer of the system that sees the ground. The regional offices, established in the years after the Act to take the Centre's forest functions out of the capital, conduct the site inspections on which appraisal relies, verify the compliance reports that unlock Stage II, and monitor diverted land after approval, reporting violations into the file. Their inspection reports are the evidentiary base of the whole structure: the committee in New Delhi decides on the strength of what the regional office records about canopy, encroachment, wildlife signs and the truth of the proponent's maps. The chronic complaint about the system, from proponents and conservationists alike, is that this layer is thin: a small professional staff carrying the inspection load of a subcontinent's diversion proposals, so that the verification on which everything depends is the function the system resources least. The complaint is structural rather than partisan, and it has survived every procedural reform because reforms rewrite rules and the inspection load is a budget line.

The rest of the machinery is procedural rather than statutory. The Forest (Conservation) Rules, first framed in 1981 and replaced in 2003 and again in 2022, prescribe the form of proposals and the sequence of examination. The Ministry's handbook of guidelines on the Act, consolidated in editions culminating in the 2019 handbook, supplies the working detail: the documents a proposal must carry, the site inspection reports, the cost-benefit analysis required for larger diversions, the enumeration of trees, the catchment treatment plans for river valley projects, and the stage-wise conditions. Applications move through the PARIVESH portal, the single-window electronic system for environment, forest, wildlife and coastal regulation zone clearances launched in 2018, which made the file, its deficiencies and its movement visible to the applicant and the public in a way the paper system never did. None of this machinery is in the Act. All of it is the Act in operation, because a five-section statute that said only "prior approval" required an apparatus to decide what an approval application looks like, who examines it, and on what material. The rules and handbook are where the statute's real procedural law lives, and any account of the Act that stops at its five sections describes the gate but not the queue.

## The rules beneath the Act, and appraisal in practice

Section 4's rule-making power has carried more law than the parent section suggests. The first Forest (Conservation) Rules of 1981 did little more than constitute the advisory committee and prescribe the form of a proposal. The Forest (Conservation) Rules 2003 rebuilt the procedure into the shape the system still recognises: a prescribed sequence from the user agency through the state to the Centre, time limits for each processing stage, the formal two-stage approval in which in-principle approval precedes compliance and final approval follows it, and the appraisal tiers. The Forest (Conservation) Rules 2022 revised the sequence again, adjusting the appraisal structure and, in the change with the deepest consequences, removing the forest rights compliance material from the in-principle stage, a shift examined in the consent section of this article. Around the rules sits the thicker layer of guidelines, clarifications and office memoranda by which the Ministry answers the questions the rules do not: the treatment of renewals, of prospecting, of leases straddling categories, of violations, of compensatory land in hill states where non-forest land does not exist. A practitioner advising on a diversion reads the Act in minutes, the rules in an hour, and the guidelines for the rest of a career, and the ratio between those reading times is the truest measure of where this statute's operative law resides.

Appraisal in practice is a discipline of returns. The committee's characteristic act is not refusal but deferral: a proposal is examined, found wanting in enumeration, alternatives analysis, wildlife comment or compensatory land detail, and returned for the deficiency to be cured, sometimes repeatedly across years. Outright rejections are rare relative to deferrals, and final approvals, when they issue, carry condition lists whose length has grown with the decades, from the bare compensatory conditions of the 1980s to the multi-page instruments of the modern file, covering reclamation bonds, wildlife passages, controlled blasting, dust and water management, local employment and monitoring committees. Two consequences follow for how the system should be judged. The first is that approval statistics flatter neither side's story: a high approval rate does not show a permissive regime when the approved file has been cut down, conditioned and priced through years of appraisal, and a low rejection rate does not show rigour when the rigour is exercised by attrition before the decision. The second is that the real discretion in the system sits with the appraisal process rather than the statutory text, in the questions the committee chooses to ask and the answers it chooses to accept, which is why the composition of the committee, and the recorded presence and absence of its non-official expert members over the years, has attracted a scrutiny the five-section statute would never suggest.

## The penalty that is not one

For its first eight years the Act contained no penal provision at all, and the sanction for breach was structural: a diversion order made without prior approval was unlawful, the work done under it unauthorised, and the state exposed to the Supreme Court's supervision and to directions for restoration or regularisation on terms. The 1988 amendment inserted the Act's only offences. Section 3A provides that whoever contravenes or abets the contravention of any provision of Section 2 shall be punishable with simple imprisonment for a period which may extend to fifteen days. Section 3B addresses offences by authorities and government departments: where an offence under the Act is committed by a department of government, the head of the department is deemed guilty; where it is committed by an authority, every person directly in charge of and responsible to the authority for the conduct of its business, as well as the authority itself, is deemed guilty; and a proviso protects the head or the person in charge who proves the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it. A companion clause reaches officers whose consent, connivance or neglect enabled the offence.

Fifteen days of simple imprisonment, with no fine specified in the section, is a penalty calibrated for a different problem than the one the Act faces. The diversion of forest land is rarely the act of an individual trespasser; it is the act of a government that has decided a project matters more than the procedure, or of a user agency that has begun work in the confidence that approval will follow. Against a state government, a prosecution of its forest secretary under Section 3A is a theoretical instrument, deployed rarely if at all, and the provision's real function is symbolic: it marks unauthorised diversion as an offence rather than an administrative irregularity. The working sanctions of the system are administrative and financial instead. A diversion discovered after the fact is met with demands for penal compensatory afforestation over additional area, penal net present value at multiples of the standard rate, and proceedings for the violation recorded against the proposal; the Ministry's guidelines prescribe these consequences, and the Supreme Court's orders in the Godavarman proceedings supplied the coercive environment in which they bite. The gap between the statute's penal text and the system's actual deterrents is one of the Act's instructive features. Parliament in 1988 treated contravention as a minor offence by individuals. The system that grew up treats it as a fiscal and procedural event by institutions, and prices it accordingly.

There is a defensible reading of that gap and an indefensible one. The defensible reading is that deterrence in a regime of government actors works through cost and exposure rather than imprisonment: a user agency that pays penal levies at multiples, loses years to regularisation proceedings, and carries a recorded violation into its next proposal has been punished in the currency it understands. The indefensible reading is that a state government which diverts forest land in defiance of a central statute faces no personal consequence for anyone, that fifteen days has never been a credible threat against a considered official decision, and that a penalty provision unused for three decades does not deter but merely decorates. Both readings are available on the record. What the record does not support is the claim that the penal section has been a working part of the enforcement system; the system's working parts are the clearance file, the levy demand and the court's continuing supervision, and it is to the court that the story next turns.

## The gate tested early: leases, renewals and subsisting rights

Before Godavarman, the courts had already been asked what the gate did to rights that existed when it was built. The mining industry supplied the test cases, because a mining lease granted before 1980 over forest land collided with the Act the moment the lessee broke new ground or sought to continue beyond the original term. In State of Bihar v Banshi Ram Modi, decided in 1985, the Supreme Court read Section 2 into a subsisting mining lease: the lessee's right to mine under a lease granted before the Act did not carry a right to break up or clear forest land after the Act without the Central Government's prior approval, because the statutory restriction fastens on the act of breaking up forest land, whenever the underlying lease was granted. In Ambica Quarry Works v State of Gujarat, decided in 1987, the Court completed the proposition for the renewal case: the renewal of a mining lease is in substance a fresh grant, and a renewal that would permit mining on forest land requires prior approval under Section 2 like any new diversion.

The two decisions look technical and are not. Together they foreclosed the argument that the Act governed only the state's paperwork and left vested private arrangements untouched. The gate reaches backwards into subsisting leases and forwards into every renewal, which is why mining on forest land became, from the late 1980s onward, a clearance discipline rather than a property entitlement: the lease could be valid under the mining laws and the work under it still unlawful until the forest approval issued. The same logic explains a recurring feature of diversion practice, the treatment of prospecting and of leases straddling forest and non-forest land, where the forest portion of a single lease area holds the whole project at the gate until its status is resolved. Project lawyers learned early what the two cases teach: in forest country, the sequence of permissions matters as much as their substance, and the forest permission is the one that cannot be assumed.

## Godavarman: the sentence that redefined the statute

T. N. Godavarman Thirumulpad was a member of the Nilambur royal family of Kerala whose ancestral janmam lands in the Gudalur region of the Nilgiris had passed to the state under land reform legislation, and who watched the forests on those slopes being felled under state authority. In 1995 he filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court about that felling. The petition named a local grievance; the proceedings it opened became the forum in which Indian forest governance was conducted for a generation. On 12 December 1996 a bench of Justices J. S. Verma and B. N. Kirpal delivered the order, reported at (1997) 2 SCC 267, that fixed the Act's meaning.

The order's reasoning begins from the Act's purpose and refuses to let classification defeat it. The Act, the Court said, was enacted to check further deforestation leading to ecological imbalance, and its provisions for the conservation of forests must therefore apply to all forests irrespective of the nature of ownership or classification. The operative sentence follows: the word forest must be understood according to its dictionary meaning. That description, the Court held, covers all statutorily recognised forests, whether designated as reserved, protected or otherwise, for the purposes of Section 2. The term forest land in Section 2 includes not only forest as understood in the dictionary sense but also any area recorded as forest in government records irrespective of ownership. Read together, the two limbs give the Act a double reach. Land that is forest in fact is covered even if no record calls it forest and even if it is privately or community owned. Land that is recorded as forest is covered even if it is bare. Neither a state's classification decision nor an owner's title can take land outside the statute.

The order travelled well beyond definition in a second respect as well: it reached the forest's occupied frontier, directing that encroachments on forest land not be regularised except in accordance with the central position, and requiring the states to account for the encroachments on their estates. Regularisation is diversion by another name, the conversion of forest land to farms and homesteads by administrative grace, and the order closed the route by which the estate could shrink in small parcels beneath the clearance system's notice, each parcel too minor for a file, the aggregate rivalling the largest industrial diversions. The encroachment directions, renewed across the proceedings, forced the states into the position they have occupied since: unable to evict at scale for reasons of politics and humanity, unable to regularise at scale for reasons of law, and administering a frozen frontier whose resolution the Forest Rights Act later attempted from the rights side.

The order travelled well beyond definition. It directed every state to constitute an expert committee to identify areas that are forests irrespective of notification, recognition, classification or ownership, areas that were earlier forests but stand degraded, denuded or cleared, and areas covered by plantation trees belonging to government and to private persons: the identification exercise whose incompleteness still shadows the system, as the deemed forest discussion in this article has recorded. It restricted the felling of trees in forests to felling carried out in accordance with working plans approved by the Central Government, displacing the practice of felling under departmental discretion. And it treated the proceeding as one the Court would keep: application after application was entertained within the same writ petition, orders accumulated into the hundreds, and forest questions of every description, mining leases, sawmills, encroachments, fund releases, committee appointments, were routed into the Godavarman file. The petitioner himself, in an interview recorded by the Supreme Court Observer, said he had been surprised by the sweep of the judgment. A grievance about one Tamil Nadu landscape had become a supervisory jurisdiction over the forests of India.

The institutional machinery the proceedings generated matters as much as the definition. Acting within the case, the Court in an order of 9 May 2002 constituted the Central Empowered Committee, a body of experts and officials charged with monitoring compliance with the Court's orders and examining the questions the Court referred to it; the committee was thereafter given statutory form by notification under Section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, the provision empowering the Centre to constitute authorities for environmental purposes. The Central Empowered Committee became the system's second centre of gravity: user agencies, states and objectors all learned to address it, its reports shaped the Court's orders on compensatory afforestation and net present value, and its existence converted the definition of 1996 from a proposition in a judgment into an administered regime. The wider pattern, a specialised tribunal-like body grown inside a writ petition and later clothed with statutory authority, is one of the defining techniques of [the Supreme Court's environmental jurisprudence](/2014/12/15/environmental-jurisprudence-supreme-court/), and the forest case is its fullest working example.

The committee's reports functioned, in practice, as a form of shadow legislation for the forest sector. It was on the Central Empowered Committee's examination and recommendation that the net present value regime took the shape it did, that the compensatory fund was designed before Parliament legislated it, and that compliance failures in particular states and projects were placed before the Court in decided form rather than as allegations. A body created to monitor orders became the system's research arm, its valuation expert and its inspectorate, which is why the committee's constitution under statutory authority mattered beyond symbolism: it acknowledged that the functions were permanent features of forest governance and gave them a legal home outside the writ petition that had improvised them. The acknowledgment cuts both ways in the institutional debate, since machinery that begins as a court's instrument and ends as a statutory authority has followed, in reverse, the route by which temporary expedients become the constitution of a sector.

Later benches applied the definition where it bit hardest. In Lafarge Umiam Mining v Union of India, decided in 2011, the Court dealt with limestone mining on community-owned forest land in the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, land held under the customary arrangements of a Sixth Schedule district, and treated the Act as fully applicable: community ownership was no answer to a clearance obligation, and the forest rights compliance process, including gram sabha certification under the Ministry's 2009 circular, was integral to a lawful diversion. The proposition generalises the 1996 holding into the tenure systems where it was most doubted. The dictionary meaning does not stop at the boundary of the forest department's estate, at a state border, or at a constitutional schedule; it follows the trees.

The continuing mandamus, the technique of retaining the case and issuing directions over time rather than disposing of it, is the point at which admiration and unease about Godavarman divide. The case for the technique is the case for the definition itself, extended over time. Forest administration had demonstrated, across the pre-1980 decades and in the identification delays after 1996, that it would not police itself; the Court's retained supervision supplied the missing enforcement, built the fund machinery the executive had failed to build, and kept a national standard from dissolving into twenty-eight state practices. The case against is institutional and democratic. A writ petition is not a legislature, a bench is not a forest service, and policy made by interlocutory order, on affidavits, without a drafting process or a vote, lacks the error-correction machinery that statutes and administrations possess even on their bad days; the same Supreme Court, in later orders within the proceedings, has itself spoken of the need to streamline and rationalise a docket swollen by hundreds of interlocutory applications. Both cases are made by serious people, and the honest summary is that the Court filled a vacuum it did not create and then found the vacuum had no exit: every reform of the system since 1996, including the fund legislation of 2016 examined below and the amendment of 2023, has had to be negotiated in the shadow of the pending proceeding.

## The orders beyond the definition: sawmills, timber and mines

The definition was the famous holding, but the continuing proceeding governed much more than classification, and a pillar account of the Act must register how far the supervision reached into the forest economy. The Court's orders of the late 1990s closed unlicensed sawmills and wood-based units across the North-East and regulated the licensing of sawmills and veneer and plywood units elsewhere, on the reasoning that an unregulated processing industry creates the demand that illegal felling supplies. Orders governed the movement of timber between states, the working of forests in Arunachal Pradesh where departmental felling had outrun any plan, and the suspension of mining in specified forest regions, each intervention issued as an interlocutory direction within the same writ petition. The forest-based industry of entire states was reordered from that file. Supporters of the supervision treat this record as the proof of concept: no other forum could have acted nationally, at speed, against a trade whose illegality fed on jurisdictional fragmentation. Critics treat the same record as the indictment: industrial licensing, trade regulation and mining suspension are executive functions, and a court performing them by order, without a statute's apparatus of consultation, transition and compensation, governs by a method that would be condemned in any other policy field. The orders also generated the proceeding's own pathologies, a docket of interlocutory applications numbering in the hundreds in which every affected interest in Indian forestry, from a single sawmill owner to a state government, eventually filed, converting a writ petition into something resembling a permanent forest tribunal with a constitutional bench's authority and a tribunal's caseload.

## Deemed forests on the ground: one definition, many Indias

What the dictionary meaning did to the map depended on whose map it was. In the states of the North-East, where the recorded forest estate sits alongside vast unclassed forests held by communities, clans and customary institutions, the definition swept in land that no forest department had ever administered. In Nagaland the overwhelming share of the forest estate is held by communities rather than the state, and the proposition that a village's ancestral forest could not be diverted, leased or cleared without the approval of the Government of India sat uneasily beside the constitutional arrangements of Article 371A, which vests land and its resources in the people of Nagaland. The Act does not resolve that tension; it overlays it, and clearances in the hill states have carried a double character ever since, statutory at the Centre and customary on the ground.

In Goa, the identification exercise took the form of successive expert committees classifying privately owned wooded tracts as private forests, applying area and canopy criteria to estates whose owners had treated the land as orchard, plantation or simply family property. The classification decided what could be built, sold for development or converted, and the litigation it generated made Goa the laboratory of the deemed forest idea: the state where the question whether your grandfather's wooded plot is a forest stopped being rhetorical. In Haryana, the running fight has concerned the Aravalli lands recorded under the Punjab Land Preservation Act, where scrub and thorn forest on ancient hill terrain answers the dictionary meaning on any honest reading while carrying land records that call it something else, and where every construction boom in the National Capital Region renews the pressure to read the records rather than the land. In Karnataka, Odisha and Madhya Pradesh the equivalent categories are revenue forests, village forests and the orange areas of disputed classification left over from princely-state settlements, each with its own committee reports and its own backlog of unidentified tracts.

Identification, once recorded, is not self-executing peace. Landowners and state governments have litigated the expert committee reports from both directions: owners contending that identified land lacks the tree cover or the contiguity the criteria require, conservation petitioners contending that the criteria themselves were set to exclude what the dictionary includes, as with thresholds that write small and fragmented forests out of protection by arithmetic. The High Courts became the arena for these contests district by district, which means the meaning of the Supreme Court's single sentence of 1996 has been supplied, in the end, not once but hundreds of times, in hundreds of local proceedings about particular survey numbers. A definition supplied by a court to avoid a patchwork produced a patchwork of its own, administered at the level where the land actually lies.

The pattern across these unlike cases is the one the brief for this article identifies as the system's standing uncertainty. The Supreme Court in 1996 directed identification because a definition without a map is an invitation to dispute; three decades of committees produced maps that are partial, dated and methodologically inconsistent between states; and the landowner, the project proponent and the forest officer at the district level all conduct their business over a boundary that exists in law with complete clarity and on the ground with almost none. The conservation response, that the boundary problem argues for completing the identification rather than abandoning the definition, is logically strong and has been available since 1996. The administrative record, states reporting and re-reporting identifications across decades without settling them, is the standing rebuke to it.

## The clearance path: two stages and a long corridor between them

A diversion begins with identification. The user agency, the project proponent, which may be a ministry, a state undertaking, a mining company or a private developer, must first establish whether its site involves forest land, and after Godavarman that inquiry is not answered by the revenue record alone: recorded forest, notified forest and tracts answering the dictionary meaning and the state's deemed forest criteria all count. The proposal is then prepared with the state forest department, whose nodal officer assembles the file: the map and enumeration of trees, the justification for the site and the alternatives examined, the compensatory afforestation scheme, the cost-benefit analysis where the rules require one, and the certificates the guidelines prescribe. The state government forwards the proposal with its recommendation to the Central Government through the Ministry's regional office, where site inspection and examination test the file against the Act, the working plan position, and the claims of the forest itself.

Appraisal follows the tiered route described earlier: the Forest Advisory Committee for the larger proposals, the Regional Empowered Committees for the smaller, with the Ministry able to seek further information, impose a site visit, or return the proposal for defects at any point. The appraisal is where the ecological questions are supposed to be forced into the open: whether the project can be sited off forest land, whether the area sought is the minimum necessary, what the diversion does to a wildlife corridor or a catchment, and what conditions would make the loss acceptable if it is accepted at all. Where the land lies inside or near a protected area, a parallel obligation runs alongside the forest clearance, since diversion affecting a national park or sanctuary engages the habitat protection scheme of [the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972](/2014/10/15/wildlife-protection-act-1972-explained/) and the scrutiny of the National Board for Wildlife's standing committee. A forest approval is therefore often one of two or three permissions a project on forest land requires, and it interacts with the land question in a way project planners underestimate: forest clearance decides whether forest land may change use, a different question from who owns the land or how it is acquired, and the general law of land acquisition, compensation and rehabilitation that governs taking land for public purposes is the subject of [the land acquisition law guide](/2013/10/01/land-acquisition-law-india-guide/) in this series.

The decision arrives in two stages, and the two-stage design is the process feature most often misdescribed outside the system. Stage I, called in-principle approval, records the Central Government's agreement that the diversion may proceed subject to conditions. The conditions are the price list of the system, stated in advance: transfer and mutation of the compensatory afforestation land in favour of the forest department, deposit of the compensatory afforestation cost and the net present value into the fund, undertakings on wildlife mitigation, catchment treatment, and, where the forest rights regime applies, the compliance certification that section of this article examines. Stage I authorises nothing on the ground. The user agency and the state then execute the conditions, the state reports compliance, the Ministry verifies it, and only then does Stage II, final approval, issue; the state government passes the formal diversion order, the land is handed over, and felling and work may begin. Each stage has a different legal character. Stage I is a conditional promise; Stage II is the permission; and the interval between them, during which land must be found, money deposited and certificates assembled, is where proposals spend most of their lives.

A Stage I condition list, read closely, is the appraisal's whole reasoning converted into obligations. The standard conditions require the legal status of the diverted land to remain unchanged, forbid any use of the land for a purpose other than the one approved, require the compensatory afforestation land to be transferred and mutated before Stage II and the levies to be deposited in the fund, require the boundary of the diversion to be demarcated on the ground with pillars at the user agency's cost, prohibit labour camps and depots on forest land outside the approved area, and reserve the Centre's power to withdraw the approval for breach. Project-specific conditions then layer on the appraisal's particular findings: the wildlife passage at the corridor crossing, the catchment treatment above the reservoir, the phased reclamation of the mine, the controlled use of explosives near habitat. The conditions are where the system's ecological judgement survives the meeting room, and their drafting quality decides whether the approval on paper governs anything on the ground, because a condition that cannot be monitored cannot be breached in any way the system will ever learn about.

The file that travels this path has a standard anatomy, and its set pieces explain both the delay and the discipline. Proposals above the thresholds the guidelines prescribe, twenty hectares in the plains and five hectares in the hills for the standard categories, must carry a cost-benefit analysis in the prescribed format, which prices the loss of forest produce and ecological services against the project's benefits and employment, and which the appraisal reads less as an answer than as a test of whether the proponent has confronted the loss honestly. The tree enumeration, the count and girth-class record of what stands on the site, converts an abstract area figure into a biological inventory. Differential GPS mapping of the diversion boundary, standard in the electronic era of the process, fixed a chronic vice of the paper system, in which the land approved and the land cleared diverged quietly on the ground. And the alternatives analysis, the documented examination of non-forest and less damaging sites, is the component appraisers read first and proponents draft last, which is why appraisal so often turns on it: a project that has not seriously looked for another site has not begun to make its case at this gate.

### Why does forest clearance take so long?

The Act sets no deadline; the time is consumed by the process itself: state examination and site inspection, committee appraisal, deficiency rounds in which the Ministry returns the file for missing material, and then the compliance interval between the two stages, in which compensatory land must be transferred and the levies deposited before final approval can issue.

The honest answer about timelines must hold two facts together. The Ministry's guidelines prescribe time limits for each processing stage, and the PARIVESH portal made those timelines visible, yet large proposals routinely take years rather than the months the charts suggest, because the delay is rarely a single official sitting on a file: it is the accumulation of defective proposals, missing enumerations, compensatory land that cannot be found in the district, state governments slow to forward or to report compliance, and appraisal committees unwilling to decide big diversions on thin material. Industry tells the story as obstruction; forest officers tell it as quality control; the file usually supports both readings in different proportions. What the structure guarantees is that no stage of the process is quick by design, because the decision is framed as irreversible, and irreversible decisions are given time in every legal system that takes them seriously.

Post-approval monitoring closes the path and exposes its weakest joint. The conditions of a final approval oblige the user agency to report compliance, the state to verify the compensatory plantations and the transferred land, and the regional office to inspect; the diverted land is supposed to be watched for the life of the use, and the compensatory land for the longer life of the plantation that answers for it. In practice the monitoring stream runs on self-reporting, with inspections spread across the thin regional establishment described earlier in this article, and the consequence is a system that is rigorous at the gate and intermittent beyond it. Diversions are discovered in violation years after the fact, plantations are audited long after the planting window has closed, and the compliance interval that the two-stage design treats as the system's centre of gravity has no equivalent discipline at the back end. The asymmetry is the fairest single criticism of the clearance state the Act built: it inspects a forest exhaustively before permitting its destruction and sporadically thereafter, when the destruction is the fact and the compensation is the promise.

One distinction protects readers of clearance news from a standing confusion. Forest clearance under this Act is not environmental clearance, which is a separate permission under the Environment (Protection) Act 1986 and its impact assessment notifications, appraised by different committees against different criteria: the forest process asks whether this forest may be converted and at what price, the impact assessment process asks what the project will do to air, water, noise and people and on what mitigation. A project on forest land ordinarily needs both, in whichever order its files mature, and the grant of one neither implies nor excuses the other; the thematic comparison of the two regimes belongs to the environmental law guide in this series, and the point here is only that the gate of 1980 is one of two, and the older and narrower of the pair.

The paper trail of a clearance is itself a public resource of the first importance. Diversion orders, compliance reports, inspection notes and committee minutes record what was promised for a forest that no longer exists, and they are the raw material for the researchers, journalists and affected communities who audit the system afterwards; keeping such files organised, searchable and protected is a discipline in its own right, and it is the kind of work for which reporters covering land and environment describe [keeping sensitive source material and research files safe with VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/). The general position described in this article is legal information, not advice on a particular parcel or proposal: whether a specific tract is forest land turns on records, identifications and criteria that vary by state and district, and anyone acting on a specific site needs the district record and advice on it.

## The gate by project type: mining, water and lines

The conditions attached to a Stage I approval are not uniform, because the losses are not uniform, and the category practice of the clearance system is where its ecological reasoning is most visible. Mining is the hardest case and receives the hardest conditions. An opencast mine consumes the forest outright for the life of the lease, so a mining diversion carries the full apparatus: compensatory afforestation, net present value, a reclamation plan for the mined-out land, safety zone planting around the lease boundary, and, for leases in wildlife habitat, a wildlife mitigation plan prepared with the state wildlife wing. The renewal doctrine from the early cases means a mining house meets the gate repeatedly across a deposit's life, at grant, at renewal, and at any expansion, and the file of a long-worked deposit is a stratigraphy of approvals. Underground mining, which leaves the surface substantially intact, is treated more lightly in the levy structure, a distinction that prices the difference between disturbing a forest and deleting it.

River valley and hydroelectric projects present the inverse geometry: the submergence zone is a total loss of valley forest, but the damage radiates beyond it into the catchment, whose degraded slopes silt the reservoir and shorten the project's own life. The standard condition set answers with a catchment area treatment plan, executed at the user agency's cost over the degraded catchment above the dam, alongside compensatory afforestation for the submerged area and free supply conditions the guidelines attach to such projects. Linear projects, highways, railways, transmission lines and pipelines, cut differently again: each takes a narrow strip, so the per-project area looks small, while the aggregate effect is the fragmentation of continuous forest into ribbons, with edge effects, roadkill and access for encroachment and poaching travelling along the cut. The conditions respond at that level: compensatory afforestation on the strip's measure, and wildlife passage structures, underpasses, overpasses and canopy connections designed on the Wildlife Institute of India's guidance for linear infrastructure in natural areas, where the alignment crosses habitat. A reader comparing the three files sees the Act's single phrase, prior approval, doing differentiated ecological work that its text never spells out: the statute supplies the gate, and the guideline system supplies the judgement about what each kind of passage through it should cost.

Two stylised passages through the system show the machinery from the applicant's side. Take an opencast iron ore mine on two hundred hectares of recorded forest in a central Indian state. The proposal enters with the lease already recommended under the mining laws, which counts for nothing at the forest gate: the nodal officer builds the file, the tree enumeration runs into tens of thousands, the site inspection records the forest type and the wildlife presence, and the appraisal asks whether the deposit can be worked from a smaller footprint and what the ore body leaves behind. Stage I issues with conditions that price the whole loss in advance. The compliance interval then governs the project's calendar: compensatory land must be found in a district where every earlier mine has already searched for it, the levies must be deposited at the rates in force, and the rights settlement under the Forest Rights Act must be certified for villages whose relationship with that forest is older than the state. A competitor's project announced later can reach production sooner if its forest component is smaller, which is the gate working exactly as designed: the forest content of a project has become a first-order commercial variable.

Take, by contrast, a state highway widening that clips eleven hectares of forest in three separate stretches, one of them a recorded corridor between two protected areas. The area is trivial; the appraisal is not. The corridor stretch converts the file from a land question into a wildlife question, the passage structures and their design are examined against the Institute's guidance, and the conditions may cost more per hectare than any other element of the road. The same eleven hectares, had they lain in degraded revenue forest away from habitat, would have cleared on a routine file. The comparison teaches the lesson applicants most often resist: under this Act the currency is not hectares but ecological function, and the system's severest scrutiny is reserved not for the largest diversions but for the ones that sever something that cannot be replanted.

## Violations and the regularisation queue

Not every diversion passes the gate in order. Work begins on the strength of a working permission that does not cover felling, a state issues its order before the Centre's approval arrives, a lease is worked beyond its approved area, or a project simply starts and presents the system with a standing structure on cleared forest. The Act's text offers the system almost nothing for this situation beyond the fifteen-day offence of Section 3A, and the response has been built administratively and judicially instead. The Ministry's violation framework treats a diversion in violation as a proposal to be examined with the violation recorded against it, and prices the breach: penal compensatory afforestation over additional area, penal net present value at enhanced multiples of the standard rate prescribed in the guidelines, and action against the responsible officers and the user agency, with the approval, if it comes at all, arriving after the penalty rather than instead of it.

The regularisation question divides the system's critics cleanly. One side, which includes much of the conservation bar that practises in the Godavarman proceedings, argues that every regularised violation teaches the next proponent that the sequence is optional: build first, pay the penal rates, and treat the penalty as a cost of acceleration. The other side answers that the alternative to priced regularisation is not pristine compliance but unpriced illegality, since a completed dam or a worked-out mine cannot be unbuilt, and that the state's choice is between capturing compensation and conditions for a loss that has occurred or capturing nothing while litigating restoration that will never happen. The guidelines have oscillated between deterrence and pragmatism across the decades, and the honest description of the record is that both predictions came true in different places: penal levies are a real cost that compliance departments budget around, and the queue of violation proposals awaiting regularisation has never emptied.

## The price of a forest: compensatory afforestation

The Act itself says nothing about compensation. The obligation that a user agency must replace the forest it takes grew out of the clearance practice the Ministry built under Section 2 and the conditions the Supreme Court's orders hardened into standard terms. Its logic is a land-for-land exchange. When forest land is diverted, the user agency must identify and provide non-forest land equal in area to the land diverted, transfer and mutate it in favour of the state forest department, and bear the cost of raising a forest on it; where non-forest land is unavailable, compensatory afforestation is taken up over degraded forest land of twice the diverted area, at the user agency's cost. The diverted hectare thus enters the books twice: once as a loss recorded in the clearance order, and once as a promised gain recorded in the compensatory scheme, and the claim that the system conserves the forest estate rests on the second entry being as real as the first.

### What land can be offered for compensatory afforestation?

The standard condition requires non-forest land equal in area to the forest land diverted, transferred and mutated to the forest department before final approval, with the user agency paying the cost of planting it. Where non-forest land is not available, the afforestation is taken up over degraded forest land of twice the area instead.

The land-for-land rule bends where land itself is scarcest. In the hill states and in specified categories where non-forest land is genuinely unavailable, the guidelines permit compensatory afforestation over degraded forest land of twice the diverted area as the standing alternative, on the reasoning that a Himalayan district cannot produce a flat revenue parcel for every ropeway, road and water scheme that crosses its forests. The relaxation is pragmatic and corrosive in equal measure: pragmatic, because the alternative is a rule that cannot be complied with and will therefore be evaded; corrosive, because afforestation over degraded forest land adds plantation to land already counted as forest, so the ledger records a gain where the landscape records, at best, an improvement, and the estate's total area quietly shrinks by the diverted hectare that no new land answered.

The conditions of equivalence are where the mechanism strains. The land offered must be suitable for afforestation and, in the standard formulation, should be in proximity to the diverted forest so that the ecological loss is answered in the same landscape; in practice it is frequently neither. Districts with heavy diversion are districts where unencumbered non-forest land is scarcest, and the land that surfaces is often rocky, encroached, disputed, distant from the diversion site, or already carrying scrub vegetation whose clearance for plantation is itself an ecological subtraction. Compensatory land banks, assembled by states in advance, were the administrative answer to the search problem, but a land bank answers the file's question, is there a survey number to enter in the scheme, without answering the ecological question of whether a plantation on that survey number replaces anything that was lost. The critique associated with environmental researchers such as Ashish Kothari of Kalpavriksha is directed at exactly this gap: a plantation of fast-growing species, raised by a forest department under audit pressure to show area covered, is an accounting equivalent of a natural forest and not an ecological one, and natural forests lost to diversion in the Western Ghats or central India have been notionally compensated by plantations that share nothing with them but the unit of measurement.

## Net present value: pricing the services a forest performed

Compensatory afforestation replaces the trees. It does not price the forest: the timber and fuelwood, the non-timber produce, the fodder, the watershed regulation, the soil retention, the habitat, the carbon storage, the sheer ecological services a standing forest supplies free to people who never appear in the clearance file. The Supreme Court addressed that gap within the Godavarman proceedings. By its order of October 2002 the Court directed that net present value be realised from user agencies for forest land diverted, in addition to compensatory afforestation charges, on the principle that the user who takes the forest must pay for the value of the services the diversion destroys. An expert committee chaired by the environmental economist Kanchan Chopra worked out the valuation, assigning monetary values per hectare to the goods and services of forests by eco-value class and canopy density, and the rates the Ministry applied from 2008 ran from Rs 4.38 lakh per hectare for the least valued class at the lowest density to Rs 10.43 lakh per hectare for the highest class of very dense forest. The Court directed that the rates be revised every three years.

The mechanics of the charge matter to anyone who has to budget it. Net present value is a one-time payment, charged per hectare of forest land approved for diversion and deposited before final approval, and it is computed off a matrix: the forest is assigned to one of six eco-value classes, reflecting the type and ecological richness of the forest, from the tropical wet evergreen and semi-evergreen forests of the first class down to the littoral and swamp forests of the sixth, and to one of three canopy density classes, very dense, moderately dense or open. The cell where class and density meet fixes the rate, so the charge discriminates finely between a hectare of dense evergreen forest in the Western Ghats and a hectare of open scrub. The guidelines load the charge further for diversion inside protected areas, where the rates are enhanced by multiples, and the total for a large diversion routinely exceeds the compensatory afforestation cost several times over, which is why the levy, and not the plantation, is the figure project financial models argue about. It is also why the rate's stagnation mattered: an unrevised matrix applied to decade-old prices converted a charge designed to internalise an ecological loss into a predictable, shrinkable line item.

The revision history is the least creditable part of the story. The rates fixed on the Chopra framework were left unrevised for well over a decade while the prices they purported to track moved. An Indian Institute of Forest Management study commissioned by the Ministry and published in 2014 recommended roughly a fourfold increase; the committee under former Cabinet Secretary T. S. R. Subramanian, appointed to review the environmental laws, recommended a fivefold increase the same year; the Ministry finally revised the rates in January 2022, escalating them broadly with price indices to a maximum in the region of Rs 15.96 lakh per hectare for very dense forest, an increase of roughly one and a half times rather than the multiples the expert bodies had proposed. A further expert committee appointed by the Court reported in January 2022 that indexation by wholesale prices was not a scientific method of valuing forest services, a criticism the Ministry's revision did not answer. Named exemptions soften the charge at the edges: guidelines exempt specified welfare and public-purpose categories, and reduce the rate for uses such as underground mining that leave the surface forest substantially standing. The pattern across twenty years is consistent enough to state as a finding: the principle that a diverted forest must be paid for at its service value is settled law, and the price actually charged has been held, by administrative choice, well below every expert estimate of that value.

## The fund: from a court-made pool to the Act of 2016

Money collected under court orders needs somewhere to sit, and the sitting place became a controversy of its own. The levies of the 1980s and 1990s had been deposited with state governments and, on the record that emerged, were substantially unspent or absorbed into general forestry budgets while the compensatory plantations they were meant to fund lagged. Acting in the Godavarman proceedings, the Supreme Court directed in 2002 that the monies be pooled in a compensatory afforestation fund at the national level rather than left scattered with the states, and an ad hoc Compensatory Afforestation Fund Management and Planning Authority was constituted to hold the fund, with dedicated accounts operating from 2006. The ad hoc authority was a judicial improvisation performing a treasury function, and it accumulated money far faster than it could release it: the Court permitted releases to states of Rs 1,000 crore a year from 2009, and a further release window from accrued interest in 2014, but the corpus grew into the tens of thousands of crore while the plantations it existed to fund remained a fraction of the obligation.

### What did the Comptroller and Auditor General find about the afforestation money?

The Comptroller and Auditor General's performance audit of compensatory afforestation in India, reported in 2013, found the machinery failing at each joint: large balances parked unspent, compensatory land frequently not identified or not transferred, plantations raised in places and forms that audit could not verify as compensating anything, and monitoring too weak to establish survival.

Parliament's answer was the Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act 2016, Act 38 of 2016, which received assent on 3 August 2016 and came into force on 30 September 2018. The Act converted the judicial improvisation into a statutory treasury. It established a National Compensatory Afforestation Fund under the Public Account of India and a State Compensatory Afforestation Fund under the public account of each state, credited with everything user agencies pay under the 1980 Act: compensatory afforestation charges, additional and penal compensatory afforestation, net present value and project-specific amounts. Receipts divide between the funds in the proportion of ten per cent to the National Fund and ninety per cent to the State Funds, reversing the ad hoc arrangement under which the Centre held the whole corpus. National and state authorities constituted under the Act administer the funds, which are non-lapsable and interest-bearing, and the money may be spent only on the purposes the Act lists: artificial regeneration and assisted natural regeneration, protection of forests, forest-related infrastructure, wildlife protection and allied activities. The fund-transfer machinery then moved the accumulated stock: after the Compensatory Afforestation Fund Rules were notified in 2018, and with the Supreme Court's approval recorded on 28 January 2019, a sum of Rs 54,685 crore held by the ad hoc authority was brought under the Government of India, and in August 2019 the Environment Minister, Prakash Javadekar, released Rs 47,436 crore of it to twenty-seven states, the state share of the accumulated levies of three decades.

The statutory fund settles the custody question and leaves the performance question where it was. The criticisms made of the Bill during its passage, pressed by opposition members including Jairam Ramesh, who had administered the clearance system as Environment Minister, were that a ninety per cent state share returned the money to the administrative hands whose record had justified central pooling, that the Act's permitted uses ran wider than plantation and protection into infrastructure of doubtful compensatory character, and that no statutory mechanism connected the release of money to verified ecological outcomes on the ground. Defenders of the design, in the government that carried the Act, answered that money raised from a state's forests should be spent in that state under its own authority's plans, that the public account structure subjected the funds to legislative and audit oversight the ad hoc body had escaped, and that the accumulation itself, not the custody, had been the scandal. A decade of unspent money is evidence for part of each case: the pooling worked as a vault and failed as a programme, and the 2016 Act is best read as Parliament deciding that a vault administered by a court was a greater constitutional anomaly than a programme administered, imperfectly, by governments.

The statutory machinery has a rhythm the ad hoc body lacked. Each state authority prepares an annual plan of operation, the year's programme of plantations, regeneration, protection and wildlife work costed against its fund, which is approved through the authority's steering and executive committees before money is released against it, and the national authority performs the same cycle for the National Fund. The plan discipline matters because the historic failure was not theft but drift: money released without a programme becomes a deposit, and money held against a programme can at least be audited against delivery. Audit remains the system's open wound. The Comptroller and Auditor General's 2013 findings were made against the ad hoc regime, and the statutory regime's claim to have answered them rests on utilisation reporting by the same state administrations whose compensatory record the fund exists to correct. The Act of 2016 therefore completes the financial architecture of the clearance system without completing its accountability, and the question the CAG asked in 2013, whether the plantations exist and live, is the question every subsequent audit cycle will ask again.

## Cover, record and the two forests of the statistics

Arguments about the Act are chronically confused by the existence of two different forests in the official numbers, and a reader of diversion debates needs them separated. Recorded forest area is a legal category: the land entered as forest in government records, whether reserved, protected or unclassed, which the Forest Survey of India reports at about 23.58 per cent of the country's geographical area. Forest cover is a physical category: all land of one hectare or more with a tree canopy density above ten per cent, whatever its legal status or ownership, which the Survey's India State of Forest Report 2021 assessed at 21.71 per cent of the geographical area, with a further 2.91 per cent returned as tree cover outside recorded forests. The two figures overlap without coinciding. Recorded forest with no trees counts in the first and not the second; a dense orchard or a coffee estate counts in the second and not the first.

The Godavarman definition is, in effect, the union of the two ideas for legal purposes: land recorded as forest is inside the Act even when bare, and land answering the description of forest is inside the Act even when unrecorded. That is why statistics about rising forest cover do not answer the diversion debate and statistics about diversion do not refute the cover figures: a plantation raised as compensatory afforestation adds to cover without restoring recorded natural forest, an estate of tea adds to cover without entering the Act's protection, and a diverted natural forest subtracts from both ledgers at different speeds. When a minister cites cover gains and a critic cites diversion losses in the same debate, both may be reading real numbers off different maps, and the map the Act cares about is the legal one its gate defends.

## The forest department inside the gate

The Act restrains state governments, and the forest department is the state government's own arm, which gives the department a double position no other actor in the system occupies: it is both the custodian the Act protects forests through and a user agency the Act restrains. Forest departments divert forest land for their own works, roads, buildings, plantations and departmental infrastructure, and they do so through the same gate as any miner or highway authority. The fourth limb of Section 2, the prohibition on clearing naturally grown trees for reafforestation, is aimed substantially at departmental practice, at the working of the forest itself: the conversion of natural stands into plantations under departmental plans was one of the losses the 1988 amendment was written to stop, and the 1996 direction that felling follow only approved working plans disciplined the department's core management activity by requiring the ten-year working plan for each forest division, prepared by the department, to carry the Central Government's approval before felling under it could proceed.

The felling discipline showed its teeth first in the North-East. The December 1996 order's requirement that felling proceed only under approved working plans met states whose plans were outdated, unapproved or non-existent, and felling across much of the region stopped until inventories and plans could be produced, a process that ran for years. The timber economy of the north-eastern states, legal and illegal together, contracted abruptly, and the episode remains the sharpest demonstration in the Act's history of what the gate can do when a court is willing to hold it shut: an entire sector paused by an interpretive order, in a region where the constitutional and customary position of the land made the intervention most contestable. The states' eventual compliance, plans prepared, inventories filed, felling resumed within the plan framework, is quoted by the supervision's defenders as the system working; the years of suspension are quoted by its critics as governance by ambush. Both descriptions fit the same facts.

Encroachment is the department's other standing encounter with the gate. Forest land occupied by cultivation and settlement, much of it predating 1980 and entangled with the same unrecorded rights the Forest Rights Act later addressed, cannot be regularised by a state acting alone: the Ministry's guidelines of 1990 contemplated review of encroachments from before the Act under strict conditions, and treated later encroachments as ineligible for regularisation, a line the approval power polices because every regularisation is in substance a diversion of forest land to a non-forest use. The department thus administers an estate whose boundaries it cannot itself adjust, occupied in places by people whose claims it cannot itself settle by eviction or by title, and the friction of that position explains much of the service's institutional ambivalence toward a statute that simultaneously empowers it against every other claimant and subordinates it to New Delhi.

## Forest rights and the consent question

The 1980 Act speaks only of governments and user agencies. The people who live in and from the forest enter its scheme nowhere, and for the Act's first quarter century a diversion could be lawfully approved over a landscape of unrecorded customary rights, because the forest settlement processes of the older law had left the rights of forest dwellers unrecorded across much of the estate. The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act 2006 changed the legal ground under the clearance system by recognising individual and community rights over forest land, including the community right to protect, regenerate and manage community forest resources, and by empowering the gram sabha to initiate the determination of rights; the statute's place in this series is [the Forest Rights Act 2006 article](/2017/03/15/forest-rights-act-2006-explained/), and the question here is the narrower one of how its rights regime connects to the diversion gate of 1980.

The connection was made by executive instruction, and the instruction stood on a rights machinery that was itself still being built: the Forest Rights Rules were amended in 2012 to strengthen the gram sabha's conduct of the recognition process, clarifying how the sabha initiates claims, verifies them and records its resolutions, the very documents the clearance system would demand as certificates. The consent file in a diversion proposal is therefore the product of two statutes working through each other's paperwork, the rights recognised under the 2006 Act becoming the precondition certificates of the 1980 Act's process. By an order of 3 August 2009, the Environment Ministry directed that no diversion of forest land for a non-forest purpose would be approved unless the process of recognising rights under the Forest Rights Act had been completed for the land in question, and it required evidence of that completion, including the written consent of each gram sabha concerned, given after the sabha had been informed of the proposal and its purpose. The requirement was carried into the Forest (Conservation) Rules framework, under which the District Collector certified the settlement of rights and the obtaining of gram sabha consent as part of the proposal before in-principle approval. The legal character of the requirement deserves precision, because argument about it is often sloppy. The consent obligation does not appear in the text of the 1980 Act, and the Forest Rights Act does not in terms impose a consent condition on diversion; the obligation is a rule of the clearance process, created by the Ministry's order and the rules, resting on the proposition that a diversion which extinguishes recognised or recognisable rights cannot lawfully be set in motion until the rights have been determined and their holders have been heard through the institution the 2006 Act made authoritative.

The Supreme Court gave that proposition judicial force in the Niyamgiri case. Vedanta's Sterlite group and the Orissa Mining Corporation sought diversion of forest land in the Niyamgiri hills of Odisha for bauxite mining, on land the Dongria Kondh, a particularly vulnerable tribal group, held sacred and used as their habitat. The Environment Minister, Jairam Ramesh, refused the forest clearance in August 2010, resting substantially on the violation of the forest rights of the Dongria Kondh and on the recommendation of the Forest Advisory Committee. A committee the Ministry had appointed under the economist N. C. Saxena had reported in the same period that the proposal had proceeded in violation of the Forest Rights Act and of the conditions of its own in-principle approval, and the refusal rested on that record as much as on policy.

The contrast between the rule and the practice is documented rather than anecdotal. Studies of diversion proposals across the forest-rich states, including the evidence assembled in the research discussed in this article's consent debate, found the gram sabha consent requirement unsatisfied in an overwhelming majority of the proposals examined in states such as Odisha, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, Andhra Pradesh and Gujarat, with resolutions in other cases obtained without the information the 2009 order required the sabha to be given, or after the decision they purported to govern. The gap does not show the consent requirement to be misconceived; it shows it to be the element of the clearance system most dependent on the district administration's good faith, since the certificate travels up the file on the Collector's signature and the appraisal in New Delhi rarely looks behind it. Niyamgiri is the case where the process was forced into the open by litigation and a ministerial refusal, and its singularity is the measure of the ordinary case. In Orissa Mining Corporation v Ministry of Environment and Forests, decided on 18 April 2013, the Supreme Court directed that the gram sabhas of the affected villages determine the claims, including the religious and community claims, under the Forest Rights Act, and that their decisions would govern the next step. Twelve gram sabhas, seven in Rayagada district and five in Kalahandi, considered the proposal in 2013 and every one of them rejected the mining. In January 2014 the Ministry refused the clearance. Niyamgiri stands for a proposition narrower than the slogan it generated: the Court did not hold that a gram sabha veto inheres in the 1980 Act, but it treated the rights-determination process of the 2006 Act, with the gram sabha as its authority, as a legal precondition the clearance system had to respect, and on the facts of Niyamgiri that precondition was decisive.

The subsequent movement has been dilutive, and it should be recorded as plainly as the earlier movement was protective. The Ministry moved in 2013 to exempt linear projects, roads, canals, transmission lines and pipelines, from the consent requirement; the Ministry of Tribal Affairs, the nodal ministry for the Forest Rights Act, objected and in 2014 directed states not to act on the exemption. The Forest (Conservation) Rules 2022 then removed the rights-compliance and gram sabha consent material from the in-principle stage of appraisal altogether, with the Ministry's stated position being that settlement of rights would be completed by the state before the land was handed over rather than examined as part of the Centre's appraisal. Jairam Ramesh, by then in opposition, criticised the change as a dilution of the protection his 2009 order had built, and researchers Manju Menon and Kanchi Kohli, who have documented the consent process across diversion proposals, have recorded the pattern the dilution formalises: consent obtained after the decision is consent in name only, because the gram sabha is being asked to bless a transfer the approving authority has already announced. The counter-position, stated by the government, is that the 2009 apparatus had converted a rights-recognition exercise into an indefinite procedural veto exercised unevenly across states, that the Forest Rights Act requires recognition of rights rather than a project-by-project plebiscite, and that sequencing the settlement before handover protects the right without stalling appraisal. The dispute is genuinely about sequence, and sequence here is substance: a right examined before approval disciplines the decision, while a right examined after approval can only mitigate it.

## The Centre's own projects at the gate

Section 2 speaks of state governments and other authorities, and a first-time reader can miss what that means for the Union's own projects. A railway line, a national highway, a defence installation or a central public sector mine does not carry a central exemption in its pocket. The diversion order is an order of the state government in whose territory the forest lies, and the central project reaches the forest only through that order, which is why the Indian Railways, the National Highways Authority and the defence services appear in the clearance system as user agencies like any state undertaking or private developer, filing proposals, paying levies and executing conditions. The design is deliberate and slightly elegant: by fastening the prohibition on the state order rather than on the project, the Act subjects every proponent in the country, including the Central Government's own ministries, to the one permission, and gives the Environment Ministry, a department of the same Union government that promotes the railways and the highways, the statutory standing to refuse its colleagues. The inter-ministerial character of the resulting contests, infrastructure ministries pressing the environment ministry within the same government, with the file as the battlefield, is one of the system's underappreciated features: the Act created an institutionalised dissenter inside the executive, armed with a statutory veto over the order that every forest diversion needs.

## Reading a diversion order

For the researcher, the objector and the affected community, the operative document is the Stage II approval and the state's diversion order that follows it, and reading one is a learnable skill. The order records the area and its legal status, the purpose and the user agency, and then the conditions, which are the enforceable residue of the whole appraisal: the compensatory afforestation scheme with the identified land, the levy receipts, the wildlife and catchment conditions, the prohibition on changing the land's use to any other purpose, the requirement that the land remains forest land in law and reverts when the use ends, and, in mining cases, the co-terminus character of the approval, which runs with the lease and expires with it. A diversion is therefore not a sale and not a permanent alienation in form: the land is released for a specified use, on conditions, and its legal character as forest land is preserved on paper against the day the use is exhausted. The distance between that paper position and the exhausted mine or the built highway is the distance the monitoring system exists to police, through the compliance reports the user agency and the state file and the regional offices' inspections, and it is the distance in which the system's failures, lapsed conditions, unraised plantations, diverted compensatory land, accumulate unobserved unless someone reads the order against the ground.

## The federal bargain: who pays for India's forests

Underneath the conservation argument runs a fiscal one, and it explains the politics of the system better than ideology does. The forest estate is not evenly spread. The states with the largest recorded forests, Madhya Pradesh, Arunachal Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Maharashtra, are states whose development indicators lag the national average, and the forest sits on top of the minerals: the coal of Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand, the iron ore of Odisha and Karnataka, the bauxite of the Eastern Ghats. The Act therefore operates as a standing transfer of decision power from some of India's poorest states to the Centre, over the very resources those states regard as their route out of poverty, while the ecological services of the same forests, carbon storage, watershed protection, climate moderation, accrue to the whole country and disproportionately to the richer downstream states. Forest-rich states have pressed the resulting claim in the vocabulary of fiscal federalism: a green bonus, compensation within the tax devolution formula for the development they are required to forgo. The claim has been partly heard. The Fourteenth Finance Commission introduced forest cover as a criterion in the devolution of taxes with a weight of 7.5 per cent, the first time standing forest was paid for as a fiscal asset, and the Fifteenth Finance Commission retained a forest and ecology criterion at 10 per cent. The compensatory fund's ninety per cent state share in the 2016 Act belongs to the same settlement: the Centre keeps the gate, the states keep most of the money the gate collects, and the Finance Commissions add a standing payment for the forest that is never diverted at all.

The bargain's critics answer from both directions. Conservation critics note that paying for forest through devolution and levies still prices the forest as an obstacle to be compensated rather than a system to be valued, and that a state can collect the forest criterion in devolution while pressing diversion proposals through the gate, so the incentives point both ways at once. State governments answer that the compensation remains trivial against the forgone royalties, industrial investment and employment of a mining belt kept under trees for the nation's carbon account, and that the approval power means the poorest states must ask permission from the government that also collects the fiscal credit for their restraint. Both arguments are live in the system's politics, and both are arguments the Act's five sections never mention, because the statute was drafted as conservation law and functions, in the federation, as resource law of the first importance.

## Who should hold the key: the recurring redesign debate

Every decade or so, the system's critics converge on the same institutional proposal from opposite directions: take the approval power out of the Environment Ministry's committee structure and give it to a purpose-built authority. The T. S. R. Subramanian Committee, appointed in 2014 to review the environmental laws, recommended the most developed version: a new umbrella statute and a National Environment Management Authority to take over the appraisal and clearance functions, on the ground that a ministry which both promotes policy and decides individual files, advised by committees of its own officials, concentrates roles that a mature regulatory state separates. Industry supporters of redesign want the authority to be faster and more predictable; conservation supporters who entertain the idea want it to be more independent and more expert; and both suspect the ministry structure of serving whichever master shouts loudest in a given season. The proposal has repeatedly failed on the same rock. The approval power is the Act's substance, not its machinery, and relocating it requires Parliament to amend the settlement of 1980 while the Godavarman proceeding, in which the Court supervises the very system the authority would replace, remains the forum of last resort for every interest the redesign would disappoint. The committee structure therefore survives not because it is admired but because every alternative must be legislated through the politics the structure was built to restrain, and the ministry's file remains, for another decade at a time, the only game in New Delhi.

## What the record shows: an honest assessment

The Act's defenders and critics argue past each other because they measure different things. Measured as a brake, the Act worked beyond its authors' plausible hopes. Diversion that ran at roughly one and a half lakh hectares a year in the decades before 1980 fell, on the estimates commonly cited from official compilations, to an average somewhere between about 22,000 and about 32,000 hectares a year across the decades after, varying by source and period; the Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav told the Lok Sabha that 1.73 lakh hectares were diverted across the ten years from 2014-15 to 2023-24, a rate at the lower end of that range. No other Indian environmental statute can show a comparable change in the behaviour it regulates, achieved by five sections and a permission slip. Measured as a conservation instrument in the fuller sense, the record is weaker. Diversion continued at scale, the compensatory mechanism priced and planted rather than replaced, the fund accumulated instead of restoring for a generation, the definition on which everything depends was supplied by litigation and left unmapped by the administrations ordered to map it, and the consent protection for the forest's own inhabitants arrived by circular a quarter century late and has been diluted since.

The composition of the diversion that survives the gate is also part of the record. In the Ministry's category accounting, mining, irrigation and river valley projects, transmission lines, roads and defence have consistently been the largest heads of approved diversion, which means the gate has functioned less as a wall than as a sorting mechanism: the development claims of the Indian state itself, power, water, minerals, connectivity, are the claims it principally admits, priced and conditioned, while the speculative and the private bear the longest odds. The disputes that define the Act politically arise where the sorting is contested on its own terms. The coal blocks of the Hasdeo Arand forests in Chhattisgarh, approved through the same two-stage process with the same conditions, became the emblematic contest of the recent period over whether a procedurally complete clearance answers an ecological objection to mining one of central India's last continuous sal forests, and over whose consent the process had in fact obtained. The file was regular. The argument was about whether regularity is enough, and that argument is the Act's permanent condition: it disciplines how India converts its forests, and it cannot by itself decide how much conversion India should want.

The institutional argument runs on a separate track. Critics of the Godavarman model, in the line of analysis associated with commentators who defend legislative supremacy in policy-making, say that three decades of forest governance by interlocutory order displaced both Parliament and the forest services, and that the 1996 definition, whatever its ecological merits, was a policy choice of the first magnitude taken by two judges on a writ petition. Defenders answer with the counterfactual: Parliament had sixteen years to define forest and did not, the states had the same sixteen years to restrain themselves and diverted instead, and the definition the Court supplied tracked the ordinary meaning of the word rather than any judge's invention. The verdict the evidence best supports distinguishes the two judicial acts. The definition of 1996 was interpretation of a high order, the reading a court gives a word Parliament left bare, and its ecological consequences were foreseeable rather than accidental. The continuing supervision that followed was something else, an administration improvised inside a courtroom, defensible year by year as a response to default and indefensible as a permanent constitution for forest governance. The fund's history is the proof in miniature: it took an Act of Parliament in 2016 to regularise what the Court had built in 2002, and the regularisation, whenever it came, was an admission that courts can hold a ring but cannot run a treasury.

## What the Act does not do

The gate metaphor misleads if it suggests a general forest protection code, and the negative space around the Act defines its limits as precisely as Section 2 defines its reach. The Act does not regulate the felling of trees on land that is not forest land: a wooded private estate outside the recorded and deemed categories is governed by state tree felling laws and transit rules, which vary widely and are a separate legal world. The Act does not create or govern protected areas, sanctuaries and national parks being creatures of the Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, and it confers no habitat rights and prohibits no hunting or trade. It does not manage forests: silviculture, fire protection, grazing regulation and the working of coupes belong to the Indian Forest Act, state forest laws and the working plan system, with the 1980 Act intervening only at the points of classification, conversion, lease and the clearing of natural growth. It does not set forest policy targets, the one-third goal being a policy aspiration of 1952 reaffirmed in the National Forest Policy 1988, which no provision of the Act enforces. And it does not, in its text, give the forest's inhabitants any procedural place at all: the hearing that Niyamgiri made famous arrived from another statute and a ministry circular, and the 1980 Act standing alone would have processed the Niyamgiri diversion as a transaction between two governments and a mining company. A reader who knows what the Act omits understands why so much of forest law had to be built around it: it is a lock on one door of a large house, fitted superbly, in a building whose other doors were left to other statutes, other courts and other decades.

## The 2023 amendment and the verdict on the Act

In 2023 Parliament returned to the statute for the first time in substance since 1988. The Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act 2023 renamed the Act the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, rewrote its preamble, and, most consequentially, redefined its field of application by tying the Act to land notified or recorded as forest in government records, with specified exemptions for categories of land and projects; the amendment's text, its exemptions, the challenges brought against it, and the Supreme Court's interim direction of February 2024 that the dictionary meaning of forest should continue to govern while the challenge is heard, belong to [the Forest Conservation Amendment 2023 article](/2014/12/01/forest-conservation-amendment-2023/) in this series, which owns those questions. What belongs here is the thread this article has followed to that door. The 2023 amendment is Parliament's answer, forty-three years on, to the question the 1980 Act refused to answer: what is a forest for the purposes of this law. That Parliament answered by pointing to its own records, rather than to the trees, is the choice the successor article examines.

The verdict on the Act of 1980 follows from its structure. As a piece of drafting it is nearly empty: one prohibition, one committee, one rule-making power, and a silence where its central definition should be. As an instrument of governance it is among the most effective statutes independent India has produced, because the prohibition was aimed at the right actor, the state itself, and because the permission mechanism forced every conversion of forest land into a documented, conditioned, priced transaction. The Act did not save India's forests; diversion proceeded, compensation underdelivered, and the forest's inhabitants waited decades for a hearing. What the Act did was end the era in which forests could be extinguished by the stroke of a state government's pen, unnoticed and unpriced. Everything in the modern law of Indian forests, the clearance file, the two levies, the statutory fund, the consent contest, and the amendment that seeks to redraw the boundary, is an argument conducted inside the gate that five sections built and one judgment widened.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Forest Conservation Act 1980 require?

It requires the prior approval of the Central Government before a state government or other authority orders any of four things: that a reserved forest cease to be reserved, that forest land be used for a non-forest purpose, that forest land be assigned by lease or otherwise to a private person or a body outside government control, or that forest land be cleared of naturally grown trees for reafforestation. The requirement sits in Section 2 and overrides other state law. In practice it means every diversion of forest land for mining, industry, infrastructure or any other non-forest use must be proposed by the state, appraised at the Centre, approved in two stages and executed on conditions including compensatory afforestation and net present value payment.

### Q: What counts as a non forest purpose?

The Explanation to Section 2 defines it as the breaking up or clearing of any forest land for the cultivation of tea, coffee, spices, rubber, palms, oil-bearing plants, horticultural crops or medicinal plants, or for any purpose other than reafforestation. Mining, quarrying, roads, railways, dams, reservoirs, townships, transmission lines and industrial projects all fall within the general limb of the definition. The Explanation expressly excludes work relating or ancillary to the conservation, development and management of forests and wildlife, such as check-posts, fire lines, wireless communications, fencing, bridges and culverts, dams, waterholes, trench marks, boundary marks and pipelines for forest purposes. Plantation crops count as non-forest purposes even though they keep the land green, because the test is the use of the land, not its appearance.

### Q: What did the Godavarman judgment decide about forests?

In its order of 12 December 1996 in T. N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v Union of India, the Supreme Court held that the word forest in the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980 must be understood according to its dictionary meaning. The Act therefore covers all statutorily recognised forests, whether designated reserved, protected or otherwise, and the expression forest land includes any area recorded as forest in government records irrespective of ownership, as well as any land that is forest in fact. The Court directed every state to constitute an expert committee to identify such forests, restricted felling to operations under working plans approved by the Central Government, and retained the case as a continuing supervision of forest governance, within which the Central Empowered Committee was later constituted.

### Q: What must a project pay as compensatory afforestation?

A project diverting forest land must provide land and money in place of the forest it takes. The user agency identifies non-forest land equal in area to the land diverted, or, where non-forest land is unavailable, degraded forest land of twice the area, and that land is transferred and mutated in favour of the state forest department. The agency pays the full cost of raising the compensatory plantation on that land, including planting, maintenance and protection for the period the scheme prescribes, together with departmental and other charges the conditions impose. The money is deposited into the compensatory afforestation fund before final approval issues. The obligation arises from the conditions of clearance under the Act and the guidelines, and it is separate from net present value, which prices the forest's services rather than replacing its area.

### Q: How is net present value charged on forest clearance?

Net present value is a one-time charge per hectare of forest land diverted, paid by the user agency in addition to compensatory afforestation, on the principle that the services a standing forest supplies, timber, fodder, watershed regulation, habitat and carbon storage among them, are lost when it is cleared. The rate is read off a matrix of six eco-value classes and three canopy density classes. The rates applied from 2008 ran from Rs 4.38 lakh per hectare to Rs 10.43 lakh per hectare, and the Ministry revised them in January 2022 to a maximum in the region of Rs 15.96 lakh per hectare for very dense forest. The charge is enhanced for diversion inside protected areas and reduced or exempted for specified categories. It is deposited into the compensatory afforestation fund before final approval.

### Q: What does the CAMPA Act do with the money?

The Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act 2016, which is the statute behind the CAMPA name, places the money user agencies pay under the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, compensatory afforestation charges, net present value and related levies, into statutory funds: a National Compensatory Afforestation Fund under the Public Account of India and a State Fund under the public account of each state. Receipts divide with ninety per cent going to the State Funds and ten per cent to the National Fund. National and state authorities administer the funds, which are non-lapsable and interest-bearing, and spend them under approved annual plans on artificial regeneration, assisted natural regeneration, forest protection, forest infrastructure and wildlife protection. The Act replaced the ad hoc authority created under Supreme Court orders and transferred Rs 47,436 crore of accumulated money to twenty-seven states in 2019.

### Q: How many stages does forest clearance take?

Two. Stage I is in-principle approval: the Central Government agrees that the diversion may proceed and records the conditions, which ordinarily include transfer of compensatory afforestation land, deposit of the compensatory afforestation cost and net present value, and compliance with the forest rights requirements where they apply. Nothing may be done on the ground on a Stage I approval. The user agency and the state then execute the conditions and the state reports compliance, which the Ministry verifies. Stage II is final approval, after which the state government issues the diversion order, the land is handed over and felling and project work may lawfully begin. The interval between the stages, spent satisfying conditions, is where most proposals spend most of their time.

### Q: Must forest dwellers consent to diversion of forest land?

The 1980 Act itself requires only the Central Government's prior approval and says nothing about consent. The consent requirement enters through the Forest Rights Act 2006 and the clearance rules built on it. By its order of 3 August 2009 the Environment Ministry directed that a diversion proposal must show that the process of recognising forest rights over the land was complete and that each gram sabha concerned had consented in writing after being informed of the proposal, a requirement the Forest (Conservation) Rules carried into the appraisal before in-principle approval. The Supreme Court's Niyamgiri judgment of 18 April 2013 treated that rights process as a legal precondition, and twelve gram sabhas' rejection of bauxite mining there ended the proposal. The Forest (Conservation) Rules 2022 removed the consent material from the in-principle stage, with the Ministry stating that rights settlement would be completed before the land is handed over.

### Q: Does the Forest Conservation Act 1980 define the word forest?

No. The Act contains no definition of forest and no definition of forest land. Its only definitional provision, the Explanation to Section 2, defines non-forest purpose, and its first prohibition borrows the expression reserved forest from the forest law in force in each state. For the Act's first sixteen years the scope of the undefined words was settled by administrative practice, which confined the Act largely to notified and recorded forests. The gap was closed by the Supreme Court in the Godavarman order of 12 December 1996, which held that forest bears its dictionary meaning. The absence of a definition is the single most consequential feature of the statute, because it transferred the fixing of the Act's reach from Parliament to the courts and made the deemed forest category possible.

### Q: When did the Forest Conservation Act 1980 come into force?

The Act is deemed to have come into force on 25 October 1980. It began as the Forest (Conservation) Ordinance 1980, promulgated on that date to stop diversions immediately, and Parliament replaced the ordinance with the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, Act 69 of 1980, later the same year. Section 1 of the Act backdates its commencement to the ordinance date, so no diversion ordered in the interval between the ordinance and the Act escaped the new requirement of prior central approval. The 1988 amendment, which added the lease and clearing prohibitions and the penal sections, commenced in stages, with the penal provisions taking effect on 15 March 1989.

### Q: What is a deemed forest?

A deemed forest is land that is treated as forest for the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980 although it is not notified as a reserved or protected forest and may not be recorded as forest in government records. The category follows from the Godavarman order of 1996, which held that forest bears its dictionary meaning: a tract that is forest in fact attracts the Act whatever the records say and whoever owns it. States were directed to identify such tracts through expert committees, applying criteria each state fixed for itself, typically a minimum area, a canopy density threshold and a requirement that the growth be predominantly natural. Because the criteria and the identification exercises vary between states and remain incomplete in many, whether a particular unrecorded tract is a deemed forest is often the most contested question in a diversion proposal.

### Q: Can a state government de-reserve a reserved forest without central approval?

No. The first prohibition in Section 2 provides that no state government or other authority shall make, except with the prior approval of the Central Government, any order directing that a reserved forest, or any portion of it, shall cease to be reserved. The prohibition opens with a non-obstante clause, so it prevails over the de-reservation powers the Indian Forest Act 1927 and state forest laws give the state. An order of de-reservation made without prior approval is an order the Act forbids, and the diversion that follows it is unauthorised. The same bar applies to the other three outcomes in Section 2: non-forest use of forest land, assignment of forest land to private or non-government hands, and clearing of naturally grown trees for reafforestation.

### Q: What is the penalty for violating the Forest Conservation Act 1980?

The Act's only offence is in Section 3A, inserted by the 1988 amendment: whoever contravenes or abets the contravention of any provision of Section 2 is punishable with simple imprisonment for a period which may extend to fifteen days. Section 3B deals with offences by government departments and authorities, deeming the head of the department, or the persons directly in charge of the authority, guilty of the offence, subject to a defence that the offence was committed without their knowledge or that they exercised all due diligence to prevent it. The original Act of 1980 contained no penalty at all. In practice the working sanctions are administrative: a diversion in violation attracts penal compensatory afforestation, penal net present value at enhanced rates, and a recorded violation in the proposal file, consequences considerably heavier than fifteen days.

### Q: What does the Forest Advisory Committee do?

The Forest Advisory Committee is the committee the Central Government constitutes under Section 3 of the Act to advise it on the grant of approval under Section 2 and on any other matter connected with the conservation of forests referred to it. It appraises the larger diversion proposals, examining the site inspection reports, the alternatives analysis, the compensatory afforestation scheme and the ecological objections, and recommends approval, approval with conditions, or rejection. Proposals below the prescribed size are appraised by Regional Empowered Committees at the Ministry's regional offices. The committee's advice is formally advisory and practically decisive: approvals and rejections follow its recommendations, and its characteristic act is deferral, returning a proposal for deficiencies in enumeration, compensatory land or wildlife comment to be cured before it will recommend a decision.

### Q: Can forest land be leased to a private company?

Not without the Central Government's prior approval. Section 2 provides that no state government or other authority shall order that forest land be assigned by way of lease or otherwise to any private person, or to any authority, corporation, agency or other organisation not owned, managed or controlled by government, except with that approval. This limb was inserted by the 1988 amendment to close a route by which forest land had passed into private hands while its classification stood untouched. Mining leases are the standard case: a lease over forest land is granted and renewed through the clearance process, runs subject to the conditions of the forest approval, and expires with it. A lease granted without the approval does not authorise the use of the forest land, whatever its standing under the mining laws.

### Q: Who has to apply for forest clearance for a project?

The user agency applies: the project proponent, whether a government department, a public sector undertaking, a state agency or a private developer, whose project needs the forest land. The application is built with the state forest department and submitted through the state government, whose nodal officer assembles the file of maps, tree enumeration, site justification, alternatives and compensatory afforestation proposals, and the state forwards it with its recommendation to the Central Government through the Ministry's regional office. The forest department of the state is therefore the examiner and sponsor of the file, not the applicant, except where the department is itself the proponent of a work. The decision is the Central Government's, taken on the advice of the Forest Advisory Committee or the Regional Empowered Committee, and applications are filed and tracked on the PARIVESH portal.

### Q: Can naturally grown trees be cleared for reafforestation without permission?

No. Section 2 forbids a state government or other authority, except with the prior approval of the Central Government, to order that forest land be cleared of trees which have grown naturally in it for the purpose of using it for reafforestation. The limb was inserted by the 1988 amendment. It prevents the conversion of a natural mixed forest into a plantation of teak, eucalyptus or another commercial species being recorded as forest activity outside the Act's control: the trees would be replaced, but the forest, as an ecosystem, would have been exchanged for a crop. Planting that does not involve clearing natural growth, such as enrichment planting in degraded or blank areas, stands outside the prohibition, but the clearing of a standing natural stand to make way for a plantation is a matter for prior approval like any other diversion.

### Q: Does renewal of a mining lease need fresh forest clearance?

Yes. The Supreme Court held in Ambica Quarry Works v State of Gujarat in 1987 that the renewal of a mining lease is in substance a fresh grant, so a renewal that permits mining on forest land requires prior approval under Section 2 of the Forest (Conservation) Act 1980. Two years earlier, in State of Bihar v Banshi Ram Modi, the Court had held that the Act reaches subsisting leases as well: a lessee holding a lease granted before the Act could not break up or clear forest land after the Act came into force without prior approval, because the statutory restriction fastens on the act of breaking up forest land. A mining lease over forest land therefore meets the clearance gate at grant, at every renewal and at any expansion, and its validity under the mining laws does not substitute for the forest approval.

### Q: What must a project proponent do after in-principle approval before final forest clearance?

The proponent must execute the Stage I conditions and prove that it has. In the standard case that means identifying the compensatory afforestation land and having it transferred and mutated in favour of the state forest department, depositing the compensatory afforestation cost and the net present value into the fund, completing the forest rights compliance and gram sabha consent material where the Forest Rights Act applies, demarcating the diversion boundary, and furnishing the undertakings the approval records, on wildlife mitigation, catchment treatment or other project-specific conditions. The state government then submits a compliance report, the Ministry verifies it, frequently through its regional office, and only on satisfactory compliance does Stage II, the final approval, issue. Until then the Stage I approval authorises nothing on the ground, and a proponent that begins work on it is in violation of Section 2.

### Q: Can the National Green Tribunal hear appeals against forest clearance?

Yes. Section 2A of the Act, inserted by the National Green Tribunal Act 2010 with effect from 18 October 2010, provides that any person aggrieved by an order or decision of the state government or other authority made under Section 2 may appeal to the National Green Tribunal, in accordance with the provisions of the National Green Tribunal Act. The appeal route covers the orders through which diversions are effected under the Act, and it gives affected persons, including communities and landowners affected by a diversion, a specialised forum instead of leaving them to writ jurisdiction alone. Before Section 2A, the Act contained no appellate provision at all, and challenges to clearances travelled to the High Courts and the Supreme Court, the latter's Godavarman proceedings having grown out of exactly that route.
