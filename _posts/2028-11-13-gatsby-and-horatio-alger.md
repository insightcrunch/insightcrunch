On the last flyleaf of a ragged copy of Hopalong Cassidy, a boy in North Dakota wrote out a daily schedule. He would rise at six, exercise, study electricity, work, practice elocution, save five dollars, and be better to his parents. Sixteen years later he was a bootlegger shot in a pool he had never used. Reading Gatsby and the Horatio Alger myth together is how you measure the distance between those two facts, and the measurement is the novel's central act of demolition.

![Gatsby and the Horatio Alger Myth](/assets/images/blog/blog-78.webp)

The schedule is not a charming detail. It is the novel's proof that Jay Gatsby began as the most conventional American product imaginable: a poor boy who had read the success literature and believed it. Fitzgerald could have made his protagonist a natural criminal or an aristocrat in disguise. Instead he gave him a childhood copy of the national self-improvement script, dated it, and had his father produce it at the funeral as evidence of what the boy might have become. The Alger formula is invoked with precision and then taken apart element by element, and the taking apart is not incidental to the book. It is the book.

## What Is Being Compared in Gatsby and the Horatio Alger Myth

The comparison here is between a novel and a formula rather than between two novels, which changes what the essay has to establish first.

Horatio Alger Jr. was born in 1832 in Massachusetts and died in 1899. He wrote something over a hundred books for boys, most of them variations on one plot, and the best known is Ragged Dick, serialized in 1867 and published as a book the following year. He trained for the ministry and served a Massachusetts parish briefly, leaving it in 1866 after a scandal, after which he wrote full time. His sales were substantial in his lifetime and enormous after his death, when cheap reprint houses put his titles into circulation by the millions in the two decades on either side of 1900.

That last fact matters for this comparison more than any other. Gatsby's schedule is dated September 12, 1906. A boy in the rural Midwest in 1906 was living through the peak of the Alger reprint boom, when the books were cheap, ubiquitous, and aimed at exactly him. Fitzgerald did not need to name Alger in the novel, and he did not. He dated a document.

On the other side sits The Great Gatsby, published in April 1925, whose protagonist is a self-invented man from a poor farm family whose rise is financed by crime and whose ending is a swimming pool and a funeral nobody attends. What is being compared is a formula and its refutation.

### What is the Horatio Alger success myth?

A poor, honest boy arrives in the city, works hard, refuses vice, seizes a chance to display his character, usually by rescuing someone or returning lost money, and is elevated by a wealthy patron who recognizes his worth. He ends in respectable security. Virtue is rewarded, luck helps, and the social order is confirmed rather than challenged.

## What the Formula Actually Contains

Most readers who invoke Alger have not read him, and the popular version of the myth differs from the books in two ways that both matter here.

The first is the size of the reward. Alger's boys do not become tycoons. Ragged Dick, at the end of his novel, has a clerk's position, a bank account of modest size, respectable clothes, and a room. The books end in security and respectability, not in mansions. The rags-to-riches phrase attached to Alger after his death inflates the ending considerably. Alger was writing about entry into the stable middle class, and his moral was that decency plus effort gets a boy off the street and into a countinghouse.

The second is the role of luck. One of Alger's own titles is Luck and Pluck, and the luck half is not decorative. His plots turn on chance meetings, on happening to be present when a rich man's child falls into the water, on a stranger noticing. The patron is not summoned by virtue; the patron happens by, and virtue is what qualifies the boy once he has been noticed. The formula therefore contains its own quiet admission that effort alone is insufficient, an admission the popular myth deleted.

Both corrections sharpen the Gatsby comparison. Fitzgerald's protagonist wants far more than an Alger ending, and he gets his patron by chance in the most literal way available, by seeing a yacht anchor and rowing out to it. The novel is not refuting a formula it has misunderstood. It engages the formula accurately, including the parts the myth forgot.

## The Schedule as Alger Document

Chapter Nine gives the schedule to Henry Gatz, who produces it from his pocket at his son's house with the words, "Look here, this is a book he had when he was a boy. It just shows you." The entries begin at six in the morning and run to nine at night. Rising, dumbbell exercise and wall-scaling, studying electricity, work, baseball and sports, practicing elocution and poise, studying needed inventions. Then a heading for general resolves: no wasting time at Shafters, no more smokeing or chewing, bath every other day, read one improving book or magazine per week, save five dollars, be better to parents.

Four features make this an Alger document rather than a generic list.

It is a program for character, not for wealth. Nothing on the list is a business plan. Elocution, poise, bathing, and being better to parents are items about becoming the kind of person to whom good things are supposed to happen. That is precisely the Alger theory of success, in which the boy's job is to make himself worthy and the world's job is to notice.

It combines the physical, the intellectual, and the moral in equal measure, which is the shape of nineteenth-century American self-improvement literature and not the shape of twentieth-century business advice. Wall-scaling next to electricity next to bathing next to filial duty is a curriculum for a whole person.

It includes savings as a moral item rather than a financial one. Five dollars is not capital. It is evidence of discipline, and Alger's boys open bank accounts for the same reason.

And it is written in a book. The schedule sits on the flyleaf of an adventure story, which means the boy was reading popular fiction about heroic self-sufficiency and writing his own program into the back of it. Fitzgerald noticed something scholars have remarked on since, that the particular title he chose was not published until 1910, four years after the date on the page, an inconsistency that does nothing to weaken the point being made about what the boy was reading and why.

### How does the boyhood schedule echo Alger?

It reproduces the Alger theory exactly: a poor boy's task is self-improvement in character rather than accumulation of capital, combining physical discipline, study, hygiene, thrift, and duty to family, on the assumption that a worthy boy will be recognized and elevated. Nothing on Gatsby's list is a business plan.

## The Franklin Ancestry Behind Both

The schedule has an older parent than Alger, and naming it strengthens any essay on this subject.

Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography, composed in the eighteenth century and a fixture of American schooling ever after, contains a daily timetable beginning early in the morning with a question about what good is to be done that day, and a list of thirteen virtues that Franklin tracked in a ruled book, marking his failures. The structure is identical to what Fitzgerald prints: hours assigned to categories of activity, followed by resolutions about conduct.

Alger did not invent this. He inherited it, secularized it slightly, and put it into fiction for boys. Fitzgerald, in turn, is reaching past Alger to the whole tradition, which is why the schedule feels older than 1906 when you read it. The line runs from a founding father's self-management system, through a century of self-help literature and Sunday school prize books, into the back of a Western novel owned by a farm boy in North Dakota.

That lineage is what gives the passage its weight in Chapter Nine. When Henry Gatz says his son was bound to get ahead and always had resolves like this, he is not describing an individual quirk. He is describing a boy who had absorbed the national curriculum correctly. The series treatment of [the self-made man myth in The Great Gatsby](/2025/01/13/self-made-man-myth-gatsby/) traces the tradition as the novel handles it, and the broader literary lineage is set out in the study of [Gatsby and the self-made man in literature](/2028/09/04/gatsby-self-made-man-literature/).
## Reading the Schedule Line by Line

The document runs to twelve entries and rewards being read as a text rather than glanced at as a curiosity. Each line does specific work.

The day begins with "Rise from bed 6:00 A.M." A fifteen-minute block follows for "Dumbell exercise and wall-scaling," which is a boy's compression of physical culture, a substantial popular movement in America around 1900 built on the premise that the body was trainable and that training it was a moral act. The misspelling of dumbbell is Fitzgerald's, preserved as the boy wrote it, and it is doing something. This is a document produced by someone with more ambition than schooling, which is precisely the reader Alger's publishers were selling to.

"Study electricity, etc." occupies an hour. Electricity in 1906 was the frontier technology, the field where a self-taught man might still make something, and the "etc." is the most touching word on the page: the boy knows there are other things to study and does not yet know what they are called. Then "Work 8:30-4:30 P.M.," an eight-hour block with no content specified, which tells you he had no idea what the work would be and had allocated time for it anyway.

Sport gets half an hour. Then "Practise elocution, poise and how to attain it 5:00-6:00," which is the entry that most clearly marks this as a success-literature document rather than a school assignment. Elocution and poise are not skills for a farm. They are the manner of the class the boy intends to join, and he has allotted an hour a day to acquiring a manner. The British spelling of practise is the edition's, and the phrase "how to attain it" reads as though lifted from the title of something he had seen advertised. Then "Study needed inventions 7:00-9:00," two evening hours given to a category the boy has invented for himself.

The general resolves are shorter and harder. "No wasting time at Shafters or [a name, indecipherable]" identifies the local temptations by name, which means this is not a copied list but a document about an actual life in an actual town. "No more smokeing or chewing," misspelled again, gives up tobacco. "Bath every other day" is a hygiene resolution that quietly tells you the household's conditions. "Read one improving book or magazine per week" adopts the exact vocabulary of nineteenth-century self-culture, in which reading is improving rather than pleasurable. "Save $5.00" is printed with the figure crossed out and replaced by "$3.00 per week," which is the single most revealing mark on the page: the boy set a target, could not meet it, and revised it downward in ink rather than abandoning the line. "Be better to parents" closes the list.

That last line is the one to build a paragraph on. It is the only entry directed at another person, and it is the only promise the boy demonstrably broke. Nick tells us that Gatsby's imagination "had never really accepted them as his parents at all," and Henry Gatz has to learn of his son's death from a Chicago newspaper because he did not know where the boy was living. The schedule's single moral obligation to another human being is the one item that failed, and the father is the one who produces the document.

## The Novel's Other Rising Men

Fitzgerald did not test the formula on one character. Three others provide controls, and using them turns a good essay into a rigorous one.

Meyer Wolfshiem is the version of the self-made man who succeeded by discarding the moral apparatus entirely. He rose from nothing, he is spectacularly effective, and he wears human molars as cuff links. He is also the only figure in the book who is still standing and still solvent at the end, and he declines to attend the funeral, explaining in a note that he cannot get mixed up in it. Fitzgerald's arrangement is deliberate: the man who kept the ethical part of the formula dies, and the man who discarded it survives. Any reading that claims the novel endorses virtue as a route to security has to account for Wolfshiem, and it cannot.

Myrtle Wilson is the formula applied to a woman, and it collapses faster. She has ambition, energy, and a clear picture of the life she wants, and the only route available to her is the one she takes, which is to attach herself to a man with money. The apartment on 158th Street is her mansion, scaled down and rented in someone else's name, and it comes with a dog bought on impulse and a broken nose. Fitzgerald puts her death at the edge of the ash heap her husband's garage sits on. The formula was never addressed to her in the first place, and the novel notices.

Nick Carraway is the control case for the legitimate route. He has the Yale degree, the family money behind him, and a job in the bond business, which is the honest version of what Gatsby does with alcohol. He works, he rents modestly, he commutes, and at the end of the summer he goes home to the Midwest with no fortune and no wife, having decided the whole arrangement is not worth it. His father's advice in the opening paragraphs, that not everyone has had his advantages, sets up the entire comparison, and Nick spends the book learning what those advantages actually consist of. The legitimate route was open to him because he was born on it.

Read together, the four cases produce a finding no single character can supply. The formula's promised outcome, security through disciplined merit, is achieved by nobody in the novel. The people who end the book intact are the ones who inherited, like Tom and Daisy, or the ones who abandoned the ethics, like Wolfshiem. That distribution is the argument, and it is much stronger than anything an essay can build from Gatsby alone.

## What Replaced the Alger Prize

The formula ends in a position and a bank account. Gatsby's ends in a woman, and the substitution is the novel's most original move on this material.

Fitzgerald marks it explicitly. Describing the night Gatsby first kissed Daisy in Louisville, Nick writes that "he found that he had committed himself to the following of a grail." The vocabulary shifts from commerce to romance quest in a single clause. Everything after that sentence is organized around an object that cannot be purchased, deposited, or held, which is a category error the Alger formula does not contain and could not survive.

This substitution explains several things about the novel that otherwise look arbitrary. It explains why the fortune is so obviously excessive: the money is not the goal, so there is no natural stopping point, and a man accumulates a mansion and a hydroplane and a private beach because none of it is what he is actually accumulating. It explains why he does not use any of it. He never swims in the pool, does not drink at his parties, and is described standing apart from them. And it explains why the collapse is so total. A bank account survives a bad afternoon. A grail does not survive the moment its holder declines to say she never loved her husband.

The Alger formula, for all its sentimentality, is about a boy entering an economy. Fitzgerald's revision is about a boy who entered an economy in order to purchase something outside it. Both projects fail in the novel, but they fail for different reasons, and an essay that distinguishes them has understood the book better than one that treats money and Daisy as the same object under two names.

## Three Misreadings to Retire

The Alger comparison attracts three errors reliably, and each is worth pre-empting inside the body of an essay.

The first is treating the bootlegging as the point of failure. It is the most visible break with the formula and the least interesting, because it invites the conclusion that Gatsby failed by cheating and that an honest boy would have succeeded, which leaves the formula intact and blames the individual. Fitzgerald blocks that exit deliberately by placing the decisive failure earlier, in the Cody legacy, before any crime. The order of events is the argument.

The second is caricaturing Alger. Essays routinely describe the formula as a naive claim that hard work alone produces wealth, which is not what the books say. They say character plus a fortunate encounter produces respectable security, and one of the titles names luck first. Getting this right costs two sentences and makes the whole essay more credible, because it shows you are dismantling the actual thing rather than a straw version.

The third is reading the schedule as comic. It is written by a child, it contains misspellings, and it allocates two hours a day to studying needed inventions, all of which invites a smile. Fitzgerald's placement forbids it. The document appears in Chapter Nine, produced by a grieving father at a house bought with crime, days after the son who wrote it was shot in a pool he had never used. Everything about the timing is designed to make the earnestness unbearable rather than charming, and an essay that plays it for irony has read the placement backwards.
## The Alger Audit: Five Elements Invoked and Voided

Here is the article's framework and its findable claim. The Alger formula has five load-bearing elements. Fitzgerald invokes every one of them and voids every one of them, and the pattern is systematic enough to be audited.

The first element is humble and honest origins. Alger's boy is poor and his poverty is honorable; his family, when he has one, is decent. Fitzgerald invokes the poverty and voids the honor, not by making the Gatz family disreputable but by having the son reject them. Nick reports that "His parents were shiftless and unsuccessful farm people" and that "his imagination had never really accepted them as his parents at all." The Alger hero rises for his family; Gatsby rises away from his. The formula's moral engine is filial devotion, and Fitzgerald removes it in a clause while leaving the poverty in place.

The second element is the demonstration of character that earns notice. Alger's boy returns a wallet, saves a child, refuses to cheat. Gatsby's equivalent moment is rowing out to a moored yacht in a borrowed boat to warn Dan Cody that the wind might swamp him. On the surface this qualifies: initiative, helpfulness, a stranger's attention earned. But Fitzgerald has already told us that James Gatz changed his name at that specific moment, which means the act of service and the act of self-invention are the same act. The Alger boy is noticed for being who he is. Gatsby is noticed for having just become someone else.

The third element is the patron. This is the formula's hinge, and Fitzgerald's handling of it is the audit's most damaging entry. Dan Cody is a product of the Western metal booms, "physically robust but on the verge of soft-mindedness," pursued by women trying to separate him from his money. Gatsby works for him for five years as the boat circles the continent three times, learns what he needs to learn, and is named in the will for a legacy of twenty-five thousand dollars. Then Fitzgerald writes four of the coldest sentences in the novel. "He didn't get it. He never understood the legal device that was used against him, but what remained of the millions went intact to Ella Kaye. He was left with his singularly appropriate education."

The patron pays nothing. The inheritance is taken by a lawyer's mechanism the boy cannot follow. The only thing transferred is an education in how rich men behave, which the novel calls singularly appropriate, meaning fitted to the career he will actually have rather than the one the formula promised. Alger's patron is the proof that the system works. Fitzgerald's patron dies and his money goes to someone else through paperwork.

The fourth element is honest labor as the engine of the rise. Alger's boys work: they black boots, run errands, keep books. Gatsby's rise is financed by bootlegging and, as Tom alleges in the Plaza, by something worse involving stolen bonds. The novel is deliberately vague about the details and entirely clear about the category. The drugstores, the phone calls from Chicago and Philadelphia, Wolfshiem's line of business: the money is criminal, and criminal money is what buys the house from which the green light is visible. Prohibition supplied a mechanism by which a poor man could accumulate a fortune in a few years, and Fitzgerald put his Alger boy through it.

The fifth element is the ending in security. Alger's endings are modest, stable, and lasting: a position, a bank account, a room, respectability. Gatsby's ending is a swimming pool in early autumn, a father arriving by train from Minnesota, a funeral in the rain attended by the servants, the minister, Nick, Henry Gatz, and one former party guest in owl-eyed spectacles who arrives late and delivers the only epitaph anyone offers. Not a diminished version of the Alger reward. Its exact negation.

### How does the novel subvert the Alger myth?

By invoking all five elements of the formula and voiding each. The origins are poor but disowned, the moment of notice coincides with self-invention, the patron's legacy is taken by legal maneuver, the labor is criminal, and the ending is a corpse in a pool rather than a clerkship and a bank book.

## Dan Cody and the Patron Inverted

The Cody material occupies barely two pages and does more damage to the Alger formula than anything else in the novel, so it repays close attention.

Alger's patron figure is a merchant, a banker, a respectable man of business who has earned his position and who extends a hand downward because the boy has demonstrated worth. The transaction is moral and the money is clean. The patron's function in the story is to certify that the social order rewards merit, which is why Alger's books are fundamentally conservative documents: they contain no criticism of the system, only encouragement to enter it correctly.

Fitzgerald's patron is a mining speculator grown rich in Nevada, Montana, and the Yukon, whose fortune is extractive and whose competence is failing. He is surrounded by people trying to take his money. He drinks heavily, and Gatsby's lifelong avoidance of liquor is learned by watching him. The education transferred is not a trade or a set of books; it is a demonstration of how a rich man looks, dresses, speaks, and is treated, delivered by a man who is coming apart.

Then the legacy fails. Fitzgerald's phrasing is worth dwelling on: Gatsby "never understood the legal device that was used against him." Not that the will was invalid or that he was cheated by a person, but that a device existed, operated, and was beyond his comprehension. This is the novel's first statement of a claim it makes repeatedly, that the machinery protecting inherited wealth is not accessible to people who did not grow up inside it. Tom Buchanan will make the same point later with a different tool, when he tells Wilson who owns the yellow car and lets the consequences run.

The result is that Gatsby leaves the Cody years with everything the formula says produces success, five years of loyal service, a demonstrated character, a patron's regard, and none of what it says the success consists of. He has manners and no capital. The next thing he acquires is a criminal business, and the sequence is causal. Fitzgerald constructed the Alger patron episode specifically so that its failure would produce the bootlegger.

## Henry Gatz and the Myth That Survives Its Refutation

The novel's most quietly devastating stroke is that the formula outlives the man it destroyed, and Fitzgerald puts the survival in the mouth of the person with the least reason to believe it.

Henry Gatz arrives from Minnesota for the funeral. He is proud. He has kept a photograph of the house, "cracked in the corners and dirty with many hands," which he has evidently shown to people. He produces the schedule as proof of what his son was. And he says, "He had a big future before him, you know," and then, "If he'd of lived, he'd of been a great man. A man like James J. Hill. He'd of helped build up the country."

Three things are happening in that speech. The father's model of greatness is a railroad magnate, a real builder of the American Northwest, which is not an Alger figure at all but the tycoon the Alger myth was popularly inflated into. The father believes his son was on that trajectory, having no idea that the money came from bootlegging. And the father is standing in a house bought with crime, holding a schedule from 1906, at the funeral of a man shot in a swimming pool, and the formula has not been dented in his mind by any of it.

Fitzgerald could have used the father to deliver a verdict. He uses him instead to show that the myth is not falsifiable by evidence. It survives the corpse. That is a stronger claim about ideology than any speech Nick could have made, and it is the reason Chapter Nine, rather than Chapter Seven, is where the Alger comparison finally lands.
## The Credentials Problem

The Alger formula assumes a world where character can be demonstrated directly, in person, to the man who matters. A boy returns a wallet, a merchant sees it, and the merchant's judgment is sufficient. There is no paperwork.

Gatsby lives in a world that runs on credentials, and the difference produces one of the novel's running preoccupations. In the car on the way to lunch in Chapter Four he tells Nick that he was "educated at Oxford, because all my ancestors have been educated there for many years," a claim delivered so badly that Nick immediately stops believing anything else in the account. Then Gatsby produces documents. First a medal, inscribed "Orderi di Danilo" and reading "Major Jay Gatsby, For Valour Extraordinary," which Nick examines and finds authentic. Then a photograph of half a dozen young men in blazers loafing in an archway, with Gatsby among them, taken at Oxford.

Both documents turn out to be true and neither works as a credential, and that is the passage's point. The Oxford months were real, a brief postwar opportunity for officers, and the medal was genuinely awarded. But a man who has to carry proof in his pocket has already lost the argument, because credentials in this world are not documents. They are the assumptions other people make about you before you speak, and those come from schooling, family, and address rather than from evidence.

Tom demonstrates the asymmetry in Chapter Seven without producing a single piece of paper. He simply investigates, discovers the drugstores, and states his findings in the Plaza, and nobody asks him for proof. His standing is the proof. Gatsby, holding an authentic medal from a sovereign government, cannot achieve in a summer what Tom achieves in a sentence.

This is the Alger formula's deepest failure in the novel and the one least often written about. The formula promises that demonstrated character will be recognized. Fitzgerald shows a man whose character is demonstrable, documented, and verified, and whose demonstration is worthless, because the mechanism of recognition had been replaced by a mechanism of assumption. Nick, who has the assumptions, needs no documents. Gatsby, who has the documents, is not believed.

Notice too what the schedule and the documents have in common. Both are pieces of paper produced to establish something about a person, and both arrive too late to work: the medal after the doubt has formed, the schedule after the funeral. Fitzgerald's self-made man spends the novel carrying evidence to a world that does not accept evidence, and the boy who wrote out his resolves at fifteen never learned that the resolves were not the currency.

### Does the novel show character being recognized at all?

Once, and it changes nothing. Nick calls across the lawn that the others are a rotten crowd and Gatsby is worth all of them together. That is the recognition the formula promises, delivered by a bond salesman with nothing to confer, on the last morning of Gatsby's life.

## The Timeline the Formula Cannot Accommodate

Set the dates out and one more break becomes visible.

The schedule is dated September 12, 1906. James Gatz becomes Jay Gatsby at seventeen, rowing out to Cody's yacht on Lake Superior. The arrangement with Cody lasts five years and the boat circles the continent three times. The legacy fails, and Gatsby is left with an education and no capital. He meets Daisy in Louisville in 1917 while in uniform, ships overseas, spends time at Oxford after the armistice, and returns to a country where she has married Tom in 1919. By the summer of 1922 he owns the house across the bay.

The compression at the end is the anomaly. Roughly three years separate a penniless officer from a man who can fill a mansion with an orchestra every Saturday night. No Alger arc runs at that speed, and none is supposed to, because the formula's entire moral content depends on duration. Time on the ladder is what converts effort into desert. Alger's boys earn their modest positions across years of visible increments, and the slowness is what makes the reward feel legitimate.

Fitzgerald removes the duration and the legitimacy goes with it. A fortune assembled in three years cannot have been earned in the formula's sense, and everyone in the novel knows it, which is why the parties hum with rumors that he killed a man or was a German spy. The guests are not being unfair. They are correctly reading a timeline that no honest process could produce, and Tom eventually supplies the process they were guessing at.

The deadline is what forced the compression. Daisy married in 1919, and a man who intends to reverse that cannot spend twenty years in a countinghouse. The Alger formula has no provision for urgency, because its reward is security and security has no expiry date. Gatsby's reward was a person with a husband, and the clock on that is not negotiable. He needed the money fast, and the only fast money available was criminal.
## The Counter-Reading: Is Gatsby a Straight Alger Hero?

The reading deserves a serious hearing, because a version of it is defensible and the essay that dismisses it too fast loses ground.

The case for it runs like this. Gatsby is poor, works relentlessly, refuses drink, keeps himself disciplined, serves with distinction in the war, and rises spectacularly. He is generous, he is loyal, and Nick's final assessment is favorable: Gatsby "turned out all right at the end," and what soured Nick on the whole summer was not Gatsby but "what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams." Nick calls across the lawn that the others are "a rotten crowd" and that Gatsby is "worth the whole damn bunch put together," the only unqualified praise anyone in the novel receives. If the novel wanted to indict the self-made man, it chose an odd way to do it, since the self-made man is the only sympathetic figure in the book.

Two answers, and both should appear in the body of an essay rather than as a footnote.

The first is that the reading confuses the man with the formula. Fitzgerald's sympathy for Gatsby is real and is not the same as endorsement of the myth. The novel's argument is not that Gatsby was unworthy; it is that worth was never the operative variable. He did everything the formula asked, and the formula did not produce its stated output. That is a harsher verdict than making him a villain would have been. A villain proves the system detects fraud. A decent man who follows the instructions and dies in a pool proves the instructions were false.

The second is that the sympathy is precisely engineered to make the demolition land. Fitzgerald withholds Gatsby's origins until Chapter Six, by which point the reader has spent five chapters liking him. He withholds the schedule until Chapter Nine, after the death, so that the boy's earnest program arrives when it can no longer be read hopefully. The structural order is the argument. A novel that wanted to celebrate the Alger hero would have opened with the schedule.

There is a third response available for a longer paper. The Alger formula's own terms disqualify Gatsby regardless of his virtues, because the formula requires legitimate labor and his labor is criminal. A reader cannot claim him as a straight Alger hero while conceding the bootlegging. What can be claimed, and this is the strongest version of the counter-reading, is that the novel shows the formula's conditions to be unmeetable, so that a boy who genuinely wanted to follow it had no legitimate route and took the only one available. That is not Gatsby as Alger hero. It is Gatsby as the Alger hero the twentieth century made impossible.

### Is Gatsby a straight Alger hero?

No. He meets the formula's conditions on discipline, effort, and abstention, and fails it on the two that are load-bearing: his labor is criminal, and his ending is death rather than security. The novel's sympathy for him is engineered to make the formula's failure land harder, not to endorse it.

| Moment of notice | A display of character seen by the right person by chance | Rowing out to warn Dan Cody that his yacht is exposed to the wind | The same moment James Gatz renames himself, so service and self-invention are one act |
| The patron | A respectable man of business who elevates the worthy boy | Five years with Cody, a place in the will, a legacy of twenty-five thousand dollars | Cody dies, a legal device takes the money to Ella Kaye, and only an education transfers |
| Honest labor | Blacking boots, running errands, keeping books | Relentless work and total abstention from drink | The fortune is built on bootlegging and, by Tom's account, stolen bonds |
| The ending | Security, respectability, a position, a bank account | A mansion, a hydroplane, a library of real books, a private beach | A body in an unused pool, a funeral in the rain, and a father who still believes |

The decisive row is the fourth. The Alger formula stands or falls on whether the patron delivers, and Fitzgerald has the patron die and the money go elsewhere through paperwork the boy cannot read.

## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The verdict this article defends: The Great Gatsby is not a novel that happens to contain an Alger echo. It is a controlled refutation of the formula, built by a writer who understood the formula accurately, including the parts the popular version had already lost.

The deciding factor is not the bootlegging. That is the easy answer and it is too easy, because it lets a reader conclude that Gatsby failed by cheating and that an honest boy would have succeeded, which leaves the formula intact and blames the individual. Fitzgerald blocks that exit by placing the failure earlier, in the Cody legacy. Before there is any crime, before Prohibition, before Wolfshiem, a young man does everything correctly for five years and is separated from his reward by a mechanism he cannot understand. The crime comes after that failure and because of it.

That sequence is the argument. The formula promises that virtue plus effort plus a patron's recognition yields security. Fitzgerald supplies all three inputs and shows the output withheld by a legal instrument that exists to protect accumulated wealth from claims by people outside it. Once the legitimate route has been demonstrated closed, the illegitimate one is not a moral failing of the individual so much as the only remaining road, and the novel's larger argument about [the corruption of the American Dream](/2024/04/29/gatsby-american-dream-corruption/) follows from that closure rather than from Gatsby's character.

The second deciding factor is the ending's shape. Alger's endings are modest and durable; Gatsby's is spectacular and instantly reversible. He accumulates in five years what an Alger boy would take a lifetime to approach, and loses all of it in an afternoon and a gunshot. Fitzgerald is arguing that the twentieth-century American fortune, made fast and made outside the law, has no floor under it. Alger's clerk keeps his position. Gatsby's estate disperses within weeks and the house is dark by autumn.
## Two Kinds of Self-Making

The comparison sharpens further if you separate two things the phrase self-made man is used to cover, because Alger means one of them and Fitzgerald means both.

The first is economic self-making: acquiring capital, position, and security without inheriting them. This is what Alger writes about and it is a straightforward material process with visible steps. His boys are self-made in the sense that nobody handed them anything, and the books track the acquisition in modest, checkable increments.

The second is ontological self-making: producing a self that did not previously exist, with a new name, a new history, a new accent, and a new set of parents disowned in the imagination. Alger's boys never do this. Ragged Dick cleans up, buys a suit, and learns to speak better, but he remains himself throughout and the books are explicit that his essential character was always there and merely needed the right clothes. His improvement is presentation, not replacement.

Gatsby does both, and Fitzgerald states the second one in theoretical terms. "The truth was that Jay Gatsby of West Egg, Long Island, sprang from his Platonic conception of himself." That sentence does not describe a boy who cleaned up. It describes a person generated from an idea, and the novel treats the generation as complete rather than partial: Nick reports that Gatsby "was a son of God" who "must be about his Father's Business," and names the business as "the service of a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty."

Here is why the distinction matters for the audit. The Alger formula can accommodate failure of the first kind. A boy who does not accumulate is simply unlucky, and the books contain minor characters who fare worse than the hero. What the formula cannot accommodate is the second kind, because ontological self-making has no stopping condition. There is no amount of money at which a manufactured self is finished, which is why Gatsby keeps building after any Alger hero would have stopped, and why the parties get larger rather than the accounts more secure.

The two projects also fail differently. The economic project failed once, at the Cody legacy, and Gatsby routed around it through crime. The ontological project cannot fail once, because it is never complete, and so it fails continuously, in small exposures: Tom's investigation in Chapter Seven, the accent slipping into "old sport," the Oxford story that requires a photograph as proof, the library of uncut books. Fitzgerald's genius on this material is that he makes the second kind of self-making feel like heroism and shows it to be structurally interminable.

## The Formula After the Novel

Fitzgerald's demolition did not retire the myth, and the afterlife is worth a paragraph in a longer essay because it demonstrates the point Chapter Nine makes about Henry Gatz.

The formula's institutional life continued and in some respects expanded. An association named for Alger was founded in 1947 to recognize Americans who had risen from humble beginnings, and the phrase Horatio Alger story entered general usage as shorthand for exactly the inflated rags-to-riches version that the books themselves do not support. The name detached from the writing entirely. Most people who use the phrase have never read a page of Alger, which means the myth now circulates without its own quiet admissions about luck and about the modest size of the reward.

That detachment is itself an argument for reading Gatsby as the more accurate document. Fitzgerald engaged the formula in its full form, including the patron and the chance encounter, and showed what happens when the guarantees are removed. The popular myth kept the promise and dropped the qualifications, which is the reverse operation.

For a student, the practical value of this is that the Alger frame is not a historical curiosity. Every American classroom contains readers who arrived holding some version of the formula, usually without a name for it, and Gatsby is generally the text through which they encounter its refutation. The comparison therefore does not require reconstructing a lost nineteenth-century context. It requires naming something the reader already believes and then showing the novel testing it, which is a much easier essay to write and a much more interesting one to read.

## What the Comparison Proves About the Novel's Method

One final observation, useful in a conclusion or in a longer paper's final section.

Fitzgerald's method throughout this material is documentary rather than declarative. He does not have Nick explain that the American success myth is false. He produces objects: a schedule with a date on it, a will that does not pay, a photograph cracked at the corners from handling, a library of real books with uncut pages, a list of party guests written on a disintegrating timetable. Each object is presented without commentary and each carries an argument the prose declines to state.

This is why the Alger comparison works so cleanly. A novel that argued against the myth in Nick's voice would be a novel a reader could disagree with. A novel that prints a boy's handwritten resolves, dates them, and then shows the reader the body, has made the case with evidence rather than with assertion. Henry Gatz is holding the exhibit and drawing the opposite conclusion from it, and Fitzgerald lets both the exhibit and the misreading stand on the same page.

That restraint is the reason the book survives readings that would flatten a more argumentative novel. A reader can finish Gatsby believing it celebrates its hero, and the text does not contradict that reader directly. It simply keeps the schedule, the will, and the pool in the same volume and trusts the arrangement.

A short note on scope, since students sometimes overreach here. The novel is not a treatise on social mobility and does not offer statistics, policy, or a general theory of American class. It offers one boy, one schedule, one patron, one legacy, and one outcome, and it lets the case stand for the claim. That is a novelist's method rather than a sociologist's, and its persuasive force comes from the specificity of the objects rather than from the breadth of the sample. An essay should match that scope. Argue what the novel demonstrates about the formula through this particular boy, and resist the temptation to conclude that Fitzgerald proved upward mobility impossible in America, which he neither claimed nor could have shown with the materials he used.
## Prohibition and the Closing of the Legitimate Route

The Alger formula was written for a nineteenth-century economy and Fitzgerald set his novel in a specific twentieth-century one, and the mismatch is part of the argument.

Alger's boys enter commerce. They become clerks, bookkeepers, junior partners in firms that make or move things. The ladder they climb is visible, its rungs are known, and time on the ladder is the price. That world existed and the books were reasonably honest about how it worked, allowing for the sentimentality.

Gatsby's world offers no such ladder to a man in his position. He returns from the war with a uniform, no capital, no family connection, and an education in how the rich behave. The legitimate options available to him are the ones Nick has: a modest job in the city, a rented house, a commute, and a timeline of decades. Nick, who has the Yale degree and the family money behind him, takes that route and describes it without enthusiasm. It would not have delivered Daisy in 1922, and Gatsby's plan has a deadline.

What Prohibition supplied was an unprecedented shortcut. The Volstead Act made the manufacture and sale of alcohol illegal in a country that continued to want it, which created, almost overnight, an enormous market with no legitimate suppliers and therefore no established firms holding the top positions. A man with nerve and organizational ability could accumulate a fortune in a few years, which was not possible in any legal sector open to him. Fitzgerald keeps the mechanics offstage, giving us drugstores selling grain alcohol over the counter, telephone calls from Chicago and Philadelphia, Wolfshiem's connections, and Tom's accusations, but the category is unambiguous.

The comparison with Alger becomes sharpest here. Both the formula and the novel agree that a poor boy needs a route upward. Alger says commerce provides one and character qualifies you for it. Fitzgerald says that by 1919 the routes proportionate to a man's ambition had closed, and that the only open one was criminal. Gatsby did not reject the Alger path out of impatience or greed. He followed it as far as it went, which was to a dead patron and a legal device, and then took the road that remained.

An essay that makes this move avoids the trap of moralizing about the bootlegging and gets to the structural claim underneath, which is far more interesting and far more defensible from the text.

## What the Comparison Illuminates in Both Directions

The pairing changes how each side reads, which is the test of a comparison worth writing.

Alger read after Gatsby looks less naive and more revealing. The formula's insistence that luck is required, visible in the title Luck and Pluck and in the chance meetings that drive the plots, is an admission the popular myth suppressed. Read after Fitzgerald, that admission becomes the interesting part: even the great optimistic success literature of the nineteenth century knew that effort alone was insufficient and quietly said so. Fitzgerald did not have to insert chance into the story. He inherited it, and simply removed the guarantee that chance would land favorably.

Gatsby read after Alger loses a good deal of its supposed mystery. Readers often treat Gatsby as an enigma, a man who appears from nowhere with an unaccountable ambition. Set beside the formula, he is entirely accountable. He is a boy who read the instructions, followed them with unusual thoroughness, encountered a patron, was defrauded of his legacy, and reconstructed the plan on a criminal basis with a woman substituted for the original prize. Every stage has a name in the source material. The enigma dissolves and something better appears, which is a precise diagnosis of what happens to a national script when it meets a national economy.

The comparison also explains the novel's durability in classrooms better than most accounts do. Every generation of American students arrives having absorbed some version of the formula, usually without ever having heard Alger's name. Gatsby is the text through which they encounter its refutation, and the refutation works because Fitzgerald did not caricature the thing he was dismantling. He built it faithfully, in a boy's handwriting on a flyleaf, and then let it run.

## How to Write the Essay

Five moves, in order.

Open on the schedule, not on the American Dream in general. The schedule is concrete, dated, and quotable, and it puts the Alger frame in play in a sentence without requiring you to define the myth first. Define it in the second paragraph, once the reader wants to know.

Define Alger accurately, which most essays do not. State that his heroes end in respectable security rather than in mansions, and that one of his own titles pairs luck with pluck. Both corrections take one sentence each and both make the rest of your argument stronger, because they show you engaging the real formula rather than a caricature.

Run the audit rather than listing similarities. Take three of the five elements and, for each, show the invocation and then the voiding in the same paragraph. Origins, patron, and ending are the three that carry the most weight, and the patron is the one to develop at length because it is where the failure precedes the crime.

Handle the counter-reading in the body. Concede that Nick admires Gatsby and that the novel's sympathy is real, then argue that the sympathy is what makes the demolition work: a decent man who follows the instructions and dies proves the instructions false more completely than a fraud would.

Close on Henry Gatz. The father holding a schedule from 1906 at the funeral of a bootlegger, comparing his son to a railroad magnate, is the strongest available image of a myth surviving its own refutation. Ending there ends on an argument. Readers who want to check the schedule and the Cody passages line by line can [read and annotate The Great Gatsby free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html), which keeps the full text beside the notes so the flyleaf entries and the legacy sentences can be marked as evidence. The chapter-level reading of the passage itself is developed in the series article on [the schedule in the boyhood book](/2023/04/24/great-gatsby-boyhood-schedule/).

## Closing Verdict

Fitzgerald did not write a novel against ambition, and reading it that way flattens everything interesting in it. He wrote a novel against a specific promise, made in a specific literature, to a specific kind of boy, and he tested the promise by supplying every input it asked for and watching the output fail.

The boy rose at six. He studied electricity. He practiced elocution. He saved five dollars. He found a patron and served him for five years without drinking. And the money went to Ella Kaye through a legal device he never understood, which left him with an education in how rich men behave and no way to become one legitimately. Everything after that, the drugstores, the shirts, the parties, the green light, the pool, follows from a formula that took the inputs and did not deliver.

The schedule is dated September 12, 1906. Fitzgerald put the date on the page because a document with a date is evidence. Read the novel as the record of what happened to the boy who wrote it, and the Alger comparison stops being a source-hunting exercise and becomes the shortest route to what the book is actually arguing.

One last thing the comparison recovers, which most readings of the novel miss entirely. Alger's books were addressed to boys who could not otherwise imagine a route out. Whatever their sentimentality and whatever their quiet dishonesty about luck, they were written to give a poor reader a picture of a life he might have. Fitzgerald knew this, and his treatment of the schedule contains no contempt for the boy who wrote it. The contempt in the novel is reserved entirely for the people who inherited and broke things and retreated back into their money. The formula is refuted; the boy is not mocked. Holding both of those at once is what the book asks of a reader, and it is the hardest thing about writing on this subject well.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does Gatsby compare to the Horatio Alger myth?

The novel invokes the formula with precision and then voids it element by element. Gatsby has the humble origins, the self-improvement program, the moment of notice, the patron, the relentless labor, and the spectacular rise. What he does not have is the formula's payoff. The patron dies and a legal device sends the legacy to Ella Kaye, the labor turns criminal because no legitimate route remains, and the ending is a body in a pool rather than a clerkship and a bank account. Fitzgerald is not caricaturing the myth. He builds it faithfully, in a boy's handwriting on a flyleaf dated September 12, 1906, and then lets it run to its actual conclusion.

### Q: What is the Horatio Alger success myth?

A poor, honest boy arrives in the city, works hard, avoids vice, seizes a chance to demonstrate his character, and is elevated by a wealthy patron who notices. Two corrections to the popular version matter. Alger's heroes end in respectable security, not in mansions, so the rags-to-riches phrase attached to him after his death inflates the reward considerably. And luck is structural rather than decorative: one of his own titles is Luck and Pluck, and his plots turn on chance encounters. The formula therefore contains a quiet admission that effort alone is insufficient, which the myth deleted and which Fitzgerald exploits.

### Q: How does Gatsby appear to embody the Alger myth?

Almost completely, on the surface. He is born poor to farm people in North Dakota, disciplines himself according to a written program, works without let-up, refuses alcohol entirely, serves with distinction in the war, and rises from nothing to a mansion on the Sound within a few years. He is generous with his house, loyal to Daisy past reason, and Nick's summary verdict is that he turned out all right at the end. If you stopped reading at the parties you would have an Alger hero scaled up for the 1920s. The novel's structure is designed to let you believe this for five chapters before Chapter Six explains where the money came from.

### Q: How does the novel subvert the Alger myth?

Systematically rather than by a single reversal. The origins are poor but the son disowns them, so the formula's engine of filial devotion is removed. The moment of notice coincides exactly with the self-renaming, so service and invention become one act. The patron dies and the legacy is taken by paperwork the boy cannot follow. The labor is criminal. And the ending is death rather than security, with the estate dispersing within weeks. Fitzgerald also controls the order of disclosure, withholding the schedule until Chapter Nine so that the boy's earnest program arrives after the corpse and cannot be read hopefully.

### Q: How does the boyhood schedule echo Alger?

It reproduces the theory rather than a business plan. Rising at six, dumbbell exercise and wall-scaling, studying electricity, practicing elocution and poise, studying needed inventions, then general resolves about tobacco, bathing, reading one improving book a week, saving five dollars, and being better to parents. Nothing on that list generates income. Every item is about becoming the sort of person to whom good things are supposed to happen, which is precisely Alger's account of how success works: the boy's job is to make himself worthy, and the world's job is to notice. The savings item is moral rather than financial, since five dollars is evidence of discipline and not capital.

### Q: Is Gatsby a straight Alger hero?

No, though the reading deserves a hearing rather than a dismissal. He meets the conditions on discipline, effort, abstention, and generosity, and Nick's admiration is unfeigned. He fails the two load-bearing conditions: the labor is criminal, and the ending is death rather than security. The stronger version of the counter-reading is not that Gatsby is an Alger hero but that he is the Alger hero the twentieth century made impossible, a boy who followed the formula until it terminated at a dead patron and a legal device and then took the only remaining road. That version is defensible and is much more interesting than the flat claim.

### Q: Who was Horatio Alger and what did he write?

An American author, born in Massachusetts in 1832 and dead in 1899, who wrote something over a hundred books for boys, nearly all variations on one plot. Ragged Dick, serialized in 1867 and published as a book the following year, is the best known. He trained for the ministry and served a parish briefly before leaving it in 1866 after a scandal, after which he wrote full time. His sales were solid in life and vastly larger after his death, when cheap reprint houses put his titles into circulation by the millions around 1900. That posthumous boom is what put an Alger-shaped script in front of a farm boy in North Dakota in 1906.

### Q: Does Dan Cody play the Alger patron role?

He occupies the position and inverts every attribute. The Alger patron is a respectable man of business whose money is clean and whose elevation of the boy certifies that the social order rewards merit. Cody is a mining speculator from the Nevada silver fields and the Montana copper booms, physically robust but on the verge of soft-mindedness, surrounded by women trying to separate him from his money, and drinking heavily. Gatsby serves him five years as the boat circles the continent three times, is named in the will for twenty-five thousand dollars, and receives none of it. What transfers is an education in how rich men behave, which Fitzgerald calls singularly appropriate, meaning fitted to the criminal career that follows.

### Q: What happens to virtue in the novel's version of the myth?

It becomes irrelevant to the outcome, which is a harsher verdict than making the hero corrupt would have been. Gatsby is not punished for a moral failing. He does everything the formula asks, and the reward is withheld by a mechanism that has nothing to do with character. Fitzgerald's sympathy for him is engineered precisely so this lands: a villain caught by the system would prove the system detects fraud, while a disciplined and generous man who follows the instructions and dies in a pool proves the instructions were false. The novel separates deserving from receiving and then shows that only the second one has any bearing on what happens.

### Q: Why does luck matter more in Alger than in Fitzgerald?

Because Alger's luck is guaranteed to arrive and Fitzgerald's is not. Chance is structural in Alger, not incidental: the boy happens to be present when a rich man's child needs saving, the patron happens to pass by. But the plots are built so that chance always lands favorably on a worthy boy, which converts luck into a delivery mechanism for merit. Fitzgerald keeps the chance and removes the guarantee. Gatsby happens to see a yacht anchor and rows out to it, which is an Alger scene exactly, and the patron dies four pages later and the money goes elsewhere. Same mechanism, opposite outcome.

### Q: Is Alger named anywhere in the book?

No, and the omission is deliberate and effective. Fitzgerald never mentions Alger, never uses the phrase rags to riches, and never has a character discuss success literature. What he does instead is date the schedule to September 12, 1906, which places it at the height of the posthumous Alger reprint boom, and print it on the flyleaf of a boys' adventure novel. A reader in 1925 would have recognized the genre of document without needing the name. The technique is characteristic: Fitzgerald supplies an artifact with a date on it and lets the reader supply the tradition.

### Q: How would an Alger story have ended for Jay Gatz?

With Dan Cody's legacy arriving intact. Twenty-five thousand dollars in 1912 was a substantial sum, enough to establish a young man in a legitimate business, and an Alger novel would have ended there: the boy's five years of loyal service rewarded, a modest fortune, a respectable position, and a marriage to a good woman of his own station. Fitzgerald writes that ending and then cancels it in four sentences. He didn't get it, he never understood the legal device used against him, the remaining millions went to Ella Kaye, and he was left with his education. Everything the novel contains happens in the space that cancellation opened.

### Q: What does Mr Gatz believe about his son's rise?

That it was legitimate and that it was headed somewhere larger. He arrives from Minnesota with a photograph of the house, cracked at the corners and dirty from being handled, which he has clearly been showing to people. He produces the schedule as proof of what the boy was. And he says his son had a big future before him, and that if he had lived he would have been a great man, a man like James J. Hill, who would have helped build up the country. The father's model of greatness is a real railroad magnate rather than an Alger clerk, and he holds this belief while standing in a house bought with bootlegging money at his son's funeral.

### Q: Is Tom Buchanan the anti-Alger figure?

He is the formula's silent premise made visible. Alger's world contains rich men who reward merit, and it never asks how they became rich. Tom answers the question by refusing to be a question: his money is inherited, enormous, and entirely unexamined by the novel, and he did nothing to earn it. He is also the person who finally destroys Gatsby, by telling Wilson who owns the yellow car. So the man who never followed the formula ends the summer intact on Fifth Avenue while the man who followed it exactly ends in a pool. Fitzgerald puts the two outcomes in the same book and does not comment.

### Q: Does the novel reject upward mobility altogether?

No, and reading it that way makes the book smaller than it is. Nick is upwardly mobile in a modest way, moves East for work, and returns West by choice rather than by defeat. The novel's target is narrower and sharper: the specific promise that disciplined self-improvement plus a patron's recognition converts into security, made by a nineteenth-century literature to boys living in a twentieth-century economy. Fitzgerald shows the conversion failing, and shows that the failure occurs before any crime is committed. What he rejects is not ambition but a false account of how ambition is supposed to pay.

### Q: How should a student use the Alger frame in an essay?

Open on the schedule rather than on the American Dream, since the schedule is concrete, dated, and quotable and puts the frame in play immediately. Define Alger accurately in the second paragraph, including that his heroes end in respectable security rather than mansions and that one of his titles pairs luck with pluck. Then run three elements of the audit, taking origins, patron, and ending, showing invocation and voiding within the same paragraph rather than in separate ones. Handle the objection that Gatsby is sympathetic inside the body. Close on Henry Gatz at the funeral, holding a schedule from 1906 and comparing his bootlegger son to a railroad magnate.

### Q: What does bootlegging do to the success formula?

It replaces the formula's ladder with a shortcut that did not previously exist. Alger's boys enter commerce, where the rungs are visible and time is the price, and the books are honest enough about that world. Prohibition created an enormous market with no legitimate suppliers and therefore no established firms holding the senior positions, so a man with nerve and organizational ability could accumulate in five years what commerce would have taken a lifetime to yield. Fitzgerald keeps the mechanics offstage, giving only drugstores selling grain alcohol, calls from Chicago and Philadelphia, and Wolfshiem. The point is structural rather than moral: the legal route had closed for a man in Gatsby's position, and the illegal one was open.

### Q: Why did the Alger myth stay popular after the novel appeared?

Because it is not falsifiable by evidence, which is exactly what Fitzgerald dramatizes in Chapter Nine. Henry Gatz stands at his son's funeral holding a document that proves the boy followed every instruction, in a house bought with criminal money, beside a body pulled from a pool, and his conclusion is that the boy would have helped build up the country. The myth absorbs the corpse without a dent. Beyond the novel, the formula's institutional life continued, including an association founded in 1947 to honor Americans who had risen from poverty and named after him. Fitzgerald's demonstration and the myth's persistence are not in tension; the persistence is what he was writing about.
