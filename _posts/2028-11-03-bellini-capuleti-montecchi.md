## The Verdict First: Bellini's Capuleti Is Not an Opera of the Play

Bellini's *I Capuleti e i Montecchi*, first performed at the Teatro La Fenice in Venice on 11 March 1830, is the most famous Romeo and Juliet opera that Shakespeare never wrote. The distinction matters, and this article defends it as the key to everything the work is. Vincenzo Bellini's score sets a libretto by Felice Romani, and Romani's text descends from the Italian novella tradition through Luigi Scevola's tragedy *Giulietta e Romeo* of 1818 and through Romani's own earlier libretto for Nicola Vaccai. No English text stands anywhere in that line of descent. The opera is therefore not an adaptation of the play, loose or otherwise. It is a parallel treatment of a shared story, composed in the same Italian line that produced the tale before the English playwright ever touched it, and its differences from the play are not liberties. They are inheritances.

![Giuditta Grisi as Romeo in Bellini's I Capuleti e i Montecchi at the La Fenice premiere - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict can be stated plainly at the outset. Judged as an adaptation of Shakespeare, *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* looks mutilated: the balcony scene is gone, Mercutio is gone, the Nurse is gone, Paris is gone, and Juliet wakes before Romeo dies, which reverses the play's most famous stage picture. Judged as what it is, a bel canto tragedy drawn from the novelle through Scevola, the work is coherent, concentrated, and in its final scene arguably closer to the emotional logic of the oldest Italian telling than the play itself. The opera keeps five singing principals, a political feud between Guelph and Ghibelline factions, a heroine betrothed to her lover's enemy, a physician instead of a friar, and an ending in which the lovers die awake and in each other's arms. Every one of those features is traceable to the Italian line. None of them is a mistake.

The strongest evidence is the ending. In the play, Juliet wakes too late. Romeo is already dead when she opens her eyes in the vault, and her last act is solitary: "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170, Folger Shakespeare Library edition). In the opera, following Scevola and the novelle behind him, Giulietta wakes while Romeo still lives. The lovers recognise each other, sing together, and die in sequence within the same scene, Romeo first and Giulietta collapsing over his body. The difference is not cosmetic. Shakespeare's ending is about failed communication, a letter gone astray, a plan collapsing through accident. The Italian ending is about recognition granted and then withdrawn, a reunion measured in minutes. Romani did not misread the English tomb scene. He never set it. His tomb scene comes from the older tradition in which the heroine of Luigi da Porto's novella wakes in time to speak with her dying husband, and that tradition, not the play, is the opera's true source text.

Three tests settle the question of descent for any disputed scene. First, is the scene present in the novelle line? The waking heroine passes that test, since da Porto and Matteo Bandello both grant the lovers a final meeting. Second, is the scene explicable as operatic compression of the play? The political feud fails that test, because compression simplifies motives, whereas Romani replaces the family quarrel with an older Guelph and Ghibelline party war that the play never contained. Third, did the librettist have a documented Italian model in hand? Romani had two: Scevola's tragedy and his own Vaccai libretto of 1825, both built on the same Italian material. The opera passes every test of Italian descent and fails every test of English derivation. The rest of this article works through the making, the music, the voices, and the strange performance history of a score that spent six decades being performed with another composer's ending, and it returns at each stage to the same conclusion: *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* is the great surviving monument of the Romeo and Juliet story's parallel Italian life.

A word on terminology, because the quarrel about this opera is partly a quarrel about a noun. To call the work an adaptation of the shared story is accurate and empty: every Romeo and Juliet work adapts the shared story, the play included, since the playwright adapted Brooke, who adapted Boaistuau, who adapted Bandello. The noun earns its keep only when the source is named. Adaptation of the play means derivation from the English text, its scenes, its inventions, its order of events. Adaptation of the novelle means derivation from the Italian prose line, its civic feud, its go-betweens, its waking heroine. Bellini's opera is the second thing, demonstrably and in every row of the comparison table this article builds below. The persistent misdescription of the opera as a free or faulty version of the play is the single largest obstacle to hearing it fairly, because it sends listeners to the tomb scene listening for a dagger that was never in the design and faulting a heroine for waking at the hour her tradition always appointed. Strip the misdescription away and the work's logic stands bare: a tragedy of refused peace and refused flight, told in the idiom its story was born in.

The work's own title page already files the correct claim. Bellini and Romani called the piece a tragedia lirica in two acts and four parts, naming a genre, not a source, and the genre label carries the Italian tragedy's programme with it: few persons, public stakes, a catastrophe of state as much as of heart. The four-part division is equally a statement, grouping the scenes into spans that behave like the movements of a symphony, exposition of the feud, the lovers' private counter-world, the machinery of the counterfeit death, and the tomb. English readers who approach the opera through the play's five-act architecture will keep misjudging its proportions, finding the first act top-heavy with politics and the second brutally swift. Read in its own four movements, the proportions resolve: the politics must be massive because the catastrophe is civic, and the ending must be swift because the tradition's tomb scene is a recognition, not a sequence of discoveries. Form follows lineage in this opera at every level, from the feud's Guelph banners down to the syllable counts of its verse, and the sections below keep returning to that single explanatory principle because no rival principle explains as much.

That conclusion carries a consequence for how the opera should be heard and staged. A production that treats the score as a cut-down Shakespeare will keep apologising for absences the work never promised to fill. A production that treats the score as the novelle set to music finds a complete design: two acts, four parts, five principals, one feud, and a heroine whose tragedy is not that she slept too long but that she woke at the wrong hour. The sections below take the design in order. The sources come first, because the sources explain everything else.

## Where Romani Found the Story: Scevola and the Novelle Behind Bellini

The libretto's genealogy is documented at every link, and it is Italian at every link. Felice Romani, the most sought-after Italian librettist of his generation, wrote the text of *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* by reworking a libretto he had already written once before: his *Giulietta e Romeo* for Nicola Vaccai, first performed at the Teatro della Canobbiana in Milan on 31 October 1825. Behind that libretto stood the tragedy *Giulietta e Romeo* by Luigi Scevola, written in 1818. Behind Scevola stood the novella tradition of Renaissance Italy, the prose tellings that carried the Verona story across two centuries before any English version existed, and at the head of that tradition stood the novella of Luigi da Porto, composed in the years around 1524 and published at Venice after its author's death, the text that first fixed the lovers' names, their city, their secret marriage, the sleeping potion, and the tomb. The wider traffic of that tradition, the movement of Italian stories into northern imaginations, is traced in [the series survey of Italian novelle and the English imagination](/2026/03/20/italian-novelle-english-imagination/), and the single most influential retelling in the chain, the version printed at Lucca in 1554, receives its own study in [the article on Matteo Bandello's novella](/2025/10/10/matteo-bandello-novella-1554/). Romani's libretto belongs to that river. It simply flows in the opposite direction from the English play: not from the novella outward into Europe, but from the novella down through Italian stages to an Italian opera house.

The musicologist Michael Collins established the shape of this descent in a short, dense article, "The Literary Background of Bellini's *I Capuleti ed i Montecchi*," published in the Journal of the American Musicological Society in 1982. Collins's subject was precisely the question careless programme notes keep blurring: which texts, exactly, lie behind Romani's words. The answer his title already announces is a literary background, not a theatrical one. Romani worked as an Italian man of letters worked, from an Italian tragedy on his desk and an Italian libretto in his drawer. The point is worth stressing because the alternative assumption, that any Romeo and Juliet text of 1830 must somehow lean on Shakespeare, reverses the actual chronology of the story's European life. Da Porto's novella predates the play by some seventy years. Bandello's retelling predates it by four decades. Scevola's tragedy is a descendant of those texts, written by an Italian for Italian audiences out of Italian materials. Romani inherited the story the way an English librettist inherits a folk ballad: from his own culture's shelf.

Against that documented chain, one claim in the standard literature deserves scrutiny. Charles Osborne, in *The Bel Canto Operas of Rossini, Donizetti, and Bellini* (1994), wrote that Romani's libretto, though set in a Verona that predates the play's world, shows signs of its author's awareness of the English dramatist's version. The claim is attractive and it may even be true at the level of general culture: Romani was a widely read critic and journalist, Shakespeare's name was known in Italian literary circles, and French translations circulated. But awareness is not derivation, and the libretto's bones argue against any direct use. A librettist working from the play, however freely, does not usually delete Mercutio, the Nurse, Paris, the balcony, the Queen Mab world of Verona street life, and the entire mechanism of the undelivered letter, while retaining almost nothing that the play invented. The play's inventions are precisely what the opera lacks. What the opera has, the waking heroine, the political feud, the heroine betrothed to Tybalt, the physician confidant, the play lacks. Descent explains that pattern. Awareness explains nothing. The adjudication therefore goes to the documents: Romani's sources were Scevola and his own Vaccai text, and any Shakespearean colouring reached him, if it reached him at all, as rumour rather than as a book open on the desk.

Scevola's share in the design deserves a paragraph of its own, because the opera's most distinctive features are his before they are Romani's. Scevola wrote as a tragedian of the Alfierian school, drawn to concentrated casts, public stakes, and civic conflict. His Verona is a city divided by party, not by two households' unexplained spite. The play's Chorus opens with a puzzle, "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue, 1-2), and never explains the feud's origin, since the ancient grudge is the given condition of the world. Scevola's feud has a history and a politics: the Montecchi lead the Ghibelline party, the Capuleti the Guelph, and the struggle between them is a chapter of the long Italian war between empire and papacy that Dante's generation fought in earnest. When Romani took that structure into the libretto, the feud gained what the play deliberately withholds, a public meaning. Romeo in the opera is not a love-struck son of a feuding house. He is the head of a party, a commander who has killed his enemy's son in open battle, and his embassy to Capellio's palace is a diplomatic act by a head of faction offering peace through marriage. The love story and the peace process are the same event, which is why the opera's first act can sustain a full political scene before the heroine sings a note.

### Did Romani ever open Shakespeare's play while writing the libretto?

No documentary evidence places the play in Romani's hands while he worked, and the text he produced argues that he used Italian models alone. His desk held Scevola's tragedy and his own Vaccai libretto of 1825, both derived from the novella tradition, and every feature of the result stands in that Italian line and is absent from the English play.

The chronology of composition reinforces the point. Romani's Bellini libretto was a revision executed at speed in January 1830, adapting a five-year-old text of his own to new singers and a new theatre. Revision of that kind works from the page in front of the writer. There was no occasion for fresh research in any language, and no letter, contract, or contemporary account records the English play entering the Fenice workshop at any stage. When later nineteenth-century French and English commentators measured the libretto against the play and found it wanting, they were applying a standard its author had never accepted. The fairest summary is the one the sources support: Romani knew the story as Italy had always known it, as a novella that had become a tragedy that had become an opera text, and Shakespeare's version was, for his purposes, a foreign treatment of Italian property.

One further figure in the genealogy should be named, because the chain passes through his hands twice. Romani wrote for Vaccai in 1825 and for Bellini in 1830, and the two libretti are close kin, not twins. For Bellini he compressed, reordered, and tightened his earlier text, and the differences between the two operas' final scenes would later become a public issue when singers began treating the two scores as interchangeable at exactly that point. That episode belongs to the performance history below. For the sources question, the double commission carries a simpler lesson. The same librettist, given the same story twice within five years, went back to the same Italian sources both times. The novella line was not a fallback for a writer who could not reach Shakespeare. It was the obvious, native, and prestigious source, the one an Italian theatre expected.

## Da Porto to Bandello to Scevola: The Novella Line in Detail

The chain behind Scevola is worth walking link by link, because the opera's features attach to specific links. The story's first surviving form is Masuccio Salernitano's thirty-third tale in *Il Novellino* of 1476, set in Siena, where Mariotto Mignanelli loves Gianozza Saraceni, marries her in secret through a friar, and loses her to a counterfeit death arranged with a sleeping potion; the tomb reunion fails and both lovers die. The skeleton is already complete: secret union, potion, tomb, double death. Luigi da Porto, a soldier of Vicenza, took that skeleton in the 1520s and gave it everything the world remembers. His novella, published at Venice in 1530 after his death, moved the tale to Verona under the rule of Bartolomeo della Scala, named the families Montecchi and Cappelletti, fixed the lovers as Romeo and Giulietta, supplied the feud's civic frame, and ended the story in the tomb with the heroine waking as her husband dies and expiring beside him by holding her breath. Da Porto's telling is the opera's ultimate source in the strictest sense: the Vaccai libretto's descent from it is the one the reference works print, and the opera inherits da Porto's Verona, da Porto's civic feud, and da Porto's waking heroine through Scevola's hands.

Bandello's retelling, the ninth novella of the second part of his *Novelle*, printed at Lucca in 1554, is the chain's great amplifier. Matteo Bandello, a Dominican friar with a courtier's ear, expanded da Porto's spare tale into a full narrative with the feud's street life, the go-between machinery, and the tomb scene rendered at a length no earlier telling attempts. His version fed the French translation of Pierre Boaistuau, and Boaistuau fed Arthur Brooke's English poem of 1562, and Brooke fed the play. The English masterpiece therefore stands at the end of the same river the opera drinks from upstream. That is the geographical fact the descent argument turns on. Shakespeare and Romani are cousins, not ancestor and heir: both descend from da Porto and Bandello, the playwright through French and English intermediaries at the end of the sixteenth century, the librettist through an Italian tragedy and his own earlier libretto at the start of the nineteenth. Scevola's tragedy of 1818 is the last link on the Italian side, and its contribution, beyond transmission, was compression to a tragedian's cast and the sharpening of the party war into the action's engine. By the time Romani opened Scevola, every feature that distinguishes the opera from the play had already been selected by Italian hands. His reworking for Vaccai in 1825 and for Bellini in 1830 changed the words and the pacing. It did not change the bloodline.

The chain also explains a casting of names that puzzles readers of the play. The opera's villain-father is Capellio, a form that puzzles until the documents explain it: Weinstock notes that Romani had used Capellio as Giulietta's family name in the Vaccai libretto, and the opera promotes the surname into the father's person. Tebaldo likewise carries a double inheritance, the name descending from da Porto's Tybalt-figure while the function absorbs the approved bridegroom the English tradition calls Paris. Even the physician Lorenzo is a product of the line's long negotiation with the Italian stage, where a friar at the centre of a love intrigue sat awkwardly with censorship and custom, and the confidant's religious habit thinned, across the centuries of retelling, into a doctor's professional discretion. Nothing in the opera's dramatis personae is an oversight. Each name and office is a fossil of the route the story travelled, and the fossils all point the same way, south and east of the play, into Italy.

Romani himself deserves a final word in this section, because the sources argument rests partly on the kind of writer he was. Felice Romani was the professional's professional, the librettist to whom Mayr, Rossini, Donizetti, and Bellini all turned, the author of *Il turco in Italia* before this period and of *L'elisir d'amore* and *Lucrezia Borgia* after it, a journalist and critic who ran Turin's official gazette and who treated the libretto as a branch of literature with classical obligations. Such a writer, commissioned at speed, reaches for the shelf his whole formation stocked: Italian tragedy, Italian novelle, his own earlier text. The notion that he secretly worked from an English play he never cites, in a language whose texts circulated in Italy chiefly in French dress, belongs to the same Anglocentric habit that calls every later Romeo a version of Shakespeare. Romani's career is the context that makes the documentary chain believable. He did not need the play because his culture had never lost the story.

## Six Weeks in Venice: Zaira's Failure and the Making of the Bellini Score

The opera exists because another opera failed and a third composer stayed away. Bellini's *Zaira*, given at Parma in May 1829, was the first outright failure of his career, and he returned to Milan that summer with no new contract in prospect. The Teatro La Fenice in Venice wanted Giovanni Pacini, Bellini's fellow Sicilian, for its 1830 Carnival season; its impresario Alessandro Lanari signed Pacini with a clause transferring the commission to Bellini if Pacini failed to appear. Pacini did fail to appear. Lanari activated the clause, and on 5 January 1830 Bellini accepted terms: he would set Romani's libretto, provisionally titled *Giulietta Capellio*, he required forty-five days between receiving the text and the first performance, and he accepted a fee of 325 gold napoleons. The contract became effective in mid-January, Romani arrived in Venice having already reworked much of his Vaccai libretto, and the two men settled to work under a deadline that shrank as Venetian winter weather worsened and Bellini fell ill. The premiere took place on 11 March 1830. Herbert Weinstock, Bellini's biographer, describes that premiere as "an unclouded and immediate success," and the local press agreed, reporting enthusiasm comparable to the Milan triumph of *La straniera*. The season closed on 21 March, after eight performances, and success travelled with the score: the work reached La Scala on 26 December 1830, Dresden on 1 October 1831, London on 20 July 1833, and New Orleans on 4 April 1837.

The deadline explains the score's most debated feature, its extensive self-borrowing. Bellini reviewed his failed *Zaira* to see what could be reset to Romani's new words, and he told correspondents that the Parma failure would be avenged by the Venetian success. How much of *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* is recycled *Zaira* has been counted two ways, and the counts disagree. Charles Osborne suggested that Bellini recycled nearly half the music. Mary Ann Smart, examining the borrowings number by number in her study "In Praise of Convention: Formula and Experiment in Bellini's Self-Borrowings" (Journal of the American Musicological Society, 2000), gives the harder figure: Bellini reused no fewer than eight numbers from *Zaira* in the new opera, alongside at least one famous theft from elsewhere in his own catalogue, since Giulietta's "Oh! quante volte" refits Nelly's romanza from *Adelson e Salvini*, his first opera. The adjudication favours Smart's method. Eight substantial numbers, plus the *Adelson* romanza, is a count that can be checked against the scores; "nearly half" is an impression that swells a list of numbers into a share of the whole evening. The honest verdict is that the borrowing was extensive, deliberate, and open: a composer rescuing strong music from a dead opera under a deadline, not a composer padding a score he could not fill.

Smart's analysis also answers the deeper charge that self-borrowing of that scale must be dramatically careless. Her prize example is the opera's most solemn passage. In *Zaira*, the prima donna's cabaletta had expressed giddy anticipation of an imminent wedding, in lines of seven syllables. In *I Capuleti e i Montecchi*, that same music, its lines recast into five-syllable verse by inserting word repetitions, becomes Romeo's lament over Giulietta's apparently lifeless body in the tomb, the romanza "Deh! tu, bell'anima." Smart's argument, and it is persuasive, is that the transformation demonstrates craft rather than economy: Bellini and Romani understood that a melody's affect lives in its pacing, its orchestration, and its verbal rhythm more than in its first occasion, and they rebuilt the number's metre to make the music mean its opposite. A wedding cabaletta became a funeral romanza without a note of the vocal line losing its fit. If that is borrowing, it is borrowing of a high order, and the section of the opera audiences have always prized most, the tomb scene, is exactly where the borrowed goods were put to their severest test.

The conditions of the salvage deserve recording, because they refute the picture of a composer coasting on old material. Bellini worked through a Venetian winter of worsening weather and fell ill during the composition, continuing under a timetable that contracted as the carnival calendar advanced, with rehearsals for the *Pirata* revival running alongside and the libretto's revisions still being agreed between the collaborators as the score grew. The work's title changed inside those same weeks: the contract's *Giulietta Capellio*, a heroine's-name title in the period's fashion, gave way to *I Capuleti e i Montecchi*, and the change is a small manifesto. The opera would be named for the two houses, not for its heroine, because its subject is the feud system that contains the lovers, the parties and their war. A title centred on Giulietta would have promised a portrait; the title Bellini and Romani chose promises a collision, and the score delivers the collision in every act. The fee of 325 gold napoleons bought the Fenice six weeks of feverish labour and the century's defining Italian setting of the tale, which suggests that Lanari, clause and all, made the best bargain of his career.

### How much of the score did Bellini compose from nothing in the six weeks?

Less than half of the principal numbers were wholly new, and the proportions can be stated precisely because the borrowings have been catalogued. At least eight numbers were reworked from *Zaira*, one celebrated romanza came from *Adelson e Salvini*, and the remainder was composed in Venice against the deadline, including the part of Romeo written for Giuditta Grisi's voice.

The score is therefore a salvage operation and a new composition at once, and its unity comes from the writing for the two lovers' voices, which Bellini created fresh for the singers in front of him. The great Act 1 finale's new dramatic joints belong to the Venetian weeks as well, the passages where borrowed melody had to be bent around confrontations the Parma score never contained.

The human instrument of that freshness was the cast Lanari had assembled, the same company then performing *Il pirata* at the Fenice. Giuditta Grisi, a mezzo-soprano of formidable reputation, sang Romeo, a role Bellini composed directly for her voice. Rosalbina Caradori-Allan sang Giulietta. Lorenzo Bonfigli sang Tebaldo, Gaetano Antoldi sang Capellio, and Ranieri Pocchini sang Lorenzo. The distribution of voices already encodes the opera's priorities. The lovers take the two highest voices in the theatre, and they are both women's voices. The tenor, the voice an English audience would expect in the hero, is assigned to the rival, Tebaldo, and the basses hold the world of fathers and physicians. Bellini's Verona is sung from the top down: feeling at the top, authority at the bottom, and the tenor's ardour given to the man whose claim on Giulietta is legal rather than chosen. That disposition was dictated by the company at hand, since the Fenice troupe was rich in female voices and, as the record concedes, relatively weak in leading men. Necessity and design coincide so neatly that separating them is pointless. Bellini wrote the opera his singers could sing, and the opera he wrote made a virtue of the constraint for two centuries.

The autograph preserves one more trace of that workshop pragmatism, in the part of Lorenzo. Bellini conceived the physician for a bass, and Pocchini, the Fenice singer, is listed in the bass register in the premiere's cast table; yet the autograph's second act writes the part in the tenor clef throughout, and for the La Scala production Bellini recast the role for tenor outright. Scholars have explained the discrepancy by the singers available at each house and by a mid-composition change of plan, and the honest summary is that Lorenzo's voice type was negotiable in 1830 in a way the lovers' voices were not. The detail matters beyond its curiosity. An opera whose confidant could migrate between bass and tenor within its first year was an opera built inside a culture of refitting, where the printed and manuscript text served the company in the room. The same culture, given sixty years and a star of Malibran's magnitude, would refit the ending itself. The line from the tenor Lorenzo to the Vaccai tomb scene is a line of degree within one theatrical civilisation, and Bellini, who drew the first segment of it himself, stands inside that civilisation rather than above it.

One document of the premiere run deserves preservation because it shows the composer at work on his own text after opening night. For the La Scala production of 26 December 1830, Bellini lowered the part of Giulietta for Amalia Schütz Oldosi, a mezzo-soprano, so that Milan heard two mezzo voices as the lovers; and he adjusted the part of Lorenzo, which the Fenice had taken with a bass, toward the tenor register. The opera was, from its first year, a text its composer expected to be refitted to voices. That expectation matters when the later history of substitutions arrives, because the line between the refitting Bellini practised and the replacement Malibran imposed is a line of degree that the nineteenth century drew in a different place than scholarship draws it. The composer who transposed his heroine downward for a new singer had limited standing to complain when a singer of genius reached for a different finale. His successors, armed with critical editions, would complain on his behalf.

## The Fenice Carnival: How the Premiere Played in Venice

The institutional frame of the premiere explains both the opera's speed of manufacture and the speed of its acceptance. Venice's Carnival season was the Fenice's great market, a run of weeks in which the city's theatres competed for a masked, pleasure-seeking public that expected novelty and forgave experiment. Lanari's programming for 1830 hedged the season across composers: Pacini engaged and then absent, *Il pirata* revived with the house company, and the new Bellini held in reserve behind a contract clause. When the clause fired, the season's machinery absorbed the emergency without drama. Romani was the Fenice's natural librettist, the company was already singing Bellini nightly, and the subject, a Verona tragedy every Italian playgoer knew from the novelle, required no selling. The premiere of 11 March fell late in the season, close against its close on 21 March, and the run of eight performances in ten days represents not a curtailed failure but saturation: the house played the new work as often as the calendar allowed, and the press, in the journal *I Teatri*, judged that the opera had aroused as much enthusiasm in Venice as *La straniera* had aroused in Milan from its first evening.

The season's informal honours confirm the verdict. Venetian report describes the composer escorted to his lodgings after the third performance by a crowd preceded by a military band playing music from his operas, the kind of street demonstration Italian cities reserved for composers who had given them a success to be proud of. Bellini's own accounting, in a letter of 28 March, runs: "My style is now heard in the most important theatres in the world, and with the greatest enthusiasm." Before he left Venice the Fenice had offered him a further new opera for the following Carnival, and an offer from Genoa for the same period, found waiting on his return to Milan, had to be refused. The La Scala staging of 26 December 1830 then opened the next Carnival round with the work already a classic of ten months' standing, in the composer's own revision, with both lovers sung by mezzo-sopranos. Few operas of the century can show so complete a first year: a triumph at the premiere house, a street serenade, a second carnival at the rival house, and a European itinerary under way. The six weeks of desperate composition bought a decade of Italian dominance, and the dominance, not the haste, is what the record remembers.

## The Plot Bellini Set: A Political Feud With No Balcony and No Mercutio

Romani's action, distributed across two acts and four parts, can be told quickly, and its speed is the point. Capellio, head of the Guelph Capuleti, opens the opera by rejecting a peace embassy expected from Romeo, head of the Ghibelline Montecchi, who has killed Capellio's son in battle. Tebaldo, Capellio's partisan and Giulietta's betrothed, vows in his cavatina "È serbata a questo acciaro" that his sword owes vengeance for that blood, and the wedding is fixed for that very day despite Lorenzo's warning that Giulietta lies ill. Romeo then enters disguised as his own envoy and makes the opera's central public offer: in "Ascolta: Se Romeo t'uccise un figlio" he concedes the killing, lays it to the fortune of battle, and offers himself to Capellio as a second son in the dead man's place, with peace sealed by his marriage to Giulietta. Capellio answers that Tebaldo already fills that place. The chorus cries war, Romeo accepts the challenge in the cabaletta "La tremenda ultrice spada," and the political plot is complete within the first scene: peace was possible, was offered by the injured party's enemy in person, and was refused by pride. The tragedy that follows is therefore not a machine of accidents. It is the consequence of a decision the audience watched being taken.

The second scene moves to Giulietta's room, where the heroine enters amid wedding preparations she dreads and sings the opera's best-known solo, "Oh! quante volte," a romanza of longing for the absent Romeo. Lorenzo, her father's physician and her sole confidant, admits Romeo by a secret door, and the lovers' duet "Sì, fuggire: a noi non resta" stages the opera's central private argument. Romeo begs her to flee with him. Giulietta refuses, in the name of duty, law, and honour, and the refusal is not a delay imposed by the plot. It is her character: the novella tradition's heroine is bound to her father's house by a code she will not break even to live, and her tragedy is that two loyalties, to Romeo and to the code, cannot both be kept. The act closes in the palace courtyard, where Romeo, waiting with a thousand Montecchi in disguise to break up the wedding, is discovered; the factions clash, the lovers are dragged apart by their own people, and the concerted finale locks the design in place. Every element of the catastrophe has by this point been set by choice: Capellio's refusal of peace, Giulietta's refusal of flight, Romeo's resort to force.

Act 2 strips the machinery to its essentials. A solo cello introduces Giulietta, who learns from Lorenzo that Romeo lives but that she will be carried to Tebaldo's castle at dawn. Lorenzo offers his device, a potion that will counterfeit death; she will be laid in the family tomb, where he and Romeo will wait for her waking. Her aria "Morte io non temo, il sai" accepts the gamble, and her cabaletta "Ah! non poss'io partire" begs her father's forgiveness as the drug takes hold, a plea Capellio rejects even as she collapses. The second scene moves outside the palace, where Romeo, in the bitter recitative Weinstock singled out, "Deserto è il loco," waits for a meeting Lorenzo, by then watched and silenced under Capellio's order, cannot keep. Tebaldo arrives; the rivals draw; and at the moment of combat the funeral procession of Giulietta passes, singing "Pace alla tua bell'anima." The duel dissolves into shared remorse. The final scene is the tomb, treated at length below. Measured against the play, the absences are systematic, not random. There is no Mercutio, so there is no wit to lose and no curse to fulfil, though the play's catastrophe turns on his dying words, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1). There is no Nurse, so Giulietta's confidant is a professional man whose device is medicine, not errands. There is no Paris, because Tebaldo absorbs the approved suitor's function entire. There is no balcony, because the lovers' meeting is moved indoors, to her room, by a secret door, an arrangement the novelle tradition sanctions and the opera's single-set economy rewards. And there is no undelivered letter: Lorenzo's plan fails not through plague or chance but because Capellio, suspicious, has his physician watched, an act of the same paternal vigilance that refused the peace. The opera's causal chain is human from end to end.

### Why does Giulietta refuse Romeo's offer of flight in their duet?

Giulietta refuses because Romani's heroine, following the Italian tragedy's code, holds her duty to her father's house and her honour as obligations stronger than her wish to live with Romeo. Flight would save the lovers and disgrace her name; the potion promises escape through a counterfeit death that breaks no vow and shames no one.

Her refusal in the duet is therefore the hinge of the whole design, the choice that makes the tomb scene necessary, and it marks the deepest difference from the play, whose Juliet urges the marriage forward and never weighs flight against honour at all.

The act's finale then converts private refusal into public catastrophe with a mechanism worth noticing, because the opera stages its discovery without any equivalent of the play's street brawl. Romeo has not come to the wedding as a guest, as the play's Romeo comes masked to the Capulet ball; he has come as a saboteur at the head of a disguised force, and the discovery is military rather than social. When the factions clash in the courtyard and the lovers are pulled apart by their own adherents, the concerted finale freezes the action into the opera's thesis image: two principals singing their farewell across a stage full of armed men who share their blood and will not share their feeling. The finale's power is architectural. Every party to the catastrophe stands inside one number, the refuser of peace, the refuser of flight, the commander who resorted to force, the bridegroom whose claim set the day, and the physician whose silence will kill them all, and the music holds them in a single span while the plot's gears lock. Act 2 then plays the consequences in three short scenes, and the gear-work deserves respect as dramaturgy: nothing in the second act is decoration, and its funeral procession, the chant of "Pace alla tua bell'anima" crossing the duel, is the one moment where the opera's Verona speaks with a single voice before the tomb.

The comparison with the play's aubade world is instructive at one further point. Shakespeare's lovers meet in an orchard, speak across a balcony that the text never names, and part at dawn with the lark and nightingale argument; the play's erotic life is outdoor, vertical, and overheard by no one. The opera's lovers meet in a bedroom, through a door, arranged by a physician, in a palace preparing a wedding; the erotic life is indoor, horizontal, and surrounded by hostile ears. Neither arrangement is a degraded copy of the other. Each belongs to its medium and its source: the play inherited the orchard from Brooke's poem and made it immortal, the opera inherited the chamber from the novelle, where the lovers' meetings are always interiors managed by go-betweens. Readers who search for the balcony in Bellini are searching for England. The opera offers them Italy instead, and the exchange, once recognised, is not a loss.

The potion repays the same comparison, because its terms change with each tradition and the opera's terms are the most austere of all. The Friar of the play is a pharmacist with a timetable, promising Juliet that the draught's effect will last "two and forty hours" (4.1.105) and building the rescue around that clock. The novelle give their potion a longer and vaguer term, days rather than hours, because prose has room for the convent and the tomb watch. Romani's libretto keeps no clock at all in any passage the action pauses on: Lorenzo's preparation counterfeits death, the household mourns, the tomb receives the body, and the plot's interest sits entirely in who knows and who is prevented from knowing. The failure, accordingly, is social rather than mechanical. In the play a quarantined friar and an undelivered letter kill the lovers, instruments of pure chance. In the opera the plan fails because Capellio posts a watch on his own physician, the act of a suspicious father who has already refused peace, refused forgiveness, and refused his daughter's plea, and who therefore kills the lovers the way he has done everything else, deliberately and in character. Critics who call the opera's plot simpler than the play's are right about the machinery and wrong about the meaning. The simplification is a moral choice: this tradition wants its catastrophe earned by its villain, not dealt by fortune.

## Romeo in a Woman's Voice: The Trouser Role From Crescentini to Grisi

Romeo had been sung by a high voice for a generation before Bellini, and the lineage runs from the castrato to the mezzo-soprano without a break. Niccolò Antonio Zingarelli's *Giulietta e Romeo*, given at La Scala on 30 January 1796, created its Romeo for Girolamo Crescentini, the last great castrato of the Italian stage, and Crescentini crowned the role with an aria of his own composition, "Ombra adorata aspetta," Romeo's prayer at the tomb, which became the most famous single page in the Italian Romeo tradition and a fixture of concert rooms for decades. Zingarelli is reported to have cursed the interpolation as his opera's misfortune; audiences disagreed, and the prayer outlived the opera around it. The point for Bellini's casting is the precedent. By 1830 the castrati were gone from Italian stages, and their heroic repertory had passed to women: the contraltos and mezzo-sopranos who inherited the travesti roles. Vaccai's Romeo of 1825 was a contralto en travesti, Isabella Fabbrica. Bellini's Romeo of 1830 was Grisi. The female Romeo was not an experiment or a makeshift. It was the standard Italian solution to a standard Italian role, and the trouser-role tradition it belongs to, the stage's long commerce in crossed and reassigned gender, is the subject of [the series study of queer readings and Mercutio's desire](/2022/09/23/mercutio-queer-reading-romeo/), where Romeo sung by a woman takes its place among the play's many re-genderings.

The convention did produce one famous enemy, and his evidence must be heard in full because the adjudication defines the opera's reputation ever after. Hector Berlioz encountered the opera at Florence and recorded his objections in his Memoirs. Two charges stand out. The first is a source charge: Romani, he complained, had abandoned the play's capital scenes, the ball and the balcony among them, and impoverished the story. The second is a casting charge: he objected strenuously to Romeo sung by a woman, remarking in effect that no law required Juliet's lover always to appear shorn of his manhood, and noting that Zingarelli and Vaccai had done the same, as if precedent aggravated the offence. Both charges fail on examination, though they fail differently. The source charge fails on fact: Romani abandoned nothing, since the ball and balcony were never in his sources, and faulting the libretto for their absence is faulting an Italian tragedy for not being an English play. The casting charge fails as argument while standing as testimony: it records what a French musician of Berlioz's formation heard in 1832, a mismatch between the voice and the character's sex, but it cannot show that the mismatch damaged the music, and Berlioz himself supplies the refutation. The one passage of the opera he praised without reserve was the lovers' singing in unison near the end of Act 1, music whose extraordinary force and bold impetus, his own words for it, depend entirely on the blend of two high voices moving as one. The effect he admired is the effect the casting he condemned made possible. Two sopranos in unison are not a tenor and a soprano in unison. The verdict follows: Berlioz heard the opera accurately and judged it by the wrong standard, and his Memoirs remain the best evidence for both halves of that sentence.

What the two-voice blend means dramatically has been the trouser role's defence ever since, and it can be stated without mysticism. Bel canto writing thinks in lines, and the love music of this opera is built as line answering line, the two voices exchanging, shadowing, and finally merging their phrases until the ear stops separating them. Romeo and Giulietta in this score are less a man and a woman than a single feeling in two registers, and the mezzo Romeo, darker than the soprano Giulietta by a shade, lets the score paint union and difference at once. A tenor Romeo breaks the geometry. The duets become conversations between unlike instruments, which is the play's geometry, dialogue between separate persons, but it is not this opera's. Bellini wrote for the voices in the Fenice company, yet the writing outlasted the company because the geometry is load-bearing: replace the mezzo and the final scene's sonority, two women over a body that is one of their own number's partner, loses its peculiar ache. The twentieth century tested the proposition experimentally, and the experiment's result appears below.

The role's nineteenth-century interpreters trace a line of great singing actresses for whom Romeo was a signature. Giuditta Pasta took the part in 1833. Wilhelmine Schröder-Devrient sang it in 1834 and 1835. Maria Malibran made it one of her glories, and her intervention in the ending is treated in its own section below. Giuseppina Ronzi de Begnis sang Romeo in 1834 in performances remembered for a distinction no other interpreter of the decade could claim, since she restored Bellini's ending where theatres had begun substituting Vaccai's. In 1859 the opera reached the Paris Opéra in Charles Nuitter's French translation, prepared for Felicita von Vestvali, whose Romeo so captivated Paris that Napoleon III presented her with a suit of silver armour; critics compared her to Malibran and to the tragedienne Rachel. A role that draws Pasta, Schröder-Devrient, Malibran, and Vestvali across three decades is not a curiosity of casting. It is one of the century's great travesti creations, the last heroic lover of the castrato line, and the opera cannot be understood apart from the women who made it.

### What did Berlioz praise in the opera he otherwise attacked?

Berlioz praised the passage near the close of Act 1 in which the separated lovers' voices unite, music he credited with an extraordinary force and bold impetus. The praise matters beyond its generosity, because the passage achieves its effect through exactly the feature his criticism condemned: two high female voices blending in unison.

His review thus preserves, against its own argument, the strongest contemporary testimony that the trouser-role casting served the score's central expressive device rather than obstructing it, the blend written for the mezzo-soprano Romeo and soprano Giulietta of the Fenice company.

The casting question also has a plain theatrical answer that criticism sometimes forgets. Romeo in this opera is a youth, the head of his faction by inheritance rather than by age, and the libretto keeps him out of every scene of council and command except his embassy, where he appears disguised and pleading. The part asks for ardour, agility, and pathos rather than authority, the exact portfolio of the primo musico tradition the travesti Romeo inherited from the castrati. Grisi's instrument, by all accounts a mezzo of unusual power and dramatic fire, fitted the portfolio completely. When the La Scala revival of December 1830 lowered Giulietta for a mezzo as well, Milanese audiences heard the lovers as near-equals in timbre, and the experiment satisfied the composer enough to stand in his own revision. The modern instinct to apologise for the convention usually rests on treating opera as photographed theatre. The opera's first century treated it as sung theatre, where a voice is an instrument before it is a body, and on that understanding the female Romeo is not a compromise at all. It is the correct instrument for the part as written.

The La Scala experiment of December 1830 carries one more implication that criticism has underexploited. Bellini lowered Giulietta's part for Amalia Schütz Oldosi without recorded protest from the public or the press, which means the composer's first audiences understood the two roles as a matched pair of high voices whose exact distribution between soprano and mezzo could flex with the company. The lovers in this opera are a sonority before they are a casting chart. That flexibility also explains the role's nineteenth-century portability: Pasta, Schröder-Devrient, Malibran, and Vestvali did not share one voice type in the modern Fach sense, yet each could take Romeo because the part was written for a singer of heroic calibre in the high register rather than for a narrowly specified instrument. The twentieth century's revivalists inherited the same latitude in reverse, since Simionato, Cossotto, Troyanos, Horne, Baltsa, Baker, Kasarova, Larmore, Genaux, Garanča, and DiDonato represent nearly every shade of the modern mezzo-soprano and contralto spectrum. A role that accommodates that range of instruments while resisting transfer to the tenor voice is telling its interpreters something precise about where its identity lives: in register and blend, not in biography.

## The Bel Canto Machine: Long Melody, the Cabaletta, and Two Voices as One

Bel canto, as Bellini practised it in 1830, is a technology for sustaining emotion at length, and *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* is its early masterpiece in the tragic register. The system's unit is the double aria: a slow cantabile in which a feeling is stated at full stretch, a disruptive middle passage, the tempo di mezzo, in which news or resolve intervenes, and a fast cabaletta in which the feeling converts to action or defiance, conventionally repeated with ornament. Around those units the score builds its larger spans: duets that follow the same slow-movement, disruption, and fast-movement grammar between two people, choruses that frame public decisions, and the great concerted finale in which private and public lines collide and hold. The system's reputation for emptiness comes from its weakest products, where the forms are filled with display. Bellini's distinction, visible already in this sixth opera, is the length and patience of his cantabile lines. His melodies unfold in long, asymmetrical breaths, sparsely accompanied, trusting the voice to carry meaning across spans that a busier orchestra would chop into phrases. The style's contemporaries heard the difference at once, and the Fenice audience's enthusiasm was for melodies that seemed to think.

Three passages fix the style's range in this opera, and each has already figured in this article under another heading, which is itself evidence of the score's economy. "Oh! quante volte" is the cantabile ideal: a romanza of pure longing, its line borrowed from an opera of Bellini's adolescence and refitted so completely that its origin is undetectable from the effect, the heroine suspended in a single emotion for the length of a full aria while the wedding she dreads is prepared around her. The Act 1 finale is the public ideal: the lovers' private catastrophe folded into a concerted ensemble of both factions, the individual lines still audible inside the mass, the act closing on separation rather than resolution. "Deh! tu, bell'anima" is the tragic ideal: the borrowed wedding cabaletta slowed into a romanza over a body, its five-syllable lines repeating their words as if speech itself were failing, addressed to a soul the singer begs to carry him upward. Cantabile, concertato, romanza: the opera's three summits are three different solutions to the same problem, how to make sustained song carry a drama whose text, scene by scene, is spare. That is the bel canto machine working at full pressure, and a catalogue of the opera's textual evidence belongs in any serious listening archive; researchers organising editions, libretto printings, and recording comparisons for study will find [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) suited to keeping that material in a single ordered collection.

The tenor's portion shows the machine's public gear. Tebaldo receives the opera's most conventional rhetoric, and the convention is the character: his entrance cavatina "È serbata a questo acciaro" states the vengeance claim in the balanced periods of a man singing the official language of his party, and his music throughout keeps the polish of a public role performed in public forms. Bellini's distribution of styles is a map of the drama's moral geography. The lovers sing in long, irregular, inward lines that break the period's symmetry whenever feeling requires it. The bridegroom sings in well-made squares. The father and the physician sing in the heavier metres of authority and counsel. A listener can hear, without a word of Italian, which characters the score believes are alive and which are reciting their positions, and that audibility is the bel canto system's dramatic intelligence at work. The cabaletta, so often dismissed as an applause mechanism bolted to an aria's end, functions in this score as the sound of a feeling hardening into an act: Romeo's acceptance of the war challenge, Giulietta's plea for forgiveness as the drug takes her, the lovers' final clinging cabaletta in the tomb. Each fast movement is a decision. The machine's reputation for emptiness survives only among those who have not watched it decide.

The spareness of the text is worth one more paragraph, because it explains the score's structure to readers arriving from the play. Shakespeare gives his lovers some of the densest verse in the language; the balcony scene alone, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks" (2.2.2), sustains a rhetoric of image upon image that no libretto could set entire. Romani gives his lovers situations and leaves the rhetoric to the music. His duet for the chamber scene is built from refusal and counter-plea, a handful of exchanges, because the eloquence is in the vocal lines, which say in duration what the words say in outline. This division of labour is the bel canto contract, and it clarifies the old quarrel about whether the opera's drama is thin. The drama is not thin. It is relocated, from the verbal surface to the musical line, and it thins only for listeners who attend to the words alone. Berlioz, the opera's great hostile witness, heard the relocation happen in the Act 1 unison passage and praised the result in spite of himself. The adjudication of his whole critique comes down to that moment. On sources he was wrong, on casting he was inconsistent, and on the music, where it mattered most, his ear overruled his doctrine and told the truth.

A word belongs to the orchestra, which bel canto legend treats as a mere accompanist and which this score repeatedly refuses to be. The Act 2 potion scene opens with a solo cello line of a melancholy so exposed that it functions as a third character in the room, the sound of the drug's approach before a word is sung. The funeral procession that halts the duel is an orchestral and choral event before it is a verbal one: the rivals' cabaletta of rage is simply stopped by the sound of "Pace alla tua bell'anima," and the score makes two armed men lower their swords at a noise. Even the tomb scene's stillness is orchestrated, the romanza floating over an accompaniment thinned to a heartbeat. Bellini at twenty-eight already wrote the pit as a dramatist. The later operas, *Norma* and *I puritani* above all, would expand the palette, but the principle is present here entire: in a score where words are few, everything else must speak, and the orchestra accepts the commission.

Beneath the large forms lies the prosodic contract that made them singable, and it deserves a paragraph because the scholarship on the borrowings turns on it. An Italian libretto of 1830 was built in counted verse: lines of five, six, seven, or eight syllables, the quinari, senari, settenari, and ottonari that Philip Gossett's *Divas and Scholars* pauses to define, each metre carrying its own gait and its own conventional affect. The librettist's craft was to hand the composer lines whose syllable counts and stress patterns a melody could ride without breaking, and the composer's craft was to fit line to line so exactly that the verse seemed to generate the tune. Smart's praise of Bellini's scrupulous matching of music and poetry describes this contract honoured at the highest level. The *Zaira* transformation shows the contract under strain and holding: a wedding cabaletta in seven-syllable lines had to become a tomb romanza in fives, and Romani's inserted word repetitions between the second and third syllables of each line stretched the shorter verse across the longer melody without violence to either. Readers who wonder why this opera's text reads as spare on the page should hold it against the metre table. The words are few because the syllables are rationed, and the rationing is what lets the music run long. Bel canto length is built out of verbal brevity, and Romani, the most professional librettist in Italy, supplied the brevity on purpose.

## The Night Malibran Replaced the Ending: Vaccai's Tomb Scene Inside Bellini's Opera

On 27 October 1832, at Bologna, Maria Malibran sang Romeo in *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* and sang a different opera's ending. At her request, the penultimate scene of Vaccai's *Giulietta e Romeo*, the tomb scene of the 1825 opera whose libretto Romani had reworked for Bellini, was substituted for Bellini's last act. The substitution was not vandalism, and it was not ignorance: Malibran knew both scores intimately, having made Vaccai's Romeo a favourite vehicle before Bellini's existed in her repertory. She preferred Vaccai's final scene as a piece of theatre for the singer of Romeo, and in the performance culture of 1832 her preference was law. What she began at Bologna became frequent practice for the remainder of the nineteenth century; in Italy it was almost the rule, and the grafted ending was still being performed as late as 8 September 1897, at Hamburg. For sixty-five years, audiences who believed they were hearing Bellini's opera heard its last act replaced by its predecessor's. The composer had been dead since 1835. The opera's fame and the opera's text had parted company almost at once.

The details of the swap repay attention, because the graft was possible only through the scores' shared parentage. Both operas set Romani libretti derived from Scevola, so Vaccai's tomb scene and Bellini's tomb scene dramatise the same events in the same order: the entry into the Capuleti tomb, the farewell over the body, the poison, the waking, the duet of recognition, the double death. A theatre could lift one scene out and set the other in its place with the plot's seams barely showing, because the plots were the same plot. What changed was the music at the opera's most exposed point, and with it the balance of the evening: Vaccai's scene gave Romeo a more expansive final span, the kind of closing display the age expected its primo musico to deliver, and it gave audiences who knew the Vaccai from 1825 the ending they had already learned to weep at. The substitution also had an honourable dissenter early. Giuseppina Ronzi de Begnis, singing Romeo in 1834, restored Bellini's ending in her performances, two years after Malibran's Bologna night. The practice therefore never went unchallenged; it prevailed not because no one objected but because star preference, audience habit, and the practical availability of the Vaccai scene in theatres' libraries outweighed textual loyalty in an age that had not yet made a doctrine of it.

The scholar who has made this episode do the most work is Philip Gossett, whose *Divas and Scholars: Performing Italian Opera* (2006) takes Malibran's Vaccai substitution as a standing example of the singer-imposed change, the alteration a composer neither wrote nor authorised that hardens into tradition. Gossett's framing is the useful one. The episode is not a scandal about one diva's ego; it is a window into a textual culture in which an opera was a repertory of performing materials rather than a fixed object, in which the composer's own revisions for new singers, Bellini transposing Giulietta downward for Schütz Oldosi in 1830, and the singers' revisions for themselves differed in degree rather than in kind. The nineteenth century did not experience the grafted ending as a corruption. It experienced it as the opera, improved at the point of maximum feeling by the greatest Romeo of the age. Only the twentieth century, armed with critical editions of the autograph, would reclassify the same performances as mutilations, and the reclassification says as much about the newer century's doctrine as about the older one's taste.

### Why did the Vaccai substitution survive for sixty-five years?

The substitution survived because every force in the nineteenth-century opera house favoured it: star Romeos preferred Vaccai's more expansive final scene, audiences across Italy knew that ending from the 1825 opera and expected its satisfactions, theatres owned the materials, and no doctrine of the inviolable text yet existed to forbid the graft.

It ended only when the performance culture that sustained it ended, when the opera itself left the repertory around the turn of the century and was later revived from printed scores and the autograph tradition rather than from living theatrical habit.

The adjudication for a modern reader is therefore double. As a document of performance culture, the Malibran ending is precious evidence, proof that the opera lived in theatres as a flexible vehicle and that its tomb scene was the contested summit two composers and two generations of singers fought over. As a text of Bellini's opera, it is simply not Bellini, and any performance claiming the opera's name owes its audience the ending Bellini wrote, the romanza "Deh! tu, bell'anima" and the recognition duet as the autograph gives them. Both judgements can stand together because they answer different questions. The error lies in confusing them, in treating the graft either as the opera's authentic form or as a crime. It was the nineteenth century's way of loving the work, and it loved the work nearly to death.

The recovery of the authentic text was the work of the edition makers. Casa Ricordi's critical edition of Bellini's operas placed the autograph tradition at the centre of the composer's catalogue, giving conductors a text of *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* from which the Vaccai graft, the transpositions of convenience, and a century of accumulated habit could be measured and set aside. Gossett, the scholar whose account of the Malibran substitution frames this section, also supplies the necessary caution from the same book: a printed edition, however critical, is a point of departure for a performance, not a blueprint to be followed with architectural precision. The modern settlement honours both halves of his teaching. The graft survives as documented history, studied and occasionally exhibited as the curiosity it is, while the opera in the theatre is Bellini's, ending where his autograph ends. What the nineteenth century joined, scholarship has put asunder, and the separation holds because it rests on paper rather than on taste: the autograph exists, the graft is datable to a single Bologna night, and the line between them can be drawn by anyone who reads the sources in order.

## Silence and Return: The Twentieth-Century Revival of I Capuleti e i Montecchi

The opera's nineteenth century closed in habit and its twentieth opened in silence. The habit had been long and honourable. Within five years of the premiere the work had been given in some thirty Italian productions, and the European itinerary ran steadily: Dresden heard it on 1 October 1831, the first staging outside Italy; London followed on 20 July 1833; the United States followed at the St Charles Theatre in New Orleans on 4 April 1837, with Boston in 1847 and New York in 1848 completing the American introduction. Through the 1840s and 1850s the opera remained a repertory piece across Europe, the vehicle of Pasta, Schröder-Devrient, Malibran's successors, and the Paris Vestvali of 1859, and it continued to be seen fairly regularly until the end of the 1860s. The work that had been performed across Europe and America faded from the repertory as verismo taste and then modernist taste made bel canto seem a museum idiom. A revival at Catania in 1935, the centenary of Bellini's death, proved the score had local guardians; a Palermo production in 1954 with Giulietta Simionato as Romeo proved more, but the decisive returns were made in the concert hall and the broadcast studio. In 1957 RAI broadcast the opera with Fiorenza Cossotto as Romeo, Antonietta Pastori as Giulietta, and Lorin Maazel conducting, the performance that reintroduced the score to a listening public. In October 1958 the American Opera Society gave the opera in concert at Carnegie Hall with Simionato as Romeo, its first New York performance in a hundred years, and repeated it in 1964. The pattern of the revival is instructive: the opera came back as singing first and staging second, carried by mezzo-sopranos of the postwar school, Simionato and Cossotto above all, who had the technique the cabalettas demand and the gravity the tomb scene deserves. The travesti Romeo, so long a period embarrassment, turned out to be the vehicle through which the work re-entered the world.

The revival's one false step is the most instructive episode in the opera's modern history, because it tested Berlioz's old objection under laboratory conditions. In 1966 Claudio Abbado prepared a version of the opera in which Romeo was rewritten for a tenor, Giacomo Aragall, first heard at Bologna and the Holland Festival with Margherita Rinaldi as Giulietta, then staged at La Scala in 1967 with Renata Scotto as Giulietta and Luciano Pavarotti as Tebaldo, in designs by Emanuele Luzzati; the production travelled to Expo 67 at Montreal and to Philadelphia in 1968. The experiment had everything in its favour: a great conductor, star casting, the prestige of Italy's first house. It failed, and its version is no longer used. The transposition destroyed the two-voice geometry on which the love music is built, unbalanced ensembles written for a high Romeo against a tenor Tebaldo, and demonstrated to a generation that had inherited Berlioz's doubt that the mezzo Romeo is not a defect the score tolerates but a condition the score requires. Later criticism said it with unusual unanimity, one discographer calling the tenor version a bastardised edition at once self-recommending and self-damning. The adjudication is closed by the market and the stage alike: since Abbado's experiment, Romeo has belonged to the mezzo-sopranos, and the episode stands as the strongest possible evidence, bought at La Scala prices, that Bellini's casting was a structural decision.

The decades after the experiment belong to the great Romeos of the modern bel canto revival. In 1975 Sarah Caldwell's Opera Company of Boston staged the opera with Tatiana Troyanos as Romeo and Beverly Sills as Giulietta, and Sills sang the same Giulietta in London that year opposite Janet Baker's Romeo, the partnership preserved on the EMI recording under Giuseppe Patanè. Marilyn Horne sang Romeo at Dallas in 1977. Agnes Baltsa made the role a European signature, at Vienna in 1977 and then, decisively, in the Royal Opera's first staging of the work at Covent Garden in 1984, opposite Edita Gruberová's Giulietta under Riccardo Muti, a production whose live recording fixed the modern standard text in the public ear: Bellini's own ending, the mezzo Romeo, the opera whole. Vesselina Kasarova's Romeo, heard at New York in 1999 under Eve Queler and on record opposite Eva Mei under Roberto Abbado, carried the role into the next generation, and the twenty-first century has kept the work in regular production, with Joyce DiDonato's Romeo opposite Nicole Cabell's Giulietta at San Francisco in 2012, Kate Lindsey's concert Romeo at Washington in 2014, and the Vienna concert performances of 2008, recorded by Deutsche Grammophon, pairing Elīna Garanča's Romeo with Anna Netrebko's Giulietta under Fabio Luisi. A score written in six weeks for one carnival season, silenced for half a century, and performed for sixty-five years with a borrowed ending, stands in the repertory in its composer's text, sung by the voice he wrote it for. The revival is complete, and it vindicates every party to the old quarrels except the doubters of the mezzo Romeo.

### Why did Abbado's tenor Romeo disappear from the stage?

Abbado's tenor Romeo disappeared because the experiment failed on the score's own terms: transposing Romeo downward broke the blend of the two high voices in the love duets, disturbed the balance against the tenor Tebaldo, and left a version that neither audiences nor later conductors chose to keep.

Its failure is the modern era's decisive evidence in the casting quarrel Berlioz opened, since a first-class conductor with first-class singers at La Scala could not make the naturalistic alternative work, while mezzo-soprano Romeos from Simionato to Baltsa restored the opera to the international repertory in the same decades.

The gramophone completed what the stage began, and the opera's recording history maps the revival's consolidation decade by decade. The RAI broadcast performance under Maazel, with Cossotto and Pastori, circulated as the work's first widely heard document. The 1975 EMI set under Giuseppe Patanè, with Janet Baker's Romeo and Beverly Sills's Giulietta supported by Nicolai Gedda's Tebaldo, gave the opera a studio monument from the revival's heroic age. Muti's 1984 Covent Garden recording with Baltsa and Gruberová fixed the authentic text, Bellini's ending included, as the commercial standard. The catalogue then normalised: Roberto Abbado's 1997 RCA set with Vesselina Kasarova and Eva Mei, Donald Runnicles's 1998 Teldec set with Jennifer Larmore and Hei-Kyung Hong, and Fabio Luisi's 2008 Deutsche Grammophon recording from the Vienna Konzerthaus, with Garanča and Netrebko, mark the opera's passage from rarity to repertory staple with competing editions. Fabio Biondi's 2014 Glossa recording with Europa Galante, Vivica Genaux singing Romeo, added the period-instrument reading, and the 2016 Zurich production under Luisi, with DiDonato and Olga Kulchynska in Christof Loy's staging, carried the work onto video in its modern form. An opera that the early twentieth century could not hear at all is, in the early twenty-first, an opera a listener can compare across six decades of interpretation, which is as complete a definition of revival as the record offers.

## The Tomb Scene Itself: Anatomy of an Ending

The final scene earns a slow reading, because everything the article has argued converges inside it. Romeo enters the Capuleti tombs at the head of his Montecchi followers, a commander arriving with an escort to mourn an enemy house's daughter, and the chorus of his own party sings the grief his disguise can no longer manage in public. He orders the tomb opened and the followers dismissed. The romanza "Deh! tu, bell'anima" follows, the borrowed wedding music slowed into an address to the departing soul, and it is essential to hear what the number is doing in the design: it is a farewell sung by a man who still believes the separation is final and one-directional, the living addressing the dead across a border he is about to cross. He drinks the poison and lies down beside her. Only then, and the timing is the tradition's whole point, does the scene reverse: a sigh, a voice, Giulietta rising. The recognition duet that follows is built as a double misunderstanding dissolving, since she wakes believing Lorenzo's plan has worked and Romeo stands there by arrangement, while he must tell her, inside the music's most tender exchanges, that he has already acted to end his life and cannot leave the tomb. Her offers of flight, the same flight she refused in the chamber scene, return as the cruellest echo in the score: the escape she would not take when it could save them is the escape she begs for when it cannot. He dies. She falls across his body, dead without a wound, and the factions enter together to find the tableau and pronounce the opera's verdict on Capellio in a single accusation.

Set against the play's fifth act, the scene's architecture explains the two endings' different reputations. Shakespeare's tomb scene is a sequence of arrivals and errors, Paris, the Page, the Friar, the Watch, each entrance recontextualising the bodies, and its emotional peak, Juliet's waking, is a soliloquy over a corpse followed by a death the audience watches her choose instrumentally, dagger in hand. Bellini's tomb scene admits no new information after the waking. Its peak is a duet, two voices in the same musical space at the same time, and its deaths are a diminuendo rather than a pair of shocks. The play's scene is superior theatre of incident; the opera's is superior theatre of duration, and the difference is the difference between the media at their most characteristic. It also explains the nineteenth century's itch to substitute Vaccai's version of the same events: the scene is the evening's summit and its most exposed span of singing, the place where a great Romeo most wanted maximum room, and two settings of identical events invited comparison at exactly the point where singers' preferences could rule. Malibran's intervention, adjudicated above as a document of its culture, was at least aimed at the right scene. She understood where the opera lives.

One further detail of the ending belongs to the sources argument and closes it. The play resolves its catastrophe institutionally: the Prince judges, the fathers shake hands, statues are promised, and the state absorbs the loss. The opera resolves its catastrophe accusatorially: the assembled factions, both of them, turn on the surviving father and name him the author of the deaths, and the curtain falls on blame rather than reconciliation. The novella line's moral bookkeeping is the opera's. Da Porto's telling ends in communal grief and judgement on the feud's makers, not in golden statues, and Romani's close, in which Capellio stands condemned by his own party and his enemies together, is the Italian ending's final signature. A listener who knows only the play experiences the last minute as an absence, the missing handshake. A listener who knows the line experiences it as a presence: the older story, finishing the way it began, with the city rather than the state pronouncing what the feud has cost.

Productions that grasp the scene's design tend to simplify everything around it: a bare tomb, the two bodies, the entering factions held at a distance while the accusation lands. Productions that miss the design tend to fill the stage with the apparatus of the play's ending, watchmen, torches, a prince's judgement, none of which the score has music for. The scene is the opera in miniature, and it grades its interpreters as reliably as it graded its two composers.

## The Parallel Line: Why Bellini's Opera Matters and What It Proves

The standard story of the Romeo and Juliet material tells of an Italian spark and an English flame: the novelle invent, the play immortalises, and everything after descends from the play. Bellini's opera breaks that story at its hinge. In 1830, more than two centuries after the play, the most successful new Romeo and Juliet work in Europe drew nothing from the English text at all. It reached back past the play to the play's own sources, renewed them in the most advanced musical language of its day, and held European stages for the rest of the century. The series thesis finds its clearest proof here: the cliché that all versions descend from Shakespeare survives only by ignoring the Italian theatre, where the story never died and never needed the Englishman's permission to live. The opera is not a tributary of the play's river. It is the older river, still flowing.

The comparison that sharpens the point stands one step away in the repertory. Gounod's *Roméo et Juliette* of 1867, the subject of [the companion study of Gounod's opera](/2028/10/27/gounod-opera-romeo-et-juliette/), is a genuine adaptation of the play, built from the English text through French translation, complete with balcony, ball, and a heroine who wakes, as in the play's sources Gounod adjusted, to sing before she dies. Set the two operas side by side and the two traditions declare themselves in every scene: Gounod follows the play and alters its ending at the margins, Bellini ignores the play and reproduces the novelle ending exactly. The French work is the greater borrower from Shakespeare and the lesser witness to the tale's independent life. The Italian work is the opposite. Any account of the story in music that begins with Gounod's fidelity and treats Bellini as a primitive attempt at the same task has the history backwards.

The final verdict on the opera itself can be given without qualification. *I Capuleti e i Montecchi* is a first-rank bel canto tragedy whose supposed defects are the signatures of its lineage: the female Romeo is the last flowering of the castrato tradition and the load-bearing timbre of its love music; the political feud is Scevola's civic frame, older in spirit than the play's grudge; the waking heroine is da Porto's ending, preserved in Italian amber while England forgot it; and the borrowed melodies are a young master's salvage, proven by Smart's analysis to be transformations rather than shortcuts. The opera does not need the play's authority, and it has never had it. What it has is a six-week score of 1830 that Venice acclaimed, that Malibran loved enough to improve and Ronzi de Begnis loved enough to restore, that the twentieth century lost and then recovered entire, and that stands in the modern repertory as the Romeo and Juliet of the tradition that invented Romeo and Juliet. Judged as Shakespeare, it is a ruin. Judged as itself, it is a masterpiece of the parallel line, and the parallel line is the truth.

The article's three adjudications can be gathered into one sentence each, for the record. On the sources, the documents defeat the rumour: Romani worked from Scevola and his own Vaccai libretto, and Osborne's suggestion of Shakespearean awareness, even if true as biography, explains none of the libretto's load-bearing features. On the borrowings, the count defeats the impression: eight reworked *Zaira* numbers and one *Adelson* romanza, catalogued by Smart, describe a salvage operation whose transformations rise to invention, and Osborne's nearly half overstates what the scores contain. On the casting, the stage has defeated the doctrine: Berlioz's objection, honestly recorded and honestly refuted by his own praise of the lovers' unison, was tested at La Scala in 1966 under ideal conditions and failed, leaving the mezzo Romeo in possession of the role by right of two centuries. Each quarrel ends the same way, with the paper record and the performed record agreeing against the inherited assumption. That agreement is rare in operatic argument, and it is the deepest reason this opera rewards study: its facts are checkable, its text is recoverable, and its verdicts can be earned.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Where and when was I Capuleti e i Montecchi first performed?

The opera was first performed on 11 March 1830 at the Teatro La Fenice in Venice, during the Carnival season. Bellini had signed his contract only in mid-January, after the impresario Alessandro Lanari activated a clause transferring the commission from the absent Giovanni Pacini, and he composed the score in roughly six weeks, partly by reworking music from his failed *Zaira*. The premiere was an immediate success, reported in the Venetian press with enthusiasm comparable to the Milan triumph of *La straniera*, but the season closed on 21 March after eight performances. Later in 1830 Bellini prepared a revised version for La Scala, Milan, which opened on 26 December, and the work then spread through the Italian and European theatres.

### Q: Who sang Romeo and Giulietta at the premiere of I Capuleti e i Montecchi?

Giuditta Grisi sang Romeo and Rosalbina Caradori-Allan sang Giulietta at the La Fenice premiere on 11 March 1830. Grisi, a mezzo-soprano of major reputation, received a part composed directly for her voice, and her instrument determined the opera's most distinctive feature, its female Romeo. The remaining principals were Lorenzo Bonfigli as Tebaldo, the tenor role assigned to the heroine's betrothed, Gaetano Antoldi as Capellio, and Ranieri Pocchini as Lorenzo. The company was the same troupe then performing Bellini's *Il pirata* at the Fenice, strong in female voices and comparatively weak in leading men, and the score's distribution of voices, two women at the top and the tenor cast as the rival, reflects the singers Bellini had in the room.

### Q: Why is Romeo sung by a mezzo-soprano in Bellini's opera?

Romeo is sung by a mezzo-soprano because the Italian stage inherited the role from the castrato tradition and because Bellini wrote the part for Giuditta Grisi's voice. Zingarelli's *Giulietta e Romeo* of 1796 had created Romeo for the castrato Girolamo Crescentini, and Vaccai's setting of 1825 used a contralto en travesti, Isabella Fabbrica. When the castrati vanished, their heroic lovers passed to women singers as a matter of course. Bellini's company at the Fenice was rich in female voices, and the mezzo Romeo also served the score's design, in which the lovers' two high voices exchange, shadow, and merge phrases. The casting was a convention, a practical response to the available company, and a musical resource at once.

### Q: Why is the feud in Bellini's opera a war between political parties?

The feud in Bellini's opera is a war between political parties because Romani inherited that frame from Scevola's tragedy and the Italian tradition behind it, where the Montecchi lead Verona's Ghibellines and the Capuleti lead its Guelphs. The party frame gives the conflict a public meaning the play withholds: Shakespeare's Chorus opens on "two households, both alike in dignity" locked in an "ancient grudge" whose origin is never explained, because the English tragedy grows from private passion. In the opera Romeo is accordingly not a feuding family's son but the head of a party and a commander in the field, and his love for Giulietta becomes entangled with diplomacy when he enters Capellio's palace as his own envoy to offer peace through marriage. The political frame is an inheritance, not an embellishment.

### Q: What part does Romeo's killing of Capellio's son play in the opera's plot?

Romeo's killing of Capellio's son is the plot's founding injury, the act that makes peace necessary and forgiveness nearly impossible. Before the opera opens, Romeo has killed the young man in battle, and Capellio's hatred is therefore grief with a name rather than inherited spite. Tebaldo's opening cavatina vows that his sword owes vengeance for that blood, and the wedding to Giulietta is framed partly as the sealing of that vow. When Romeo, disguised as his own envoy, concedes the killing and offers to take the dead son's place in Capellio's household, the offer carries real weight: the killer asks to become the son. Capellio's refusal, on the ground that Tebaldo already fills the place, is the decision from which the whole catastrophe descends.

### Q: How long did Bellini take to compose I Capuleti e i Montecchi?

Bellini composed the opera in about six weeks, the month and a half his contract allowed between the delivery of Romani's libretto and the premiere. He had accepted the Fenice commission on 5 January 1830, required forty-five days for the work, and reached the first performance on 11 March despite illness and worsening Venetian weather. The speed was possible because Romani's libretto was a reworking of his own 1825 text for Vaccai, requiring adaptation rather than invention, and because Bellini salvaged extensive material from *Zaira*, his failed opera of 1829, resetting its melodies to the new words. The part of Romeo, written for Giuditta Grisi, and the score's dramatic joints were composed fresh in Venice. The result is a score that is simultaneously a rescue operation and a new creation.

### Q: What music did Bellini reuse from Zaira in the opera?

Bellini reused no fewer than eight numbers from *Zaira*, according to the count established by the musicologist Mary Ann Smart in her study of the composer's self-borrowings. The salvage was systematic rather than furtive: Bellini reviewed the Parma score, his failure of May 1829, expressly to find music that could be reset to Romani's new text, and he remarked that *Zaira*, hissed at Parma, was avenged by *I Capuleti e i Montecchi*. The most striking transformation turned a *Zaira* cabaletta of wedding anticipation into Romeo's tomb romanza "Deh! tu, bell'anima," its verse metre recast from seven-syllable to five-syllable lines by inserted word repetitions. Charles Osborne's suggestion that nearly half the score is recycled goes beyond the documented count; eight major numbers, extensively reworked, is the figure the scores support.

### Q: Where did the music of "Oh! quante volte" come from?

The music of "Oh! quante volte" came from Bellini's first opera, *Adelson e Salvini*, where the same melody served as Nelly's romanza "Dopo l'oscuro nembo." Bellini reached back to the opera of his Naples student years, written in 1825, and refitted its most affecting slow melody to Giulietta's longing for the absent Romeo as she watches her unwanted wedding prepared around her. The borrowing is invisible in performance, since the tune's elegiac character fits the new situation completely, and the number became the opera's most famous solo, the aria by which Giulietta is judged. The case illustrates the composer's lifelong habit of treating his early music as a storehouse, and it complicates any simple division of the score into new and recycled material: the hit of the evening was five years old.

### Q: Why does Giulietta wake before Romeo dies in Bellini's opera?

Giulietta wakes before Romeo dies because the Italian novella tradition, which Romani followed through Scevola, grants the lovers a final meeting that Shakespeare's play withholds. In da Porto's founding novella and in Bandello's retelling, the heroine wakes in the tomb while her husband still lives, and the two speak before death takes them in sequence. The opera preserves that design exactly: Romeo swallows poison, hears a sigh, and turns to find Giulietta rising, and the lovers sing a recognition duet before he dies and she falls over his body. Shakespeare's contrary arrangement, in which Juliet wakes over a corpse and dies alone by the dagger, is the English line's innovation. Judged against its true sources, the opera's ending is not a softened Shakespeare. It is the older ending, intact.

### Q: How does Giulietta die at the end of Bellini's opera?

Giulietta dies of grief over Romeo's body, without a weapon, in the tomb of the Capuleti. After Romeo's poison takes him in her arms, she falls dead across his corpse as the two factions rush in, and the opera closes with both parties turning on Capellio and naming him responsible for the double death. The manner of her death follows the Italian novella line, in which the heroine's end at the tomb is a collapse of life rather than a second suicide by steel. The contrast with the play is total: Shakespeare's Juliet finds the dagger, calls it happy, and makes a sheath of her own breast. Romani's heroine has no dagger and needs none, because her tragedy is completed by loss itself, and the final image of the opera is two bodies and a father's accusers.

### Q: Who is Lorenzo in Bellini's opera, and how does he differ from Friar Lawrence?

Lorenzo in Bellini's opera is a physician in Capellio's household, not a friar, and he is Giulietta's doctor and sole confidant rather than the lovers' secret priest. The change descends from the Italian tradition and the practical censorship of the Italian stage, which resisted clergy in such roles, and it alters the machinery of the plot: no marriage ceremony stands behind the lovers' bond in the opera, and the sleeping potion is a medical preparation supplied by a doctor practising his art. Lorenzo arranges the lovers' meeting through a secret door, provides the drug that counterfeits death, and plans to wait with Romeo at the tomb. His plan fails because Capellio, suspicious, has him watched and cut off from all contact, a human obstruction replacing the play's accidents of plague and missed letters.

### Q: What happens to Tebaldo in Bellini's opera?

Tebaldo survives Bellini's opera, a fate that separates him sharply from Shakespeare's Tybalt, who dies by Romeo's sword in the street fighting of Act 3. In the opera Tebaldo is Giulietta's betrothed and Capellio's partisan, a tenor role combining the functions of the play's Tybalt and Paris: he is both the family's champion and the approved bridegroom. He vows vengeance for Capellio's slain son, presses the wedding forward, and in Act 2 draws on Romeo in a duel outside the palace. The duel is interrupted by Giulietta's funeral procession, and the rivals lower their swords in shared remorse. With no Mercutio in the cast, the opera has no street killing and no banishment, and Tebaldo lives to enter the tomb scene's final tableau as a witness rather than a corpse.

### Q: Which characters from Shakespeare's play are missing from Bellini's opera?

Mercutio, the Nurse, Paris, and the Prince are the major figures missing from Bellini's opera, and their absence defines its five-singer design. Romani's cast, following Scevola, contains only Romeo, Giulietta, Tebaldo, Capellio, and Lorenzo, with choruses for the two factions. Mercutio's wit and death vanish, and with them the play's turning curse and the banishment plot; his functions are not replaced, because the opera's catastrophe needs no street brawl to ignite it. The Nurse's place as confidante passes to Lorenzo, Paris's place as approved suitor passes to Tebaldo, and the Prince's place as public authority passes to Capellio himself, whose refusal of peace opens the action. The compression is not poverty. It concentrates the story onto the two households' heads and the lovers, which is where the novella tradition had always kept it.

### Q: What did Hector Berlioz object to in I Capuleti e i Montecchi, and what did he praise?

Berlioz objected to Romani's abandonment of the play's famous scenes and to the casting of Romeo as a woman, and he praised the lovers' unison singing near the end of Act 1 as music of extraordinary force and bold impetus. His Memoirs record the encounter at Florence, where his devotion to the English play, the work in which he had first seen Harriet Smithson, made him measure the libretto against a text Romani had never used. The source objection fails on the documents, since the ball and balcony were never in the Italian line. The casting objection is undercut by his own praise, because the unison passage he admired depends on the blend of two high female voices. His review survives as the opera's most quoted hostile testimony and, read whole, its most self-defeating.

### Q: When did theatres stop replacing Bellini's ending with Vaccai's tomb scene?

Theatres stopped replacing Bellini's ending with Vaccai's tomb scene when the opera itself left the repertory around the end of the nineteenth century, and the grafted version was last documented at Hamburg on 8 September 1897. The practice had begun at Bologna on 27 October 1832, at Maria Malibran's request, and had become almost standard in Italy, because star Romeos preferred Vaccai's more expansive final scene and audiences knew it from the opera of 1825. Giuseppina Ronzi de Begnis had restored Bellini's ending in her own performances as early as 1834, but habit prevailed while the work lived on stage. When the twentieth-century revival rebuilt the opera from printed scores and the autograph tradition rather than from theatrical memory, Bellini's ending came back with the text, and the Vaccai scene returned to its own opera.

### Q: Why did Claudio Abbado recast Romeo as a tenor, and what became of that version?

Claudio Abbado recast Romeo as a tenor in 1966 in an attempt to make the opera stageable for a modern house uneasy with the female Romeo, answering in practice the objection Berlioz had raised in the previous century. Giacomo Aragall sang the rewritten role, first at Bologna and the Holland Festival with Margherita Rinaldi, then at La Scala in 1967 with Renata Scotto as Giulietta and Luciano Pavarotti as Tebaldo, and the production travelled to Montreal and Philadelphia. The version failed and is no longer used. Transposition broke the blend of the two high voices in the love music and unbalanced a score written for a mezzo Romeo against a tenor rival, and the experiment's collapse, under ideal conditions, settled the casting question: Romeo has remained a mezzo-soprano role in the revivals that followed.

### Q: How many acts does I Capuleti e i Montecchi have, and how is each act divided?

The opera has two acts, described in its full designation as a tragedia lirica in two acts and four parts. Act 1 moves through three scenes: the council in Capellio's palace where peace is refused, Giulietta's chamber where the lovers' duet argues flight against honour, and the courtyard where the attempted abduction of the wedding collapses into the concerted finale. Act 2 likewise moves through three scenes: the potion scene in the palace, the interrupted duel outside it, and the tomb. The four parts of the original division group these scenes into the evening's larger spans. The design is notably compact against the play's five acts, and the compactness is structural, since a cast of five principals and two choruses generates fewer subplots to service and lets both acts drive at the tomb.

### Q: How did Giuditta Grisi shape the role of Romeo?

Giuditta Grisi shaped the role of Romeo by being its intended instrument: Bellini composed the part in Venice directly for her mezzo-soprano voice, and the writing's range, agility, and weight reflect what she could do. Her presence in the Fenice company also influenced the choice of subject itself, since the story offered a heroic travesti lover of exactly the kind her repertory prized, and the weakness of the company's male singers made a female-led opera the practical as well as the artistic solution. The role she created became the century's defining travesti part, taken up by Pasta, Schröder-Devrient, Malibran, and Vestvali. When modern mezzo-sopranos from Simionato to Baltsa and Garanča sing Romeo, they are working inside a design whose dimensions were fixed by Grisi's voice in the six weeks before the premiere.
