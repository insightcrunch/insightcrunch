The most repeated fact about Baz Luhrmann's *Romeo + Juliet* is also the most misleading: the film keeps Shakespeare's language. It does, and it does not: it keeps the language and cuts the play that language builds to a third of its length. Every spoken line in the film is Shakespeare's, because Craig Pearce and Baz Luhrmann set themselves a rule against writing a single sentence of imitation verse, yet the play they speak from has been taken apart and rebuilt. Roughly a third of the text survives. The lovers' lyric peaks survive almost entire. The play's public architecture, its Chorus, its civic judgments, its closing audit, its servants, messengers, musicians and mourners, is demolished, and the weight those passages carried is transferred to a television newsreader, a police captain, a montage of headlines and the cutting rhythm of editor Jill Bilcock. How Luhrmann cut and kept the text is therefore not one decision but two, taken at different altitudes: at the level of the line, a fidelity so strict it became the film's marketing; at the level of the structure, a demolition so thorough that the same marketing conceals it. This article measures both, passage by passage, and reaches a verdict: the film is the most faithful screen version of the play ever made in its diction and among the least faithful in its architecture, and the two facts are a single method, not a contradiction.

![A television news broadcast framing the recut text of Romeo and Juliet in Verona Beach - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The method matters because the cliché about the film gets the direction of its daring exactly backwards. The received story says Luhrmann modernized the play: guns for swords, cars for horses, a swimming pool for a balcony. In fact the modernization is the conservative half of the enterprise, a change of costume over an unchanged tongue. The radical half is invisible in the posters. Luhrmann treated the text the way his own generation treated recorded music, as material to be sampled, looped, re-sited and dropped, and the film's admirers and detractors have been arguing past each other ever since because each side is describing a different layer of the result. The admirers hear the lines and call the film faithful. The detractors miss the scenes and call it a travesty. The breakdown assembled in this article, passage by passage, shows both hearings to be accurate about the layer each attends to, and it shows which layer carries the play's meaning when the two are separated. A companion study of [Baz Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet as a cultural event, its making, its casting and its box-office afterlife](/2028/08/04/baz-luhrmann-romeo-plus-juliet-1996/) covers the film whole; the subject here is narrower and stranger: the surgery performed on the words themselves.

## How Much of the Play Did Luhrmann Keep? The Count and the Quarrel

Any honest account of the cutting has to begin with a number, and the number has to come with its measurer attached, because the figures in circulation differ by enough to change the argument. The loosest estimates, repeated across review essays and teaching materials, credit the film with keeping about forty per cent of the play. The careful count is lower. Courtney Lehmann, the film scholar whose work on this adaptation runs from her Shakespeare Quarterly essay of 2001 to her study volume on the play's screen afterlife, puts the Luhrmann film's retention at roughly thirty per cent of Shakespeare's text, and she sets it in a ranking that reverses most assumptions about the screen tradition: George Cukor's 1936 film, so often dismissed as an embalmed prestige object, keeps the most of any major version at around fifty per cent; Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film, the supposed gold standard of fidelity, keeps around forty; Luhrmann keeps the least of the three, under a third, while speaking not one word that is not Shakespeare's. The ranking is the single most useful corrective in the film's literature. Verbosity and fidelity turn out to be different quantities. Zeffirelli's film feels faithful because its world looks like the play's world; Luhrmann's film is denounced as vandalism because its world looks like a music video; measured by the text, the vandal kept less than the classic, and the classic rewrote more than its reputation admits, since Zeffirelli's screenplay paraphrased and simplified as it went, while Pearce and Luhrmann's rule forbade paraphrase altogether.

The season of the film's release sharpens the point, because 1996 offered the experiment's control. In the same months, Kenneth Branagh released his *Hamlet*, a production whose entire claim was completeness: the full text, conflated from the early editions, spoken entire across some four hours, marketed as an act of restoration. Luhrmann's film is the same season's opposite pole, half the running time, a third of the text, and it was Luhrmann's film, not Branagh's, that changed what the industry believed Shakespeare could earn. The pairing is usually told as a parable of dumbing down, but the textual record refuses the parable. Branagh's completeness was itself an editorial construction, a conflation no early text contains, and Luhrmann's butchery was performed under a rule of verbal abstinence Branagh never imposed. Fidelity, the two films together prove, is not a quantity that can be read off a running time. It is a choice about which layer of a play to honour: the inventory of its words, the order of its scenes, the world those scenes build, or the effect they were built to produce. Every adapter chooses. The interest of Luhrmann's film is that the choice is so unusually pure, one layer honoured entire and the others sacrificed without apology, which is what makes its text worth auditing in detail rather than merely deploring or celebrating.

What is being counted needs stating, because the percentage conceals a distribution. A play is not a uniform substance. Romeo and Juliet runs to roughly three thousand lines in a modern edition, and those lines divide into kinds: the lovers' lyric exchanges, dense with the sonnet forms and shared rhymes that bind the pair into a single verse instrument; the public and procedural matter, decrees, negotiations, explanations and audits, through which Verona's institutions speak; the prose of servants, clowns and musicians, the play's ground bass of appetite and gossip; and the connective narration, the Chorus above all, which tells the audience what the scenes will mean before the scenes occur. Luhrmann's thirty per cent is not drawn evenly from these strata. It is drawn overwhelmingly from the first. The lyric core of the play, the meeting, the balcony, the parting at dawn, the deaths, survives at something closer to completeness, while the public stratum is cut to splinters and the prose stratum nearly to nothing. A percentage that averaged the strata would describe no scene in the film. The breakdown in this article therefore proceeds by passage and by kind, because the film's real unit of decision was never the line count. It was the kind of speech: who is talking, to whom, in what register, and whether a camera could be made to carry the same information in the speech's place.

### Did Pearce and Luhrmann add any dialogue of their own?

No line of spoken dialogue in the film was written by its screenwriters. Pearce has described the discipline directly: the pair cut, pasted and moved Shakespeare's words, and reduced the text in places, but they never wrote extra Shakespeare. The film's additions are real, but they are not verbal.

Luhrmann has repeated the claim with evident pride, calling the refusal the project's first principle. Headlines, brand names, a tattoo, a licence plate, the words on a gun: everything the film adds is seen rather than heard, and everything the audience hears as speech is the play's, which is what makes the scale of the cutting so easy to miss.

The discipline explains the film's strangest textual quality, its mixture of completeness and absence at the level of the individual speech. Where a passage survives, it tends to survive in Shakespeare's sentences, with Shakespeare's syntax and Shakespeare's jokes, because the screenwriters had denied themselves the tool with which film adapters usually soften a passage, the quiet rewrite. Where a passage failed their test, it could not be diluted; it could only be moved, reassigned, converted into an image, or deleted. The binary produced a text of peaks and cliffs. It also produced the film's governing paradox, the one the brief for any account of this adaptation must engage head-on: the language is kept, and the language is cut to a third, and both halves of that sentence describe the same screenplay. The resolution is that keeping and cutting were applied to different things. Keeping was applied to wording. Cutting was applied to everything else: to sequence, to setting, to speakers, to the proportion of the whole that any given kind of speech is allowed to occupy. The play's diction survives under new management.

### How long is the film beside the play spoken entire?

The film runs two hours, and the uncut play, spoken at a pace an audience can follow, needs the better part of three. The arithmetic is the whole method in miniature: Shakespeare's Prologue promises "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, 12), a promise the printed text cannot keep at any human speaking speed.

Luhrmann, alone among the play's major film directors, chose to keep the promise and sacrifice the text to it. Zeffirelli also reached two hours, but he reached it by slowing the verse inside a naturalistic world and cutting as he went; Luhrmann reached it by raising the velocity of everything, speech included, and cutting deeper. The two hours of the film are therefore not a container the text was poured into. They are a term of the adaptation's contract, fixed in advance, and the text was edited until it fit, the way the Prologue's own sonnet is edited until it fits a news bulletin.

The measurers and the makers, then, agree more than the quarrel suggests. Pearce and Luhrmann never claimed to have kept the play; they claimed to have kept its words, and on their own evidence the claiming was anxious as much as proud. Luhrmann recalls the two of them at work in Miami on matching computers, calling themselves, half in jest and half in genuine moral unease, the Butchers of the Bard, hats and all. The butcher's metaphor is more exact than the vandal's. A butcher does not destroy the animal; he portions it, discarding what the customer will not eat and presenting the rest as cuts. What follows is a butcher's ledger: the portions served, the portions discarded, and the portions quietly served to a different diner under a different name.

## Why Is the Prologue a News Broadcast, and Where Did the Second Chorus Go?

The play opens with a single figure, the Chorus, who steps forward and spends fourteen lines telling the audience the entire plot, including the ending: "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, 6). Luhrmann's film opens with a television set materializing out of darkness, and a newsreader at a studio desk delivers the same sonnet as a news bulletin, in the flattened cadence of a local broadcast, while a broken eternity ring turns in the corner of the screen as the story's logo. The reassignment is the most analysed single decision in the film, and its textual meaning is precise. The Chorus in the theatre is a person, present in the room, asking for the audience's patience: the sonnet ends by begging the crowd to attend "with patient ears" (Prologue, 13). The film's Chorus is an institution, and it asks for nothing. It informs. The sonnet's closing couplet, the invitation to patient attention, is the first casualty of the adaptation, dropped from the broadcast because news does not request patience; it assumes a channel that can be changed. What the film does with the sonnet after the bulletin is stranger still, and it is the clearest early evidence that cutting was not the only tool in the room. The Prologue is not used once. It is used three times, in three states of matter. The newsreader speaks it. A voiceover speaks much of it again across the opening montage, while helicopters, riot police and the twin towers of the Montague and Capulet empires fill the screen. And its phrases detach from speech altogether and circulate as print: "two households," "civil blood makes civil hands unclean," "a pair of star-cross'd lovers," set as newspaper headlines and magazine captions, the last phrase's T rendered as a cross. Shakespeare's most concentrated passage, fourteen lines that contain the whole tragedy in embryo, is the one passage the film refuses to cut, and it marks the refusal by multiplying the passage across every medium Verona Beach possesses.

The montage performs one more reassignment in passing, quieter than the sonnet's. A play introduces its people through scenes: the audience learns who the Montagues and Capulets are by watching them quarrel, negotiate and command. The film's opening minutes introduce its principals as television introduces them, by caption: Ted and Caroline Montague, Fulgencio and Gloria Capulet, Mercutio, Captain Prince, each presented in a freeze of characteristic action with name and role set on the screen as type. Exposition, which in the play is a social process conducted in dialogue across the first act, becomes typography, delivered in seconds. The device is usually discussed as a borrowing from crime films and title sequences, but in the breakdown it belongs with the Chorus's fate: the film repeatedly converts what the play says about people into what a screen can print about them, and the audience's knowledge of Verona arrives pre-digested, as information, in the form the city's own media would supply.

The multiplication has a cost, and the cost is the second Chorus. The play has two choral sonnets; the second, opening "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie" (2.0, 1), stands at the hinge of the action and performs the tragedy's coldest piece of narration, registering that Romeo's desire has changed objects and that the change itself, not any family's decree, is the engine of what follows. Luhrmann's film deletes it entire. No trace survives: no headline does its work, no character speaks its information, and the film's Romeo passes from Rosaline to Juliet inside the single continuous rush of the ball sequence, his earlier attachment present only as a mood the audience is shown at the beach. The deletion is consistent with the method. The first Chorus could be reassigned because Verona Beach has an institution that does its job: the news tells the city what has happened and what it means. The second Chorus has no institutional heir. Its job is interpretation, a cool voice pausing the action to say what the action signifies, and the film's Verona has no such voice; its media report and its police pronounce, but nothing in the city reflects. So the hinge of the play is simply removed, and the film covers the joint with velocity, cutting from the feast's aftermath to the balcony before the audience can feel the missing articulation. Readers who want to watch the two texts side by side, the play's fourteen-line units against the film's three-state Prologue, will find the screenplay printed with the play in a single published volume, and keeping both texts open in one study file is the surest way to see the surgery plain; a local-first workspace such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) exists for exactly that kind of side-by-side keeping, the film's script in one entry and the play's in the next.

The frame closes as it opened, and the closing is where the reassignment completes itself. The play ends with the Prince standing over three bodies, delivering an audit in verse: who is pardoned, who is punished, and the couplet that has become the story's epitaph, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3). The film dismantles that speech and distributes its pieces to its two substitute institutions. Captain Prince, on the ground outside the church as the bodies are carried out in bags, speaks one shard of it in person, the play's "All are punished" (5.3), hurled at both sets of parents as a policeman's rebuke. The epitaph couplet goes to the other heir: the film returns to its first image, the television set, and holds it as it recedes into darkness, and the same newsreader who opened the broadcast closes it by speaking the Prince's last lines as a sign-off, after which the set dissolves into static and dies. The effect on the play's ending is total. Shakespeare's Verona ends in speech, a community assembled to hear its ruler narrate the catastrophe and assign its meanings. Luhrmann's Verona Beach ends in coverage. The story is not judged; it is reported, packaged between the ring logo and the static, and the audience is left in the position of the city's own citizens, who have watched everything and been told, in the play's own words, exactly what it was, by a voice that was never in the room. The Chorus has eaten the Prince. That sentence, more than any statistic, is the film's textual signature.

## Which Scenes Did Luhrmann Cut Entire?

Trimming is the ordinary fate of a play on film, and Luhrmann trims everywhere, but the breakdown only becomes legible when the deletions are sorted by size, because the film's largest cuts are not trims at all. Whole mechanisms of the play are removed, and in each case the mechanism's work is either abandoned or handed to a device that does it faster. The play opens its plot with an errand: Capulet's servant, who cannot read, carries a guest list through the streets and begs Romeo to read it for him, so that Romeo learns of the feast and of Rosaline's name upon it (1.2). The scene is the tragedy's first lesson in its own method, catastrophe by failed literacy, and it is cut entire. In the film the invitations are in Mercutio's hands, distributed as party favours with a dancer's flourish, and Romeo goes to the ball not because a text fell into his path but because his friend is the feast's master of ceremonies in waiting. The play's chain of textual accidents, a letter misread here, a letter undelivered there, is shortened at its first link, and the shortening is a choice about what the film thinks the tragedy is: not a story about writing that fails, but a story about a city that watches.

Friar John is the second link, and his removal is the film's most consequential single deletion after the Chorus. In the play, the explanation of the catastrophe is a scene: Friar Laurence sends his brother Franciscan to Mantua with the letter that would save everyone, and Friar John returns with the report that he was locked into a plague house by the town's searchers and could not travel (5.2). The scene gives the disaster a mechanism and a face, and it gives Laurence his terrible recognition, "Unhappy fortune" (5.2). Luhrmann deletes Friar John, the plague, and the scene. In the film the letter travels by overnight courier to the trailer park that serves as Mantua, and it is delivered into emptiness: Romeo is not there to receive it, the envelope waits unopened, and the tragedy's hinge becomes a logistics failure, a package signed for by nobody. Laurence retains only the splinters of his part in the exchange, the recognition that Romeo "hath no notice of these accidents" (5.2) and the resolve to "write again to Mantua" (5.2), spoken as fragments of a plan the audience watches fail in cross-section. Lehmann's reading of the film fastens on precisely this substitution, and it is her essay's governing insight: in Verona Beach, Shakespeare's words themselves circulate as dead letters, dispatched with perfect fidelity and never received, which is also an exact description of what the film fears its audience will do with the verse.

The remainder of the wholesale deletions can be taken faster, because they share a profile. The musicians who attend Juliet's supposed corpse, and Peter's comedy with them (4.5), are gone; the play's strangest tonal swerve, practical men discussing music over a bier, has no heir in a film whose music comes from a soundtrack album rather than from hired hands. Lady Montague's death is gone: in the play Montague arrives at the tomb with the news that his wife "hath died this night" of grief for her exiled son (5.3), a third parental corpse that widens the catastrophe's account; the film's Caroline Montague survives to be chastised at the church door, and the account stays narrower, two children and no parent. The citizens, watchmen, pages and attendants who crowd the play's public scenes are dissolved into police and press. And in the final scene, as a later section of this article examines in detail, Paris's vigil at the tomb and his death at Romeo's hands are deleted entire, removing the play's third corpse from its last movement and the last obstacle between Romeo and the body of Juliet. Each deletion removes a person whose function was to slow the ending down and make it answer questions. The film's ending answers no questions. It is witnessed, at speed, and then it is on the news.

## The Gas-Station Brawl: Prose as the First Casualty

The play's first scene is a hundred lines of prose: two Capulet serving-men, Sampson and Gregory, trading puns about coal and colliers and choler, working themselves up to the insult that starts the war, a bitten thumb (1.1). It is funny, filthy and structurally essential, the feud rendered as something servants perform for sport before their masters arrive to make it policy. The film's first scene is the Sycamore Grove petrol station, a spaghetti-western standoff staged in freeze-frames and whip-pans, in which the Montague boys and Tybalt's Capulets face off over a dropped match and a forecourt slick with fuel. Of Shakespeare's hundred lines, a handful of shouted fragments survive, and the scene's meaning has migrated almost entirely into its choreography. The bitten thumb survives, as pure gesture, a quotation with the words stripped off. The punning duel that in the play escalates word by word until swords are drawn is replaced by a duel of looks, engines and spilled petrol, and the escalation is legible to any viewer alive without a single line being understood. This is the pattern the rest of the film obeys, visible here in its purest form because the source passage is the play's most disposable by reputation: when words were cut, they were cut from the bottom of the play's social order upward, and prose went before verse, servants before masters, jokes before curses.

The order of sacrifice is the finding. Stage directors cutting the play for the theatre have always known that its prose is softer tissue than its verse, and the series study of [cutting the text as directors practise it, what is lost and what the losses do](/2028/05/19/cutting-the-text-directors-choices/) documents the same hierarchy across three centuries of promptbooks: prose passages trimmed first, verse defended longest. Luhrmann's film carries the hierarchy to its limit and thereby reveals its logic. Prose in this play is the medium of the world's ordinary business, bargaining, gossip, arrangements, the Nurse's errands, the servants' sport, the musicians' complaints. Verse is the medium of the feud's public rituals and the lovers' private one. A film that keeps the rituals and the romance while deleting the business will look, from a distance, like the whole play, because the parts everyone remembers are the parts that survive. What has actually happened is a change of genre inside the adaptation: the play's Verona is a society, dense with transactions, and the film's Verona Beach is a stage on which two families and a pair of lovers perform, with the society replaced by its representations, news footage, police cordons, the logos of two corporations. The prose was the society. Cutting it did not shorten the play's world; it abolished the world and kept the duel.

One population of the scene vanishes so completely that its absence is easy to overlook: the crowd. Shakespeare's brawl draws in the citizenry, club-wielding householders who beat down both factions impartially and curse the feud from below, and their intervention is the play's first statement that the feud has a public, a city that suffers it and judges it in the street. The film's petrol station is emptied of citizens. Its public arrives as the play's does not, from above and afterward: helicopters, sirens, the police cordon, the news camera. Verona's crowd, the third party to every quarrel in the play, has been reassigned to the machinery of coverage, and the judgment the citizens once shouted in person is deferred to the broadcast that will open and close the story. The feud in the film is thus never opposed by the society it afflicts, because the society has been edited into an audience, and audiences, as the film's ending demonstrates, watch.

The brawl's ending shows the same reassignment at work in miniature. In the play the Prince arrives in person, halts the fight, and delivers the decree that sets the plot's legal machinery in motion, death to whoever disturbs the streets again (1.1). The film compresses the decree into the idiom of its substitute state: Captain Prince descends on the aftermath as a police chief descends, by vehicle and loudhailer, and the sentence is delivered as an order to a crowd rather than an address to a city. The words shrink; the function survives; the register changes from law to crowd control. Nothing in the film better illustrates the difference between its two fidelities. A transcript of the scene would scandalize a purist. A diagram of the scene's functions, threat, interruption, judgment, sentence, would show every box filled. Luhrmann's cutting is best understood as the keeping of diagrams and the discarding of transcripts, everywhere except the few places, the sonnet, the balcony, the curse, where the transcript itself is the event.

## Where Did the Comedy Go? The Nurse, the Servants and the Prose Stratum

The hierarchy of sacrifice has a human cost that the passage-by-passage record makes visible, and its name is the Nurse. In the play she is the third-largest presence in the lovers' world, and her material is the prose stratum at its richest: the long reminiscence in which she dates Juliet's childhood by an earthquake and a fall (1.3), the fencing match with Mercutio when she comes to find Romeo (2.4), the exquisite delay in which, returned from that errand, she tortures Juliet with her aches before surrendering the news (2.5). These speeches are the play's ground bass. They establish a Verona in which the tragedy is surrounded by appetite, memory and gossip, a world that finds the lovers' urgency funny because it has seen urgency before and buried it. The film reduces the Nurse's material to function. Miriam Margoyles plays her with a comic timing that survives in looks, wheezes and bustle, and her errands are all performed: she finds Romeo, she carries the ring, she discovers the body. But the reminiscence is cut to fragments, the fencing match is thinned to glances, and the great delay of the second act is compressed into a brief performance of breathlessness whose text has been stripped to its errand. What remains is a messenger. What is lost is the play's memory, the one voice that remembers Juliet as a child and can therefore measure what the five days have done.

The same reduction governs every servant in the film, and one scene shows the principle with diagrammatic clarity. Lady Capulet comes to Juliet's bedroom to announce the Paris match, and in the play she does it through the play's most sustained conceit of courtship as reading: Paris is a book, "this precious book of love, this unbound lover," whose face Juliet is invited to read over, finding delight writ there in a fair hand (1.3). The speech is the mother's whole character in a figure, a woman who experiences marriage as a text to be admired rather than a fate to be suffered. The film's version of the scene is brisk family business: Gloria Capulet, in full evening dress, delivers the news of the match as an instruction, with the Nurse in attendance, and the volume of young Paris's face is never opened. The conceit could not survive the film's method, because it is pure commentary, a metaphor sustained for its own beauty across a dozen lines, with no event inside it. Yet the cut has a consequence the film does not advertise. Shakespeare's Lady Capulet is culpable in a particular way: she has aestheticized her daughter's marriage into a book she will not read closely. The film's Gloria is culpable in a simpler way, as an accomplice of her husband's will. The prose and conceit stratum was where the play kept its adults' self-deceptions. Deleting it does not merely shorten the film; it simplifies the guilt.

Mercutio is the exception that proves the rule of the stratum, because his comedy survives at a rate no other comic material approaches, and the reason is instructive. Mercutio's wit is combat: his speeches are duels, his jokes are feints, and his death is a duel's consequence, so a film organized around event can keep him because his talk is already action. Perrineau's performance retains the Queen Mab frame, the banter of the beach, the curse, and turns the ball's entertainment into a full musical number, so that Mercutio is the one figure whose verbal energy the film amplifies rather than trims. Set his survival beside the Nurse's reduction and the selection principle stands out in its final form. It is not verse that the film protects, since the Nurse's prose and Mercutio's prose begin in the same register. It is eventfulness. Speech that fights, woos, curses, vows or kills is kept, in prose or verse alike. Speech that remembers, describes, compares or digresses is cut, in verse or prose alike. The play's own deepest division is not between its poetry and its prose but between its present tense and its past, and Luhrmann's film, whatever else it discarded, kept the present tense entire and abolished the past. Verona Beach has no history. It has only footage.

## The Sonnet Through the Aquarium Glass: What Was Kept Verbatim

Against the demolished strata stands the lyric core, and its survival rate is of a different order. The lovers meet at the Capulet ball across a fish tank, in a wash of blue light and slow motion, and the first thing they exchange, once the film has hurried them through the party's noise into the service lift, is the play's most formal structure entire: the shared sonnet of the first meeting, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand" (1.5), in which Romeo and Juliet complete each other's quatrains and trade the rhymes of pilgrims and saints until the form itself delivers the kiss. The sonnet is not sampled or modernized. It is kept, divided between the aquarium's dumbshow and the lift's dialogue, and the division is itself a reading of the poem: Shakespeare's sonnet is about hands and lips, about touch approaching by degrees of metaphor, and Luhrmann splits it at exactly its own joint, letting the eyes perform the first quatrain through glass and reserving the words for the moment the pair are enclosed and the metaphor can become the act. In a film accused of vandalism, this is scholarship of a high order, performed with a fish tank. The sonnet's survival also exposes the selection principle at its clearest. The passage is kept because it is an event, not an ornament: remove it and the meeting has no mechanism, the lovers no contract. Passages that are events survive the cutting of this film almost regardless of length. Passages that are commentary, however beautiful, do not.

The ball around the sonnet shows the same economy from the other side, because the time the film spends at the feast is time the cutting bought. Shakespeare's first-act feast is a compact machine: Capulet's welcome, the dance, Tybalt's recognition of a Montague voice, "This, by his voice, should be a Montague" (1.5), Capulet's restraint of his nephew, the meeting, the discovery of names. The film expands the machine into a set piece that runs for a substantial stretch of its first hour, a costume ball of fireworks, drag performance and choreographed excess, and it pays for the expansion by thinning the text that surrounds the sonnet to cues and fragments. Capulet's welcome becomes a host's circulation; Tybalt's recognition survives, because it is an event, a threat issued; Capulet's long self-restraint, the speech in which an old man remembers his own masking days and chooses the peace of his house over his nephew's honour, is compressed to a rebuke delivered in passing. The feast's spectacle is therefore not decoration added to the play from outside. It is the play's own material, converted: the lines in which the older generation explains itself have been exchanged for the sight of the older generation enjoying itself, and the audience learns what the Capulet world is worth, and what it costs to enter it, from the party's scale rather than from its master's apologia. Whether the exchange profits the tragedy is a fair quarrel. That it is an exchange, text out and image in, at a rate the breakdown can measure, is not.

The balcony scene obeys the same law at greater length. Relocated from an orchard wall to the Capulet mansion's courtyard and swimming pool, with Romeo climbing down through the trellis into the water and the lovers conducting their negotiation soaked, whispered and interrupted by a patrolling guard, the scene keeps the great majority of its text: the window speech, "But, soft! What light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2), the name speech, the exchange of vows, the thousand times good night. What is trimmed is the scene's connective tissue, and what is added is water, which does in the film what the play's darkness does in the theatre, it makes the lovers invisible to their world and visible to each other, and it converts Juliet's anxiety about the orchard's danger into a guard's torch beam sweeping the pool's edge. Luhrmann has said that everything in the film exists to clarify the language, and the pool is the clarification of this scene: an audience that cannot parse "I have no joy of this contract tonight" (2.2) can read two bodies in lit water, one of them fully dressed, as a contract being joyfully and dangerously made. The claim of clarification is the film's official theory of itself, and this scene is its best evidence, because here the image genuinely does translate rather than decorate. The verse is slowed, spaced, given air and echo, and the cutting rate of the film, frantic everywhere else, drops to the speed of speech. Where the play is most itself, the film stops cutting and listens.

The curse survives on the same terms. The beach fight of the third act, staged at Sycamore Grove beneath a ruined proscenium arch as a storm gathers, keeps the architecture of Mercutio's death entire: the wound under Romeo's arm, the jesting that curdles, and "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1) delivered not as an exit line but as the hinge of the film's second half, the moment the comedy's licence is revoked. Harold Perrineau's Mercutio dies in Romeo's arms on the sand, in the open, rather than being carried off to die out of sight as the text directs, and the change is again a clarification by staging: the film needs the curse witnessed at close quarters because the curse is the plot's true engine, the speech that converts a brawl into a tragedy. Around these peaks the pattern repeats with mechanical consistency. The aubade at dawn, "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5), survives in substance, with Romeo's exit from the bedroom ending in the pool below, the film's water signing the parting as it signed the meeting. Juliet's potion soliloquy is trimmed and its terrors, the vault, the bones, Tybalt's ghost, are handed to the camera as images intercut with her face. Speech by speech, the rule resolves: if the play's words at a given moment are the action, they stay; if they describe, explain, negotiate or remember, they are candidates for the knife, and the camera inherits their duties.

## The Soundtrack as Substitute Text: What the Songs Say Instead

A full accounting of the replacement system has to include the film's second substitute institution, because where the verse is removed the silence is rarely allowed to stand. It is scored. The play's own music is internal, a matter of sonnets, songs mentioned and dances described; the film replaces that internal music with a commercial soundtrack that is assigned, at the decisive moments, the duties of cut text. Mercutio's case is the clearest. The Queen Mab speech in the play performs a complete arc, from jest through invention to disturbance, inside a single voice. The film splits the arc between two media: the speech's trimmed frame is spoken in the driveway, and its energy, the rising hysteria that in the play frightens Romeo into interrupting, is discharged inside the party as a musical performance, Mercutio in drag singing "Young Hearts Run Free" to the room. The number is usually read as camp decoration. In the breakdown it is a transfer: the speech's second half, the part where fantasy curdles, has been converted into the idiom Verona Beach uses for curdled fantasy, a pop performance at once euphoric and desperate, and the transfer explains why the spoken Mab could be cut so short. Its remainder exists. It is on the soundtrack.

The love music works the same transfer in the opposite register. Under the aquarium meeting, the pool scene and the church, the film lays Des'ree's "Kissing You," a slow devotional ballad whose burden is a vow to love beyond the grave, and the song behaves in those scenes exactly as the play's lyric machinery behaves in the scenes Shakespeare wrote for it: it slows time, it suspends the plot, it tells the audience what the lovers cannot yet say to each other in full sentences. Where the text is kept, as in the sonnet and the balcony negotiation, the music withdraws to a murmur; where the text is cut or falls silent, as in the waking in the church, the ballad carries the meaning entire. The credits complete the pattern with the film's one commissioned act of aftermath. The play ends with an audit spoken by its ruler; the film ends with a couplet spoken by a newsreader and then, over the credits, Radiohead's "Exit Music (For a Film)," a song written for this ending, whose whole burden is escape from a house of violence and a wish for the lovers' peace. An epilogue has occurred. It has simply been outsourced to the recording industry, in the form Verona Beach's citizens would actually consume, and the audience that would not sit for the Prince's hundred lines sits happily for the song. The substitution is the film's method confessed in its own credits: every function of the play's text that the film removed, framing, commentary, lyric expansion, judgment, mourning, can be found somewhere in the finished work, wearing the clothes of newsprint, police procedure or pop music. Nothing was dropped. Everything was rehoused, and the rent, in meaning, varied by the house.

### What does the camera say at the points where the verse was removed?

It says the same things in a different grammar. The aquarium glass says what the sonnet says about partition, two creatures in one element who cannot yet touch; the pool says what the balcony scene says about exposure, a lit surface under surveillance; the body bags and the receding television say what the Prince's audit says about judgment.

The film's defenders call this translation and its detractors call it replacement, but the breakdown shows a consistent division of labour: wherever the camera takes over, the lost material is the play's commentary on the action, and wherever the verse is kept, the words are the action itself.

## Queen Mab on a Fingertip: Kept Speeches in New Places

Between the wholesale deletions and the intact peaks lies the film's most characteristic operation, the kept speech in a new place, cut down and re-sited until its meaning changes key. The Queen Mab speech is the famous case. In the play it belongs to the street outside the Capulet house, where Mercutio, invited guest and licensed wit, spins his set piece on the fairies' midwife for a lovesick Romeo who has dreamed a dream (1.4). The speech is long, some ninety lines of accelerating invention, and its dramatic point is its excess: Mercutio talks himself out of jest into genuine disturbance, and Romeo has to stop him. The film keeps the speech's location, the approach to the feast, and transforms everything else. Mercutio arrives at the mansion by car, dispenses the party invitations, and delivers a Mab cut to a fraction of its length as a kind of conjuring trick, and at the speech's most quoted measurement, the fairy "in shape no bigger than an agate-stone / On the fore-finger of an alderman" (1.4), he produces the speech's subject on his own fingertip: a tablet of ecstasy, heart-shaped, which Romeo duly swallows. The remainder of the set piece, the catalogue of dreamers, lawyers, ladies, soldiers, is mostly gone; what survives is the frame, "Queen Mab hath been with you" (1.4), the galloping, the curdling mood, and then the speech is finished off not by Romeo's interruption but by a song, Mercutio in drag inside the party performing "Young Hearts Run Free" as the drug takes hold. The operation deserves its fame because it is neither vandalism nor fidelity. It is a judgment about what the speech is for. Luhrmann decided that Mab's catalogue is expendable and her function is not: she is the spirit of involuntary fantasy, the thing that rides sleeping brains, and a heart-shaped tablet performs that function in Verona Beach with an economy the catalogue cannot match. The speech's text is cut by three quarters. The speech's argument, that dreams are a drug the mind takes whether it will or no, is arguably clearer than in any stage production that keeps every line.

The Friar's speeches show the same operation applied without the compensating invention, and the losses are graver. Laurence enters the play with a forty-line soliloquy on the earth's twofold bounty, herbs that heal and herbs that kill, the doctrine that governs everything he does with potions thereafter (2.3). The film reduces the soliloquy to a fragment, a single sentence of its doctrine, "mickle is the powerful grace that lies / In herbs, plants, stones, and their true qualities" (2.3), glimpsed as the priest moves among his plants. The character who results is a plot function with a collar: he marries, he schemes, he despairs, but the play's one philosopher of means and ends, the man whose botany is a moral system, has been edited out of his own scenes. The wedding scene keeps more, and keeps the right thing: the marriage itself is staged in the great church that will also house the deaths, and Laurence's warning survives in its sententious core, "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6), the play's thesis spoken over the kiss it prophesies. But the scene around the warning is compressed to the ceremony's bones, and the Friar's longer mediations, in the cell scene after the banishment, where he lectures a suicidal Romeo on philosophy as "adversity's sweet milk" (3.3), are cut to their practical residue: the plan, the night, the exile, the ring. The pattern is exact. Laurence keeps any line that operates the plot or states the theme in a sentence. He loses every line that thinks. A production could hardly make its priorities plainer: this film trusts speeches that do things and distrusts speeches that mean things at length, reserving its patience for the lovers, whose meaning and doing are the same act.

Rosaline is the limiting case of re-siting, a character reduced to a location. In the play she never appears, but she is argued over for two acts: Romeo's Petrarchan performance for her is the text's standing joke and its measure of his immaturity, and Benvolio's plot to cure him by comparison drives the visit to the feast (1.1, 1.2). The film compresses the whole Rosaline economy into imagery: Romeo alone at Sycamore Grove at the ruined beach theatre, smoking, filling a journal with verse and sketches, lovesickness as a visual condition requiring no name. Her name survives at most in passing talk among the Montague boys; her function, to be the before in Romeo's before-and-after, is performed by the beach, the notebook and DiCaprio's face. It is an efficient cut and a costly one, because the play's Romeo is defined by the speed of a substitution the audience has watched him perform in language, whereas the film's Romeo simply begins in the condition of a poet and then meets Juliet, and the substitution, the hinge the second Chorus existed to mark, happens inside the montage without commentary. The film is consistent even here: what the Chorus would have said, nothing says. The cut and the silence are the same decision.

One more re-siting deserves its sentence, because it is the hinge between the ball and the balcony and the film steps over it without a glance. In the play, Romeo leaves the feast and leaps the Capulet orchard wall, and Mercutio, searching for him in the dark with Benvolio, conjures him by the inventory of Rosaline's body, "I conjure thee by Rosaline's bright eyes" (2.1), a speech of bawdy necromancy that fixes the old love's name at the very moment the new one is about to eclipse it. The film cuts the conjuration entire and passes from the party's interior to the courtyard in a single movement, so that Romeo's whereabouts between the dance and the window are simply the mansion's geography. The loss is small in bulk and large in kind: the conjuration is the play's last public performance of the Rosaline fiction, the friends' agreed story about who Romeo is, and deleting it means the film's Romeo never has to be seen escaping his own reputation. He slips from feast to balcony as if the night had only one room. The cut is of a piece with the second Chorus's fate, since the conjuration and the Chorus do the same work from opposite sides of the wall, telling the audience that a substitution of loves has occurred. The film performs the substitution and deletes both of its announcements.

## The Apothecary Scene: A Long Scene Cut to Its Bargain

No passage in the film better repays a line-by-line comparison than the apothecary scene, because the play's version is itself a small essay on the film's theme, a scene about what poverty does to speech. Romeo, in Mantua, having heard from Balthasar that Juliet lies in the Capulet vault, speaks the tragedy's most famous act of defiance, "Is it e'en so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1), a line the film keeps entire and gives to DiCaprio kneeling in the dust of the trailer park, face turned up to a sky full of weather. Then the play slows down. Romeo remembers a man: sixteen lines of description, the apothecary's starved face, his needy shop, the tortoise shell and stuffed alligator and beggarly account of empty boxes, before the man is even summoned (5.1). The description is Romeo's, and it is the point: a mind in extremity reads the world's poverty as a text and draws the syllogism that will kill him, that a man this poor can be bought. The film deletes the description entire. Its apothecary, a dealer in a rundown room, is summoned without a portrait, and the syllogism is never spoken; the audience is shown a squalid interior for a few seconds and left to perform Romeo's reading for itself, which, in fairness, the production design makes easy. But something has been traded away that the film elsewhere refuses to trade: a speech that is an action, since Romeo's description of the shop is the moment he talks himself into the purchase. Here the camera's inheritance is a subtraction, and the scene that results is the film's method at its most exposed, brilliant and diminished at once.

The cut has a history that deepens its interest, because the passage Luhrmann deletes is the passage Shakespeare added. In Arthur Brooke's *Romeus and Juliet*, the poem from which the play was made, the whole apothecary episode is dispatched in some twenty-three lines: a poor man, a bargain, a vial. Shakespeare expanded the episode to nearly ninety lines, and the expansion is almost exactly the part the film removes, the remembered shop, the starved face, the moral reading Romeo performs on both. The adapter's knife, in other words, has found the playwright's seam. Luhrmann's scene is not a vandalized Shakespeare scene so much as the scene restored to its source's economy, Brooke's proportions with Shakespeare's dialogue, and the restoration exposes what Shakespeare's expansion was for. Brooke's Romeo buys poison the way a man buys rope, as an errand. Shakespeare's Romeo has to talk himself into the purchase, reading the shop as a sermon on the world that made it, and the film, by cutting the sermon, returns its Romeo to Brooke's simpler psychology: a man who has heard the news and requires an instrument. Whether that simpler Romeo is a loss depends on the value set on the play's habit of pausing at the brink to think. The film's whole method is the refusal of the pause, and nowhere is the refusal more legible, or more costly, than here, at the counter where the thinking was the tragedy in miniature.

What survives of the scene is its skeleton, and the skeleton survives verbatim, which is why the scene still lands. The haggling is kept in its essential exchanges: the dealer's refusal, "Such mortal drugs I have, but Mantua's law / Is death to any he that utters them" (5.1), in the film's geography re-pointed, as the next section's evidence shows; Romeo's counter, "The world is not thy friend, nor the world's law" (5.1); the capitulation that is the play's bleakest sentence about money, "My poverty, but not my will, consents" (5.1); Romeo's bleak answer, "I pay thy poverty, and not thy will" (5.1); the dealer's specification of the dose; and the first half of Romeo's great paradox, "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1). Then the cutting resumes mid-speech. The play's Romeo completes the paradox, "I sell thee poison; thou hast sold me none" (5.1), dismisses the man with a blessing, "Farewell. Buy food, and get thyself in flesh" (5.1), and names the vial "cordial and not poison" (5.1) as he turns for Verona. The film drops the completion, the blessing and the naming. Its Romeo pays, pockets the vial and goes, and the scene ends not on a verbal paradox but on the transaction's fact. The pattern could not be cleaner if it had been designed for a seminar: the lines that survive are the lines two people say to each other across a counter; the lines that die are the lines one of them says to himself, and to the audience, about what the counter means.

### What work does the deleted description of the shop do in the play?

It performs Romeo's reasoning in public. The sixteen lines are a syllogism spoken aloud: the shop is beggarly, its keeper starved, therefore his conscience is for sale, and Romeo talks himself across the counter before the man appears. The speech is the purchase happening in language before it happens in fact.

Delete it, and the purchase loses its deliberation. The film's Romeo does not reason his way to the counter; he arrives at it, as he arrives everywhere in the film's second half, carried by the plot's momentum, and the audience is denied the play's most uncomfortable insight into its hero, that his suicide is not a spasm but a conclusion, argued from premises about poverty and law that the scene makes him state in full.

The purchase itself, finally, has been moved, and the move leaves a fingerprint inside a kept line. In the play the poison is bought in Mantua, a jurisdiction away from the tomb, and the law the dealer fears is Mantua's law. The film relocates the transaction to the return journey's end, inside Verona Beach's own limits, and the line is re-pointed to match: its dealer pleads Verona's law as the bar to the sale, a single word exchanged. The relocation tightens the plot's geography, Romeo buys his death in the city where he will use it, and it tightens the theme, since the law that fails to keep poison from Romeo is the same law, in the same streets, that has failed to keep the peace all film long. It is the only kind of alteration the screenwriters permitted themselves, the word that had to change for the scene to be where they had put it.

## The Church Ending: Paris Deleted, Juliet Waking, the Audit in Fragments

The play's last scene is its longest, a public inquest conducted over three bodies in a tomb, and the film rebuilds it from the foundations. Begin with the place. Shakespeare's tomb is a burial vault, crowded with the ancestral Capulet dead and, by the scene's end, with Tybalt's fresh corpse and Paris's. Luhrmann's last scene is the great church of the wedding, entered by the same soaring vertical movement of camera that opened the marriage, its floor a field of candles and neon crosses, Juliet laid on a bier before the altar like a saint on a feast day. A scene-by-scene audit of the film's diction makes the substitution countable: the play's vocabulary of burial, grave, vault, tomb, the words with which its last act thinks about death, is spoken in the film almost not at all, the word grave surviving, by that audit's count, exactly once, against more than fifteen occurrences in the text. The tomb's population has been edited as firmly as its vocabulary. Tybalt's body is not there. Paris is not there. Nobody is there, and that emptiness is the precondition of everything the film does next, because the film's ending is not an inquest but an assignation: it clears the stage of every witness so that the lovers can be alone in a way the play never permits them.

Paris is the deletion that does the most work, and it is the deletion most often misreported, because the play's tomb fight is so famous that summaries of the film routinely import it from the text. In the play, Paris keeps a vigil at the tomb with flowers and sweet water, hears Romeo approach, takes him for a vandal come to desecrate the Capulet dead, and fights him; Romeo kills him, grants his dying request to be laid beside Juliet, and only then turns to the body (5.3). It is the tragedy's last and coldest killing, the one that makes Romeo a murderer at the threshold of his own suicide, and it costs the play's Romeo the audience's uncomplicated pity. Zeffirelli shot the fight and then removed it, reasoning that a Romeo who had killed Paris could not carry the emotion the suicide requires. Luhrmann never stages it at all. His Paris is last seen in the film's domestic sequences, a suitor processed by the Capulet parents; the church door is held not by a rival but by the state, Captain Prince's police, with whom Romeo fights his way in through a hostage standoff and a hail of covering fire. The substitution is exact in function and opposite in meaning. Paris at the door makes Romeo's entry a crime against a mourner; the police at the door make it a crime against an order, and a Romeo who outruns policemen remains a romantic fugitive where a Romeo who kills Paris becomes a killer. The film even enlarges a servant to fill the space the rival vacates: Balthasar, who in the play brings the news and vanishes, is in the film shown witnessing Juliet's funeral cortege and riding straight for Mantua, and at the church he serves as the decoy who draws the police pursuit away from his master, a promotion from messenger to accomplice that gives Romeo's entry its cover. The last act's personnel have been recast by function with an almost bureaucratic neatness: the mourner out, the policeman in, the servant promoted, and the Prince, as the audit's fate showed, divided between a captain on the ground and a voice on the television. The film protects its Romeo at the price of the play's moral arithmetic, and the protection is a textual decision before it is a visual one: a whole character's death, some thirty lines of vigil and challenge, is cut entire so that the final scene can be a duet.

Inside the church, the cutting follows the familiar law, with one enormous exception. Romeo's great speech over the body, the forty-line valediction that reads Juliet's face for signs of life, apologizes to Tybalt, and drinks to his love, is reduced to fragments, the farewell and the toast: "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3) survives, the reasoning around it does not. The exception is the timing of the waking. Shakespeare's Juliet wakes after Romeo is dead; the play is explicit that the lovers never see each other alive again, and the pathos is precisely the near-miss measured in minutes. The alteration that lets her wake in time descends not from Shakespeare but from David Garrick, whose 1748 stage version first allowed the lovers a final exchange in the tomb, an addition that held the stage for a century. Luhrmann keeps Garrick's timing and deletes Garrick's added dialogue. His Juliet stirs as the poison works; her hand moves; her eyes open; she touches Romeo's face, and he sees her, and understands, and dies, and the entire transaction, which Garrick needed lines to perform, is done by the camera in silence. Juliet's own final speech, the play's "O happy dagger" sequence in which she finds the sheath in her own breast (5.3), is cut entire: the film's Juliet says nothing more, takes Romeo's gun from his hand, and shoots herself, the dagger translated into the film's weapon-system and the speech translated into a wail and a shot. It is the film's boldest single exchange of word for image, and its most defensible, because the play's Juliet at this moment is beyond rhetoric in everything but the text's provision of it. What is indefensible, or at least what must be counted, is the sequel. The play spends its last hundred lines accounting for the catastrophe: the Friar's narrative, the letters produced, the fathers' handshake, the golden statues promised, the Prince's audit. The film spends none. The Friar's account is cut entire, the reconciliation never occurs, the statues are never promised, and the audit survives as the two fragments already traced, a policeman's "All are punished" over the body bags and a newsreader's couplet over the static. Verona Beach is denied the play's closing act of public speech, and the denial is the ending's meaning: a city that experiences its tragedy as content has no use for an inquest, and the lovers, who in the play are finally made into the city's story by the city's own voice, are in the film made into a broadcast by a voice that belongs to no one.

### Which single speech loses the most in the film?

The Friar's closing account of the plot: the long narrative in the tomb in which Laurence lays the whole machinery before the Prince, some forty lines of explanation, confession and evidence, the play's own summary of itself, delivered over the bodies it explains.

Nothing else of its size vanishes so completely. Other large speeches are trimmed, reassigned or fragmented; this one is abolished, because the film has already given its function to the news, and a story the audience has watched being reported cannot be reported again by a priest.

The point can be put as a ratio. The play ends by spending roughly a fifth of its final scene on the explanation of what has happened. The film ends by spending nothing on explanation at all, and the saving is structural, not incidental: explanation is the one commodity Verona Beach produces in surplus, from its own channels, and the film's ending trusts the audience to notice that the city's account of the lovers and the lovers themselves have parted company for good.

## Jill Bilcock's Second Cut: Editing as the Film's Metre

Everything described so far happened on paper, in the screenplay Pearce and Luhrmann built from the play. It happened again in the cutting room, because a screenplay's decisions about what survives can be unmade or sharpened by the rate at which the surviving material is allowed to pass. Jill Bilcock, the film's editor, BAFTA-nominated for her work on it, is the second cutter of this text, and her own account of the method is the most useful piece of evidence in the film's whole literature, because she describes the cutting not as violence but as music. Everything, she has said, is about rhythm; she and Luhrmann think in tapestry, trusting that an audience can register an image in a few frames and that the sum of the fragments, not any single shot, produces the dramatic effect. The pair had a private name for the style, the maximal cutting of the Red Curtain films, and whatever else the name suggests, its practitioner's gloss is metrical: the film is cut the way verse is scanned, in beats, and the beats are set by the text's own tempo map. This is verifiable from the breakdown. Where the play's business is public and procedural, the gas station, the decrees, the negotiations, the film cuts at its fastest, sometimes several shots to a line, as if the surviving fragments of prose and command were being hurried off the screen before they could slow the story. Where the play turns lyric, the aquarium, the pool, the church, Bilcock's cutting relaxes to the speed of breath, and shots are held past the point of information into the region of feeling. The film's pace is thus not a uniform MTV assault, as its first reviewers complained; it is a tempo map of the play itself, fast where Shakespeare is public, slow where he is intimate, and the editor, not the screenwriters, drew the map.

The tempo map can be read off the film's two set pieces of cutting, which stand at opposite ends of its scale. The opening sequence, bulletin, montage and petrol station, is cut at a rate that treats even Shakespeare's lines as footage: the Prologue's phrases flash as headlines at a speed set for recognition rather than reading, the brawl is assembled from freeze-frames, speeded gestures and inserts, and the surviving fragments of the first scene's dialogue land like sound effects inside a percussive track. The church sequence at the close is cut at the opposite extreme: long-held shots of candle fields and faces, a camera that drifts rather than darts, and the few surviving lines given room to decay into silence. Between those poles the film's middle movements grade the rate scene by scene, the ball brilliant and quick, the pool and the aubade progressively slower, as if the editing were a tide going out toward the tomb. The map is not a decoration laid over the adaptation. It is the adaptation's second screenplay, written in durations, and it consistently spends the film's scarcest resource, sustained attention, on exactly the passages the first screenplay chose to keep entire. Cutters and editor agree on the hierarchy; they merely enforce it in different currencies, lines on the page, seconds on the screen.

### Who cut this text twice, and where does each cutting survive?

Two cutters, at two benches. Pearce and Luhrmann cut the play on paper, in the screenplay, deciding which passages would exist at all; that cutting survives in print, in the published volume that binds their script beside the full text of the play.

Bilcock cut the result on film, deciding how long each surviving passage would be allowed to occupy the screen; that cutting survives only in the finished work, in the felt difference between a line hurried and a line held.

The distinction matters for the fidelity quarrel, because the two cuttings pull in opposite directions. The screenplay's cutting is what the film's detractors mean: it deletes, and its deletions are permanent and countable. The editor's cutting is what the film's defenders mean: it interprets, and its interpretations are the reason passages the screenplay kept at full length do not feel embalmed. A line can survive the first cutting entire and still be murdered by the second, rattled off in a montage; a line can be halved by the first and crowned by the second, held in a close-up until it becomes the film's centre. The balcony scene is the second case, the Friar's speeches the first. Any verdict on Luhrmann's textual method that attends to only one bench will be half a verdict, and most of the published quarrel is exactly that, scholars of the text reviewing the screenplay and reviewers of the film reviewing the rhythm, each party certain the other has missed the point. Both are right about their own bench. The film is the sum of the two cuttings, and its text has to be audited twice.

## Bate Against Anderegg: The Critics' Quarrel over the Verse

The quarrel over the film's text has a clean shape once the evidence is sorted by bench. At one pole stands Jonathan Bate, the play's editor and one of its most eminent living scholars, who in the film's own DVD materials calls it one of the great achievements of the age precisely because it "keeps the authentic text" while updating the setting, making Shakespeare familiar to a generation that would not otherwise have met him. At the opposite pole stands Michael Anderegg, the historian of Shakespeare on screen, who argues that the film's final movement sheds exactly those elements of the play that exhibit what he calls rhetorical excess or violations of decorum, the elements, that is, where the play is most literary and least naturalistic, and that a cinema which must delete the play's literariness to function has not translated the play but declined it. Between the poles stands Lehmann with her percentages, refusing the terms of both: the question is not whether the film keeps the authentic text, since authenticity is precisely what her count dissolves, Cukor keeping half, Zeffirelli two fifths, Luhrmann under a third, all of them speaking some of the words and none of them the play. Her own reading gives the dissolution a figure. In Verona Beach the text behaves like its own failed letter: written with care, dispatched with confidence, and delivered to an addressee who is not at home, so that the film becomes a study of Shakespeare's words in circulation, endlessly forwarded by newsreaders, policemen and lovers, and never quite received in full by anyone, including the audience, who are given the lovers' third in ravishing close-up and the city's two thirds not at all.

The breakdown assembled in this article adjudicates the quarrel, and it does not split the difference. Bate is right about the words and wrong about the text. Every line spoken in the film is authentic, and the discipline that guaranteed it, the refusal to write extra Shakespeare, is a genuine form of respect that neither Cukor's nor Zeffirelli's production imposed on itself; but a text is not a bag of lines, and the thing Bate praises, the authentic text, is the one thing the film most thoroughly does not keep, since its architecture, the Chorus's framing, the prose's society, the Friar's philosophy, Paris's death, the closing audit, has been demolished and its functions reassigned to institutions Shakespeare never imagined. Anderegg is right about the architecture and incomplete about the compensation. The literariness is indeed shed, and the shedding is systematic, not accidental; but the breakdown shows the shed material being replaced by a working equivalent more often than his charge allows, the sonnet's partition performed by glass, the audit's judgment performed by a broadcast, the curse's function preserved by a staging that puts the death in the open air. Where no equivalent is found, the Friar's thinking, the servants' world, the second Chorus's cold interpretation, the loss is real and should be recorded as loss, not laundered into translation. Lehmann's is the account the evidence supports, with one amendment drawn from the second bench: her count measures the screenplay, and the film an audience experiences is the screenplay as Bilcock timed it, so that the effective retention, the text as received, is higher at the lyric peaks than thirty per cent suggests and lower everywhere else. A fourth account deserves its place in the quarrel because it explains the selection from the market's side. Leah Guenther's reading of the film as a "Top 40" Shakespeare argues that Luhrmann treats the text the way radio treats a catalogue, programming the famous passages as hits for an audience of consumers who recognize them. The breakdown supports her further than she presses it: the kept column of the table is, passage for passage, the play's chart of excerpts, the sonnet, the window speech, the curse, the defiance of the stars, the very items an anthology prints in bold. But her consumption frame, adjudicated against the operations column, explains only the keeping. A hits programmer does not bother to reassign the Prologue to a newsreader or split the Prince's audit between a policeman and a broadcast; those are acts of interpretation, not of marketing, and they show the film thinking about the play's machinery even as it discards the machinery's parts. The film is a Top 40 compilation, yes, but one whose sequencing is an argument. Toby Malone's dramaturgical study of the film reaches a compatible conclusion from the production's side, tracing how the adaptation was built like a stage text even as it was shot like a video. The industry's own verdict deserves its footnote in the quarrel: the screenplay, this most radically cut of Shakespeare scripts, won the BAFTA for best adapted screenplay, an award for writing given to a text whose writing consisted, by its authors' own proud account, entirely of choosing, moving and deleting another writer's words. The butchers were honoured as authors. Shakespeare, unbilled in the category, supplied the meat.

## Verdict: Faithful Words, Rebuilt Architecture

The verdict follows from the ledger. Luhrmann's textual method is a translation conducted under a single rule of exchange: a passage of Shakespeare survives if it can be performed as an event between people in a room, and it is converted, reassigned or deleted if it functions as commentary, machinery or society. Judged by that rule the film is ruthlessly consistent, and its famous paradox, the kept language inside the radical cut, dissolves, because the language that is kept is the language of event and the language that is cut is the language of everything else. The comparison with the film's siblings fixes the method's place in the tradition. Zeffirelli cut less and rewrote more, smoothing the verse into a naturalism his young cast could carry; the result feels faithful and is, by Lehmann's count, only somewhat more complete. Carlo Carlei's later film, examined in the study of [Carlo Carlei's film and the Fellowes script](/2028/08/11/carlo-carlei-2013-film/), ran the experiment in mirror image, keeping the play's plot and pageantry while replacing much of its language with a screenwriter's approximation, and was savaged for exactly the half of the bargain Luhrmann refused. Between the two failures of nerve, the smoothing and the substituting, Luhrmann's is the only method that trusts its audience with the real sentences, and the trust is vindicated every time the film slows down: the sonnet behind glass, the vows in the pool, the curse on the sand are the scenes a generation remembers, and they are remembered in Shakespeare's words. Against them stands the cost, which this article has refused to minimize. A play is not only its events. The Chorus's cold eye, the second sonnet's judgment on desire's fickleness, the Friar's botany of means and ends, Paris's flowers at the tomb, the Prince's patient audit, these are the play's intelligence about itself, and the film deletes the intelligence and keeps the experience, wagering that a modern audience receives meaning through feeling faster than through commentary. The wager paid: the film grossed its fortune, revived Shakespeare as a commercial cinema property, and sent its audience back to the text in numbers no faithful production has matched, a consequence weighed in the debate over [which film version of the play is best](/2029/05/25/which-film-version-is-best/). The deepest consequence of the cutting is the one least often named, because it concerns not what the audience knows but how it knows. The play's framing machinery exists to keep the audience ahead of the lovers: the Chorus announces the deaths before the lovers meet, the Prince's decrees define the stakes before they are tested, the Friar's explanations run behind the action tidying its meaning, so that the spectator watches every joy under a settled interpretation and the tragedy's irony is an irony of knowledge possessed in full. The film keeps the Prologue and thereby keeps the facts, but it reassigns the Prologue to a newsreader and deletes every later act of interpretation, the second Chorus, the Friar's narrative, the audit in full session. The result is an audience that knows the ending and understands nothing in advance, that receives the catastrophe as the city receives it, as footage and then as a bulletin. Luhrmann's Verona Beach does not lack the play's dramatic irony; it redistributes it, from understanding to information. The lovers die, as the Chorus promised in the first minute, inside a story their world has already turned into content, and the audience's helplessness before that conversion, its inability to hear the play's own voices explain what it is watching, is the film's final and most faithful reproduction of the tragedy's effect, achieved by deleting the very passages that produce the effect in the theatre.

But the breakdown is the breakdown. What Luhrmann cut and kept was not the play abridged. It was the play disassembled into the parts a camera can love, and reassembled, brilliantly, in the order of feeling. The words are Shakespeare's. The architecture is Luhrmann's. The film's honesty, and its limit, is that it never pretended otherwise, except in its title.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who counted how much of the play survives in Luhrmann's film, and what did the count find?
The count most often cited in the scholarship is Courtney Lehmann's. Lehmann measured the major screen versions against the play's text and found that Cukor's 1936 film retains the largest share at roughly fifty per cent, Zeffirelli's 1968 film retains roughly forty per cent, and Luhrmann's film retains the least of the three at roughly thirty per cent, under a third of Shakespeare's lines. The finding matters because it reverses the usual assumption: the version that looks most reverent keeps less of the text than its reputation claims, while the version accused of vandalism keeps none of its own words at all, every spoken line being Shakespeare's. The percentage describes lines retained, not scenes, and the distribution is uneven, the lovers' scenes surviving at a far higher rate than the play's public and prose material.

### Q: Why did Luhrmann and Pearce call themselves the Butchers of the Bard?
The name was the screenwriters' own, adopted during the work in Miami where the screenplay was assembled on two computers in one room, and Luhrmann has recalled that they treated it as more than a joke, having hats made and feeling a genuine moral anxiety about altering what Shakespeare had put on the page. The anxiety shaped the method. Because they felt the weight of the text, they forbade themselves the adapter's usual escape, the quiet rewrite, and confined their work to cutting, pasting and moving, reducing passages or deleting them but never diluting them. The butcher's metaphor is exact: the animal is portioned, not replaced, and what reaches the table is the original meat in chosen cuts. The name also confesses the division of feeling that produced the method, reverence for the wording joined to ruthlessness toward the whole, and it turned out to be the film's most accurate piece of self-description, the one its critics and defenders both ended up quoting.

### Q: What happens to the Act 2 Chorus in the film?
It is cut entire. The play's second choral sonnet, which opens by registering that Romeo's old desire is dead and a new one has inherited its place, has no counterpart in the film: no character speaks it, no headline carries it, and no scene performs its work of pausing the action to interpret the lovers' haste. The film covers the joint with momentum, passing from the ball to the balcony inside a single rush of energy, and replaces the Chorus's interpretation with sheer sequence. The deletion is of a piece with the film's treatment of the first Chorus, whose lines survive only after being reassigned to a news broadcast. Verona Beach has institutions that report events; it has no voice that reflects on them, and the second Chorus is nothing but reflection.

### Q: Is Paris killed at the tomb in Luhrmann's film?
No. Paris's vigil at the tomb, his challenge to Romeo, the fight and his death, one of the play's most consequential episodes, are absent from the film entire, whatever summaries borrow the episode from the play and credit it to the screen. Luhrmann's Paris remains in the film's domestic plot as the parents' chosen suitor and never reaches the church as a mourner. The resistance at the church door is supplied instead by Captain Prince's police, against whom Romeo fights his way in. The change protects the hero: a Romeo who shoots his way past policemen stays a fugitive, while a Romeo who kills the unarmed Paris at his vigil becomes, at the threshold of his own suicide, a murderer, and the film was not willing to spend its audience's sympathy at that price. Zeffirelli had reached the same conclusion a generation earlier by the harder road, shooting the tomb fight and then removing it in the cutting room for the stated reason that a Romeo who had killed Paris could not carry the emotion the suicide requires.

### Q: Why does Juliet wake while Romeo is still alive in the film?
Because the film inherits an alteration older than cinema. Shakespeare's Juliet wakes after Romeo's death, and the lovers never see each other alive in the tomb. David Garrick's stage version of 1748 first changed the timing so that Juliet wakes in time for a final exchange, an addition that held the stage for generations because audiences demanded the meeting the play denies them. Luhrmann keeps Garrick's timing and deletes Garrick's added dialogue: his Juliet wakes as the poison works, opens her eyes, touches Romeo's face, and the recognition passes between them in silence before he dies. The moment is the film's method in one gesture, a stage adapter's structural inheritance rendered as pure image, with the words that once filled it cut away.

### Q: What replaces Friar John's failed journey in the film?
A courier. In the play, Friar Laurence entrusts the letter that would save the lovers to Friar John, who is detained in a plague house and returns with the explanation, so that the catastrophe has a mechanism and a witness. The film deletes Friar John and the plague, and sends the letter by overnight delivery to Romeo's trailer in the Mantua desert. The delivery is made to an empty home; Romeo is absent, the letter waits unopened, and Balthasar's news of Juliet's death reaches him first. The substitution modernizes the failure from disease to logistics, and it sharpens the tragedy's account of itself: in Verona Beach even a perfectly written, correctly addressed message fails, not because a friar was quarantined but because nobody was there to sign for it. The play's explanation takes a scene and two friars to deliver; the film's takes a single image, an envelope in an empty room, and the time saved is spent on the chase and the church, the places where the film prefers its meanings to happen.

### Q: How is the apothecary scene shortened, and which lines survive whole?
The scene's first half is deleted and its second half kept almost intact. Shakespeare gives Romeo a sixteen-line description of the apothecary and his needy shop, the tortoise, the alligator, the empty boxes, before the man is summoned; the film cuts the description entire and shows a squalid room for a few seconds instead. The bargain itself survives in its essential exchanges, spoken in Shakespeare's words: the dealer's refusal on grounds of the law, Romeo's answer that the world is not the dealer's friend, the capitulation "My poverty, but not my will, consents," the reply "I pay thy poverty, and not thy will," and the first half of the closing paradox about gold being worse poison than the compounds. What is cut from the bargain's end is its completion, Romeo's blessing of the man and his naming of the vial as a cordial: the film ends the scene on the transaction, not on the reflection.

### Q: Why does the film's apothecary speak of Verona's law rather than Mantua's?
Because the film has moved the purchase. In the play Romeo buys the poison in Mantua, where he is exiled, and the law the apothecary fears is Mantua's law, death to any who sell such drugs. Luhrmann relocates the transaction to the end of Romeo's return journey, inside Verona Beach's own jurisdiction, and the kept line is re-pointed to match, a single word exchanged so that the dealer pleads Verona's law. The change is the smallest in the film and one of the most revealing: it shows the screenwriters' rule operating at the level of the syllable, altering only what the scene's new geography forced them to alter, and it binds the poison purchase to the same failed legal order that has mismanaged the feud from the first scene onward.

### Q: How much of the balcony scene's text survives its move into the pool?
The great majority of it. The scene is re-sited, from the Capulet orchard to the mansion's courtyard, its terrace and its swimming pool, with Romeo descending through the planting into the water and the lovers conducting the negotiation soaked and whispering under the sweep of a security guard's patrol, but the text is trimmed rather than rewritten: the window speech, the speech on names, the exchange of vows and the parting all survive in Shakespeare's sentences, with the cuts falling on connective passages rather than on the scene's peaks. The relocation does the work that cutting does elsewhere. The water, the light and the risk of discovery carry the scene's meanings of exposure and haste, so the words that remain can be spoken slowly, and the scene becomes one of the film's stillest passages precisely because its staging is doing so much of its talking.

### Q: How is the lovers' shared sonnet divided between the aquarium and the lift?
The sonnet of the first meeting survives near-entire, and its division is a reading of its form. Shakespeare writes the meeting as a single sonnet shared between the lovers, its quatrains traded line by line on the metaphors of pilgrims, saints and prayer, ending in the kiss the form has been negotiating. Luhrmann splits the poem at its own joint. The first movement is played as dumbshow across the aquarium glass in the mansion's restroom corridor, where Romeo and Juliet see each other through water and glass and cannot speak, the partition of the metaphor made literal. The words are then delivered in the service lift, where the pair are enclosed and alone, and the sonnet's dialogue of hands and lips proceeds to its scripted conclusion. The poem is not sampled or updated; it is staged in two media, sight and speech, in the order the poem itself prescribes.

### Q: Who speaks the play's final lines in the film?
A newsreader. The play ends with the Prince's closing speech over the bodies, an audit that pardons some, punishes others and finishes with the couplet "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo." The film breaks that speech apart and gives its pieces to the two institutions that have replaced the play's public voices. Captain Prince speaks one fragment in person at the church, the words "All are punished," addressed to the assembled parents as the bodies are carried out. The closing couplet is reserved for the film's frame: the picture returns to the television set of the opening, receding this time into darkness, and the same newsreader who delivered the Prologue closes her broadcast with the Prince's epitaph before the set dissolves into static. The last voice of Shakespeare's Verona, in this Verona, is a broadcaster signing off.

### Q: What happens to Friar Laurence's long explanation at the end of the film?
It is abolished. In the play's final scene Laurence stands before the Prince and narrates the entire plot, the marriage, the potion, the failed letter, his own part in all of it, in a speech of some forty lines that functions as the tragedy's official account of itself. The film deletes the speech entire, along with the scene of judgment that contains it. No one explains the catastrophe to anyone. The Friar's narrative function is inherited by the closing news broadcast, which has been telling the story all along, and the difference is the film's final statement about its world: the play's Verona requires an explanation and assembles to hear one, while the film's Verona Beach requires only coverage, and gets it, in fragments, over images of body bags. Laurence is left with splinters of his part, recognitions without an audience to receive them.

### Q: Do the Montagues and Capulets reconcile on screen?
No. The play closes its feud in speech: the fathers shake hands over the bodies, each promises the other a golden statue, and the Prince dismisses a community formally, if gloomily, reconciled. The film stages none of it. Its families are last seen assembled outside the church as the bodies are carried to an ambulance, where Captain Prince chastises them with the fragment "All are punished," and the film then withdraws into its television frame. There is no handshake, no promise of statues, no joint grief performed as policy. The omission is one of the film's largest structural cuts and the easiest to miss, because the newsreader's closing couplet supplies an emotional full stop in place of the social one the play provides. What the audience feels as an ending is the broadcast's ending, not Verona's.

### Q: Does Lady Montague's death appear in the film?
No. In the play's final scene Montague arrives at the tomb with a second bereavement to report: his wife has died that night of grief for Romeo's exile, a death that widens the catastrophe's ledger to three parents' losses and makes the feud's cost generational in both directions. The film cuts the report entire. Its Lady Montague, Caroline, is present among the parents chastised at the church door, alive to hear the Captain's rebuke, and the ledger stays at the two children. The cut is characteristic of the film's handling of the final scene, which deletes every element of the play's closing account, the explanations, the judgments, the secondary deaths, the reconciliations, that would slow the movement from the lovers' bodies to the closing broadcast. The report is also the play's last widening of the tragedy's ledger, proof that the feud kills in both directions, parents by grief as surely as children by violence, and its deletion narrows the film's ending to a symmetrical pair of young corpses and a pair of scolded households.

### Q: Which scenes does the film cut entire rather than trim?
The list is longer than the film's reputation suggests. It includes the servant's invitation errand that in the play delivers Romeo to the feast; Friar John's mission and the plague house that explains the letter's failure; the second Chorus; the musicians and Peter at Juliet's supposed funeral; Paris's vigil, challenge, fight and death at the tomb; Lady Montague's reported death; Friar Laurence's closing narrative; and the reconciliation of the fathers with its promised statues. Around these wholesale deletions sits a second tier of scenes reduced to fragments, among them the Friar's opening soliloquy, the servants' opening brawl and Romeo's description of the apothecary's shop. The pattern in both tiers is consistent: what vanishes is machinery, commentary and society, and what survives is encounter. Even the play's smallest public figures meet the same fate: the watchmen who discover the bodies and Paris's page who flees the tomb are absorbed into the film's police cordon and camera crews, witnesses converted into personnel.

### Q: How does the film handle the text of the wedding scene?
It keeps the scene's thesis and compresses everything around it. The marriage is staged in the great church, the same space that will receive the lovers' bodies, under the identical soaring camera movement, so that the building itself rhymes the two ceremonies. Of Laurence's part, the warning survives in its core: "These violent delights have violent ends," with its figure of fire and powder consuming as they kiss, spoken over the union it prophesies. The surrounding material, the Friar's cautions about haste, the lovers' exchanges before the ceremony, the scene's reasoning about why the marriage might reconcile the houses, is cut to the ceremony's bones. The result concentrates the play's own irony: an audience hears the tragedy's thesis stated in full at the wedding and then watches the film spend its remaining hour proving the sentence true.

### Q: Where can a reader set the film's text beside the play's, line by line?
In the published volume issued with the film, which prints Pearce and Luhrmann's screenplay together with the complete text of the play in a single book, with an introduction by the director. The pairing was a deliberate act of confidence: the adaptation invites the comparison that exposes it, and the volume remains the quickest instrument for verifying any claim about the cutting, since the film's retained lines and the play's full text sit pages apart. Students of the adaptation use it to do what this article's breakdown does, passage by passage, and its existence underwrites the film's central claim about itself. A production that had rewritten Shakespeare would not have published the evidence; this one bound the evidence into the same covers as the original and sold them together.

### Q: What did Jill Bilcock say her editing was listening to?
Music. Bilcock, the film's editor, has described the cutting of the Luhrmann films as a matter of rhythm before everything: she and the director think of the film as a tapestry whose effect is the sum of its fragments, trusting an audience to register an image in a few frames and building dramatic emotion out of accumulation and beat rather than out of duration. Her account explains the film's otherwise puzzling tempo map, its fastest cutting in the public scenes and its sudden stillness at the lyric peaks. The verse is not being assaulted by the editing; it is being conducted by it, hurried where the play is procedural and held where the play sings, and the editor's bench is where that second, interpretive cutting of the text was performed. Her gloss on the method is the cutting room's version of the screenwriters' rule: nothing is added there either, and everything depends on the order, the beat and the duration of what remains.

### Q: Why does the screenplay's BAFTA matter to the argument about fidelity?
Because the industry's highest honour for screenwriting was given to a text whose authors insist they wrote none of it. Pearce and Luhrmann won the BAFTA for best adapted screenplay for a script assembled entirely by selecting, reordering and deleting Shakespeare's lines, a fact that crystallizes the film's whole textual question. If the script is a work of writing, then cutting at this scale is authorship, and the film's departures from the play are a writer's choices to be judged as writing. If it is not, the award is a category error. The breakdown in this article supports the first reading: the pattern of the cuts is too consistent, too legible as an interpretation, the lovers kept, the city dismissed, the storyteller replaced by television, to be anything but a authored reading of the play, executed with a knife instead of a pen.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Luhrmann-text breakdown to argue that the film keeps Shakespeare's diction and rebuilds his architecture?
Begin from the table's three columns of operation. The kept column, the sonnet, the balcony, the curse, the defiance of the stars, establishes the diction claim: at the level of the sentence the film is the most faithful of the major versions, speaking only Shakespeare's words and keeping his hardest passages near-entire. The cut and reassigned columns establish the architecture claim: the Chorus, the prose society, the Friar's philosophy, Paris's death and the closing audit are deleted or handed to television and police, so the play's structure of framing, commentary and judgment has been rebuilt in other materials. An essay that assigns one paragraph to each column, with Lehmann's percentages as the frame and the apothecary scene as the worked example, can sustain the double verdict without contradiction: the fidelity and the vandalism are both real, and they operate at different heights of the same building.
