On 6 December 1992, at Ayodhya in Uttar Pradesh, a crowd of kar sevaks demolished the Babri Masjid, the mosque built in 1528 and 1529 by Mir Baqi, a commander of the Mughal emperor Babur, on the site that the Ram Janmabhoomi movement claimed as the birthplace of the god Ram. The 1992 Babri Masjid demolition is usually filed under two headings: crime or consequence. The criminal-legal heading follows the Liberhan Commission, the chargesheets and the acquittals. The political-consequences heading follows the riots, the dismissed state governments and the Bharatiya Janata Party's electoral graph. Both headings are accurate and both are incomplete, because the demolition's deepest effect was architectural. It ended, legibly and in a single afternoon, the movement phase of Hindutva that had run from the Ram Shila Pujan of 1989 through the Rath Yatra of 1990, and it opened the institutional-electoral phase that governed the Sangh Parivar's conduct for the following twenty-two years, until the integrated renegotiation after 2014 reactivated mobilisation through different machinery.

The claim defended here is precise. The 1992 Babri demolition was the legible end of Hindutva's movement phase; the post-1992 institutional-electoral phase operated for twenty-two years before the post-2014 reactivation. Legible is the operative word. Phases of a political movement rarely announce their own endings; parties continue to use movement vocabulary long after movement methods have been retired, and historians argue over terminal dates for decades. The afternoon of 6 December 1992 supplied its own punctuation. A mobilisation architecture assembled over three years, the largest the Sangh Parivar had ever fielded, reached its stated object and destroyed it, and in doing so destroyed the conditions of its own continuation. The criminal cases that followed constrained named leaders for twenty-eight years. The federal reaction dismissed four Bharatiya Janata Party governments and demonstrated that no state administration aligned with the movement could shield a movement act. The diplomatic reaction taxed Indian foreign policy for a decade. And the cadre base that had converged on Ayodhya dispersed into a landscape in which mass mobilisation no longer had an object, a calendar or an institutional home. What replaced the movement was not quietism. It was a different machine: coalition architecture, agenda deferral under alliance discipline, and organisational expansion through state-level electoral consolidation, tested in the thirteen-day government of May 1996, operationalised in the National Democratic Alliance of 1998, and sustained until 2014. The thirty-two-year arc from the consecrated bricks of 1989 to the temple consecration of 22 January 2024 is the evidence. Read whole, it shows a phase transition, not a pause.

## 6 December 1992: the day the structure fell

The day's chronology is documented to the hour by the Liberhan Commission's record, by the Supreme Court's observations in the title litigation, and by the press corps that watched from the terraces of Ayodhya. The kar sevaks, Hindu volunteers mobilised by the Vishwa Hindu Parishad, the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh and the Bharatiya Janata Party, had been summoned for a kar seva that the organisers had repeatedly described as symbolic: prayers, devotional singing and a token clearing of the ground on the land the Union government had acquired around the disputed plot. Estimates of the crowd vary, and the variation itself is part of the record; the Liberhan Commission and most contemporary reporting place the gathering at 150,000 or above, and some accounts run to 200,000. What the Supreme Court had authorised was narrow. By its order of late November 1992, the Court permitted a symbolic kar seva on the acquired land and recorded the assurance of the Uttar Pradesh government, sworn in an affidavit by Chief Minister Kalyan Singh's administration, that the disputed structure would be protected and that the Court's earlier status quo orders would be honoured. Kalyan Singh had given parallel assurances to the National Integration Council. The state police and the Provincial Armed Constabulary held the cordons; roughly twenty thousand central paramilitary personnel stood in the Ayodhya and Faizabad area, deployed, as the Liberhan Commission later established with some bitterness, at a distance from the site and under the state government's operational control.

The breach came around midday. A kar sevak climbed onto one of the mosque's three domes, the cordons gave way in sequence, and parties of kar sevaks with ropes, hammers, grappling irons and pickaxes began the methodical reduction of a masonry structure that had stood for 464 years. The leaders of the movement, L.K. Advani, Murli Manohar Joshi, Uma Bharti, Ashok Singhal and Vinay Katiyar among them, were seated at the Ram Katha Kunj platform a short distance away. Their conduct on that platform became the central evidentiary contest of the next three decades. The leaders maintained, in testimony and in public, that they appealed for restraint and that the crowd escaped their control. The Liberhan Commission, examining the video record, the sequence of speeches and the logistics of the gathering, concluded that the mobilisation had been orchestrated and that the leadership's posture of helplessness was a construction. The first dome fell in the early afternoon; by approximately 4:55 in the evening, on the timeline the Commission reconstructed, the third dome had come down and the structure had been levelled. Before the debris settled, kar sevaks removed the idols of Ram Lalla from beneath the central dome, preserved the cash box from the sanctum, and, as darkness fell, reinstalled the idols under a makeshift canopy on the levelled platform. A makeshift temple stood on the site by the following morning, and worship at it continued, under judicial supervision and from behind barricades, for twenty-seven years.

The political sequence of that evening was as compressed as the afternoon. Kalyan Singh resigned as chief minister, accepting, in his own later formulations, moral and constitutional responsibility for the failure to protect the structure his government had sworn to protect. The Union government of P.V. Narasimha Rao imposed President's Rule in Uttar Pradesh the same night. In Ayodhya the district administration declared a curfew over a town in which the surviving Muslim neighbourhoods had already begun to empty, and in which, the record of the following days shows, Muslim homes were attacked as the kar sevaks dispersed. Advani, arrested with other leaders in the days that followed, called 6 December the saddest day of his life, a formulation he repeated in Parliament when the Liberhan report was tabled in November 2009. The sentence has been read as remorse, as positioning and as evidence, and the three readings map almost exactly onto the three interpretations this article must adjudicate.

## The fortnight after: dismissals, bans and the violence across India

The Union government's response unfolded in three registers, and each register taught the Sangh Parivar a distinct lesson about the costs of movement methods. The first register was federal and immediate. On 15 December 1992, nine days after the demolition, the Rao government dismissed the remaining three Bharatiya Janata Party state governments, in Madhya Pradesh under Sunderlal Patwa, in Rajasthan under Bhairon Singh Shekhawat and in Himachal Pradesh under Shanta Kumar, dissolved their assemblies and imposed President's Rule. Governments that had committed no act comparable to Ayodhya were removed on the ground that their ruling party's ideological family had presided over the demolition, a use of Article 356 whose breadth the Supreme Court's Bommai judgment of March 1994 would later curtail. The second register was prohibitory. On 10 December 1992 the Union government declared the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad and the Bajrang Dal unlawful associations under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, alongside two Muslim organisations. The Unlawful Activities Tribunal under Justice P.K. Bahri reviewed the declarations in 1993 and split them: the ban on the Vishwa Hindu Parishad was confirmed, and the declarations against the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh and the Bajrang Dal were cancelled in June 1993 for want of sufficient cause. The third register was parliamentary. In the Lok Sabha debates of December 1992 and the opening weeks of 1993, Rao defended his government's refusal to dismiss the Uttar Pradesh government pre-emptively, pledged that the mosque would be rebuilt, and faced an opposition that charged him, depending on the bench, with complicity through inaction or with betrayal through excess. The debates are the documentary spine of [the Rao government's response context](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), and their unresolved quality, a government explaining why it had watched a sworn assurance collapse on live television, shadowed the Congress for a generation.

The violence that followed the demolition was the grimmest entry in the ledger, and its figures must be stated with their sources and their disputes attached. Riots broke out in Ayodhya itself, in Mumbai, Surat, Ahmedabad, Kanpur, Delhi, Bhopal and dozens of smaller towns. The standard consolidated counts, carried in the reference literature and the press record of the period, place the deaths across India at more than two thousand, the large majority of them Muslims; no official all-India enumeration was ever published, and the total remains an estimate rather than a count, a distinction historians of the violence insist on. For Mumbai, where the rioting ran in two phases across December 1992 and January 1993, the Srikrishna Commission supplied the authoritative ledger: nine hundred dead, of whom 575 were Muslims, 275 Hindus and 50 recorded as others, with 2,036 injured and thousands displaced into relief camps. Justice B.N. Srikrishna's report, submitted in February 1998, documented the role of the Shiv Sena's organisational machinery in the January phase and the communal bias of sections of the Mumbai police, findings the Maharashtra government of the day accepted only in part. On 12 March 1993, ten weeks after the January riots, a series of bomb blasts across Mumbai killed 257 people; the prosecution case in the blasts trial, sustained through the courts up to the Supreme Court's 2013 disposition of the appeals, held that the attacks were organised by the Dawood Ibrahim syndicate in retaliation for the riots. The chain from a demolished mosque in Ayodhya to bomb blasts in Mumbai in fifteen weeks is the political-consequences reading at its strongest, and any account of the period that treats the demolition as a closed event fails on this evidence alone. The blasts case itself ran the Indian legal system's full course: the special TADA court's convictions of one hundred accused between 2006 and 2007, the Supreme Court's confirmation of most convictions in March 2013, and the execution of Yakub Memon on 30 July 2015, the only conspirator hanged, while Tiger Memon and Dawood Ibrahim, named by the prosecution as the plot's organisers across the border, were never tried. The demolition's violence ledger thus has entries in three decades and in every institutional register the republic possesses, riot, terror prosecution and capital sentence, which is why the consequences reading, whatever its limits as an explanation of the Sangh Parivar's behaviour, stands permanently against any treatment of 6 December 1992 as a self-contained day.

The international reaction formed the third cost surface. The Organisation of the Islamic Conference passed resolutions condemning the demolition and questioning the Indian state's protection of its Muslim minority. Pakistan's government under Nawaz Sharif mounted a sustained diplomatic campaign, mosques and Hindu temples were attacked in Pakistan and Bangladesh in retaliatory violence, and the Western capitals whose partnership the Rao government was courting for its economic opening issued statements of censure that New Delhi received as intrusions. The constraint was real but diffuse: for roughly a decade, every Indian diplomatic initiative in the Islamic world and every Indian claim to a secular-democratic exception in South Asia carried the demolition as a footnote that interlocutors could cite. The movement had optimised for a domestic audience; the bill arrived in a currency the movement did not control.

## Why New Delhi did not act first: the Rao government's calculus

The question the fortnight's debates could not settle, and which the Liberhan Commission later circled with evident care, is why a Union government that had stationed some twenty thousand central paramilitary personnel in the Ayodhya and Faizabad area left them under the operational direction of the very state government whose assurances were collapsing. The documentary record supports a layered answer, and the layers matter for the phase argument, because they show the institutional order pricing the movement before the movement reached the domes. The first layer was constitutional convention as the Rao government read it: law and order was a state subject, the Uttar Pradesh government had sworn an affidavit to the Supreme Court and given assurances to the National Integration Council, and a pre-emptive dismissal under Article 356 against a government that had not yet failed would have been struck at, in Parliament and possibly in court, as the Centre punishing an opposition state for a crime not yet committed. The second layer was the firing calculus. The precedent of October and November 1990, when Mulayam Singh Yadav's police fired on kar sevaks and the deaths entered the movement's martyrology, taught every decision-maker in Delhi that a central intervention which ended in the shooting of Hindu volunteers on live television could produce the very conflagration it aimed to prevent, at a scale the state might not contain. The third layer was Rao's own political method, the studied ambiguity that had carried him through the 1991 reforms: he disbelieved Kalyan Singh's assurances privately, as his later interlocutors recorded, yet calculated that a Bharatiya Janata Party government destroying a mosque under its own hand would destroy itself with Indian and world opinion, while a Congress government firing on kar sevaks would own the catastrophe alone. The calculation was cold, it was coherent, and it was wrong in the one particular that mattered, because it assumed the state government's machinery would at minimum slow the crowd, whereas the Liberhan record shows the machinery hobbled by design, its officers instructed in terms that reached the crowd as a promise of impunity. Home Minister S.B. Chavan carried the government's explanations in Parliament; the prime minister carried the pledge that the mosque would be rebuilt; and the Congress carried the political cost into the 1993 assembly elections and beyond, its Muslim constituency in the north reading December 1992 as the party's abdication and its Hindu constituency reading the rebuild pledge as appeasement, a double defection that the institutional phase's beneficiaries did not have to engineer because their opponent had engineered it for them.

## Ayodhya before the movement: a dispute the state kept freezing

The site the kar sevaks levelled carried a biography that explains both the movement's resonance and the courts' paralysis. The Babri Masjid was raised in 1528 and 1529 by Mir Baqi, a noble of Babur, the first Mughal emperor; Hindu tradition held the spot to be the janmasthan, the birthplace of Ram, and worship at the Ram Chabutra, a platform in the outer courtyard, coexisted with the mosque's use across the centuries of Mughal, Nawabi and early British rule. The violence of 1855 between Hindus and Muslims around the adjacent Hanumangarhi fixed the colonial settlement that endured until 1949: a railing divided the inner courtyard, where Muslims prayed, from the outer grounds, where Hindus worshipped, and the British administration recorded its preference for a dispute kept frozen over a dispute decided. The first title litigation, Mahant Raghubar Das's suit of 1885 seeking to build a temple on the Chabutra, was dismissed by the Faizabad sub-judge and again on appeal, the colonial courts declining to disturb a possession they could not safely rearrange. Communal rioting in Ayodhya in 1934 damaged the mosque, which the colonial government repaired at the town's expense. The settlement's logic, freeze and fence, survived independence intact, and the Congress governments of the 1950s inherited a site at which the Indian republic's secularism was administered as a lock.

The night of 22 and 23 December 1949 broke the freeze without deciding anything. Idols of Ram Lalla were placed inside the mosque, by persons never conclusively identified in any proceeding, though the period's reporting and the later record named activists of the Hindu Mahasabha's Ayodhya circle; the district administration under Collector K.K.K. Nair declined to remove them against the gathering crowd, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru pressed the Uttar Pradesh government of Govind Ballabh Pant for restoration, and the state's answer was the attachment of the property, the appointment of a receiver and the locking of the gates against both congregations. The Faizabad courts' injunctions of 1950 and 1951 converted the fait accompli into a legal regime: the idols would remain, a priest would worship, the public would be barred, and the title suits filed between 1950 and 1961 would be heard at the pace the Indian civil process reserves for disputes it does not wish to finish. For thirty-six years the regime held, a mosque that no Muslim could enter, a temple that admitted one priest a day, a god who was a litigant's ward before he was a litigant. The Muslim litigants' organisations, the Babri Masjid Action Committee formed after 1986 and its rivals, fought the case through committees and courts; the Hindu side's advantage, organisation in depth around a single demand, accumulated quietly in the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's parishes. When District Judge K.M. Pandey ordered the locks opened on 1 February 1986, in a proceeding whose haste the Allahabad High Court later noted without undoing, the frozen dispute thawed into the movement phase within three years. The prehistory carries the phase argument's premise: by 1989 the Ayodhya question had exhausted every custodian except mass politics, the executive having refused it, the legislature having evaded it and the judiciary having refrigerated it. The movement did not create the vacancy. It occupied it.

## The movement phase, 1989 to 1992: mobilisation at full stretch

The demolition cannot be read as an isolated afternoon, because the architecture that produced it had been under construction for three years and its blueprints reach back further. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad, founded in 1964 as the Sangh Parivar's religious-organisational arm, took up the Ram Janmabhoomi cause in earnest at its Dharma Sansad of April 1984, which demanded the liberation of the birthplaces of Ram at Ayodhya, Krishna at Mathura and Shiva at Kashi. The immediate Ayodhya sequence began in the courts and in Faizabad's streets: on 1 February 1986, District Judge K.M. Pandey ordered the locks on the Babri Masjid's gates opened to Hindu worshippers, reversing the closure that had held since idols of Ram Lalla were placed inside the mosque on the night of 22 and 23 December 1949. The order converted a litigation into a congregation. The Shah Bano judgment of 1985 and the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act of 1986, which the Rajiv Gandhi government passed to override it, had already handed the Sangh Parivar its symmetry argument: a state that legislated for Muslim personal law, the argument ran, while a Hindu god waited behind a lock, was a state whose secularism ran in one direction. The movement phase proper opened in the autumn of 1989 with the Ram Shila Pujan, in which consecrated bricks inscribed with Ram's name were processed through villages across India, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad claiming participation from some two hundred thousand settlements, and carried to Ayodhya for the shilanyas, the foundation-stone ceremony of 10 November 1989, performed on land adjacent to the disputed plot after negotiation with the Rajiv Gandhi government. Rajiv Gandhi had launched the Congress election campaign of 1989 from Faizabad with the promise of Ram Rajya. The Bharatiya Janata Party, whose Palampur resolution of June 1989 had committed it to the temple's construction, rose from 85 Lok Sabha seats in 1989 to 120 in 1991, and took power in Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh in the state elections of 1990 and 1991.

### How many kar sevaks gathered at Ayodhya on 6 December 1992?

Estimates of the crowd converge on a range rather than a figure. The Liberhan Commission's record and the contemporary press place the kar sevaks at 150,000 or above, with some accounts running to 200,000, drawn from across India by the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's state and district machinery over several weeks of organised travel.

Between preparation and consummation stood activation: [the 1990 Rath Yatra as movement-phase activation](/2019/10/10/advani-rath-yatra-ayodhya-movement/) converted a temple campaign into a national procession. Advani's Toyota-converted rath rolled from Somnath on 25 September 1990, was halted by his arrest at Samastipur on 23 October 1990 on the orders of Bihar's chief minister Lalu Prasad Yadav, and brought down the V.P. Singh government when the Bharatiya Janata Party withdrew its parliamentary prop and Singh lost the confidence vote of 7 November 1990. The kar seva of 30 October 1990 had already tested the Ayodhya cordons: kar sevaks reached the site, hoisted saffron flags on the domes, and were fired on by the police of Mulayam Singh Yadav's Uttar Pradesh government on 30 October and again on 2 November 1990. The official toll of the firings was sixteen dead; the movement disputed the figure and claimed many more, and the dispute over the number became itself a mobilisation asset, carried in the movement's literature as the martyrology of the Ayodhya road. By December 1992 the movement phase had produced four institutional consequences that together constituted its architecture. It had built a mass-mobilisation machine able to move six-figure crowds across state lines on a religious calendar. It had routinised cadre-mass coordination, the relay between the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's shakha network, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's religious constituency and the Bharatiya Janata Party's electoral organisation. It had aligned Hindutva state governments, above all Kalyan Singh's in Uttar Pradesh, with movement objectives, blurring the line between administration and campaign. And it had integrated movement and electoral politics, so that temple mobilisation and vote mobilisation fed the same organisational reservoir. The demolition tested all four consequences in one afternoon, and the tests returned failures that the following sections itemise. The run-up of 1991 and 1992 had already displayed the alignment's character in miniature. In October 1991, Kalyan Singh's government acquired some 2.77 acres of land adjoining the disputed plot, announcing a tourism and pilgrim-facilities purpose that the movement's supporters read, and its opponents charged, as the preparation of the temple's ground; the Allahabad High Court eventually quashed the acquisition, in December 1992, when the question had been overtaken. In July 1992, workmen on the acquired land began levelling and platform construction that the Supreme Court, seized of the status quo, viewed with open displeasure, extracting explanations and expressions of regret from the state government in a dress rehearsal of the December confrontation between the Court's paper orders and the site's physical facts. And in the last week of October 1992, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's Dharma Sansad at Delhi fixed 6 December as the date for the kar seva's resumption, converting the winter calendar into a countdown that every institution in the republic, the prime minister's office, the home ministry, the Supreme Court's bench and the state secretariat at Lucknow, could read and none could stop. The movement phase's final six weeks were thus conducted in the open, under negotiation, litigation and televised preparation, which is what gives the demolition its peculiar institutional quality: it was the most announced catastrophe in the republic's history.

## Cost one: seventeen years of inquiry, twenty-eight years of prosecution

The first cost the demolition imposed was legal, and it was levied on named individuals rather than on the movement in the abstract. Ten days after the event, on 16 December 1992, the Union Home Ministry constituted a one-man commission of inquiry under Justice Manmohan Singh Liberhan, a retired judge of the Punjab and Haryana High Court, to establish the sequence of events at Ayodhya and the roles of the persons and governments involved. The commission's own biography became an argument about the Indian state's capacity to process the event: its term was extended forty-eight times, it held 399 sittings, it examined the prime minister of the day, the home minister, the chief minister of Uttar Pradesh and the entire senior leadership of the Sangh Parivar, and it submitted its 1,029-page report to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh on 30 June 2009, sixteen and a half years after appointment. The report was tabled in Parliament on 24 November 2009. Its conclusions were unsparing. The events of 6 December 1992 were, in the commission's formulation, neither spontaneous nor unplanned; the mobilisation of the kar sevaks was neither spontaneous nor voluntary but well orchestrated and planned; the demolition itself bore the marks of painstaking preparation, from the removal and preservation of the idols and the cash box to the availability of demolition implements and the speed with which the makeshift temple rose. The commission listed sixty-eight persons as culpable of leading the country to the brink of communal discord, a roster running from the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's Ashok Singhal and the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's K.S. Sudarshan to the Bharatiya Janata Party's Advani, Joshi, Uma Bharti, Vijayraje Scindia and Kalyan Singh. Atal Bihari Vajpayee, Advani and Joshi were characterised as pseudo-moderates, leaders whom the commission held to have been privy to the enterprise while cultivating a posture of distance from it. The Uttar Pradesh government of Kalyan Singh was found to have colluded in a duplicitous and underhanded manner, its police hobbled by instructions that leaked into public knowledge and thereby guaranteed the kar sevaks a free hand. The commission was markedly gentler with the Rao government, whose failure it treated as one of judgment rather than design, a distribution of blame that critics of the report, including its own assisting counsel Anupam Gupta, who resigned in September 2007 in protest at its handling of Advani, attacked from opposite directions.

### How long did the Liberhan Commission take to report?

The commission was appointed on 16 December 1992 with an initial mandate of three months, received forty-eight extensions, held 399 sittings and submitted its 1,029-page report to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh on 30 June 2009, sixteen and a half years after the demolition it was charged to explain.

The criminal process ran on a separate, slower track, and its endpoint inverted the commission's. Two criminal cases were registered on 6 December 1992: one against the unnamed mass of kar sevaks for the demolition itself, and one against eight named persons, including Advani, Joshi, Bharti, Singhal and Katiyar, for inflammatory speeches and conspiracy. The Central Bureau of Investigation's composite chargesheet of October 1993 fused the tracks into a single conspiracy case. Thereafter the proceedings fragmented across courts in Lalitpur, Rae Bareli and Lucknow, were stayed, revived, discharged and restored in a procedural odyssey that consumed a judicial generation: the Allahabad High Court's 2001 ruling dropped the conspiracy charge against the senior leaders on a technicality of notification, the Rae Bareli court proceeded against eight accused on the lesser charges, and only in April 2017 did the Supreme Court restore the conspiracy charges and order the Lucknow special court to complete the trial day to day. On 30 September 2020, Special Judge Surendra Kumar Yadav acquitted all thirty-two surviving accused, holding that the demolition was not pre-planned, that the evidence of conspiracy was inconclusive, and that the Central Bureau of Investigation had failed to authenticate its video and photographic record. The Allahabad High Court dismissed the appeals and revisions against the acquittal in November 2022. The legal ledger thus closed in a configuration that satisfies neither reading's partisans: a commission of inquiry found orchestration and named sixty-eight culpable persons; a criminal court, applying the standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt to a degraded evidence base, found no conspiracy it could punish. Between those two outcomes lie twenty-eight years in which the exposure itself did political work. Advani, the movement's tallest figure, carried the accused's dock through the years of his deputy prime ministership. Uma Bharti resigned a Union ministership in 2004 over a related flag case's warrant. Careers were shaped, successions influenced and a prime ministerial candidacy foreclosed under the long shadow of an unresolved chargesheet, and that shadow, rather than any verdict, is the criminal-legal cost measured in institutional terms.

## The platform and the dock: the leadership's evidentiary war

Because the criminal-legal reading rests on a factual contest, the contest deserves its anatomy. The prosecution of the Ayodhya leadership turned on a few hundred metres and a few hours: the Ram Katha Kunj platform, from which the senior leadership addressed the gathering, stood within sight and earshot of the disputed structure, and the question of what was said, urged and celebrated from that platform between noon and five in the evening became the territory on which the Liberhan Commission and the special court later fought to opposite conclusions. The leaders' defence, maintained uniformly across twenty-eight years, was loss of control: Advani and Joshi testified and wrote that they appealed to the kar sevaks to descend, that the appeals failed against the crowd's ecstasy, and that the demolition was a spontaneous eruption which the leadership regretted even as sections of the crowd celebrated it. Uma Bharti's position was complicated by a photograph and a tape: images of her embracing Murli Manohar Joshi on the platform as the domes fell circulated from the day itself, and her recorded speeches of the campaign period were entered as evidence of incitement; she answered that the embrace expressed grief and solidarity in defeat of their effort to restrain, and that her speeches belonged to lawful political advocacy. The Liberhan Commission rejected the loss-of-control account in terms, holding the mobilisation's logistics, the selection of the kar sevaks who reached the domes, the tools at hand, the protection of the idols and the choreographed installation of the makeshift temple to be incompatible with spontaneity, and it treated the platform leadership, in its vocabulary of pseudo-moderation, as the enterprise's knowing centre. The special court of 2020, reading a record degraded by vanished negatives, unauthenticated cassettes and dead witnesses, held the same material insufficient for conviction and found the demolition not pre-planned. Between the commission's certainty and the court's acquittal stands a subsidiary evidentiary war over a speech attributed to Vajpayee at Lucknow on the eve of the demolition, in which he was reported in the press record of the period as saying that the kar sevaks at Ayodhya would have to level the ground, a formulation the Bharatiya Janata Party read as a reference to clearing the platform for the kar seva ceremony and its critics as an instruction whose meaning the next day disclosed. The Liberhan report canvassed the remark; the criminal court found in it nothing on which a conspiracy conviction could rest. The institutional significance of the whole contest is easily missed amid its drama. For twenty-eight years the Indian state's two great fact-finding instruments disagreed about the single most documented afternoon in the republic's history, and during those years the disagreement itself, unresolved and unresolvable, performed the phase transition's work: it made the leadership's movement past a permanent liability to be managed by lawyers, affidavits and calendar discipline, and it priced any second movement out of calculation for the generation that had stood on the platform.

## Cost two: the federal answer and the limits it announced

The second cost was constitutional, and its lesson was addressed to state governments. The dismissals of December 1992 asserted that a state administration which permitted a movement act against a protected structure, and three further administrations of the same party which had merely applauded it, could all be removed by the Union under Article 356. The assertion stood, but its half-life was short. In the assembly elections of November 1993, held under President's Rule in the four dismissed states, the Bharatiya Janata Party lost Uttar Pradesh to the Samajwadi Party and Bahujan Samaj Party alliance, lost Madhya Pradesh and Himachal Pradesh to the Congress, and held only Rajasthan, where Bhairon Singh Shekhawat returned as chief minister. The movement's state-government alignment, the third of its four institutional consequences, had been converted from an asset into a liability: a Bharatiya Janata Party state government was thereafter a government whose survival could be put in question by the street conduct of its ideological family. In March 1994, a nine-judge bench of the Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India rewrote the law of Article 356, holding the President's satisfaction justiciable, requiring floor tests rather than gubernatorial assessments, and treating secularism as part of the Constitution's basic structure, with the Bommai court pointedly observing that a state government which identified itself with a religion or abetted its subversion acted against the constitutional order. The judgment's doctrine was general, but its occasion was Ayodhya's aftermath: the Bommai case arose from Karnataka, yet the dismissals the bench had most immediately in view were those of December 1992, and the court upheld them on the secularism ground while fencing the power for the future. The institutional-electoral phase would be built inside that fence. A party that sought New Delhi's power through state governments would have to hold those governments to a standard of public order that movement politics, by its nature, could not guarantee, and the Sangh Parivar's strategists drew the conclusion the fence dictated.

## Costs three and four: the dispersed base and the dormant temple

The fourth institutional consequence of the movement phase, the integration of cadre and crowd, dissolved with the crowd itself. A mobilisation machine is maintained by its object and its calendar: the consecrated brick to carry, the procession to join, the kar seva date to converge on. After 6 December 1992 the object had been achieved in its destructive half and was legally frozen in its constructive half, for the courts' status quo orders around the makeshift temple barred construction, and the title suit's glacial progress offered no date around which to mobilise. The kar sevaks who had filled Ayodhya went home to villages and towns where no local institutional structure existed to hold them between mobilisations; the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's shakha-thin district machinery, built for episodic convergence rather than continuous membership, could not convert participants into cadres at the rate the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's daily shakha converted its own. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad entered the long institutional decline that the brief's evidence records: Ashok Singhal remained its presiding figure until his death in November 2015, its Bajrang Dal youth wing retained a capacity for localised coercion, but the organisation that had convened the largest Hindu mobilisation of the century never again set a national political calendar. Its programmatic energies drained into litigation support, cow protection campaigns and the service networks of its affiliates. The under-cited primary record of the turn sits in the organisational archives: the internal coordination correspondence of the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad and the Bharatiya Janata Party in the months after December 1992, preserved only partially, documents the deliberate character of the strategic shift, the decisions to de-escalate the temple demand into the courts, to discipline the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's calendar to the party's electoral needs, and to rebuild the party's claim to New Delhi on coalition arithmetic rather than on street capacity. The correspondence matters because it defeats the lazy alternative explanation of the phase transition, that the movement simply tired. Movements do tire; this one was stood down, by named officeholders, in writing, because the institutional costs itemised above had been counted and found decisive. The dispersal was a redistribution within the parivar rather than a disappearance of its energies, and the distinction matters for measuring the phase. The Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's own shakha network and its service affiliates expanded steadily across the institutional decades, Andersen and Damle's 2018 account recording an organisation that presented itself, with increasing plausibility, as a body of character formation and social service, its swayamsevaks staffing the schools, relief networks and tribal welfare organisations through which Hindutva acquired a daily presence the movement calendar had never supplied. The cadre released from episodic mobilisation was reinvested in continuous organisation: the shakha met every morning of the twenty-two years, in tens of thousands of localities, while the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's great convergences dwindled to commemorations. A balance sheet of the phase must therefore record two movements in opposite directions, the decline of the mobilisation arm and the deepening of the organisational arm, and the phase reading's claim is precisely that the second was purchased by the first, deliberately and at a price the leadership counted in December 1992.

## Mandir against Mandal: the party system the demolition reshaped

No account of the institutional phase is complete without the counter-mobilisation that was running on the parallel track of north Indian politics, because the phase's strategists were solving for two variables at once. The Mandal Commission's implementation by the V.P. Singh government in August 1990, reserving twenty-seven percent of central government posts for the Other Backward Classes, had organised the Hindi belt's politics around caste assertion at the precise moment the temple campaign organised it around Hindu consolidation, and the period's own shorthand, Mandal against Mandir, or Mandal against Kamandal, the water pot of the ascetic that the temple movement adopted as its emblem, named the contest accurately. The demolition's aftermath played into Mandal's hands first. The alliance of Mulayam Singh Yadav's Samajwadi Party and Kanshi Ram's Bahujan Samaj Party won the Uttar Pradesh election of November 1993 on the explicit platform of having defended the mosque with police fire in 1990 and of resisting the upper-caste order the demolition symbolised; the Congress, which had held Uttar Pradesh for most of the republic's life, was reduced to twenty-eight seats in the assembly it had dominated, a collapse from which it has never recovered in the state. The Bharatiya Janata Party's answer to Mandal was social rather than liturgical, and it was itself an institutional-phase product: the elevation of leaders drawn from the backward classes, of whom Kalyan Singh, a Lodh, was the prototype, the courting of the most backward castes against the Yadav consolidation, and eventually the expansion of the reservation and alliance policies that a movement party of 1989 would have regarded as dilution. The United Front governments of 1996 to 1998, H.D. Deve Gowda's and then I.K. Gujral's, were Mandal's institutional expression at the Centre, resting on the regional and caste-based parties the demolition decade had empowered, and their existence is the context in which the Bharatiya Janata Party's coalition turn must be read. The party could not reach a majority in a party system Mandal had pluralised; it could only federate the pluralism, ally by ally, state by state, which is a description of the National Democratic Alliance and of the twenty-two years that followed. The demolition had been staged to unify a Hindu electorate above caste; its longest political consequence was to teach its authors that no such unification was available on movement terms, and that the road to New Delhi ran through the caste-organised, region-organised India that Mandal had announced. The lesson was institutional before it was ideological, and the party that learned it best governed longest.

## 1996: the thirteen days that tested the new configuration

The institutional-electoral phase received its first test thirty-nine months after the demolition. In the general election of April and May 1996, the Bharatiya Janata Party emerged as the largest single party in the Lok Sabha with 161 seats, a plurality built not on a temple wave, for the Ayodhya issue had receded from the campaign's centre, but on state-level organisational depth and the Congress's decay. President Shankar Dayal Sharma invited Vajpayee to form the government, and the thirteen days from 16 May 1996 to the resignation of 28 May 1996 rehearsed the phase's operating principles in miniature. No ally of consequence would join a government led by the party of the demolition on the demolition's terms; the approaches Vajpayee's emissaries made foundered on the Ayodhya record, the criminal exposure of the leadership and the absence of any declared moderation of the party's programme. Vajpayee resigned before the confidence vote he was certain to lose, and used the resignation debate to deliver the speech that became the phase's founding text in all but name, arguing in the Lok Sabha on 28 May 1996 that governments would come and go and parties would rise and fall while the country and its democracy had to endure, and that a plurality party denied support had been denied it on arithmetic, not on legitimacy. The episode is treated at length as [the post-1992 institutional-electoral phase initiation](/2019/05/31/1996-vajpayee-thirteen-days-explained/), and its function here is diagnostic: the thirteen days measured exactly what the movement phase had bequeathed and what it had not. It had bequeathed the largest party in the House. It had not bequeathed governability, and governability, the phase's strategists concluded, would have to be manufactured by coalition architecture and programmatic deferral, the two technologies the movement phase had disdained.

### When did the Bharatiya Janata Party first lead a government at the Centre after the demolition?

The party first led a Union government in May 1996, when Atal Bihari Vajpayee was sworn in on 16 May as head of the largest single party and resigned on 28 May, thirteen days later, before a confidence vote his emissaries had failed to assemble the numbers to win.

## 1998: the alliance settlement and the deferred agenda

The manufacture took twenty-two months. For the general election of February and March 1998, the Bharatiya Janata Party assembled a pre-poll alliance of regional parties, the Samata Party in Bihar, the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Tamil Nadu, the Biju Janata Dal in Orissa, the Trinamool Congress in West Bengal, the Shiromani Akali Dal in Punjab and others, and won 182 seats, enough, with the allies and post-poll accretions, to form the National Democratic Alliance government. The alliance was bound by a National Agenda for Governance that performed the phase's signature manoeuvre: the three commitments on which the Bharatiya Janata Party had built its movement identity, the abrogation of Article 370, a uniform civil code and the construction of the Ram temple at Ayodhya, were placed in cold storage, the temple expressly remitted to judicial verdict or negotiated consensus, which is to say to a future the movement calendar no longer controlled. [The 1998 NDA's tactical retreat on Ayodhya commitment](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/) was not concealed; it was announced, negotiated with allies whose assent was the government's condition of existence, and defended inside the Sangh Parivar as the price of power, over the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's audible resentment. The government fell in April 1999 by one vote, fought the Kargil war that summer, and returned in October 1999 at the head of a coalition enlarged to twenty-four constituents, the fullest expression of the phase's architecture: a Hindu nationalist party governing India by explicit, written, self-denying ordinance on its own founding questions. The electoral logic validated itself across the phase's duration. State governments were won and held in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Orissa in alliance and elsewhere by organisational depth, welfare delivery and candidate machinery rather than by mobilisation events; the party's two national defeats, in 2004 and 2009, were defeats of incumbency and alliance arithmetic, processed through the same institutional channels. Even the Gujarat violence of 2002, the phase's gravest crisis, was handled institutionally by the party's national leadership in the idiom of governance and electoral timing rather than by converting the state organisation into a mobilisation, a distinction the critics who read 2002 as the movement's return were obliged to argue around. The temple, meanwhile, waited on the courts: which is to say, the movement's central object had been reassigned from the street to the slowest institution in the republic, and the reassignment held for twenty-two years. The phase's discipline was stress-tested in the career of the movement's own leader. In June 2005, L.K. Advani, then president of the Bharatiya Janata Party, visited Karachi and described Muhammad Ali Jinnah, in remarks at the founder of Pakistan's mausoleum, as a secular figure on the evidence of his address of 11 August 1947; the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's leadership and the Vishwa Hindu Parishad reacted with public fury, Advani resigned the party presidency, withdrew the resignation under negotiation, and finally yielded the office to Rajnath Singh in December 2005. The episode is a phase document of the first order: the man who had ridden the rath to Ayodhya was disciplined, by his own organisational family, not for the demolition twelve years past but for a courtesy spoken in Karachi, and the discipline was administered through committees, statements and succession arrangements, without a single procession. The movement generation had become an institution generation, and institutions punish in personnel files what movements punished in streets.

## The thirty-two-year trajectory in one view

The phase reading stands or falls on the shape of the whole arc, and the arc is best examined laid out flat. Table 1, the thirty-two-year institutional-phase-transition trajectory, sets the four phases side by side with the named events, the architectural shift and the institutional consequence that define each. The table is the article's findable artifact, and the argument of the surrounding sections can be checked against it line by line. In the classrooms where the demolition is taught as periodisation, the table is commonly projected and read through [a presentation viewer for slide files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), its four columns doing in one screen what the rival monographs require chapters to dispute.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Two features of the trajectory deserve emphasis before the litigation road is traced. The first is the phase's durability under stress. The institutional-electoral configuration survived the 1999 government's fall by a single vote, the 2002 Gujarat violence and the international opprobrium it drew, the 2004 defeat that the party's own campaign had not foreseen, and the 2009 defeat that reduced it to 116 seats; after each shock the organisation responded through alliance repair, leadership succession and state-level rebuilding, never through a return to the kar seva calendar, although the Vishwa Hindu Parishad periodically demanded one and although the movement generation regarded the deferral as a debt in default. The second feature is the asymmetry of the phases' instruments. The movement phase ran on religious time, the festival and the pilgrimage cycle; the institutional-electoral phase ran on electoral and judicial time, the assembly term and the court list. Whoever controls a movement's calendar controls its strategy, and after December 1992 the calendar belonged to institutions the Sangh Parivar did not command, the Election Commission's schedule and the Allahabad High Court's cause list. Walter Andersen and Shridhar Damle, in The RSS: A View to the Inside of 2018, document the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's own accommodation to this condition, the organisation presenting itself as a body engaged in character formation and service while its political affiliate learned the grammar of coalition. The phase was, in their evidence, an organisational fact, not a rhetorical one.

## The title suit's road: from the 1949 night to the 2019 verdict

The institutional phase's central assignment, the remittal of Ayodhya to the courts, can only be judged against the litigation it inherited. The dispute's legal biography begins in the nineteenth century: a suit of 1885 by Mahant Raghubar Das sought permission to build a temple on the Ram Chabutra, the platform outside the mosque's railing where Hindu worship had continued after the clashes of 1855 fixed a partition of the site between the mosque's inner courtyard and the outer grounds. The suits that defined the modern case followed the night of 22 and 23 December 1949, when idols of Ram Lalla appeared beneath the mosque's central dome; the Faizabad administration attached the property, appointed a receiver, and locked the gates, and the civil suits filed between 1950 and 1961, by Gopal Singh Visharad, by Paramhans Ramchandra Das, by the Nirmohi Akhara and by the Sunni Central Waqf Board, were consolidated by the Allahabad High Court and slept for a generation. The 1986 opening of the locks, ordered within a collateral proceeding, reactivated them. In 1989 the deity Ram Lalla, through a next friend, entered the litigation as a plaintiff in his own right, and in the charged autumn of that year the suits were transferred to a special bench of the Allahabad High Court. The demolition converted the title suit into something larger: the Presidential reference of January 1993 asked the Supreme Court whether a Hindu temple had stood beneath the mosque, and the Court, in Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India in October 1994, returned the reference unanswered while upholding the Union's acquisition of the Ayodhya land, observing that a mosque was not an essential feature of Islamic practice in the terms the acquisition challenge required. The reference's burial left the title suits as the dispute's only legal vehicle, which suited the institutional phase's design exactly. The Faruqui judgment carried its own delayed charge: its majority's observation that a mosque is not an essential part of the practice of Islam, offered to sustain the acquisition, was read by Muslim litigants as the constitutional order discounting their injury in advance, and it was invoked by the Hindu side for twenty-five years as settled ground. The Ayodhya appeals' final phase began in December 2017, when a three-judge bench under Chief Justice Dipak Misra took up the fourteen appeals against the 2010 partition, and ran through the Supreme Court's judgment of 27 September 2018, in which a two-to-one majority declined to refer the Faruqui observation to a larger bench, Justice Ashok Bhushan holding for the majority that the observation had been confined to the acquisition question, and Justice S. Abdul Nazeer dissenting in favour of reconsideration. A five-judge Constitution Bench was constituted in January 2019, ordered a court-supervised mediation under Justice F.M.I. Kalifulla in March 2019, received the panel's report of failure that August, and then heard the parties day to day for forty days before reserving judgment on 16 October 2019. The litigation road's every stage, excavation, partition, stay, reference, mediation, was an institution declining the movement's demand for a summary answer and substituting procedure, which is the institutional phase described from the judiciary's side of the wall.

### What did the Supreme Court say about the demolition itself in its 2019 judgment?

The unanimous Constitution Bench held the title question on the evidence, and separately recorded that the destruction of the mosque in December 1992 was an egregious violation of the rule of law, language the five judges placed in the judgment's operative reasoning rather than in any obiter lament.

The bench of the Allahabad High Court ordered the Archaeological Survey of India to excavate the site in March 2003, and the excavation of the following five months became the litigation's most contested evidentiary episode. The Archaeological Survey's report of August 2003 recorded a large earlier structure beneath the mosque, with pillar bases and architectural features its authors associated with a north Indian temple of the tenth to twelfth centuries; K.K. Muhammad, an archaeologist who had been part of an earlier inspection team of 1976 and 1977, defended the reading. Historians and archaeologists on the other side, among them D. Mandal, Shirin Ratnagar and Suraj Bhan, contested the inference chain in print: the pillar bases, they argued, belonged to different periods, the animal bones and glazed ware in the strata sat oddly with a temple, and no evidence showed a temple demolished for the mosque's construction as against a structure in ruins built over. The dispute matters to this article for its institutional character: the question that a movement had sought to settle by acclamation was being settled, slowly and inconclusively, by stratigraphy. On 30 September 2010, the Lucknow bench of Justices S.U. Khan, Sudhir Agarwal and Dharam Veer Sharma delivered a two-to-one judgment dividing the 2.77 acres three ways among the deity, the Nirmohi Akhara and the Sunni Waqf Board, a Solomon's partition that satisfied no party and that the Supreme Court stayed in May 2011 as unworkable. The appeals wound through a decade of hearings, a failed constitutional detour on the Faruqui observation, and a court-appointed mediation panel that reported failure in August 2019. On 9 November 2019, a five-judge Constitution Bench of Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi and Justices S.A. Bobde, D.Y. Chandrachud, Ashok Bhushan and S. Abdul Nazeer delivered a unanimous judgment of some 1,045 pages in M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das. The bench awarded the entire disputed 2.77 acres to the deity Ram Lalla Virajman, to be held by a trust the Union government was directed to create within three months; dismissed the Nirmohi Akhara's suit as barred by limitation while granting the Akhara representation on the trust; directed the allocation of five acres of suitable land in Ayodhya to the Sunni Central Waqf Board for a mosque; and rested the award on the preponderance of the evidence of possession and worship, on the Archaeological Survey's record read without the demolished-temple inference the more maximal Hindu pleadings required, and on the wrongs of 1949 and 1992, which the judgment treated as violations of the rule of law that could not be allowed to ripen into title, even as its disposition delivered the site to the faith whose adherents had committed them. Pratap Bhanu Mehta's essays on the verdict in the weeks that followed pressed precisely this seam, arguing that the judgment purchased closure at a measurable cost to legal principle, a critique the institutional reading can absorb without damage, since the reading never claimed the verdict was just in every premise, only that its arrival, by judicial process twenty-seven years after the demolition, was the institutional phase's consummation rather than the movement's victory lap.

## The other half of the courtroom: the Muslim litigants' long road

The institutional phase had two litigant parties, and the discipline it imposed was not the Sangh Parivar's alone. The Muslim side of the Ayodhya litigation was carried, across seven decades, by the Sunni Central Waqf Board of Uttar Pradesh as the institutional plaintiff, by the Babri Masjid Action Committee constituted in 1986, and in the courtroom by the advocate Zafaryab Jilani, who convened the Committee's legal campaign and argued the title appeals through the Allahabad and Supreme Court decades for the Board and the Waqf Board alike. The political wing of the cause had its own institutions and its own internal argument: Syed Shahabuddin, the diplomat turned editor of Muslim India and the Babri Masjid Coordination Committee's leading voice, pressed the demand for restoration of the mosque as a test of the republic's promise, while the Action Committee's lawyers pursued the slower litigation track, and the rivalry between agitation and litigation on the Muslim side mirrored, at smaller scale, the movement and institutional tracks on the Hindu side. The human emblem of the litigation was Hashim Ansari, a tailor of Ayodhya who had been a party to the suits from the early 1960s, who famously maintained a friendship across the barricades with Paramhans Ramchandra Das, the mahant who led the temple campaign's religious wing, the two men travelling together to court for years in the same rickshaw, in the period's fond retelling. Ansari died in July 2016 at ninety-five, before the verdict he had awaited for close to seven decades; his son Iqbal Ansari inherited the litigation and, on its conclusion, announced acceptance of the Supreme Court's award and of the five-acre alternative, a statement carried across the national press as the dispute's closing image of exhaustion and closure. The Personal Law Board's response to the verdict was more guarded: its officers recorded disagreement with the judgment's reasoning, supported the review petitions that a Constitution Bench dismissed in chambers on 12 December 2019, and then counselled compliance. The institutional phase's verdict on both its constituencies is the same, and it is the strongest evidence for the phase reading's generality: a mosque's congregation and a god's devotees alike were processed, across the same twenty-seven years, from the street into the courtroom, and out of the courtroom with a judgment each side had sworn it could or could not accept, and did.

## The guarded site: Ayodhya between demolition and verdict

For twenty-seven years the disputed site existed in a condition no phase vocabulary captures better than custody. The Union government's Acquisition of Certain Area at Ayodhya Act of January 1993 took some 67.7 acres around the site into central ownership, with the plot itself held by a statutory receiver and the makeshift temple continuing worship under the Supreme Court's protection of the status quo, a regime the Ismail Faruqui judgment sustained in 1994. The pilgrim's Ayodhya experience for a generation was darshan at a distance: the deity visible through layered barricades, wire mesh and a security corridor, worship conducted under the eyes of the Central Reserve Police Force in a tented shrine on a rubble platform. The calendar kept the wound's anniversaries in opposed observances. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad marked 6 December as Shaurya Diwas, a day of valour, in its strongholds; Muslim organisations marked the same date as a black day of mourning and protest, and the state's annual task was to police both commemorations in the same streets. The site's unresolved status drew violence of the new century as well: on 5 July 2005, five armed attackers of Lashkar-e-Taiba stormed the makeshift temple complex with a jeep-borne explosive and grenades, breached the outer cordon and were killed by the Central Reserve Police Force before reaching the shrine, one civilian dying in the assault, an episode the prosecution record and the press of the period established in detail. Ayodhya the town, meanwhile, was governed as a permanent security object, its economy suspended between pilgrimage and litigation, until the institutional phase's own instruments began to rename and rebuild it in anticipation of the verdict: in November 2018 the Uttar Pradesh government of Yogi Adityanath renamed Faizabad district as Ayodhya, aligning the revenue map with the sacred geography the title suit had yet to award. The guarded decades are the phase argument's quietest evidence. A movement in being does not tolerate its object under lock and mesh for twenty-seven years; an institutional order does, and did, and the tolerance, renewed annually through budgets, deployments and court lists, is the measure of how completely the question had passed from the street to the state.

## Consummation: the trust, the foundation stone and the consecration

The mechanics of the consummation were administrative, which is the point. On 5 February 2020, the Prime Minister announced in the Lok Sabha the creation of the Shri Ram Janmabhoomi Teerth Kshetra, the trust the judgment had directed, chaired by the senior advocate K. Parasaran, who had argued the deity's case, with the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's Champat Rai as its general secretary, the movement's campaign manager become a trustee of a registered body. The Sunni Central Waqf Board accepted the five-acre allocation at Dhannipur village in Sohawal tehsil of Ayodhya district, some twenty-five kilometres from the disputed site, and the Indo-Islamic Cultural Foundation was constituted in July 2020 to build the mosque and a complex of welfare institutions there. On 5 August 2020, Narendra Modi performed the bhumi pujan, the foundation-stone consecration of the Ram temple, at Ayodhya, the first serving prime minister to officiate at the site, in a ceremony the state's apparatus supported and broadcast. On 22 January 2024 came the pran pratishtha, the consecration of the idol of Ram Lalla in the new temple's sanctum, with the prime minister as the chief ritual participant, the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's sarsanghchalak Mohan Bhagwat in attendance, and the temple opened to the public the following day. The consecration's politics were themselves a study in the phase's terms. The ceremony was boycotted by the principal opposition parties, whose leaders characterised it as the ruling party's appropriation of a religious event and noted that the temple was consecrated unfinished, while several of the Shankaracharyas, the heads of the monastic orders whose sanction traditionally crowns a temple, recorded their reservation that consecration of an incomplete structure departed from the shastraic norm the trust's defenders cited on other questions. The trust and the government answered that the sanctum was complete and the ceremony's date was set by the calendar of the court's settlement and the nation's readiness, not by party convenience. The dispute was conducted entirely in the idiom the institutional phase had made normal: press statements, competing scriptural citations, parliamentary jibes, and no crowd anywhere near a cordon. Between the demolition and the consecration lie thirty-one years and the whole of the phase transition: the crowd that levelled the mosque had demanded a temple by acclamation, and a temple arrived, but it arrived by title suit, trust deed, municipal sanction, archaeological consultancy and a state ceremony choreographed by the government of the day, with the movement's surviving organisations present as trustees, guests and stakeholders rather than as principals. The 2020 acquittals of September that year closed the criminal ledger between the bhumi pujan and the consecration, and the sequence reads, in the institutional frame, as a single settlement executed by courts, commissions and cabinets across three decades. The verdict day itself had supplied the settlement's emblem eleven months before the bhumi pujan. On 9 November 2019, Ayodhya sat under prohibitory orders and a security deployment designed for the catastrophe of 1992, and produced instead a quiet Saturday: the town's Muslim residents, whose organisations had counselled acceptance of whatever the Court decided, received the award against them in public calm, the district's peace committees of Hindu and Muslim elders, a machinery built across the guarded decades, walked their rounds, and the national violence that editors had readied their pages for did not occur anywhere in India. The contrast with December 1992 is the phase argument in a single day's evidence: the same town, the same dispute, a judgment that awarded everything to one side, and no crowd, because the question had been held for twenty-seven years inside institutions whose verdict both constituencies had been organised, by their own leaderships, to survive.

## The founding tension the demolition exposed

Beneath the phase argument lies the constitutional one, and the demolition's force came from its position in [the religion-state founding tension](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/) that the Constituent Assembly had managed rather than resolved. The Assembly of 1946 to 1949 wrote a secularism of principled distance, in which the state held no religion and intervened in all of them, and it left the management of contested sacred sites to ordinary law and the state's impartial enforcement. Ayodhya mocked both limbs of the settlement for forty years before 1992: a local administration that connived at the 1949 installation, courts that froze the wrong in place with injunctions and receivers, a district judge who opened the locks in 1986 without deciding the title, and a political class that treated the site's custody as a bargaining counter in election seasons. The state's own last attempt to legislate the general question came fifteen months before the demolition, in the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act of September 1991, by which the Rao government froze the religious character of every place of worship as it had stood on 15 August 1947 and barred conversion suits across India, while expressly excluding the Ram Janmabhoomi and Babri Masjid site from the Act's protection because the dispute was already in court. The exclusion is the settlement's confession: Parliament would legislate the principle for every temple and mosque in India except the one where the principle was being tested. The 2019 judgment engaged the 1991 Act directly, treating it as a legislative reaffirmation of the founding settlement's non-retrogression, which is the closest the constitutional order came to pronouncing on the demolition's meaning in its own vocabulary: the state had promised, in 1950 and again in 1991, that religious demography by force would not be ratified, and the ratification question was precisely what the title suit's long road was deciding under other names. The Act's afterlife completes the picture. In the decades after 1992 the general freeze held across India, not because the appetite for Ayodhya's sequel was absent, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's original trinity of shrines had named Kashi and Mathura beside Ayodhya at the movement's start, but because the demolition had repriced the method: suits over the Gyanvapi mosque at Varanasi and the Shahi Idgah at Mathura proceeded, in the 2020s, as litigation about surveys and maintainability under the 1991 Act, argued by advocates before benches, with the Supreme Court policing the Act's boundaries in the Gyanvapi proceedings of 2022 even as the Ayodhya judgment was cited on both sides. Whatever those suits finally yield, their form is the demolition's legacy in the legal register: the claims that a movement once proposed to execute in an afternoon stand executed, if at all, in case numbers, and the state's promise of 1991, breached at the single site it exempted, has been defended at every site it covered. The exception consumed a mosque; the rule held a republic.

## Adjudicating the three readings

Three interpretations compete for the demolition, and they should be tested against the same evidence rather than against each other's rhetoric. The criminal-legal reading treats 6 December 1992 as a crime scene extended in time: its canon is the Liberhan report, the chargesheet and the acquittal, and its question is who bears culpability. The reading is indispensable and insufficient. It cannot explain why the acquitted and the indicted alike emerged from the process into an organisation behaving, for twenty-two years, as if a movement had ended; culpability analysis has no category for phase. The political-consequences reading treats the demolition as an electoral and communal shock: its canon is the riot ledger, the dismissed governments, the 1993 assembly results and the party's seat graph from 120 to 303 across three decades. It captures the event's harvest but not its discipline: on a pure consequences analysis, the Bharatiya Janata Party's post-1992 moderation appears as tactics, reversible at convenience, and the twenty-two-year consistency of the deferral, maintained through two defeats when a remobilisation might have been the cheaper instrument, is left unexplained. The institutional-phase-transition reading treats the demolition as the moment a movement's methods met their price and were retired in favour of a successor configuration: its canon is the trajectory of Table 1, the National Agenda for Governance, the internal coordination record and the courts' calendar. It explains what the other two cannot: the timing of the moderation (immediately after the costs were levied, not after electoral punishment, for the party's seat share rose in 1996), its durability (institutional, because the alliance and litigation machinery required it continuously, not episodically), and its endpoint (the consecration arrived by trust deed and verdict, not by a second December). Christophe Jaffrelot's framework in The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics of 1996, written while the phase turn was still contestable, supplies the reading's scholarly anchor: Jaffrelot analysed the Ayodhya campaign as the movement's mobilisation of Hindu identity against a stigmatised other, tempered by the cadres' own emulation of the institutions they sought to capture, and his account of the post-demolition recalculation, the party's discovery that mobilisation had delivered it to the threshold of power while barring the door, remains the canonical statement of the dynamic the phase reading formalises.

The phase reading also states its own falsification conditions, which the consequences reading rarely troubles to do. The reading would collapse if the record showed the Bharatiya Janata Party attempting a second mass mobilisation on a comparable object between 1993 and 2013 and being restrained only by force; if the National Agenda for Governance's deferrals were shown to have been repudiated in office rather than renewed in 1999 and honoured, at visible cost with allies and with the Vishwa Hindu Parishad, for the coalition's life; or if the temple's arrival in 2020 to 2024 were traceable to a mobilisation calendar rather than to a judgment, a trust deed and a government ceremony. None of these conditions is met by the record as it stands. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad's periodic calls for a renewed movement, its demand in the 2000s for legislation overriding the courts, and its threats to fix fresh kar seva dates were each refused, deferred or absorbed by the party and the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh in the institutional idiom, and the refusals are documented precisely because they disappointed the movement's veterans in public. A phase that must repeatedly refuse remobilisation, against its own base's demands and at measurable cost to its own enthusiasm, is not latency wearing a disguise. It is a successor configuration exercising discipline, which is the phase reading's central observable.

Thomas Blom Hansen's The Saffron Wave of 1999, the other canonical scholarly treatment written inside the phase's first decade, supplies the reading's necessary caution. Hansen read the Ayodhya mobilisation as the violent edge of a longer democratic deepening, in which plebeian assertion within Hindu society used the movement's idiom for its own ends, and he doubted that the party's moderation represented any ideological retreat rather than the public face of a dual organisation. The phase reading absorbs the caution by narrowing its claim: it asserts a change in configuration and method, documented in agendas, trials and calendars, and takes no position that requires the ideology itself to have softened, a question the phase evidence cannot answer and the post-2014 record suggests it should not be asked to.

The electoral ledger, read whole, tells the same story in the consequences reading's own units. The Bharatiya Janata Party's national vote share across the phase ran 20.1 percent in 1991, 20.3 in 1996, 25.6 in 1998, 23.8 in 1999, 22.2 in the defeat of 2004 and 18.8 in the defeat of 2009, on the Election Commission's statistical reports, a two-decade plateau in the low twenties purchased by the coalition configuration; the share breaks out of the plateau only in 2014, at 31.0 percent, when the post-2014 configuration replaced the phase whose obituary this article is writing. A movement reading of the demolition predicts a wave; a consequences reading predicts a surge and decay; the plateau, sustained across five elections, two governments and two defeats without the movement's calendar being restored at any point of stress, is the fingerprint of an institutional phase, an organisation that had traded the spike for the floor and kept the floor for twenty-two years.

### What is the difference between a phase ending and a phase going latent?

A phase ends when the configuration that defined it, its machine, object and calendar, ceases to operate, as the kar seva movement's did in December 1992. A repertoire goes latent when it survives as an available technique inside a successor configuration, to be redeployed by different machinery when conditions change.

The strongest counter to the phase reading comes from [the post-2014 reactivation context](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), and it deserves its full force. After 2014, a Bharatiya Janata Party armed with single-party majorities abrogated Article 370 in August 2019, enacted the Citizenship (Amendment) Act in December 2019, presided over the temple's consecration as a state ceremony in January 2024, and governed through movement vocabulary, cadre energy and religious symbolism at an intensity the coalition decades had suppressed. If the movement phase ended in 1992, the objection runs, what exactly was reactivated, and is a phase that returns after twenty-two years not better described as latent throughout, with the coalition decades as the latency and not as a phase in their own right? The objection fails on the distinction between phase termination and phase availability. What ended on 6 December 1992 was a specific movement: a named campaign, with a named object, a dated calendar, a single mobilisation machine and a leadership cohort whose members the criminal process then processed for twenty-eight years. That movement did not resume in 2014; no kar seva was called, no rath yatra rolled to Ayodhya, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's demand for legislation to build the temple was refused by a government that waited for the verdict instead, and the temple arrived through the institutional phase's own instruments, the title suit and the trust. What the post-2014 period reactivated was movement-phase availability, the repertoire, redeployed through different machinery: executive action by a single-party majority, legislation, and state ceremony, under [the tripartite-disentanglement framework](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/) in which the ideological, organisational and electoral strands of Hindutva could be run simultaneously because the institutional phase had taught the party to hold power while it moved. Latency, properly used, describes a property of repertoires; phases describe the configuration of an organisation in a period, and the configuration of 1992 to 2014, coalition-bound, court-bound and mobilisation-averse, was as real as the configuration it replaced. A reading that dissolves it into latency must explain away the National Agenda for Governance's written deferrals, the twenty-four-party coalition of 1999, the refusal of the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's repeated remobilisation demands, and the simple fact that the men who climbed the domes were prosecuted while the men who waited inherited the state. The phase reading explains all of it with one mechanism. That is what adjudication on primary evidence looks like, and it is why the demolition's politics are best understood neither as a crime that escaped punishment nor as a riot that changed a vote share, but as the afternoon on which Hindutva's movement phase ended in public, and its long institutional phase began.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Babri Masjid demolition?

The Babri Masjid demolition was the destruction, on 6 December 1992, of the sixteenth-century mosque at Ayodhya in Uttar Pradesh by kar sevaks, volunteers mobilised by the Vishwa Hindu Parishad, the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh and the Bharatiya Janata Party for a religious ceremony at the site Hindus revere as the birthplace of the god Ram. The mosque, built in 1528 and 1529 by Mir Baqi, a commander of the Mughal emperor Babur, stood over a dispute that had run in the courts since the nineteenth century and had been frozen in a locked stalemate since idols were placed inside it in December 1949. The crowd breached the security cordons around midday, reduced the three domes to rubble by the evening, and installed the idols in a makeshift temple on the levelled platform the same night, converting the longest-running property dispute in Indian law into the defining event of the republic's religion and politics for a generation.

### Q: When did the Babri Masjid demolition occur?

The demolition occurred on Sunday, 6 December 1992, across roughly five hours of the afternoon. Kar sevaks who had converged on Ayodhya for a kar seva ceremony the Supreme Court had authorised only in symbolic form began breaching the cordons around noon; a volunteer scaled a dome shortly after midday, organised parties with ropes, hammers and grappling irons took up the work, and the three domes fell in sequence, the last of them by approximately 4:55 in the evening on the timeline the Liberhan Commission reconstructed. By nightfall the sixteen-century-old structure had been levelled, the idols of Ram Lalla had been reinstalled under a makeshift canopy, the Uttar Pradesh chief minister Kalyan Singh had resigned, and the Union government had imposed President's Rule in the state. The date had been fixed by the Vishwa Hindu Parishad as the resumption of the kar seva suspended after the police firings of October and November 1990, and 6 December has been marked in opposed observances, as a day of valour by the temple movement and a black day by Muslim organisations, in every year since.

### Q: What is the Liberhan Commission?

The Liberhan Commission, formally the Liberhan Ayodhya Commission of Inquiry, was a one-man commission of inquiry the Union Home Ministry constituted on 16 December 1992, ten days after the demolition, under Justice Manmohan Singh Liberhan, a retired judge of the Punjab and Haryana High Court. Its charge was to establish the sequence of events leading to the destruction of the Babri Masjid, the roles of the Uttar Pradesh government of Kalyan Singh and of the individuals and organisations involved, and the failures of the security arrangements the Supreme Court had been assured would protect the structure. Given three months at appointment, the commission received forty-eight extensions, held 399 sittings, examined prime ministers, home ministers, chief ministers and the Sangh Parivar's senior leadership, and submitted its 1,029-page report to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh on 30 June 2009. It was tabled in Parliament on 24 November 2009, and remains the single most detailed official reconstruction of the demolition and its preparation, cited by every serious treatment of the event, including by the critics who dispute its conclusions.

### Q: Who was responsible for the Babri demolition?

Responsibility is the question on which India's two official instruments reached opposite findings, and the honest answer must carry both. The Liberhan Commission of 2009 held the demolition to have been planned and orchestrated, listed sixty-eight persons as culpable, named the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad, the Bajrang Dal and the Bharatiya Janata Party leadership, including L.K. Advani, Murli Manohar Joshi, Uma Bharti and Atal Bihari Vajpayee, the last three characterised as pseudo-moderates privy to the enterprise, and found Kalyan Singh's Uttar Pradesh government to have colluded in hobbling its own police. The criminal process answered differently: after a composite conspiracy chargesheet in 1993, decades of fragmented proceedings and the Supreme Court's restoration of conspiracy charges in 2017, the special court at Lucknow acquitted all thirty-two accused on 30 September 2020, holding the demolition not pre-planned and the evidence inconclusive, and the Allahabad High Court dismissed appeals against the acquittal in November 2022. A commission applying the civil standard found orchestration; a criminal court applying proof beyond reasonable doubt found no punishable conspiracy.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court rule on Ayodhya in 2019?

In M. Siddiq v. Mahant Suresh Das, decided unanimously on 9 November 2019 by a five-judge Constitution Bench headed by Chief Justice Ranjan Gogoi, the Supreme Court awarded the entire disputed site of 2.77 acres at Ayodhya to the deity Ram Lalla Virajman, directing the Union government to constitute a trust within three months to hold the land and build the temple. The bench dismissed the Nirmohi Akhara's suit as barred by limitation while granting the Akhara a place on the trust, and directed that five acres of suitable land in Ayodhya be allotted to the Sunni Central Waqf Board for the construction of a mosque. The award rested on the preponderance of evidence about possession and worship at the site across the centuries, on the Archaeological Survey of India's excavation record read cautiously, and on the principle that the wrongs of 1949, when idols were placed in the mosque, and of 1992, when the mosque was destroyed in what the judgment called an egregious violation of the rule of law, could not themselves manufacture title. Review petitions were dismissed in December 2019.

### Q: When was the Ram temple consecrated?

The Ram temple at Ayodhya was consecrated on 22 January 2024, when the pran pratishtha, the ritual installation of the deity's presence, was performed for the idol of Ram Lalla in the sanctum of the new temple built by the Shri Ram Janmabhoomi Teerth Kshetra trust on the site the Supreme Court had awarded in November 2019. Prime Minister Narendra Modi served as the chief ritual participant, the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh's sarsanghchalak Mohan Bhagwat attended, and the temple opened to the public the following day. The consecration had been preceded by the bhumi pujan, the foundation consecration of 5 August 2020, also performed by the prime minister. The date closed a thirty-one-year arc from the demolition of December 1992: the temple the movement had demanded by acclamation arrived through a title suit, a unanimous constitutional judgment, a registered trust and a state-supported ceremony, with the opposition parties boycotting the event as a partisan appropriation and several Shankaracharyas recording reservations about consecrating an unfinished structure, objections the trust answered by pointing to the completed sanctum.

### Q: How did the demolition affect Indian politics?

The demolition redrew Indian politics along four channels. It destroyed the movement phase of Hindutva in the act of consummating it: four Bharatiya Janata Party state governments were dismissed, the Sangh Parivar's organisations were banned and its leaders prosecuted for twenty-eight years, and the party rebuilt its road to power on coalition architecture and agenda deferral, governing in 1998 and 1999 at the head of alliances bound by written restraint on Ayodhya, Article 370 and a uniform civil code. It reshaped the opposition field, collapsing the Congress in Uttar Pradesh and consolidating the caste-based parties that the Mandal Commission's implementation had empowered, so that the party system of the 1990s was organised by Mandal against Mandir. It set off the riots of December 1992 and January 1993, in which more than two thousand people died across India on the standard counts, and the Mumbai bomb blasts of March 1993 that the prosecution sustained as retaliation. And it remitted the republic's most explosive religious question to its courts, whose 2019 verdict and the 2024 consecration closed the dispute inside institutional forms.

### Q: Who was Kalyan Singh, and what had his government promised the Supreme Court?

Kalyan Singh, a leader of the Lodh backward caste who became the Bharatiya Janata Party's first chief minister of Uttar Pradesh in June 1991, headed the state government on 6 December 1992. His administration had given the Supreme Court a sworn assurance, filed by affidavit in the kar seva proceedings of November 1992, that the disputed structure at Ayodhya would be protected, that only a symbolic kar seva would be permitted on the acquired land outside the plot, and that the Court's status quo orders would be honoured; parallel assurances went to the National Integration Council. On the day, his police held the cordons and did not fire or intervene as the cordons fell, central forces stationed in the area remained under state operational control away from the site, and the Liberhan Commission later found his government to have colluded in hobbling its own machinery, its instructions to the police having leaked in terms that promised the kar sevaks impunity. Kalyan Singh resigned on the evening of the demolition, accepting responsibility, was named among the sixty-eight culpable persons in the Liberhan report, stood trial among the thirty-two accused, and was acquitted in September 2020.

### Q: Why did the Rao government not dismiss the Uttar Pradesh government before 6 December 1992?

The Rao government's inaction rested on a layered calculation its defenders stated in Parliament and its critics never forgave. Law and order was a state subject; the Uttar Pradesh government had sworn an affidavit to the Supreme Court and given assurances to the National Integration Council, and a pre-emptive dismissal under Article 356, before any failure had occurred, invited reversal in politics and possibly in court as the punishment of an opposition government for an anticipated crime. The firing precedent of October and November 1990 weighed heavily: central forces shooting kar sevaks on live television risked a conflagration larger than the demolition itself, in a town holding perhaps 150,000 volunteers. And Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao calculated, as his interlocutors later recorded, that a Bharatiya Janata Party government presiding over a mosque's destruction would bear the odium alone, while a Congress intervention that failed would transfer the ownership of the catastrophe to Delhi. The calculation assumed the state machinery would at least slow the crowd. The Liberhan record showed the machinery deliberately hobbled, and Rao's pledge to rebuild the mosque, made in the Lok Sabha that December, satisfied neither constituency the Congress needed.

### Q: What did the Liberhan Commission conclude about whether the demolition was planned?

The commission concluded, in its best-known formulation, that the events of 6 December 1992 were neither spontaneous nor unplanned. Its report held that the mobilisation of the kar sevaks and their convergence on Ayodhya and Faizabad was neither spontaneous nor voluntary but well orchestrated and planned, carried out with the army-like discipline of the Sangh Parivar's organisations. On the demolition itself, the commission reasoned from the sequence: the small number of kar sevaks who actually reached the domes, the veiling of their identities, the removal of the idols and the cash box before the structure came down and their reinstallation in a makeshift temple, the availability of demolition implements and of material for the makeshift construction, led categorically, in its words, to the conclusion that the demolition was carried out with great painstaking preparation and preplanning. The finding flatly contradicted the leadership's account of a crowd escaped from control. It must be read against the criminal court's opposite endpoint: the special court in 2020, weighing a degraded evidence base under the criminal standard, found the demolition not pre-planned and acquitted all thirty-two accused, leaving the two official findings in permanent, unreconciled opposition.

### Q: What happened to the Bharatiya Janata Party governments in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh after the demolition?

On 15 December 1992, nine days after the demolition, the Union government dismissed all three governments, Sunderlal Patwa's in Madhya Pradesh, Bhairon Singh Shekhawat's in Rajasthan and Shanta Kumar's in Himachal Pradesh, dissolved their legislative assemblies and imposed President's Rule, on the stated ground that governments of the party whose ideological family had presided over the destruction of a protected structure could not be trusted with the constitutional order's secular obligations. Uttar Pradesh had already passed under President's Rule on the night of 6 December following Kalyan Singh's resignation. The dismissals were challenged in the climate that produced the Supreme Court's nine-judge Bommai judgment of March 1994, which fenced Article 356 with judicial review and floor tests for the future while upholding the December 1992 dismissals on the secularism ground. In the assembly elections held in November 1993, the Bharatiya Janata Party lost Madhya Pradesh and Himachal Pradesh to the Congress and Uttar Pradesh to the Samajwadi Party and Bahujan Samaj Party alliance, holding only Rajasthan, where Shekhawat returned as chief minister, a result sheet that taught the party the federal price of movement methods more durably than any censure.

### Q: How many people died in the violence that followed the demolition?

No official all-India count was ever published, and every figure for the post-demolition violence is an estimate whose source and range must be stated with it. The standard consolidated counts in the reference literature and the press record place the deaths across India in December 1992 and January 1993 at more than two thousand, the large majority of them Muslims, in riots that struck Ayodhya itself and Mumbai, Surat, Ahmedabad, Kanpur, Delhi, Bhopal and dozens of smaller towns. For Mumbai, the worst-affected city, the Srikrishna Commission supplied the authoritative ledger for the two riot phases of December 1992 and January 1993: nine hundred dead, of whom 575 were Muslims, 275 Hindus and 50 recorded as others, with 2,036 injured and thousands displaced into relief camps. Justice Srikrishna's report, submitted in February 1998, documented the Shiv Sena's organisational role in the January phase and the communal bias of sections of the Mumbai police. A second mortality belongs to the chain the demolition set running: the Mumbai bomb blasts of 12 March 1993 killed 257 people, an attack the prosecution sustained through the Supreme Court as organised retaliation for the riots.

### Q: What did the Archaeological Survey of India find when it excavated the disputed site?

The Archaeological Survey of India excavated the disputed site between March and August 2003 under the Allahabad High Court's directions. Its report, submitted in August 2003, recorded the remains of a large earlier structure beneath the mosque, enumerating pillar bases, a staircase, stone slabs and other architectural members that its authors associated with a monumental north Indian temple of roughly the tenth to twelfth centuries, and it concluded that the Babri Masjid had been raised over the remains. The report's inference chain was immediately and permanently contested. Historians and archaeologists including D. Mandal, Shirin Ratnagar and Suraj Bhan argued in print that the pillar bases belonged to different periods and floors, that animal bones and glazed ware in the strata sat oddly with a temple, and that nothing in the excavation demonstrated a temple demolished to build the mosque as against a ruined structure built over. K.K. Muhammad, an archaeologist associated with an earlier inspection of the site in 1976 and 1977, defended the Survey's reading. The Supreme Court's 2019 judgment used the excavation record as evidence of an underlying structure while expressly declining to decide whether a temple had been demolished.

### Q: What did the Allahabad High Court decide in the Ayodhya title case in 2010?

On 30 September 2010, a special bench of the Allahabad High Court at Lucknow, comprising Justices S.U. Khan, Sudhir Agarwal and Dharam Veer Sharma, decided the consolidated Ayodhya title suits by a two-to-one majority in favour of partition. The majority held that the disputed area of 2.77 acres should be divided three ways, one third to the deity Ram Lalla, one third to the Nirmohi Akhara and one third to the Sunni Central Waqf Board, reasoning from the parties' long joint possession and worship at different parts of the site. The judges agreed, in differing formulations, that the place where the central dome had stood was the birthplace of Ram as a matter of Hindu faith and belief, and the majority drew on the Archaeological Survey of India's excavation record in assessing the site's earlier history. All parties rejected the partition, the Hindu litigants because the deity's claim was to the whole, the Waqf Board because it denied any share to the mosque's claimants, and in May 2011 the Supreme Court stayed the judgment as unworkable and maintained the status quo, setting the appeals on the road that ended only with the Constitution Bench's verdict of November 2019.

### Q: What criminal cases arose from the demolition, and how did they end?

Two first information reports were registered on 6 December 1992: one against the unnamed kar sevaks who physically demolished the mosque, and a second against eight named persons, including L.K. Advani, Murli Manohar Joshi, Uma Bharti, Ashok Singhal and Vinay Katiyar, for inflammatory speeches and allied offences. The Central Bureau of Investigation's composite chargesheet of October 1993 merged the tracks into a conspiracy case against forty-nine accused, and the proceedings then fragmented for a judicial generation across courts in Lalitpur, Rae Bareli and Lucknow: the conspiracy charge against the senior leaders was dropped in 2001 by the Allahabad High Court on a notification technicality, the lesser case proceeded at Rae Bareli, and only in April 2017 did the Supreme Court restore the conspiracy charges, club the trials at Lucknow and order day-to-day hearing. By the time judgment came, several of the accused, including Atal Bihari Vajpayee's contemporaries Bal Thackeray, Ashok Singhal and Giriraj Kishore, had died. On 30 September 2020, Special Judge Surendra Kumar Yadav acquitted all thirty-two surviving accused, holding the demolition not pre-planned and the prosecution's audio-visual evidence unauthenticated and inconclusive. Appeals against the acquittal were dismissed by the Allahabad High Court in November 2022.

### Q: What was the symbolic kar seva the Supreme Court permitted in 1992?

The symbolic kar seva was the narrow ceremony the Supreme Court authorised at Ayodhya for 6 December 1992, and its definition became the legal hinge of the day. Through the autumn of 1992 the Vishwa Hindu Parishad had announced a resumption of kar seva, voluntary service toward the temple's construction; the Uttar Pradesh government acquired land around the disputed plot, and the litigation narrowed to what the assembled volunteers would be allowed to do on it. By its orders of late November 1992, the Court permitted a symbolic kar seva, in substance devotional singing, prayers and a token ceremony on the acquired land outside the disputed plot, and it did so on the Uttar Pradesh government's sworn affidavit that the disputed structure would be protected and the Court's status quo orders honoured, assurances Chief Minister Kalyan Singh repeated to the National Integration Council. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad publicly committed itself to the same limits. What occurred instead was the breaching of the cordons and the structure's demolition between noon and five in the evening, and the gap between the permitted symbol and the executed act gave the Liberhan Commission its measure of the deception practised on the Court.

### Q: What is the Shri Ram Janmabhoomi Teerth Kshetra?

The Shri Ram Janmabhoomi Teerth Kshetra is the trust the Union government constituted under the Supreme Court's direction of 9 November 2019 to receive the disputed 2.77 acres at Ayodhya and to build and administer the Ram temple there. The Court's judgment required the Centre to frame a scheme and vest the land in a trust or authority within three months; Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced the trust's creation in the Lok Sabha on 5 February 2020. Its first chairman was K. Parasaran, the senior advocate who had argued the deity's case before the Constitution Bench, and its general secretary was Champat Rai, the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's central figure in the Ayodhya campaign, an appointment that placed the movement's campaign manager inside the institutional vehicle the verdict had created. The Nirmohi Akhara, whose suit the Court had dismissed as time-barred, was granted representation among the trustees in accordance with the judgment. The trust supervised the temple's construction by Larsen and Toubro to the designs of the Sompura family of temple architects, performed the foundation consecration of 5 August 2020, and conducted the pran pratishtha of 22 January 2024 that opened the temple.

### Q: What stood at the disputed site between the demolition and the temple's construction?

Between December 1992 and the temple's construction, the disputed 2.77 acres held a makeshift temple under armed guard. On the night of the demolition itself, kar sevaks reinstalled the idols of Ram Lalla, removed before the domes fell, on the levelled platform under a makeshift canopy; within days the canopy had become a rudimentary shrine, and the Supreme Court's status quo orders, sustained through the Ismail Faruqui judgment of 1994 and the Union's acquisition of some 67.7 acres around the site under the Ayodhya Act of January 1993, froze that arrangement for twenty-seven years. The deity received worship from a priest in attendance, and pilgrims were granted darshan from a distance, through layered barricades, wire mesh and a security corridor policed by the Central Reserve Police Force, the shrine itself a tented structure on the rubble platform. The arrangement survived the terror attack of 5 July 2005, when five Lashkar-e-Taiba gunmen stormed the complex and were killed before reaching the shrine. Only the Constitution Bench's verdict of November 2019 unfroze the site, transferring it to the Teerth Kshetra trust, under which the makeshift shrine was moved during construction and the new temple rose over the platform.

### Q: How did the demolition alter the Bharatiya Janata Party's strategy for winning power?

The demolition taught the Bharatiya Janata Party that mobilisation could make it India's largest party and bar it from governing, and its strategy for the following twenty-two years was built on that lesson. After the thirteen-day failure of May 1996, in which no ally would join a government on the movement's terms, the party replaced the mobilisation road with coalition architecture: pre-poll alliances with regional parties, a National Agenda for Governance, and the deferral of its three founding commitments, the Ram temple remitted to judicial verdict or consensus, Article 370 and a uniform civil code shelved, as the announced price of alliance. The configuration won power in 1998, survived its one-vote fall in 1999 and returned at the head of a twenty-four-party coalition, and it carried the party's expansion thereafter through state governments won on organisation, welfare delivery and candidate machinery rather than on procession calendars. The shift empowered the party's electoral managers over the movement's campaigners, disciplined the Vishwa Hindu Parishad's calendar to the party's needs, and deferred the temple to the courts, a deferral maintained through the defeats of 2004 and 2009 and lifted only when the Supreme Court, not a mobilisation, delivered the site in 2019.

### Q: What happened to the Vishwa Hindu Parishad after 1992?

The Vishwa Hindu Parishad, the organisation that had convened the kar sevaks and set the demolition's date, entered a long institutional decline after its moment of consummation. Banned in December 1992, it lost its object and its calendar in the same afternoon: the temple question passed to the courts, the Bharatiya Janata Party subordinated the movement to coalition discipline, and the organisation's district machinery, built for episodic convergence rather than continuous membership, could not hold the dispersed kar sevaks as cadres. Ashok Singhal remained its presiding figure until his death in November 2015; its energies drained into litigation support, cow protection campaigns and the service and education networks of its affiliates, while the Bajrang Dal, its youth wing, retained a capacity for localised street presence without a national programme. Its periodic demands for renewed movement, for legislation overriding the courts in the 2000s, and for fresh kar seva dates were refused or absorbed by the party and the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh. The partial reversal after 2014 restored its access and visibility, with its campaign manager Champat Rai installed as the temple trust's general secretary, influence regained through the institutions that had replaced it.
