The honest answer to how to stop Erling Haaland is not a marking instruction. It is a decision about where your last line stands, who applies pressure to the man about to pass, and which two chances you are willing to give away in order to remove the one that beats you. Almost every version of this question answered on television or in a match preview reduces to "get tight on him," and against this particular forward that instruction is not merely weak. It is the mechanism by which he scores.

![How to stop Erling Haaland, a defensive counter-plan and phase-by-phase blueprint - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-49.webp)

State the plan in one sentence before any of the detail arrives. You defend the passer rather than the runner, you hold a controlled medium line with the offside reference set by the ball rather than by the striker, you assign your calmest centre-back to the space behind and your most physically robust one to the near post, and you accept conceding possession, territory, and a high volume of harmless wide entries in exchange for removing the two actions that produce almost all of his goals: the run onto a first-time ball played into the space behind a stretched line, and the arrival at the near post ahead of a recovering defender on a cutback. Nothing in this plan drives the probability of conceding to zero. The measurable objective is the count and the quality of the reception opportunities he gets inside the penalty area, and that number moves when a team defends the right thing.

Everything that follows is the reasoning behind those choices and the sequence for delivering them. A coach who reads to the end should be able to walk onto a training pitch and set a line height with a defensible reason attached, name the two centre-back profiles who can execute the assignment and explain why a third profile cannot, brief a screening midfielder on the exact pass he exists to prevent, tell a goalkeeper where to stand and when to travel, organize a box on cutbacks, and say out loud, in front of the squad, what the plan is deliberately giving away. That last item is the part most plans omit, and its absence is why they collapse in the twenty minutes after the first goal.

## Why the shape and line height frame everything else in a plan against Erling Haaland

Line height is the first decision because every other decision inherits from it. A defensive line is a wager about which of two spaces you would rather defend: the ground behind your last defender, or the ground inside your own penalty area. You cannot compress both at once. A high line shrinks the second and enlarges the first. A deep line does the reverse. Against a forward whose value is concentrated in running behind and in finishing inside twelve yards, both wagers lose something that matters, which is why so many teams arrive without a coherent position and end up drifting between the two across ninety minutes, which is the worst outcome available.

Start with the deep block, since it is the instinctive choice and the one most amateur and semi-professional sides reach for. Dropping the line to the edge of the penalty area removes the space in behind almost entirely. A ball played over the top has nowhere to land except the goalkeeper's hands. This feels like a solution and is treated as one by a large share of the commentary around this fixture. It is not, and the reason is spatial rather than a matter of courage. When your back line sits inside your own box, you have relocated the contest into the exact zone where his work is strongest and where his height, his first-time striking, and his ability to attack a low ball across the six yard box are all maximized. You have also stretched the distance between your defensive line and your forward line to something like fifty yards, which means the moment your side wins the ball there is no outlet, the ball comes straight back, and the pressure is continuous. A team that defends this way for an hour concedes not to a single failure but to accumulated volume: twenty low crosses and cutbacks into a crowded area, one of which reaches him with a yard of separation.

Now take the opposite pole. A genuinely high line, pushed to the halfway line and held with an aggressive offside trap, removes the box territory and turns the contest into a foot race and a timing test. Against most forwards this is a reasonable trade. Against this one it is a poor trade delivered by most teams, for a specific reason: he is unusually fast for a player of his size, and he starts his run from the shoulder of the last defender rather than from a standing position level with him. That starting position means the trap is not being sprung against a static opponent. It is being sprung against a player already moving, already accelerating, and already reading the passer's hips. A trap that is a fifth of a second late does not produce an offside flag. It produces a clean run at your goalkeeper.

### Where should the last line stand against a forward who attacks the shoulder?

Roughly ten to eighteen yards inside your own half, and dynamically rather than at a fixed mark. Depth is set by pressure on the ball: forward when the carrier cannot look up, back five to eight yards the moment he can. That keeps the space behind defensible without relocating the contest into your box.

That controlled medium line is the recommendation of this blueprint, and the phrase carries two conditions rather than one. The first condition is that the line's position is dynamic and tied to the state of the ball, not to a chalk mark. When the ball carrier is under real pressure and cannot lift his head, the line steps forward, because the pass that punishes you cannot be played. When the carrier is free and facing forward with his head up, the line drops five to eight yards immediately, before the pass, not after it. This is the whole discipline. Sides that concede in behind are almost never sides whose defenders were slower than the forward. They are sides whose line was standing still at the moment the passer got his head up.

The second condition is that the line must be flat enough to make the offside law work for you without being so flat that a single mistimed step opens a fifteen yard corridor. The practical convention is that the deepest outfield defender sets the line and the others align to him within a yard, with the far-side centre-back checking his shoulder on a rhythm rather than on a cue. That check is a habit, not a reaction, and it is the single most trainable element of this entire plan at any level.

Shape follows from the line. A back four with two screening midfielders in front of it is the most reliable structure here, whether that is expressed as a 4-2-3-1, a 4-4-2 with a disciplined second striker, or a 4-1-4-1 in which the single pivot is supported by an eight who drops on a trigger. What matters is not the notation but the presence of two bodies covering the pocket in front of the last line, because that pocket is where the pass originates, and preventing the pass is easier than defending the run. A back three, played as a 5-3-2 or a 3-4-3 dropping into a five, is defensible for a specific reason and a specific squad: it gives you a spare centre-back who can be permanently oriented to the space behind rather than to a man, which is genuinely useful. It costs you a body in central midfield, which is where the passes are played. If you have a squad in which one centre-back is markedly quicker than the others, the back three converts that quickness into a permanent structural advantage, and the trade becomes worth it. If your three defenders are of similar pace, the back four is better, because the extra midfielder does more work than the extra defender.

There is one shape to avoid outright, and it is common. A back four with a single holding midfielder, playing high, with attacking full-backs and no rest structure. That arrangement produces exactly the picture this forward is designed to attack: a large pocket in front of the last line, no cover for the space between the centre-backs and the full-backs, and a recovery problem on every turnover. If your side plays that way as a matter of identity, the honest advice is to change it for this fixture rather than to hope. The general version of this shape argument, applied across forward types rather than to one player, belongs to the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/), which sets out why a template built for one threat type actively improves a forward of another type.

## The passer-first principle

Here is the claim this article advances and the one worth carrying away from it. Against a depth runner, the first defender is the player nearest the ball, not the player nearest the striker, because the run only becomes dangerous at the moment the passer is allowed to lift his head. Call it the passer-first principle. It sounds obvious written down and it is violated by almost every team that concedes a goal to this pattern.

The reason it is violated is that the eye is drawn to the duel. A coach watching from the technical area sees a large forward drifting across his centre-back and shouts at the centre-back. A supporter watching a replay sees the same picture and blames the same player. The camera follows the ball into the space and the last image before the goal is a defender trailing a runner, so the defender is the one who looks culpable. The actual failure happened three to four seconds earlier and thirty yards away, when a midfielder in possession was allowed to receive facing forward, settle, and scan. Once that happens, no back line on earth defends the resulting pass reliably, because the runner chooses the moment and the defender can only respond to it.

Break the sequence into its components and the intervention points become visible. The pattern requires four things to occur in order. A passer must receive in a position from which a forward pass is available. He must be permitted to face forward. He must be permitted to lift his head and locate the run. And the run must be timed to begin as his hips open rather than before. Remove any one of the four and the pattern fails. The cheapest of the four to remove, by a wide margin, is the second. Making a midfielder receive facing his own goal costs one defender a five yard sprint and a decision about approach angle. Defending the fourth, the run itself, costs you a forty yard sprint against a faster player with a head start.

This reframing changes who gets coached. In a team that has internalized the passer-first principle, the most heavily briefed player before this fixture is not the centre-back. It is the screening midfielder, and the second most heavily briefed is whichever forward or wide player is responsible for the initial angle of pressure on the ball. The centre-back's brief becomes comparatively simple: hold your reference, check your shoulder, do not follow the man out of the line, and be the player moving backward at the moment the ball is struck rather than the player standing still.

There is a corollary worth stating plainly, because it is the source of the most common in-game error. If your first defender cannot get to the passer in time, do not compensate by getting tighter to the striker. Compensate by dropping the line. Tightness without pressure on the ball is the worst combination in football against a runner, because a tight defender is a defender who has been given a fixed reference point that the runner controls. He can be spun, he can be pinned, and he can be attacked on either side because he has committed to a distance. A defender holding a controlled gap of two to three yards, oriented half open so he can see both the ball and the man, with the line behind him deep enough to allow a recovery step, is a much harder defender to beat than one breathing down the forward's neck. The instruction that gets shouted from the touchline is exactly inverted.

## The two centre-back profiles that can execute this plan

Two profiles work and a famous third does not. The first is the reader: a centre-back whose value is anticipation, line management, and the early step, who defends the pass before it happens. The second is the recoverer: a centre-back with genuine pace who can survive being wrong. The profile that fails is the pure aggressor who wins his reputation by getting tight and physical.

That third profile deserves a full explanation, because selecting him is the most frequent selection error in this fixture and because the reasoning behind the error is superficially sound. A large, combative, front-foot centre-back who dominates most forwards physically appears to be the natural answer to a large, powerful forward. Match strength with strength. The problem is that this forward's threat is not a strength contest and almost never resolves into one. He does not receive with his back to goal and try to hold off a marker for three seconds. He runs. The aggressor's defining habit, stepping in early and tight to make contact before the reception, hands him precisely the reference point he wants: a defender committed to a position, from which he can peel across the front, spin the far side, or simply hold his run half a yard beyond the shoulder and wait for the ball. Against a drop-and-link forward the aggressor is the right answer. Against this one he is a liability dressed as a solution.

The reader is the better of the two working profiles, and if you have one, he is the primary. This is the defender who sets the line by looking at the ball rather than at the man, whose head is on a swivel every two or three touches of the opposition's build-up, and who takes the first backward step half a second before the situation demands it. His advantage is that he is never actually caught flat, so the foot race that would defeat him never begins. His method is to make the pass in behind arrive into a space he has already retreated into. He is also the player who can hold an offside line when it genuinely helps, because he can distinguish between a carrier who can pass and a carrier who cannot, which is the only judgment that makes a trap safe.

The recoverer is the insurance policy and, at most levels below the very top, the more available profile. He may read the game only adequately, but he can be beaten by a yard at the moment of the pass and still arrive at the ball. His value is that he permits a slightly higher line, which compresses the whole block and improves your side's ability to sustain pressure and to counter. The pairing to aim for is one of each: the reader as the primary reference who organizes the line and the space, the recoverer as the covering defender positioned half a body behind him on the side the ball is most likely to come from. If you have two readers, hold a slightly deeper line and be disciplined. If you have two recoverers, you can be braver with the line and will need more coaching on the pressing triggers, because your defenders will be tempted to rely on their legs and step out of position.

Full-backs are part of this selection question and are usually treated as an afterthought. The relevant quality is not one on one defending in isolation. It is recovery running discipline, specifically the instinct to recover goal-side and inside rather than ball-side. A full-back who chases the ball carrier from behind on the outside is contributing nothing to the picture that actually hurts you, which is the cutback. A full-back who abandons the chase and sprints to the near post area is contributing a great deal. This is a coached behavior, not a talent, and it is one of the highest-return things a coach can install in a single week. It also generalises to every other fixture, which makes it a rare piece of opposition-specific preparation that leaves the team permanently better.

The last selection note concerns the screening midfielder. The job asks for a player who defends space rather than a player who defends a man, who is comfortable being the deepest of the two screens for long periods, and who has the positional patience to hold the pocket in front of the last line rather than being drawn to the ball. Ball-winning aggression is not the priority here and can be actively unhelpful, because the aggressive screen leaves the pocket to make a tackle he does not need to make. The instruction is to occupy the passing lane into the pocket, force the ball wide or backward, and never be the player who vacates the middle to chase a lateral pass.

## The Haaland containment sequence

The plan is easier to deliver as a sequence of phases than as a set of principles, because a player can hold one instruction at a time and because a coach needs to know which phase has failed when a chance is conceded. The table below is the whole blueprint in compressed form. Each phase carries a trigger that starts it, a primary job, and a failure signal, which is the observable sign that the phase has broken and the next one is now live. Coaching the failure signals matters as much as coaching the jobs, because a side that recognizes phase two has collapsed will execute phase four correctly instead of standing and watching.

| Phase | Trigger | Primary job | Failure signal | Immediate response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One: supply denial | Opposition centre-back or goalkeeper has the ball in the first phase | Curve the forward's press to close the inside lane, force the ball to a full-back on the touchline | A central midfielder receives facing forward between your lines | Screen steps to the ball side, line drops five yards before the pass |
| Two: mid-block reference | Ball settled in the opposition half or in midfield | Line height set by pressure on the ball, screen occupies the pocket, defenders check shoulders on a rhythm | Carrier gets his head up unopposed with a forward lane open | Both centre-backs take the retreating step immediately, goalkeeper advances to the top of the box |
| Three: box organization | Ball enters the wide area at or below the level of your penalty box | Far centre-back takes the near-post cutback line, near centre-back stays with the ball-side threat, screen drops to the penalty spot | Two defenders collapse toward the ball at the near post together | Nearest midfielder fills the vacated near-post lane, full-back recovers goal-side and inside |
| Four: transition recovery | Your side loses the ball in the opposition half | Nearest player delays the first forward pass, back line retreats as a unit rather than tracking individually | The first forward pass is played cleanly within two seconds of the turnover | Deepest defender abandons the line, becomes a pure spare man, goalkeeper starts his travel |
| Five: dead ball | Corner, wide free kick, or long throw against you | Blocker on the run, marker on the body, zone player defending the near-post flight | He receives a free run at the flight from more than four yards | Concede the corner again rather than the clearance, reset and re-mark |

Two things about this table are worth stating before the phases are described individually. The first is that the phases are not equally important. Phases one and two together determine roughly the entire outcome, because a chance that never begins does not need to be defended, and a team that executes them well will spend very little time in phase three. Coaches with limited preparation time should spend it disproportionately on the first two rows and accept that the last three will be imperfect. The second is that the failure signals are deliberately observable from the touchline and from a phone camera on a tripod at an amateur ground. None of them require data, tracking, or a paid subscription. That is intentional, because the majority of coaches who need this plan are working without any of those things. ## Phase one: cutting the supply before the runner matters

Denying supply is not the same as pressing high, and the confusion between the two costs teams goals. Pressing high is a decision about where you engage. Denying supply is a decision about which passing lane you close while you engage, and it can be executed from a mid-block just as well as from a front-foot press. The lane that matters is the one from a first-phase player, usually a centre-back or a deep-lying midfielder, into the pocket in front of your last line. Close that lane and the ball has to travel wide, and a ball in a wide area is a ball that must be crossed or cut back rather than played straight in behind.

The mechanics are a curve and a shoulder. Your forward approaches the ball carrier on a curved run that arrives with his body angled so that the inside lane sits in his cover shadow, and he continues moving to keep that shadow as the carrier shifts the ball. The instruction is not "press him" but "press him from an angle that leaves him only one direction." Delivered well by a single forward, this removes half the pitch. Delivered by two forwards working as a pair, with the second closing the pass into the pivot while the first curves onto the carrier, it removes the central corridor entirely and forces every entry into the wide channels, where the ball will need two or three additional actions before it becomes a chance.

The second element of supply denial is less discussed and more valuable: the treatment of the wide creator once the ball has been forced there. Having pushed the ball to a touchline, you have not solved anything if you then allow that player to turn inside and play an early diagonal. The full-back's job in that moment is not to win the ball. It is to hold a position that permits the pass down the line, which is harmless, and denies the pass inside, which is not. Against a side whose forward attacks the shoulder, the early diagonal from a wide midfielder is one of the two or three highest-value passes on the pitch, and it is defended by body angle rather than by tackling.

There is a third supply route that gets underrated because it looks crude. The direct ball from a goalkeeper or a centre-back into the channel, aimed to be run onto rather than headed. Teams that spend a week preparing for a possession-based build-up sometimes leave this entirely undefended, and it is the fastest way to reach the pattern you are trying to prevent. The defense against it is not a defensive one at all. It is the striker's and the wide forward's positioning at the moment your side loses the ball in the opposition half, which is why phase one and phase four are more closely related than they appear.

### Which passing lane has to be closed first?

Close the central lane into the pocket in front of your last line with a curved press and a screening midfielder, force every entry into the wide channels, then deny the wide player the pass inside by body angle rather than by tackling. Supply denial is a lane decision, not a decision about how high you press.

Applying this at a level below professional football requires one simplification, because the curved press with a maintained cover shadow is a genuinely hard skill and will not hold for ninety minutes with players who train twice a week. The simplification is to abandon the front-foot version and instead set the block ten yards deeper with an instruction that the two central midfielders never both leave the middle. You lose the aggressive supply denial and gain a permanently occupied pocket, which for most amateur sides is the better trade. What you must not do is attempt the sophisticated version, execute it at seventy percent, and end up with a high line and an empty middle, which combines the costs of both approaches.

## Phase two: the mid-block and its reference points

A block is a set of reference points, not a formation. Players who are told to hold a 4-4-2 shape will hold it relative to each other and drift as a unit toward the ball, which is how a compact side ends up with an entire half of the pitch undefended and a line that is simultaneously too high and too narrow. Players who are given reference points hold their position relative to something fixed, and the block keeps its meaning.

Four reference points carry this phase. The first belongs to the deepest defender and it is the ball, not the striker. He sets his depth by asking one question repeatedly: can the player on the ball play a forward pass into the space behind me right now. If the answer is yes, he is retreating. If the answer is no, he is advancing or holding. This is the reference that most directly prevents the goal you are trying to prevent, and it is why the passer-first principle is a positional instruction rather than an abstraction.

The second belongs to the far-side centre-back and it is his own shoulder. In the pattern that hurts most, the ball is on one side and the run is made across the face of the far centre-back, into the diagonal space between him and his full-back. The defender who is beaten in that pattern is almost always the one who has been watching the ball for four or five seconds without a single check behind him. The coaching point is a rhythm rather than a cue: a shoulder check roughly every two touches of the opposition's possession, made as a habit whether or not anything is happening. Trained as a habit it survives fatigue. Trained as a reaction it disappears in the eightieth minute, which is when it is needed.

The third reference belongs to the screening midfielder and it is the pocket itself, defined as the area roughly eight to fourteen yards in front of the last line and between the width of the penalty area. His instruction is that he occupies that ground and does not leave it to press a lateral pass, does not leave it to double a wide player, and does not leave it to follow a dropping forward beyond its far edge. If a runner from midfield arrives into the pocket, he passes him on to the nearest centre-back's awareness rather than following him out. Sides that concede in behind after a period of comfortable defending have usually just watched their screen get pulled fifteen yards to the touchline by a pass that was never a threat.

The fourth reference belongs to the full-backs and it is the inside shoulder of the centre-back next to them. The channel between a full-back and a centre-back is the second most productive route to a depth run and the one the runner will attack when the central lane is closed. The full-back holds a position that keeps him tight enough to that inside shoulder that the channel is not a corridor, accepts that the winger opposite him will therefore have space on the outside, and relies on the block's compactness and the screen's coverage to make that outside space unprofitable. This is a deliberate concession and needs to be named as one in the team meeting, otherwise the full-back will drift wider each time he is beaten on the outside and open the channel again by the hour mark.

Compactness numbers are worth putting into concrete terms, because "be compact" is not coachable and a distance is. The target is a block roughly thirty to thirty-five yards from the deepest defender to the highest forward and roughly the width of the penalty area plus five yards on each side. That is tighter than most sides think they are, and it will be uncomfortable for wide forwards who feel they are abandoning their winger. It is also the only geometry in which a screening midfielder can actually cover the pocket, because if the block is fifty yards deep the pocket is too large for one player and the instruction becomes meaningless.

One frequently asked question deserves a direct answer here. Should the block be zonal or should someone be given him as a man? The recommendation is zonal with a designated awareness, which is a different thing from man marking. A designated awareness means one centre-back is told that this forward is his primary reference within the zonal structure: he knows where he is at all times, he communicates his position to the line, and he takes him if he enters his zone. He does not follow him out of the zone. Pure man marking hands the runner control of the structure, because he can then take a defender wherever he wishes and open the space he actually wants for a teammate. It also removes the spare-man principle that makes the last line survivable. Man marking is defensible in one narrow case, discussed later, and it is a fallback rather than a plan.

## Phase three: organizing the penalty box on cutbacks and low crosses

The far centre-back takes the near-post cutback line and does not collapse toward the ball. The near-side defender handles the ball-side threat. The screening midfielder drops onto the penalty spot. The recovering full-back runs goal-side and inside, not ball-side. Two defenders converging on the near post is the failure that produces the goal.

The cutback is the pattern most likely to beat a well-organized side, precisely because a well-organized side is doing the other things correctly. If you have closed the central lane and denied the ball in behind, the opposition's remaining route is to reach the byline or the corner of your penalty area and pull the ball back across. That is not a failure of your plan. That is your plan working, and it produces the situation you must have rehearsed.

The geometry of the cutback threat is worth describing in detail, because it is misunderstood. A cutback is dangerous not because it is hard to defend but because it arrives into a zone that defenders are moving away from. Every defender in the box at that moment is facing the ball and drifting toward it, which means the six yard box and the penalty spot area behind them are opening at the same rate that the ball is traveling. A forward whose specialty is arriving at the near post and finishing first time is exploiting a structural drift, not beating a marker. He does not need to beat anyone. He needs to be the only player moving forward while everyone else moves sideways.

The answer is a designated non-participant. One defender, and it should be the far centre-back, is explicitly forbidden from following the ball into the wide area. His job when the ball reaches the byline zone is to occupy the near-post cutback line, which is the ground between the six yard box and the penalty spot on the ball side, facing so that he can see both the ball and the arriving runner. He will feel useless for large stretches. He will be doing the single most valuable defensive job on the pitch. This assignment must be explicit and named in the meeting, because the natural instinct of every centre-back in football is to help toward the ball, and an unnamed instruction loses to instinct.

The near-side defender's job is the ball-side threat, which is usually a second forward or an arriving midfielder, and the full-back's is the delivery. The screening midfielder, who has spent phase two occupying the pocket, drops to the penalty spot and handles the pull-back to the edge, which is the other end of the same pattern and the shot that punishes a side that has over-corrected toward the near post. The second screen or the far-side midfielder covers the back post. Once written out, this is five defenders with five distinct assignments and no duplication, and the reason it fails in matches is duplication: two players taking the same ground and none taking the ground behind them.

There is a variant to prepare for, the low cross from a deeper position rather than from the byline, which arrives faster and flatter. The defensive answer is the same assignment structure with one change: the far centre-back attacks the flight of the ball rather than holding the line, because a flat delivery across the six yard box is a ball that can be intercepted and a byline cutback usually is not. The distinguishing cue is the position of the crosser's standing foot and his body angle at the moment of contact. This is exactly the kind of detail that pays for the two hours an analyst spends clipping wide entries from previous matches, and it is the sort of preparation the [weaknesses explained breakdown](/2020/02/01/erling-haaland-weaknesses-explained/) treats from the other direction, where the same evidence is examined as a question about the limits of the forward's game rather than as a plan.

## Phase four: transition and the recovery hierarchy

Most goals of this type are conceded within eight seconds of the defending side losing the ball, and the defending side in that description is the one attacking. This is the phase that a coach preparing an opposition plan spends the least time on and should spend the most, because a block that is well organized in settled play is irrelevant if the goal arrives before the block exists.

The transition problem has a specific shape against a forward who lives on the last shoulder. At the moment your side loses possession in the opposition half, he is already positioned beyond your deepest defender or level with him, facing your goal, with no requirement to receive under pressure, turn, or beat anyone. The only thing standing between him and a run at your goalkeeper is the availability of a forward pass. Which means the transition instruction is a hierarchy of one item repeated: prevent the first forward pass.

The nearest player to the ball at the moment of the turnover does not attempt to win it back. He attempts to stand in the line of the forward pass and to slow the carrier by half a second, which is enough time for the second and third players to arrive. Counterpressing to win the ball is a legitimate strategy in general and a poor one in this specific fixture, because a counterpress that fails leaves you with fewer players between the ball and your goal than you had before you attempted it. The instruction is delay and deny, not win.

Behind that, the back line's instruction is to retreat as a unit rather than to track individually. Individual tracking is the failure mode: one centre-back turns and runs with the forward, the other holds, and a fifteen yard gap appears between them into which the pass is played. Retreating as a unit means all four defenders turn and travel at the same moment, keeping their spacing, giving up ground deliberately, and re-forming a line twenty yards deeper. Ground is cheap in transition. Structure is not.

The deepest defender has a modified role here. Once the transition is live and the first forward pass has been conceded, he stops being a line-setter and becomes a pure spare man, dropping five yards behind his partner and orienting himself entirely to the space rather than to any opponent. This is the moment the reader profile pays for itself, because the judgment of when a phase has moved from settled to transitional is a reading judgment and cannot be coached as a rule.

Rest defense, meaning the structure your team holds while it is attacking, is the upstream fix and the more valuable one. Against this profile the requirement is not complicated: two central defenders and one screening midfielder must remain behind the ball at all times, positioned so that the screen occupies the central lane and the two defenders are staggered rather than flat. Full-backs may push on, but not both, and the one who pushes does so on the far side from where the opposition's counter-attacking outlet sits. Sides that attack with both full-backs high, a single pivot, and two centre-backs on the halfway line are choosing to lose this fixture in the transition phase regardless of how well they defend in settled play.

The goalkeeper is part of transition and is usually briefed too late. His starting position when your side is attacking should be near the edge of his own penalty area, not on his line, and his instruction on the turnover is to begin traveling forward before the forward pass is played rather than after. Two or three of those early journeys per match convert a run at goal into a tidy clearance, and the cost of being wrong is a shot from forty yards which is a far cheaper mistake than a one on one.

## Phase five: set pieces, second balls, and the near-post flight

A dead ball removes almost everything the plan is built on. There is no passer to press, no line to manage, no lane to close. The contest is reduced to a body, a run, and a flight path, and a tall forward with a good leap in a crowded box is a genuine problem that no amount of shape will solve.

The recommendation is a hybrid rather than a pure system. A pure zonal scheme concedes the run, because a zonal defender defends a space and a good attacking header is made by a player arriving into that space with momentum against a player standing in it. A pure man scheme concedes the block, because attacking sides rehearse screens and a marker who is picked off has no cover behind him. The hybrid assigns a marker to the body, a blocker to the run, and two zonal players to the highest-value flight paths, which against this profile means the near-post area and the space between the six yard box and the penalty spot.

The blocker's job deserves elaboration because it is the part most sides omit. He does not mark anyone. He positions himself in the path the runner must take to arrive at the near post with momentum, and his objective is to make that run bend or slow. A run that arrives bent or slow is a run that produces a header without power or a header that misses the flight entirely. Contact must be legal and early, made while both players are moving and before the ball is delivered, and this is a coached skill that takes a session to install and pays out for the rest of the season.

The second ball is the other half of the dead-ball problem and the more frequently conceded one. A cleared corner that falls to the edge of the area against a side with no rest structure produces a second delivery into a disorganized box, which is where the marking scheme breaks down. Two players positioned at the edge of the box, one on each side of the D, is the standard answer, and the instruction on winning a second ball is to clear it wide and long rather than to attempt a counter-attack, because the counter is worth less than the reset.

Attacking free kicks and corners of your own carry a transition risk that is specific to this fixture and needs a decision made in advance. If a forward of this type is left up the pitch during your corners, you must leave two defenders back, which costs you two bodies in the box and reduces your own set-piece threat. That is a real cost and there is no clever way around it. The judgment is a scoreline judgment: leaving two back is correct at any state of the match other than chasing a goal in the final ten minutes, at which point the arithmetic changes and the risk becomes acceptable.

There is a related question about the tactical foul, and it is raised constantly. The answer is that a deliberate foul on a runner in the middle third is a legitimate and well-established defensive tool, that it is best committed by a midfielder rather than by a defender who is already booked, and that it must be committed early in the sequence rather than late. A foul committed as the runner is accelerating past the halfway line is a yellow card at worst and often not even that. A foul committed twenty yards later is a red card and a free kick in a dangerous area, which converts one problem into two. What the tactical foul is not is a plan. It is a release valve for a plan that has already failed, and a side that relies on it will run out of fouls, out of cards, or out of both.

## The goalkeeper's brief behind a controlled line

A controlled medium line is only coherent if the goalkeeper agrees to it, and the most common reason this plan collapses is a goalkeeper who defends his line while his defenders defend the space. The two jobs must be one job.

The starting position is the first item. Behind a line sitting fifteen yards inside its own half, the goalkeeper's default position in settled opposition possession is around the penalty spot, not on his goal line. That feels extreme to keepers trained in shot-stopping and looks reckless to supporters, and it is the correct position, because the space he is responsible for is the corridor between his defenders and his goal, and a keeper standing on his line cannot reach any of it. The adjustment is dynamic in the same way the line is: he advances as the ball goes wide or comes under pressure and retreats as the carrier gains a clean forward view.

The travel decision is the second item and it is a decision made early or not at all. A keeper who waits to see whether the through ball is reachable has already lost, because the runner is faster over the first ten yards than the keeper is and has a shorter distance to cover. The instruction is to commit at the moment the passer's hips open, which is the same cue the back line uses, and to commit fully. A keeper who travels ten yards and stops is in the worst position on the pitch. He is out of his goal and not at the ball.

The technique when he arrives late and the forward reaches the ball first is the third item and the one worth training separately. Against a first-time finisher, spreading and staying big for as long as possible beats going to ground early, because the finish arrives before a diving keeper has completed his movement and the shot is usually struck low and hard rather than lifted. Delaying the commitment to a shape until the striker's standing foot is planted is the coaching point. Against a forward who takes a touch first, the calculation changes and the early spread becomes correct, which is why the analyst's pre-match note about which finishing pattern is likely in which zone has real value to the goalkeeping department rather than being an academic exercise.

The fourth item is distribution, and it links back to phase four. A goalkeeper who claims a ball or makes a save and then plays a short pass to a centre-back under pressure has handed the ball back into the zone where the counter begins. Behind this plan, the safe outlet is long and wide toward the far touchline, and it should be rehearsed as a default rather than treated as a failure of composure. Playing out is a virtue in most contexts and a liability in this one.

The fifth item is communication, and it is the goalkeeper's largest contribution. He is the only player facing the entire pitch, which makes him the only player who can see both the passer and the runner simultaneously. His job in phase two is to call the line's depth out loud, continuously, based on the pressure on the ball. A back four that is being talked into position by a keeper who can see everything is a back four that is never caught standing still, which is the entire objective of the plan.

## Fallbacks when the first plan fails

Every counter-plan needs a stated second and third version, decided in advance and triggered by an observable condition rather than by panic. A coach who improvises a change while a goal is being celebrated makes it badly.

The first fallback triggers when the controlled medium line is being beaten repeatedly in behind despite correct pressure on the ball, which is the specific case where your centre-backs are simply not quick enough to survive the geometry. The observable condition is two clean reception opportunities in behind within twenty minutes, not one. The response is to drop the line eight to ten yards and to convert the second screening midfielder into a permanent extra body in the pocket, effectively turning a 4-2-3-1 into a 4-1-4-1 with a low block posture. You are accepting a worse territorial position and more crosses in exchange for removing the space that is beating you. This is the moment where the deep block, rejected as an opening posture, becomes the right answer, and the difference between a good coach and a stubborn one is recognizing it.

The second fallback triggers when the cutback pattern is producing repeated near-post arrivals despite the assignment being correct, which usually means the wide entry point is not being contested at all and your box is being asked to defend an unlimited number of deliveries. The observable condition is a run of wide entries with no pressure on the crosser. The response is not inside the box. It is to add a body to the wide area by dropping a wide midfielder alongside the full-back permanently, accepting that your own attacking outlet on that side is now gone. This is a genuine cost and it should be paid on one side only, chosen by which flank the deliveries are actually coming from rather than by which one you assumed pre-match.

The third fallback is man marking, and it is the last resort rather than a plan, for reasons already given. There is one narrow case where it becomes correct: when your side is defending a lead late in a match, the block is deep by necessity, and the space behind the line has therefore been eliminated by the scoreline rather than by choice. In that specific picture the marker's disadvantages largely disappear, because there is no space for the runner to be taken into and the marking assignment reduces to a body contest in a crowded area, which is a contest a strong defender can hold. Assign it to the physically strongest centre-back, tell him explicitly that he now follows the man everywhere including out of the box, and reorganize the remaining three defenders as a back three with a spare man. Do not attempt this in the first half.

The fourth fallback concerns the scoreline rather than the pattern. If your side goes two goals behind, this plan should be abandoned rather than adjusted, because a containment plan that requires patience is incompatible with a chase. The honest statement to make in the meeting beforehand is that the plan has a scoreline range within which it applies, and that outside that range the team is playing a different match with different risks. Teams that try to hold a containment posture while also chasing a two-goal deficit produce the picture that concedes four.

### Which observable condition tells you to change the plan?

Drop the line eight to ten yards and add a permanent second body to the pocket if you are being beaten in behind. Add a wide midfielder alongside the beaten full-back if the cutback volume is the problem. Switch to man marking only late, only when defending a lead, and only with a deep block already in place.

## What this plan concedes on purpose

A plan without a stated concession is a wish. Every defensive arrangement gives something away, and the difference between a plan and a hope is whether the coach chose what to give away or discovered it at half time.

This plan concedes possession, and it concedes it in volume. A side defending a controlled medium line with a compact block and a screening midfielder holding the pocket will not have the ball for long stretches, and the players must be told this in advance so they do not interpret it as failure. It concedes territory, particularly in the opposition's own half and in the middle third, because the block does not chase.

It concedes wide entries, which is the deliberate trade at the heart of the whole arrangement. Closing the central lane means the ball goes wide, and the ball going wide means crosses and cutbacks. A team executing this plan well will face a large number of deliveries and should expect to, because each one requires two or three further actions to become a chance while a ball played in behind requires none. The scoreboard measure that matters is not deliveries faced but receptions inside twelve yards conceded, and a coach who does not distinguish between the two will change a working plan at half time because the raw volume of crosses feels alarming.

It concedes shots from distance, since a compact block with a covered pocket invites the ball to be recycled and struck from twenty-five yards. That is a trade almost every serious defensive plan in football makes and it is a good one.

It concedes set-piece volume, because a side that defends deep and clears wide gives away corners and throws. This is the least comfortable of the concessions, since a dead ball is where the plan's protections evaporate, and it is the reason phase five is not optional. A team that executes phases one through four beautifully and defends corners badly will lose the match anyway, and will lose it in a way that makes the whole plan look wrong when it was not.

What the plan does not concede, and must not, is the reception in behind on a first-time ball, and the free arrival at the near post from a cutback. Those are the two actions the arrangement exists to remove. Every other loss on the list is affordable. If either of those two is happening repeatedly, the plan is not working and a fallback is required, which is why the observable failure conditions were named rather than left to feel.

## How the plan degrades with a slower or less organized squad

Almost everything written above assumes players who can hold a line, check a shoulder, and hold a positional discipline for ninety minutes. Most teams reading this do not have that, and a plan that only works at the top is not a plan. Here is what survives contact with a school team, a Sunday side, an academy age group, or a semi-professional squad training twice a week, and what should be discarded.

Discard the curved press with a maintained cover shadow. It is a genuine skill, it degrades quickly under fatigue, and a seventy percent version produces a high line with an empty middle, which is the worst possible arrangement. Replace it with a simple instruction that the forward stands in the passing lane into the pivot and does not chase, and set the block ten yards deeper than the professional version.

Discard the dynamic offside line. Amateur defenders cannot step up in unison reliably, and a mistimed step produces a corridor rather than a flag. Replace it with a fixed depth and a spare man. Nominate one centre-back as the permanent cover who plays three to five yards behind his partner at all times, with no line-stepping responsibility whatsoever. You lose the offside trap, which you were never going to execute anyway, and you gain a body in the space, which is the thing you actually needed. This single change is the highest-return adjustment available at amateur level and it does more than any amount of talking about compactness.

Keep the shoulder check. It costs nothing, it requires no athleticism, and it is trainable in a fortnight. A defender who looks behind him every two or three touches of the opposition's possession is a fundamentally different defender from one who does not, and the habit transfers to every fixture.

Keep the near-post assignment on cutbacks, and keep it simple: one named defender, told he is not allowed to move toward the ball when it goes wide, standing between the six yard box and the penalty spot. Do not attempt the five-role box structure. Two clear jobs executed are worth more than five jobs half understood.

Keep the goal-side recovery instruction for full-backs. This is coachable, cheap, and the behavior it replaces is actively harmful.

Discard the sophisticated rest defense and replace it with a rule expressed as a number: one full-back forward at a time, never two, and the deepest midfielder never crosses the halfway line. Rules that are numbers survive pressure. Rules that are principles do not.

There is a version of this fixture that youth and school coaches face more often than the professional one, which is a much stronger opponent with one physically dominant forward playing against a back line that is smaller and slower. The temptation is to double up on him permanently. Resist it for the same structural reason given earlier: doubling hands him the ability to remove two of your players from the picture by standing somewhere useless, and it opens the space that beats you. The better answer at that level is the spare man plus a deeper line plus a permanent extra midfielder in the middle, which is a 5-4-1 in all but name and which is an honest response to a genuine mismatch rather than a tactical embarrassment.

The physical demands of holding a compact block against a side that will have most of the ball are real and are often the point at which the plan collapses rather than any tactical failure. Preparing the squad to sustain the running load, and structuring the week so the block is defended as well in the last twenty minutes as in the first, is a conditioning question as much as a coaching one.

## The three observable triggers an analyst should clip before the match

An analyst preparing this fixture without a data budget can produce most of the value in about two hours of viewing, and the value is concentrated in three specific things rather than in a general impression.

The first is the passer. Identify which two or three players in the opposition side actually play the ball into the space behind, and clip every instance. There will be fewer of them than expected, and they will be identifiable by role rather than by name: usually a ball-playing centre-back, a deep-lying midfielder with a long passing range, and one wide creator. Once the list exists, the pressing brief writes itself, because you are no longer pressing a team, you are pressing three players, and the instruction to your midfield becomes concrete.

The second is the trigger moment. Watch the runs rather than the passes and note what starts them. Look for the relationship between the run and the carrier's body position, and clip the frames where the run begins. The purpose is to hand your back line a visual cue that they can recognize in real time rather than an instruction they can only apply retrospectively. A defender who has seen twenty clips of the same starting cue reacts half a second faster, and half a second is the entire margin in this contest.

The third is the wide entry. Chart where the deliveries come from and what type they are, distinguishing byline cutbacks from deeper low crosses from high balls to the back post. Each requires a different box arrangement, and a side that prepares for one and faces another will look disorganized despite having prepared. This is also where you identify whether the near-post arrival is being fed from the left or the right, which determines which of your centre-backs takes the non-participant role rather than leaving it to be worked out on the pitch.

What not to spend time on is a compilation of goals. Goal reels are the least useful preparation material available, because a goal is the end of a sequence and the information you need is in the middle of it. Clip build-ups that did not produce goals and you will learn more, because the pattern is the same and the defensive intervention that worked is visible.

## Installing the plan on the training pitch in a single week

A blueprint that is only a document changes nothing. The behaviors in this plan need rehearsal, and a normal match week gives a coach three sessions, one of which has to be light. What follows is what fits.

The first session belongs to the line and the passer. The exercise is a phase practice on two thirds of the pitch, with a defensive unit of four defenders and two midfielders against six or seven attackers who are instructed to play forward at every opportunity. The condition that produces the learning is a scoring rule: the attacking side scores double for any goal that originates from a pass into the space behind, and the defending side scores a point for every ten-second period in which no forward pass is available. That rule forces the defensive unit to solve the problem structurally rather than by tackling, and it makes the connection between pressure on the ball and line depth visible within about fifteen minutes without the coach having to explain it.

The coaching points during that session are narrow and should stay narrow. Who is the first defender. Is the line moving before the pass or after it. Is the screen in the pocket or drawn to the ball. Resist the urge to coach anything else, including things that are going wrong, because a session that coaches six things installs none.

The second session belongs to the box. The exercise is a repeated wide-entry practice with a live crosser and a functional box of four defenders, a goalkeeper, and three attackers, with the delivery type varied deliberately across byline cutbacks, deeper low crosses, and back-post balls. The condition is that the far centre-back may not enter the wide half of the box, enforced by a line of cones he cannot cross. Physical constraints teach positional discipline far faster than instructions do, because a player who runs into a cone learns something a player who is shouted at does not.

The third session, the light one, belongs to transition and dead balls. Transition work is short, sharp, and repeated: a small-sided game with a turnover rule where the coach calls a change of possession at random and the defending side has three seconds to prevent a forward pass. Six repetitions of five seconds each teaches more than twenty minutes of continuous play. The dead-ball work is walked through rather than played, with the blocker's path rehearsed at half speed until the geometry is obvious to everyone in the box.

What cannot be installed in a week is the shoulder-check habit, which takes longer and should be treated as a season-long behavior introduced independently of any single fixture. Coaches who attempt to install it in the same week they install everything else will get a squad that checks its shoulders for the first fifteen minutes and then stops.

Sustaining a compact block against a side that keeps the ball is a physical problem as much as a tactical one, and the last twenty minutes are where plans of this kind actually fail. The demand is not long-distance running. It is repeated short recoveries with almost no rest between them, made by players who are also being asked to hold exact positions while tired, and a week structured around the tactical work alone will produce a side that defends the first hour correctly and the last half hour by instinct. Build the load around the shape you intend to hold. A coach who wants to keep the phase table, annotate it against the squad, and assemble the whole match-week plan in one place can do that inside the same opposition dossier on VaultBook, which is built to hold the session plan, the clips, and the assignments together rather than in three separate places.

## Managing the plan during the match

Preparation ends at kick-off and the second half of the job is a set of decisions made in real time, most of which should be pre-decided rather than improvised.

The first decision is what to change after conceding. The instinct after conceding a goal in behind is to drop the line, and it is frequently wrong, because a single goal is not evidence that the structure has failed. Look at the mechanism of the goal rather than the fact of it. If the passer was under pressure and the line was moving and the pass was still perfect, that is an accepted cost of a working plan and the correct response is to change nothing. If the passer had four seconds and a clear view, the plan did not fail, the execution did, and the correction is a coaching one about the first defender rather than a structural one. Only when the same picture repeats with correct execution should the line drop.

The second decision is the substitution trigger. The most valuable substitution in this plan is not an attacking one. It is the replacement of the screening midfielder around the hour mark, because that role's positional discipline degrades with fatigue faster than any other on the pitch and the degradation is invisible until a goal exposes it. Plan for it. The second most valuable is a fresh full-back, for the same reason applied to recovery running.

The third decision concerns the state of the match. A one-goal lead against a side with this kind of forward is the most dangerous scoreline in football to defend, because it invites the defending team to drop deeper by instinct, which relocates the contest into the box where the forward is strongest. The instruction from the touchline with fifteen minutes remaining should be to hold the line, not to drop it, and to keep at least one forward high enough to make the opposition's centre-backs think about a counter. A team that surrenders the halfway line with fifteen minutes to go has invited the pattern it spent eighty minutes preventing.

The fourth decision is about your own attacking risk, and it is the one most often made by feel. Every attacking commitment you make is a transition risk in a fixture where transition is the primary danger. That does not mean you should not attack. It means the commitment should be made in a structured way, with the same two centre-backs and one screen behind the ball, and it means the moments to take a genuine risk are chosen rather than continuous: after a set piece you have won in a wide area, after a substitution has just changed the opposition's shape, and in the ten minutes after half time when their structure is most likely to be resetting.

The fifth is the message at half time, and it should be prepared in advance. If the plan is working, the danger is that the players do not believe it is, because they will have had very little of the ball and the raw volume of opposition territory will feel like a battering. Naming the concessions before kick-off is what makes the half-time message credible, because the coach can then point to the list and say that everything that has happened was chosen. A squad that believes the plan holds it. A squad that suspects it is being overrun abandons it around the sixtieth minute, which is exactly when it stops working.

## Answering the loudest arguments about how to stop Erling Haaland

Six positions circulate constantly whenever this question comes up, in match threads, on social media, and in television analysis. Each deserves a straight answer rather than being ignored.

The first is that he cannot be stopped and no plan matters. This is the most seductive of the six because it contains a truth. No defensive arrangement drives the probability of conceding to zero against a forward of this quality, and no serious analyst claims otherwise. What follows from that truth is not that planning is pointless. It is that the objective needs stating correctly. The measurable target is the number and quality of reception opportunities he gets inside the penalty area, and that number demonstrably moves depending on how the defending side is set up. Teams that hold a controlled line and defend the passer restrict it. Teams that arrive with no plan and get tight do not. The fatalist position is usually deployed as permission to prepare badly.

The second is that a deep block is the obvious answer. Addressed at length already and worth restating because it is the single most consequential error available. A deep block removes the space behind and relocates the contest into the twelve yard zone where his finishing, his height, and his near-post arrival are all at their most dangerous, while simultaneously guaranteeing that your side cannot get out and that the delivery volume will be enormous. It is the correct posture in two specific circumstances, which are a squad whose centre-backs genuinely cannot cope with any space at all, and the closing stages of a match you are leading. It is a poor opening posture for most teams.

The third is that the way to handle him is to foul him early and make him aware of you. There is a legitimate tactical foul argument, covered above, and this is not it. The version circulating in the discussion is about intimidation, and it fails on mechanics rather than on ethics. Physical contact before the ball arrives is not the problem this forward is solving, because his threat is a timing and space problem. Early contact tells him where you are, which is information that helps a runner. It also risks a card that removes your best defender from a match you needed him for, and it hands over a set piece in a dangerous area, which is the phase where your plan is weakest.

The fourth is that he only scores from close range, so denying crosses is sufficient. The premise is closer to true than most premises in this discussion and the conclusion does not follow. A high concentration of close-range finishes describes where a forward finishes, not how he gets there, and it is a product of movement, timing, and service rather than a limitation. Denying crosses is one of two necessary jobs. The other is denying the ball played into the space behind, which does not involve a cross at all and which is the action that generates his most dangerous chances. A team that prepares only for the cross has prepared for half the problem, and the half it has ignored is the faster half.

The fifth is that a big, physical centre-back can handle him. This is the selection error dismantled earlier and it survives because it feels like common sense. Strength matters at set pieces and in the box, where the physically strongest defender should indeed be positioned. It matters much less in open play, where the contest is decided before contact occurs. The defender who handles this profile best is the one who reads the passer, not the one who wins a shoulder-to-shoulder duel. A squad that has both qualities in one player is fortunate. A squad choosing between them should choose the reader for open play and use the physical defender in the box and on dead balls.

The sixth is that pressing his team high is suicidal. This one is half right and gets applied too broadly. A maximal press with a genuinely high line against a side that can play direct into the space behind is a bad idea. A structured mid-block with aggressive pressure on the ball and a controlled line is not the same thing and is a very different proposition, because pressure on the ball is precisely what makes the space behind undefendable for the opposition rather than for you. The distinction the argument misses is between line height and ball pressure, which are separable decisions. High pressure with a medium line is the combination this plan recommends and it is neither of the two things the argument imagines.

A seventh position deserves mention because it drives a large share of the discussion without being a tactical claim at all, which is the comparison with other forwards of similar standing and the argument about who is genuinely harder to prepare for. That is a real question with a defensible answer and it belongs to the [worked comparison of the two defensive problems](/2020/05/15/haaland-vs-mbappe-harder-to-defend/), which resolves it on the defensive resources each threat consumes rather than on which forward is better.

## The duel itself: what a centre-back does in the two seconds that decide it

Everything structural has now been covered, and structure removes most of the danger. It does not remove all of it, and a centre-back will find himself in a live one against one somewhere between three and eight times in a match. Those moments have their own craft and it is teachable.

The starting distance is the first variable and the one most often wrong. Two to three yards is the working range in open play, which is closer than a defender expects to be told and much further than the touchline instruction to get tight implies. Inside two yards the defender has no reaction space and can be spun. Beyond four yards he has surrendered the reception, which against a forward who can strike a ball first time is a real cost rather than an acceptable one. The distance is also not fixed. It shortens as the ball travels toward the striker and lengthens as the passer looks up, because the danger in the second case is the run rather than the reception.

Body orientation is the second variable and it is worth more than distance. The defender should be half open, meaning his hips are turned roughly forty-five degrees so that he can see the ball and the man in the same field of view without turning his head fully in either direction. A defender square to the striker cannot see the pass coming. A defender square to the ball has lost the man. The half-open posture is uncomfortable for defenders trained to face their opponent and it is the single technical adjustment that most improves this duel.

The side to show is the third variable and it should be decided in advance rather than by feel. Against a left-footed forward inside the penalty area, forcing the reception onto the right side narrows the angles from which the finish arrives at his usual standard, which is a genuine and observable pattern rather than a claim about a deficiency. Outside the box, in the running duel, the side to show is away from the goal and away from support, which usually means the touchline. Both instructions can hold at once because they apply in different zones, and the defender needs to be told which zone he is in rather than being given a single rule for the whole pitch.

The first contact rule applies here as it does in most duels against powerful forwards, and it is a starting-position decision rather than a strength one. The defender who is moving forward at the moment of contact wins the contact regardless of relative size, and the defender who is standing still or moving backward loses it regardless of relative size. Which means the outcome of a physical contest is usually determined two seconds earlier, by whether the defender read the pass early enough to be traveling toward it.

### What is the correct starting distance in the duel?

Two to three yards in open play, half open so the ball and the man are both visible, shortening as the ball travels toward him and lengthening as the passer lifts his head. Tighter than two yards surrenders reaction space and hands a runner a fixed reference point he can attack on either side.

The last element of the duel is recovery after being beaten, and it is a discipline rather than a skill. A defender who has been beaten by a run should not attempt to re-establish contact with the runner's body, because contact from behind at speed produces fouls and cards without producing tackles. He should run the same line as the runner, half a yard inside, and aim to arrive at the point of the shot rather than at the man. That angle turns a clean strike into a blocked or deflected one often enough to be worth coaching, and it keeps the defender out of the referee's notebook.

## Two defenders on one forward: when doubling helps and when it hands over the pitch

The question of whether to double up is asked in every meeting about this fixture and it is usually answered badly in both directions.

Permanent doubling, meaning two defenders assigned to one forward for the duration, is a losing arrangement and the reason is arithmetic rather than tactics. A forward who occupies two defenders by standing still has removed two players from your defensive structure at no cost to his own side, which means the remaining nine are defending eleven. Against a team that can pass, that deficit will be found within twenty minutes, usually by a midfielder arriving unmarked into the space your second defender vacated. The forward does not even need to touch the ball for this to work in his favor, which is what makes the arrangement so poor.

Situational doubling is different and is a normal part of good defending. It happens when the ball and the man arrive in the same place and a second defender is already nearby, which requires no assignment at all, only the habit of covering. The nearest midfielder dropping in behind a centre-back who has stepped out is situational doubling. So is a full-back tucking inside as a spare man when the ball is on the far side. Both are structural coverage rather than a marking decision, and they cost nothing because the second player was going to be in that area regardless.

The intermediate case, and the one worth thinking about carefully, is the temporary double in a defined zone. This is an instruction of the form: if he receives inside the penalty area with his back to goal, the nearest midfielder joins immediately. Zone-limited doubling is defensible because the trigger is specific, the duration is short, and the vacated space is one the opposition cannot immediately use. It is also easy to coach, because the condition is observable rather than a judgment call.

The related question about the second forward matters here and is often overlooked entirely. A plan built entirely around one player is a plan that a good attacking side will beat by using someone else, and a defensive structure that has committed extra bodies to one threat has by definition weakened itself elsewhere. Any version of this blueprint has to be checked against the question of who else is in the team and what happens when your extra attention creates a free player. The general principle is that the marginal defender is worth more as a spare man in the structure than as a second body on any individual, which is the same conclusion the doubling arithmetic reaches by a different route.


## When the reference points move: coping with a change in his role

A blueprint written against one role becomes fragile the moment the role changes, and opposition coaches change roles specifically because a well-drilled defensive plan is a target. Three variations turn up often enough to prepare for.

The first is the drift wide. Instead of holding the central shoulder, the forward starts from a wider position, usually toward the side where your weaker full-back is, and attacks the channel between that full-back and the near centre-back rather than the space between the two central defenders. The defensive error this produces is predictable: the centre-back follows him wide, the back line becomes a back three plus a passenger, and the space he actually wanted opens in the middle for a runner from deep. The correction is that the centre-back does not travel beyond the width of the penalty area. Past that point the forward becomes the full-back's problem with the screening midfielder covering the inside lane, and the centre-back stays home and takes the runner instead. This is a hand-off rule and it must be rehearsed, because the moment of the hand-off is where hesitation produces a free reception.

The second variation is the drop into the pocket. A forward who normally plays beyond the last line occasionally comes short to receive, and the defensive question is whether the centre-back follows. Against this profile the answer is usually no, for the same structural reason that has applied throughout: following him out breaks the line and opens the space behind for someone else, which is very often the actual purpose of the movement. The screening midfielder handles the reception from behind, arriving on the forward's blind side as the ball travels, and the centre-back holds. There is one exception, which is a reception in the pocket with acres of space and no midfielder within range, at which point the centre-back steps and the covering defender slides across to become the spare man. The trigger for the exception is the absence of a screen, not the presence of the forward.

The third variation is the front two. Playing alongside a second striker changes the arithmetic of the plan more than the other two variations combined, because it removes your spare man. A back four against a lone forward has two central defenders for one attacker plus a covering full-back, which is what makes the zonal structure work. A back four against two central attackers has a one to one relationship in the middle and no natural cover, which means the reading centre-back can no longer take the early backward step without leaving his partner isolated. There are two answers and both are legitimate. The first is to drop the screening midfielder permanently into the line when the ball is in the final third, effectively becoming a back five in the moments that matter, which costs you midfield presence but restores the spare man. The second is to change shape properly and start with a back three, which is more honest and easier to execute but commits you before kick-off.

The fourth situation is not a role change at all but a scoreline change, and it is worth naming because it produces the same disorientation. A side chasing a goal will push its full-backs high and commit numbers, which means the forward is now working against fewer defenders with more space and your carefully constructed reference points have gone. Prepare for this by deciding in advance which two players never join the attack regardless of the state of the match, and by treating that as a non-negotiable rather than a preference. Sides that make this decision at the moment of desperation do not make it.

Across all four situations the underlying instruction is the same and is the reason the passer-first principle is a principle rather than a tactic. Every one of these variations changes where the forward starts, which changes who marks him and where the duel happens. None of them changes the fact that the threat requires a passer with his head up and a forward pass available. A team that has genuinely internalized the passer-first idea adapts to all four variations without a new plan, because the intervention point has not moved even though everything downstream of it has.
## The verdict

The plan is a passer-first plan delivered from a controlled medium line. Defend the moment the ball carrier is allowed to lift his head, tie the depth of the last line to the pressure on the ball rather than to the position of the striker, pair a reading centre-back with a recovering one, give the screening midfielder the pocket and nothing else, name one defender as the non-participant who owns the near-post cutback line, and instruct the goalkeeper to defend the corridor in front of him rather than his goal line. Rehearse the transition rule that the nearest player delays the first forward pass rather than trying to win the ball, and treat the dead ball as a separate problem with a hybrid marking scheme and a designated blocker.

Then say out loud what you are giving away, which is possession, territory, wide entries, shots from distance, and corners, and hold the line at half time when the volume of those things makes the squad uncomfortable. The plan does not produce clean sheets by right. It produces a match in which the reception opportunities inside twelve yards are few and contested, which is the only version of this contest a defending side can realistically win.

The instruction that will be shouted from every touchline and repeated in every commentary box is to get tight. Against this profile that instruction hands a runner a fixed reference point, removes the defender's reaction space, and leaves the passer entirely unpressured, which is the exact combination the goal requires. The whole of this blueprint can be compressed into a refusal to follow it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Erling Haaland?

You defend the passer rather than the runner. Hold a controlled medium line roughly ten to eighteen yards inside your own half, tie its depth to the pressure on the ball rather than to the striker's position, and keep two screening midfielders in the pocket in front of it so the pass into that pocket is never available. Assign a reading centre-back as the primary reference and a quicker one as cover. When the ball reaches a wide area, one defender is explicitly forbidden from following it and instead owns the near-post cutback line. Accept that this concedes possession, territory, and a large volume of wide entries. No arrangement reduces the danger to zero, but the number of receptions he gets inside twelve yards moves substantially depending on whether the side in front of him is defending the pass or the man.

### Q: What defensive shape works against Erling Haaland?

A back four with two screening midfielders is the most reliable structure, expressed as a 4-2-3-1, a 4-4-2 with a disciplined second forward, or a 4-1-4-1 with an eight who drops on a trigger. What matters is two bodies covering the pocket in front of the last line, since that is where the decisive pass originates. A back three, defending as a 5-3-2, is a legitimate alternative if one of your central defenders is markedly quicker than the others, because it converts that pace into a permanent spare man oriented to the space rather than to an opponent. The arrangement to avoid is a high back four with a single pivot and both full-backs advanced, which produces a large unguarded pocket, an open channel on each side, and a recovery problem on every turnover.

### Q: Which type of centre-back handles Erling Haaland best?

Two profiles work. The reader defends by anticipation and line management, taking his backward step half a second before the situation demands it, so the foot race never begins. The recoverer has genuine pace and can survive being wrong, which lets the whole block sit higher. Pair one of each, with the reader as the organizing reference and the recoverer positioned half a body behind on the side the ball is likely to arrive from. The profile that fails is the aggressive front-foot defender who builds his reputation on getting tight and physical. His defining habit hands a runner a fixed reference point to attack on either side, and the contest almost never resolves into the strength duel he is selected to win.

### Q: Should you play a high line against Erling Haaland?

Not a maximal one, and the reason is his starting position rather than his speed. He runs from the shoulder of the last defender rather than from a standing position level with it, so a trap is being sprung against a player already accelerating and already reading the passer's hips. A trap that is a fifth of a second late does not produce a flag, it produces a clean run at your goalkeeper. The recommendation is a controlled medium line inside your own half, dynamic rather than fixed, stepping forward when the carrier is under pressure and dropping five to eight yards the moment he is free to look up. High ball pressure and a moderate line are separable decisions, and that combination is the one this plan asks for.

### Q: Does an offside trap work on Erling Haaland?

As an occasional tool inside a coherent structure, yes. As a system, no. A trap only becomes safe when the player on the ball cannot play the pass at the moment the line steps, which means the trap is really a function of pressure on the ball rather than of defensive courage. Sides that trap on a call, a raised arm, or a fixed cue against a forward who starts beyond the shoulder are gambling on timing they do not control. A reading centre-back can judge when the carrier is genuinely unable to release the ball and can step in those moments, which is worth a handful of restarts across a match. Building a plan on it invites the one failure that produces a run at your goalkeeper rather than a free kick in midfield.

### Q: Should two defenders mark Erling Haaland at once?

Not permanently. A forward who occupies two defenders by standing still has removed two players from your structure at no cost to his own side, leaving nine to defend against eleven, and a passing side will find that deficit within twenty minutes through a runner arriving unmarked into the vacated ground. Situational doubling is different and is simply good defending: a midfielder dropping behind a centre-back who has stepped out, or a far full-back tucking in as a spare man. The useful intermediate version is a zone-limited instruction, such as the nearest midfielder joining immediately if the reception happens inside the penalty area with his back to goal. Specific trigger, short duration, no lasting hole.

### Q: How do you cut off the service to Erling Haaland?

Supply denial is a lane decision rather than a decision about how high to press. Close the central corridor into the pocket in front of your last line, using a forward who approaches on a curved run with the inside lane held in his cover shadow, supported by a screening midfielder who occupies that pocket and never leaves it to chase a lateral pass. Once the ball has been forced wide, the full-back's job is body angle rather than tackling: allow the pass down the line, which is harmless, and deny the early diagonal inside, which is not. Do not neglect the direct ball from a goalkeeper or centre-back into the channel, which is defended by where your forwards stand at the moment you lose possession rather than by anything your back line does.

### Q: What should a goalkeeper do when Erling Haaland is through on goal?

The decision is made earlier than the moment itself. Behind a controlled line the keeper's default position in settled possession is near his own penalty spot, and his commitment to travel should begin when the passer's hips open rather than after the ball is struck, because the runner is faster over the first ten yards and has less ground to cover. Half a journey is the worst outcome available, so the commitment must be full. If he arrives late and the forward reaches the ball first, spreading and staying big for as long as possible beats going to ground early, since a first-time finish arrives before a diving keeper has completed his movement and is usually struck low and hard rather than lifted.

### Q: How do you defend a cutback with Erling Haaland in the box?

The danger is a structural drift rather than a marking failure. Once the ball reaches the byline zone every defender is facing it and moving toward it, which opens the ground behind them at the same rate the ball travels, and the forward simply becomes the only player moving forward. The answer is a designated non-participant. Name the far centre-back and forbid him from following the ball into the wide area; his ground is between the six yard box and the penalty spot on the ball side, facing so he can see both the delivery and the arriving runner. The near-side defender takes the ball-side threat, the screening midfielder drops to the penalty spot for the pull-back, and the recovering full-back runs goal-side and inside. Duplication is the failure mode.

### Q: Is a tactical foul the right answer against Erling Haaland?

A deliberate foul in the middle third is a legitimate defensive tool and is best committed by a midfielder rather than by a defender already carrying a card, and it has to arrive early in the sequence. A foul as the runner is accelerating past halfway is a caution at worst. The same foul twenty yards later is a dismissal and a free kick in a dangerous zone, which converts one problem into two. What the tactical foul is not is a plan. It is a release valve for a plan that has already failed, and a side relying on it runs out of fouls, cards, or both. The separate argument about fouling early to intimidate fails on mechanics: contact before the ball arrives tells a runner where you are, which is information that helps him.

### Q: What does a low block need to do against Erling Haaland?

A low block accepts that the contest happens inside twelve yards, so it has to be exceptional at the one thing that zone demands, which is box organization on repeated deliveries. That means the near-post cutback line is owned by a named defender for ninety minutes, the penalty spot is occupied by a dropping midfielder, the back post is covered rather than assumed, and the full-backs recover goal-side rather than chasing the crosser. It also has to solve the outlet problem, because a block that cannot get out concedes continuous volume until something breaks. Leaving one forward high enough to threaten a counter is not a luxury in this arrangement, it is what stops the siege from becoming permanent. The low block is a correct posture late while leading, and a poor one from kick-off.

### Q: How do you stop Erling Haaland in transition?

Prevent the first forward pass. At the moment you lose the ball in the opposition half he is already beyond or level with your deepest defender, facing your goal, needing neither to turn nor to beat anyone, so the only variable left is whether the pass is available. The nearest player to the turnover does not try to win the ball back. He stands in the line of the forward pass and delays the carrier by half a second, which is enough for the second and third players to arrive. Behind him the back line retreats as a unit rather than tracking individually, since individual tracking is what opens a fifteen yard gap between the two central defenders. Upstream, the fix is rest structure: two central defenders and one screening midfielder behind the ball at all times, and never both full-backs advanced.

### Q: How should an amateur side plan against an Erling Haaland type striker?

Discard the parts that need professional repetition and keep the parts that do not. Abandon the curved press with a maintained cover shadow, since a seventy percent version produces a high line with an empty middle. Abandon the dynamic offside line entirely and replace it with a permanent spare man: nominate one centre-back who plays three to five yards behind his partner at all times with no line-stepping duty whatsoever. Keep the shoulder-check habit, which costs nothing and is trainable in a fortnight. Keep one named defender on the near-post cutback line with a simple instruction not to move toward the ball. Replace principles with numbers wherever possible, such as one full-back forward at a time and the deepest midfielder never crossing halfway, because numeric rules survive fatigue and abstract ones do not.

### Q: How do you defend set pieces when Erling Haaland attacks the box?

Use a hybrid rather than a pure system. Pure zonal marking concedes the run, because an attacker arriving with momentum beats a defender standing in a space. Pure man marking concedes the block, because rehearsed screens leave a picked-off marker with no cover. Assign a marker to the body, a blocker to the run, and two zonal defenders to the highest-value flight paths, which here means the near-post area and the ground between the six yard box and the penalty spot. The blocker marks nobody; he stands in the path the run must take and makes it bend or slow, with contact made legally and early while both players are moving. Add two players on the edge of the area for second balls, and clear those wide and long rather than trying to counter.

### Q: What is the fallback when the first plan against Erling Haaland fails?

Decide the triggers before kick-off so the change is not made in the emotion of conceding. If you are beaten in behind twice within twenty minutes despite correct pressure on the ball, drop the line eight to ten yards and convert the second screening midfielder into a permanent extra body in the pocket. If the problem is cutback volume with no pressure on the crosser, add a wide midfielder alongside the beaten full-back on that flank only, accepting the loss of your attacking outlet there. Man marking is the third fallback and belongs only to the closing stages while leading, when the scoreline has already eliminated the space behind. Two goals down, abandon the plan rather than adjusting it, because a containment posture and a chase cannot coexist.

### Q: Which sides have coped with Erling Haaland and how did they do it?

The sides that cope share a profile rather than a formation. They keep the ball themselves for meaningful stretches, which removes the transition moments where the threat is cheapest to produce. They defend with a compact block whose depth responds to pressure on the ball rather than sitting at a fixed height. They put a genuine screen in front of the back line and keep it there. They have at least one central defender comfortable making the early backward step, and they defend the penalty area with named assignments on cutbacks rather than with collective drift toward the ball. What they do not do is arrive with a marking instruction and hope. The common factor is that the preparation is aimed at the supply and the space, and the duel is treated as the last line of the plan rather than the first.
