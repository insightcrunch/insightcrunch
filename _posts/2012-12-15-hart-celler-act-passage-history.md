A reader who finishes this legislative history should be able to answer one test question: why did a proposal to end the national origins quota formula, which had failed for nearly two decades, pass by lopsided bipartisan margins in 1965? The answer has three parts. First, name the trade that secured restrictionist votes. Second, state precisely what the sponsors predicted the bill would do, in their own words and with proper citations. Third, explain what actually happened afterward. This account covers the long failure, the 1952 veto, the changed conditions of the 89th Congress, the path of H.R. 2580 through both chambers, the bargains struck along the way, the predictions entered into the record, and the debate among historians over whether those predictions were deception or honest error.

### What trade secured restrictionist votes for the Hart-Celler Act?

Two bargains carried the bill past its opponents. In the House, the family-weighted preference structure won over restrictionist members who expected it to preserve the existing ethnic composition. In the Senate, a new ceiling of 120,000 on Western Hemisphere immigration, demanded by Sam Ervin and Everett Dirksen, brought restrictionist senators aboard.

### Why did sponsors expect family preferences to preserve the ethnic mix?

The sponsors reasoned that relatives of citizens and residents would mirror the existing population, which in 1965 was overwhelmingly of European origin. Because people in Africa and Asia had few relatives in America, sponsors argued, comparatively few could immigrate under a family-weighted system. The premise treated kinship networks as a demographic mirror of the country.

### Where did the famous predictions actually come from?

The most quoted predictions did not come from Senate floor debate. Senator Ted Kennedy delivered them in his opening statement at Senate Judiciary subcommittee hearings on February 10, 1965, and they are routinely mislabeled as floor speeches. The setting determines how the words should be read: prepared testimony before a subcommittee, not extemporaneous remarks in a chamber fight.

### What happened to the Western Hemisphere in the final bill?

The House-passed bill contained no Western Hemisphere cap. The Senate added a ceiling of 120,000, demanded by restrictionist senators as the price of passage, and the conference kept it. Under section 21(e), the ceiling took effect for the fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968, with immediate relatives of United States citizens excluded from the count.

## The Long Failure

To understand why passage in 1965 was remarkable, begin with how many times reform had failed. The national origins formula, enacted in 1924, allocated immigration quotas by country in proportions reflecting an earlier American population. For four decades it stood as the architecture of American immigration law. Proposals to dismantle it appeared repeatedly after the Second World War, and each one died.

In 1952 the contest was direct. President Harry Truman backed a liberal alternative to the restrictive McCarran-Walter bill, an alternative advanced by Representative Emanuel Celler and Senator Herbert Lehman of New York. The restrictionist bill prevailed, and the liberal alternative went down with the rest of the reform effort that year. The defeat taught reformers a durable lesson about where the obstacles sat: the committee chairmen who controlled immigration legislation were restrictionists, and they could bury any bill they disliked.

President Dwight Eisenhower took up the cause twice. In 1956 and again in 1960 he sought to abolish the quota system, lending presidential prestige to a reform that his own party's congressional leadership showed little appetite for. Neither effort moved. The pattern held through the early 1960s: presidential interest, committee indifference, no floor vote.

Senator Philip Hart of Michigan, who would later manage the 1965 bill in the Senate, tried a different design in 1962. His bill proposed a quota formula based on the pattern of immigration over the previous fifteen years, adjusted for source-country population, an attempt to replace ancestry with recent reality. It did not advance. In 1963 President John F. Kennedy sent an administration immigration reform bill to Congress, and Hart introduced the 1963 Administration Bill in the Senate. That measure stalled as well. Related predecessor bills accumulated across Congresses, including H.R. 6888 in the 84th Congress and S. 1932 and H.R. 7700 in the 88th, each a marker of intent that produced no law.

One caution belongs here. Some accounts speak loosely of repeal bills in the late 1940s. The verified record does not support naming specific late-1940s abolition measures, and this history will not invent them. What can be said is that at the signing ceremony in 1965, President Lyndon Johnson recalled that Celler and House Speaker John McCormack had first criticized the quota system in their maiden speeches almost forty years earlier, which places the origins of the critique in the late 1920s and early 1930s, not in any specific late-1940s bill.

The significance of the long failure is procedural. Every previous attempt had foundered on the same rocks: hostile committee gatekeepers, a Congress that treated immigration as a matter of ethnic arithmetic rather than principle, and no outside pressure strong enough to force a vote. When the bill finally moved in 1965, it moved because those conditions had changed, not because the arguments were new. The arguments for ending the formula had been made for years. What changed was the room.

## The 1952 Veto and Truman's Message

The 1952 fight deserves close attention because it fixed the terms of the later debate. The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, known as the McCarran-Walter Act, preserved the national origins quota system. President Truman vetoed it on June 25, 1952. Congress overrode the veto with two-thirds votes in each house, and the act took effect on June 27, 1952. A president had called the quota system indefensible, and Congress had kept it anyway.

Truman's veto message is worth quoting at length, because the 1965 sponsors would later echo its moral logic. Of the country-by-country limitations, Truman wrote that they "create a pattern that is insulting to large numbers of our finest citizens, irritating to our allies abroad, and foreign to our purposes and ideals." He charged that the bill "would continue, practically without change, the national origins quota system, which was enacted into law in 1924." At the same time he drew a careful distinction, writing, "With the idea of quotas in general there is no quarrel. Some numerical limitation must be set." The objection was not to limits but to the country-by-country pattern of allocating them.

The message matters for two reasons. First, it placed a presidential imprimatur on the argument that the formula was a moral embarrassment, an argument the 1965 reformers would inherit and sharpen. Second, it demonstrated the scale of the obstacle: even a presidential veto could not dislodge the formula when the restrictionist coalition held its majorities. Thirteen years later the reformers would need not only the argument but the votes, and they would get both from a Congress transformed by the elections of 1964.

The 1952 override also shaped the tactics of 1965. The reformers had learned that a frontal assault on restrictionism would fail. They would need converts inside the restrictionist camp, concessions that looked like safeguards, and a framing that made opposition sound like bigotry rather than prudence. The veto message supplied the framing. The bargains would supply the rest.

## From 1952 to 1964: The Wilderness Years

The thirteen years between Truman's veto and Johnson's signing were a wilderness for immigration reform, and the wilderness years explain why 1965 felt like a dam breaking. The 1952 defeat had taught the reformers that presidential support was not enough. Truman had vetoed the McCarran-Walter Act, had denounced the quota system in terms no president had used before, and had lost anyway. Congress overrode him, and the formula survived. The lesson was institutional: the presidency could frame the issue, but only Congress could change the law, and Congress was controlled by restrictionists.

Eisenhower's efforts confirmed the lesson. The popular two-term president asked Congress to abolish the quota system in 1956 and again in 1960, and the committee system absorbed the pressure and returned nothing. The early 1960s brought new bills and the same result: Hart's 1962 quota-revision measure died, the Kennedy administration's 1963 bill stalled, and predecessor measures accumulated across Congresses without reaching a floor.

Two developments cracked the pattern before the 1964 election. The first was the death of Francis Walter in 1963. Walter, the coauthor of the 1952 act, had embodied restrictionism as a committee chairman, and his immigration subcommittee had been the place where reform bills went to die. His successor, Feighan, inherited the gavel and, to general astonishment, not the intransigence. The second was the gathering force of the civil rights movement, which was changing the moral vocabulary in which all ancestry-based laws were discussed. By 1964 the quota system was being described, even by its defenders, in terms that sounded increasingly like the segregationist arguments being defeated elsewhere. The wilderness was ending before the election confirmed it.

The 1964 landslide then supplied what the wilderness years had lacked: a Congress whose majorities favored reform and whose leadership owed its position to the new coalition. The 89th Congress did not invent the arguments for ending the formula. It inherited them from Celler, Truman, Eisenhower, Hart, and Kennedy, refined across two decades of failure. What it supplied was the votes, and the votes were enough.

## The 89th Congress and Changed Conditions

The 89th Congress, elected in the Democratic landslide of 1964, was the most liberal Congress in a generation. It is the Congress that passed the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and the immigration bill moved through the same corridors at the same time, carried by the same majorities and the same ideological current. The voting rights legislation of that year and the immigration reform shared more than a calendar. They shared a premise: that the federal government should not allocate rights or opportunities by ancestry.

That framing was new, and it was decisive. For decades immigration restriction had been debated as a question of numbers, labor markets, and national security. In 1965 the reformers recast it as a question of discrimination. The national origins formula did not merely limit immigration; it ranked the world by bloodline, assigning generous quotas to northern and western Europe and token quotas to Asia and Africa. Once the civil rights movement had made ancestry-based classification morally radioactive in domestic law, the same classification in immigration law became difficult to defend. The reformers pressed that parallel relentlessly, and it gave wavering members a language for voting yes that had not existed in 1952.

The connection to the civil rights struggle was substantive, not merely rhetorical. The passage of the civil rights legislation of 1964 had broken the procedural and moral monopoly of the Southern segregationist bloc, and the voting rights campaign of 1965 kept that bloc on the defensive. Members who had watched the country repudiate racial classification at lunch counters and ballot boxes found it harder to defend racial classification at the quota desk. The 89th Congress had the votes and the ideological framing to treat ancestry-based allocation as a civil rights question, and it used both.

None of this means the outcome was foreordained. Large majorities do not pass controversial bills by themselves; they must be organized, and the opposition must be bought off or outmaneuvered. The civil rights framing opened the door. The bargains walked the bill through it. For the fuller account of how the 1964 legislation broke the old coalition, see the companion history at /2010/01/15/civil-rights-act-1964-passage-history/, and for the voting rights campaign that shared the 1965 calendar, see /2010/06/15/voting-rights-act-1965-passage-history/.

## The Bill's Path Through Congress

H.R. 2580, titled "An Act to amend the Immigration and Nationality Act, and for other purposes," was introduced in the House on January 13, 1965, by Representative Emanuel Celler of New York, the longtime chairman of the House Judiciary Committee and the reformers' most persistent champion. In the Senate the bill was managed by Senator Philip Hart of Michigan, with Senator Ted Kennedy of Massachusetts, chairman of the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Immigration and Naturalization, serving as floor leader. The division of labor mattered: Celler owned the House, Hart carried the chamber prestige, and Kennedy, the youngest brother of the assassinated president whose 1963 bill had stalled, supplied the public face of the campaign.

The House Judiciary Committee's immigration subcommittee was chaired by Representative Michael Feighan of Ohio, a longtime restrictionist and the central figure in the House story. Feighan had succeeded the arch-restrictionist Francis Walter, who died in 1963, and to the surprise of many he became a convert to reform. His alternative bill largely shaped the version that came to the House floor, introducing the seven-preference system and increasing the share of visas allocated to relatives of American citizens. The Johnson White House negotiated directly with Feighan, and the resulting compromise gave the bill its distinctive family-weighted architecture. Without Feighan's conversion, the bill would likely never have reached the floor.

The House debated and passed H.R. 2580 on August 25, 1965, by a vote of 318 to 95. The party breakdown tells the story of the new coalition: Democrats voted 209 to 71 in favor, Republicans 109 to 24. Bipartisan is the right word, but it needs qualification. The Democratic majority supplied the margin, and the Republican minority supplied a comfortable supplement. Opposition came chiefly from Southern Democrats and a smaller band of conservative Republicans, the remnant of the old restrictionist coalition, left outnumbered and outflanked.

One floor fight in the House deserves mention because it foreshadowed the Senate's price. Representative Clark MacGregor, a Minnesota Republican, offered an amendment to cap Western Hemisphere immigration at 115,000. The House defeated the amendment 189 to 218 and passed the bill with no Western Hemisphere ceiling at all. The restrictionists lost the amendment but kept the issue alive, and they would collect their payment in the other chamber.

The Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Immigration and Naturalization had opened hearings on February 10, 1965, under Kennedy's chairmanship, and those hearings produced the prediction record examined below. When the bill reached the Senate floor, the restrictionist senators led by Sam Ervin of North Carolina threatened to block action in the subcommittee unless concessions were made. The concession was the Western Hemisphere ceiling. As one peer-reviewed account of the bargaining puts it, Johnson and the pro-immigration lawmakers compromised with Ervin and his restriction-minded colleagues on an annual ceiling for Western Hemisphere immigration. The Center for Immigration Studies, drawing on the historian Steven Gillon, corroborates the shape of the deal: Kennedy accepted the demand of Ervin and Senate Minority Leader Everett Dirksen that the bill include a cap on immigration from the Western Hemisphere. Ervin had originally hoped to preserve the national origins quotas, but, realizing he lacked the votes, switched to supporting a cap on Western Hemisphere immigration as his fallback.

The Senate added the ceiling at 120,000 and passed the bill on September 22, 1965, by 76 to 18. Democrats voted 52 to 15, Republicans 24 to 3. The regional split inside the Democratic vote was stark: non-Southern Democrats voted 43 to 2, Southern Democrats 9 to 13, a reminder that the old coalition survived in the South even as it lost the chamber. The Senate version then went to conference, and the House accepted the conference report, essentially the Senate version with its new ceiling, on September 30, 1965, by 320 to 70, with Democrats at 202 to 60 and Republicans at 118 to 10.

President Johnson signed the bill on October 3, 1965, on Liberty Island in New York Harbor, at the base of the Statue of Liberty. The setting was chosen for symbolism, and Johnson's remarks leaned into it. The enacted law became Public Law 89-236, recorded at 79 Stat. 911. It took partial effect on December 1, 1965, and came fully into force on July 1, 1968. The long failure was over. The bargaining had determined the text. What remained was for the text to behave in ways its authors had not modeled.

## What the Bill Proposed

Before following the bill through Congress, it helps to see exactly what the text provided, because the surprises of the story are all in the machinery. H.R. 2580, introduced by Representative Emanuel Celler on January 13, 1965, and managed in the Senate by Senator Philip Hart with Senator Edward Kennedy as floor leader, bore the formal title "An Act to amend the Immigration and Nationality Act, and for other purposes." Its central move was simple to state: it abolished the national origins quota system and replaced ancestry-based allocation with a preference system. The details of that replacement are where the forecasting errors lived.

The bill set an annual ceiling of 170,000 visas for the Eastern Hemisphere, the half of the world that had been subject to the quota formula. Within that ceiling, visas were distributed through a seven-preference system that ranked applicants by family relationship to American citizens and residents and by occupational skills. The distinctive feature of the design, the one that Representative Feighan's alternative bill had introduced and the Johnson White House had accepted, was the weighting: family unification took priority over employability. Relatives of citizens and permanent residents stood ahead of workers with needed skills. The system also included a per-country ceiling of 20,000 within the Eastern Hemisphere cap, so that no single country could take more than that number of the capped preference visas in a year. That per-country limit would matter enormously to the sponsors' predictions, and its limits would matter enormously to the outcome.

The most consequential provision was the one that looked the least consequential. Immediate relatives of United States citizens, meaning spouses, unmarried minor children, and parents of citizens, were placed entirely outside the numerical caps. They did not count against the 170,000 ceiling, they did not count against the per-country ceiling, and in the Senate's added Western Hemisphere system they would not count against the 120,000 ceiling either. The exemption was a humanitarian provision, the kind of clause that passes without debate because no one wants to argue that a citizen should be barred from bringing a spouse or a small child to America. No witness at the February 1965 hearings modeled what would happen when that exemption interacted with rising naturalization rates across decades. The exemption was treated as a residual, a small and stable carve-out. It became the engine.

The bill also retained a labor protection. As Celler explained on the House floor, no employment-based immigrant could enter without an individual certificate from the Secretary of Labor guaranteeing that American workers would not be displaced. That certification requirement was part of the sponsors' answer to the charge that the bill would cost Americans their jobs, and Kennedy's four-sentence summary at the hearings, that the bill would not cause American workers to lose their jobs, rested partly on it. The sponsors treated the labor certificate as a brake on numbers. In practice it applied only to the employment preferences, a minority of the flow, and it did nothing to meter the family-based admissions that would dominate the system.

The Western Hemisphere stood outside the bill as the House passed it. Under the law in effect before 1965, immigration from the Americas had never been subject to the quota formula, and the House version of H.R. 2580 left it that way: no ceiling, no preference system, just the existing non-quota treatment. That was the feature the Senate would change, adding the 120,000 ceiling effective in the fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968, after the select commission's study. The enacted statute, Public Law 89-236, 79 Stat. 911, was partially effective on December 1, 1965, and came fully into force on July 1, 1968. Those dates matter because the fiscal 1968 immigration figures, the first full year under the complete system, are the earliest clean test of the sponsors' predictions.

Taken together, the bill's architecture was a capped preference system wrapped around an uncapped family exemption. The sponsors looked at the caps and saw control. They looked at the exemption and saw compassion. They did not look at what the exemption would do to the caps across time, because their model of the system was a snapshot, not a film. Everything that follows in this history, the lopsided votes, the confident predictions, and the falsification of those predictions, runs through that architectural fact.

One more feature of the architecture deserves notice: the select commission mechanism for the Western Hemisphere ceiling. Section 21(e) did not simply impose the 120,000 cap. It created a commission to study Western Hemisphere immigration and provided that the cap would take effect in the fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968, unless Congress enacted inconsistent legislation before June 30, 1968. The design gave Congress a three-year window to reconsider, which made the cap easier for pro-reform members to accept: it was a ceiling with an escape hatch, a concession that a future Congress could revisit. No future Congress did. The commission studied, the deadline passed, and the cap took effect as written. The episode is a small lesson in legislative temporizing. A provision designed to make a bargain palatable by deferring its bite will bite on schedule if no one acts, and the 1965 Congress, having bought its peace with a future decision, left the future to decide nothing.

## The Trade: Family Preferences and the Western Hemisphere Ceiling

Two bargains made the lopsided margins possible, and each left its mark on the statute. The first was struck in the House, the second in the Senate, and together they explain how restrictionist votes were secured for a bill that abolished the restrictionists' favorite formula.

The House bargain centered on Representative Feighan and the preference system. Feighan insisted that family unification take priority over employability in the allocation of visas. His premise, widely reported in the historical accounts though resting on secondary reconstructions rather than a floor quotation, was that weighting the system toward relatives of citizens and residents would maintain the existing ethnic profile of the country. The logic ran as follows: the American population of 1965 was overwhelmingly of European origin, so the relatives available to sponsor new immigrants would be overwhelmingly European, so a family-weighted system would reproduce the European-heavy pattern that the national origins formula had enforced by design. Feighan was convinced, in the words of one account, incorrectly as it turned out, that reserving most visas for immigrants with family ties would decidedly favor European applicants and thus maintain the nation's ethnic and racial makeup.

The Johnson White House accepted the premise because it needed Feighan's subcommittee and his credibility with restrictionist colleagues. The resulting seven-preference system allocated the bulk of numerically limited visas to family members of citizens and permanent residents, with employment-based preferences in a subordinate role. At the signing ceremony, Johnson himself named Feighan among those who had helped guide the bill to passage, a public acknowledgment of the debt. The family-weighted structure was thus not a liberal ideal imposed on reluctant conservatives. It was a conservative reassurance purchased at the price of the bill's architecture, a safeguard that restrictionists accepted because they believed it would do their work for them.

The Senate bargain was cruder and more visible: the Western Hemisphere ceiling. The House bill had imposed no limit on immigration from the Western Hemisphere, which had historically entered outside the quota system. Restrictionist senators regarded unlimited hemispheric immigration as the hole through which the new law's promises would leak. Ervin and Dirksen demanded a cap, and the reformers paid it. The ceiling was set at 120,000 per year, a default mechanism rather than an immediate one. Section 21(e) of the act provided for a select commission to study Western Hemisphere immigration, and stipulated that unless inconsistent legislation was enacted on or before June 30, 1968, Western Hemisphere numbers would not exceed 120,000 in the fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968, or in any fiscal year thereafter. Immediate relatives of United States citizens were excluded from the count.

The two trades shared a structure. In each case the reformers surrendered a design element that restrictionists believed would hold the line on numbers and composition: family weighting in the House, a hemispheric cap in the Senate. In each case the restrictionists voted yes because they trusted the mechanism to preserve something close to the status quo. The thesis of this history is that procedure and bargaining determined the text, and the enacted text then behaved in ways its authors had not modeled. The trades are the first half of that thesis. The predictions are the second.

## The Alternatives of 1965

The bill that passed was not the only bill that could have passed, and the alternatives that were considered and rejected illuminate the bargain. On the House floor, on the Senate side, and in the committee rooms, three distinct alternatives to the enacted text were on the table: MacGregor's Western Hemisphere cap at a lower number, Ervin's original hope of preserving the quotas outright, and Feighan's alternative bill that became the vehicle. Each alternative's fate shows what the winning coalition was willing to pay and what it refused to buy.

MacGregor's amendment is the most precisely documented of the alternatives. On August 25, 1965, Representative Clark MacGregor of Minnesota offered an amendment to cap Western Hemisphere immigration at 115,000. The number was close to the 120,000 the Senate would later adopt, but the House rejected it 189 to 218. The defeat is instructive. The House majority that supported repeal of the quotas did not want any Western Hemisphere cap at that stage, and the 218 votes against the amendment included liberals who opposed caps on principle and members who simply preferred the administration's clean bill. The 189 votes in favor showed that the cap idea had a substantial constituency, but not a majority. When the Senate later imposed the cap at 120,000, it was overriding the House's expressed preference, and the House's acceptance of the cap in conference was a concession to the Senate, not a change of mind. The five-thousand difference between MacGregor's 115,000 and the Senate's 120,000 is a small window into the bargaining: the Senate's number was marginally more generous, which made it marginally easier for the House to swallow.

Ervin's alternative was simpler and never got a vote: keep the national origins quotas. The North Carolina senator had originally hoped to preserve the quota formula outright, and his shift to demanding the Western Hemisphere cap was a retreat forced by the arithmetic. A law-review account of the bargaining describes the sequence plainly: Ervin realized he lacked the votes to preserve the quotas and switched to supporting the cap. That switch is the moment the restrictionist cause in the Senate changed from prevention to damage control. Ervin was too skilled a parliamentarian to waste a fight he would lose, and the cap gave him something to show for the retreat. The legislative history should record what his retreat meant: by the summer of 1965, even the Senate's leading restrictionist conceded that the quota formula was finished. The only question was the price of its funeral.

Feighan's alternative bill is the most important of the three, because it became the bill. The Ohio chairman did not merely amend the administration's proposal. He produced an alternative that largely shaped the version reaching the floor, introducing the seven-preference system and increasing the share allocated to relatives of citizens. In legislative terms, Feighan captured the bill: the vehicle that moved was his, even though the destination, repeal of the quotas, was the administration's. That capture is why the family-preference weighting has the shape it does. It was not designed by liberal reformers to maximize immigration. It was designed by a restrictionist chairman to reassure restrictionists, on the premise that family ties would preserve the existing composition. The irony that the restrictionists' own design became the accelerator is not a footnote to this history. It is the history.

## Inside the Committee Rooms

The decisive work on H.R. 2580 happened in committee rooms, not on the chamber floors, and the record of those rooms explains the shape of the final text. In the House, the Judiciary Committee's immigration subcommittee under Feighan became the workshop where the White House and the reformers hammered out the compromise. Feighan's alternative bill introduced the seven-preference system and enlarged the family share, and the administration accepted those features as the cost of his support. This was not the reformers imposing their ideal design; it was the reformers accepting a restrictionist's design because the restrictionist controlled the room. The preference architecture that later generations would blame for the act's unintended consequences was, in origin, a concession.

The full Judiciary Committee under Celler then processed the subcommittee product, and the House floor fight centered on the MacGregor amendment. MacGregor's proposal to cap Western Hemisphere immigration at 115,000 drew 189 votes, a substantial minority, before falling 189 to 218. The closeness of that vote told the Senate reformers exactly what they would face: a restrictionist minority too large to ignore and too small to win outright, the classic profile of a faction that must be bought. The House's refusal to cap the hemisphere did not settle the issue; it merely moved the auction to the Senate.

In the Senate, the Judiciary Subcommittee on Immigration and Naturalization opened its hearings on February 10, 1965, with Kennedy in the chair. Those hearings served two purposes. Publicly, they built the record of expert and official testimony behind reform, including the prediction statements examined in this history. Privately, they established the subcommittee as the venue where the final bargaining would occur. When Ervin threatened to block action in the subcommittee, he was threatening the venue itself, and the threat worked because everyone understood that a bill bottled in subcommittee never reaches the floor.

The Senate Judiciary Committee added the 120,000 Western Hemisphere ceiling, and the full Senate accepted it. The committee's action is sometimes described as a technical amendment, but it was the substantive price of the bill. Without the ceiling, Ervin and Dirksen had no reason to let the measure advance; with it, they had a control they could defend to their constituents as a safeguard. The committee room thus produced both of the act's defining features: the family-weighted preferences from the House side and the hemispheric cap from the Senate side. Floor votes ratified what committee bargains had built.

## The Floor Fights

Committee bargains set the terms, but the floor votes tested them, and the debates left the prediction record that later generations would quarrel over. The House took up H.R. 2580 on August 25, 1965. Celler managed the bill, Feighan's subcommittee product carried the day, and the MacGregor amendment's defeat cleared the way for final passage at 318 to 95. Celler's floor speech on that day supplied the House's canonical predictions: few relatives in Africa and Asia meant few immigrants from those regions, labor certification would protect American workers, and the cost of passage would deter the rest. The Congressional Record preserves the speech at pages 21757 to 21758, the citation carried by the peer-reviewed literature, though some secondary sources cite page 21812, a discrepancy this history notes without resolving.

The House party breakdown, Democrats 209 to 71 and Republicans 109 to 24, showed a coalition that crossed party lines but leaned on the Democratic majority. The thirteen Democrats and six Republicans not voting do not change the picture. Opposition clustered where the civil rights framing predicted it would: among Southern Democrats defending the old order and conservative Republicans allied with them. The margin was lopsided, but the lopsidedness was manufactured by the Feighan compromise, which had moved the subcommittee chairman and his followers from opposition to support.

The Senate debate culminated on September 22, 1965, with passage at 76 to 18. Hart managed the bill, Kennedy led on the floor, and the Western Hemisphere ceiling rode along as the price of the Ervin-Dirksen acquiescence. The Democratic split, 52 to 15 overall, concealed the regional chasm: non-Southern Democrats at 43 to 2, Southern Democrats at 9 to 13. Republicans voted 24 to 3. Six senators did not vote. The regional numbers are the clearest quantitative evidence of the bill's civil rights character. On immigration, as on voting rights, the South stood apart.

The conference reconciled the versions, kept the Senate's ceiling, and sent the report back to the House, which accepted it on September 30 by 320 to 70. The conference vote is sometimes treated as a formality, but it ratified the most consequential change the Senate had made. The House that had refused to cap the hemisphere in August accepted the cap in September, because the alternative was no bill at all. Procedure determined text to the very end.

## The Predictions, in Their Own Words

The sponsors of the 1965 act made specific, on-the-record predictions about what the bill would do. Those predictions are among the most quoted and most misquoted passages in the history of American immigration law. Accuracy about who said what, where, and in what context is not a nicety here. It is the difference between a defensible history and a weaponized one. The rule followed throughout is simple: quote sponsors accurately and in context, or paraphrase precisely, and never truncate a passage in a way that changes its meaning.

Begin with the necessary correction. The most famous prediction statements were not made on the Senate floor. They were made by Senator Ted Kennedy in his opening statement at the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Immigration and Naturalization hearings on February 10, 1965, recorded at pages 1 to 3 of the hearing transcript. Countless later accounts label them floor debate. They were subcommittee testimony, prepared remarks delivered as chairman opening a hearing, and they should be cited that way.

Here is what Kennedy said, in the fuller form that the record preserves:

"First, our cities will not be flooded with a million immigrants annually. Under the proposed bill, the present level of immigration remains substantially the same ... Secondly, the ethnic mix of this country will not be upset ... Contrary to the charges in some quarters, [the bill] will not inundate America with immigrants from any one country or area, or the most populated and deprived nations of Africa and Asia ... In the final analysis, the ethnic pattern of immigration under the proposed measure is not expected to change as sharply as the critics seem to think."

And here is his concluding summary from the same pages:

"The bill will not flood our cities with immigrants. It will not upset the ethnic mix of our society. It will not relax the standards of admission. It will not cause American workers to lose their jobs."

The commonly quoted one-liner, "The bill will not flood our cities with immigrants. It will not upset the ethnic mix of our society," is the tail of this longer statement. Quoted alone it is accurate as far as it goes, but it drops the mechanism Kennedy had in mind, which was the family preference structure, and it drops the venue, which was a hearing room in February, not the Senate floor in September. Both omissions have fueled later misuse.

Representative Celler spoke on the House floor on August 25, 1965, the day the House passed the bill, and his remarks are preserved in the Congressional Record. As quoted in a peer-reviewed economic history of the act, citing the Record at pages 21757 to 21758, Celler said:

"since the people of Africa and Asia have very few relatives here, comparatively few could immigrate from those countries because they have no family ties in the United States... no one can come without the individual certificate from the Secretary of Labor guaranteeing that the American workman will not be displaced... few of them can even pay the cost of the ticket to come here. There is no danger whatsoever of an influx from the countries of Africa and Asia."

The three mechanisms in Celler's reassurance are worth separating: the family-tie premise (few relatives, therefore few immigrants), the labor certification requirement (no displacement of American workers), and the practical barrier of travel cost. Each would prove leakier than he supposed.

Senator Robert Kennedy offered numerical predictions at two points. As Attorney General in 1964 he predicted that abolishing Asian restrictions would mean a net increase of approximately 5,000. As a senator in 1965 he said the net increase attributable to the bill would be at most 50,000 a year. Attorney General Nicholas Katzenbach, testifying at the February 10, 1965 hearings, said, "This bill is not designed to increase or accelerate the numbers of newcomers permitted to come to America." Senator Hiram Fong of Hawaii, the first Asian American senator and a supporter of the bill, told the same hearings that Asian immigrants "will never reach 1 percent of the population" and that "Our cultural pattern will never be changed."

President Johnson added his own reassurance at the signing ceremony on Liberty Island, calling the measure "not a revolutionary bill" and predicting it would not affect the lives of millions, reshape the structure of daily life, or add importantly to the country's wealth or power.

One alleged quotation must be excluded. A line frequently attributed to Celler, to the effect that the bill before the House left the basic numbers of immigrants essentially unchanged, could not be verified to a Congressional Record page and appears only in secondary aggregations. It is omitted from this history. The predictions above are the ones the record supports, quoted in accurate context. For the companion guide to the statute's provisions themselves, see /2012/12/01/immigration-nationality-act-1965-guide/.

## What Actually Happened

The numbers tell the second half of the story, and they should be stated with their years and sources attached. In fiscal year 1965, the last full year under the old system, 296,700 persons obtained lawful permanent resident status, according to the Department of Homeland Security's Office of Homeland Security Statistics. In fiscal year 1968, the first full year under the new act, the figure was 454,450. By fiscal 1980 it had reached 524,300, and by fiscal 2000, 841,000. The increase did not arrive all at once, and not all of it is attributable to the 1965 act alone, a point the historians stress and this history will return to. But the direction and the scale are beyond dispute, and they contradict every prediction quoted above.

The composition changed even more dramatically than the volume. In the 1950s, more than half of all immigrants to the United States were Europeans, and just 6 percent were Asians. By the 1990s, only 16 percent were Europeans and 31 percent were of Asian descent. In the three decades following passage of the 1965 act, more than 18 million legal immigrants entered the United States, more than three times the number admitted over the preceding thirty years. Kennedy had said the ethnic mix would not be upset. Celler had said there was no danger of an influx from Africa and Asia. Fong had said the cultural pattern would never change. The record answered each of them.

How did a statute built on family reunification produce a result its architects associated with the opposite? The answer is the mechanism the sponsors trusted. The family-ties miscalculation: the sponsors expected family preferences to act as a brake on demographic change because they assumed family ties would mirror the existing population, and the immediate-relative exemption combined with a small initial base from Asia and Latin America turned the same provision into an accelerator, which is a forecasting failure about mechanism rather than about intent.

Unpack that sentence, because it carries the whole argument. The sponsors looked at the American population of 1965, saw a country of overwhelmingly European descent, and reasoned that a system favoring relatives would favor Europeans. What they missed was the dynamic. Every immigrant admitted under the new system acquired the right to sponsor further relatives. The initial base from Asia and Latin America was small, but it was not zero, and a small base compounding through sponsorship rights grows faster, in proportional terms, than a large base. Meanwhile the immediate relatives of United States citizens sat outside the numerical limitations entirely, which meant the fastest-growing channel had no cap to hit. The sponsors had modeled a snapshot. The statute operated as a film.

There is a second, related error in the predictions. The sponsors treated the end of the national origins formula as a reallocation of a fixed flow, when the preference system they installed created new demand. Under the old quotas, a would-be immigrant from a low-quota country faced a wait measured in decades or a door closed entirely, so many never applied. Under the new system, the same person with a sponsoring relative had a real path, so applications surged. The flow was not fixed. The statute changed the incentives, and the incentives changed the flow.

A third factor lay outside the statute altogether. Later scholarship emphasizes that much of the massive subsequent increase in immigration often attributed to the 1965 act was, in fact, due to side effects that would have been hard to predict in 1965, or to policies implemented in the 1970s and beyond. The 1965 act was the hinge, but it was not the whole door. Refugee admissions, later legalizations, and enforcement choices all shaped the totals. A fair account attributes to the act what the act produced and notes where later policy took over. For the demographic consequences in full, see the companion analysis at /2013/02/15/immigration-act-1965-demographic-impact/.

## The Machinery of the Error

The sponsors' model can be reconstructed from their statements, and reconstructing it shows exactly where it broke. The exercise is not anachronistic. It is what the table above summarizes, laid out as a sequence, so that each assumption and each failure is visible in order.

The model began with a stock and a flow. The stock was the American population, overwhelmingly of European origin in 1965. The flow was the annual immigration the bill would produce. The sponsors assumed the flow would mirror the stock, because the bill's dominant mechanism was family sponsorship, and family ties, they believed, were distributed across the world in proportion to the American population's origins. Europeans in America had relatives in Europe. Asians in America were few, so their relatives in Asia were few. Africans in America were fewer still. Therefore a family-weighted system would produce a European-weighted flow, and the existing composition would reproduce itself year after year. Celler stated this logic on the House floor in its purest form: the people of Africa and Asia had very few relatives in the United States, so comparatively few could immigrate from those countries. The premise was true as a snapshot. It was false as a forecast.

The second assumption concerned the immediate-relative exemption. Spouses, minor children, and parents of citizens were placed outside all caps, and the sponsors treated this channel as a small humanitarian residual. In their mental ledger, the capped preferences did the work of metering admissions, and the exemption handled the hard cases: the citizen's foreign-born spouse, the citizen's aging parent. What the ledger omitted was that every capped immigrant was a future citizen, and every future citizen was a future sponsor of uncapped relatives. The exemption was not a residual. It was a multiplier with a time delay, and the delay was roughly the number of years between arrival and naturalization. The sponsors counted the first iteration and stopped. The statute kept iterating.

The third assumption concerned the per-country ceiling. The sponsors believed the 20,000 per-country limit within the Eastern Hemisphere cap would prevent any single country from dominating the flow, and Kennedy's assurance that the bill would not inundate America with immigrants from any one country or area rested on it. But the per-country ceiling applied only to the capped preference visas. It did not apply to the uncapped immediate-relative channel, which had no per-country limit at all. A country with an active sponsorship network could therefore send far more than 20,000 immigrants a year through the exempt channel, and the capped channel's per-country discipline did nothing to restrain it. Fong's prediction that Asian immigration would never reach 1 percent of the population assumed a constraint that did not bind the channel through which the growth actually came.

The fourth assumption concerned labor certification and travel costs. Celler argued that the individual certificate from the Secretary of Labor, guaranteeing that American workers would not be displaced, would hold down numbers, and that few prospective immigrants from Africa and Asia could even pay the cost of a ticket. Both were real frictions, but both applied at the margins. The labor certificate governed only employment-based admissions, a minority of the flow under a family-weighted system. The cost of travel was a genuine barrier in 1965, but it was a barrier that fell steadily as air travel cheapened and as established immigrant communities financed the passage of relatives. Neither friction compounded. The family multiplier did.

The fifth assumption, the most abstract and the most fatal, was stationarity: the belief that the future would resemble the past. The sponsors projected the immigration of the 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s from the immigration of the 1950s and early 1960s, adjusting only for the change in selection principle. They did not model rising naturalization rates, they did not model the feedback loop from naturalization to sponsorship, and they did not model the way a small initial base could grow geometrically through the exempt channel. Barone's formulation is the classic statement of this error: the mistake was to think that immigration and the American labor market of the future would look like immigration and the American labor market of the past. The model was a snapshot. The statute was a film.

None of these errors required bad faith. Each is a recognizable failure mode of legislative forecasting: mistaking a stock for a flow, treating an exemption as a residual, assuming a constraint binds every channel, overweighting visible frictions, and projecting the past forward without feedback. They were operating with the standard tools of 1965 policy analysis on a mechanism whose dynamics those tools could not capture. That is why the scholarly consensus calls it miscalculation rather than deception, and why the table above lists structural reasons rather than motives.

## Counting What Followed

The post-enactment numbers repay careful reading, because they show both the scale of the change and the limits of attributing it entirely to the 1965 act. Start with the Department of Homeland Security's Office of Homeland Security Statistics, the authoritative source for legal immigration levels. In fiscal 1965, the last full year under the old system, 296,700 persons obtained lawful permanent resident status. In fiscal 1968, the first full year under the new act, the figure was 454,450, an increase of more than half in three years. By fiscal 1980 the number reached 524,300, and by fiscal 2000 it stood at 841,000. The trajectory is unmistakable, and it contradicts every prediction the sponsors entered into the record.

The composition figures tell an even starker story. In the 1950s, more than half of all immigrants were Europeans and just 6 percent were Asians. By the 1990s, only 16 percent were Europeans and 31 percent were of Asian descent. The ethnic mix that Kennedy said would not be upset was transformed. The influx from Africa and Asia that Celler said posed no danger became one of the central facts of American immigration. The cultural pattern that Fong said would never change changed beyond recognition. In the three decades following passage, more than 18 million legal immigrants entered the United States, more than three times the number admitted in the preceding thirty years. These are the figures the prediction-and-outcome table summarizes, and they are not in dispute.

What is in dispute is attribution. The 2024 economic history by Facchini, Hatton, and Steinhardt cautions that much of the increase often laid at the act's door was, in fact, the product of side effects that would have been hard to predict in 1965 or of policies adopted in the 1970s and beyond. Refugee admissions expanded through separate legislation and executive action. Later legalizations added populations the 1965 Congress never contemplated. Enforcement choices, economic conditions, and global crises shaped the flows. The 1965 act created the preference machinery and the uncapped immediate-relative channel, and those were necessary conditions for much of what followed. They were not sufficient conditions, and an honest history says so.

The distinction matters for judging the sponsors. If the act alone had produced the entire transformation, the deception theory would be harder to dismiss, because the gap between promise and outcome would be total and direct. Once later policies and side effects share the responsibility, the gap narrows to what the sponsors could plausibly have foreseen, and the miscalculation theory fits better. The sponsors failed to model compounding, base rates, exemptions, and induced demand. Those were failures about the statute they wrote. What later Congresses and presidents did with the machinery was not written in 1965, and the sponsors cannot be graded on it.

## After the Signing: The First Years

The statute's phase-in shaped its early reception. Partial effectiveness on December 1, 1965, brought the new law into operation in stages, and full force arrived on July 1, 1968, the same date the Western Hemisphere ceiling's default took effect. The thirty-one-month transition gave administrators time to build the preference machinery and gave the select commission time to study hemispheric migration. It also gave the sponsors a grace period: the transformation they had promised would not happen did not begin visibly until the system was fully running.

Fiscal 1968, the first full year under the new act, recorded 454,450 new lawful permanent residents, against 296,700 in fiscal 1965. The increase of more than half in three years was the first quantitative rebuke to the predictions, though its full significance took longer to register. Contemporary observers could plausibly attribute part of the jump to pent-up demand, the clearing of backlogs accumulated under the quota system, rather than to the new machinery's dynamics. The distinction between backlog-clearing and structural growth muddied the early debate and gave defenders of the predictions a plausible, if temporary, refuge.

The commission on Western Hemisphere immigration completed its study against the June 30, 1968, deadline built into section 21(e), and the 120,000 default ceiling took effect for the fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968, as the statute provided. The ceiling's arrival altered the rules governing hemispheric migration, which had previously operated outside the quota system. The immediate-relative exemption meant the cap's bite was softer than its number suggested. The restrictionists had purchased a ceiling; the statute delivered a ceiling with the largest growth channel exempted. The pattern of the entire legislative history repeated itself in miniature: a safeguard negotiated in good faith, designed around a static model, and outflanked by dynamics nobody modeled.

By the mid-1970s the direction was unmistakable, and the scholarly reassessment began. Reimers's archival work in the early 1990s established that the reassurances had been believed at the time, Graham's formulation of legislative self-deception gave the phenomenon a name, and the later quantitative literature confirmed the mechanisms. The first years after the signing thus divide into two phases: the administrative phase, in which the machinery was built and the numbers began to move, and the interpretive phase, in which historians reconstructed why the builders had expected otherwise. This history belongs to the second phase and tries to meet its standards.

## The Commission, the Cap, and the Transition

The Western Hemisphere ceiling deserves a closer look, because its design reveals how little the bargainers understood the forces they were bargaining about. Section 21(e) of Public Law 89-236 did not simply impose a number. It created a select commission to study Western Hemisphere immigration and provided that, unless Congress enacted inconsistent legislation on or before June 30, 1968, hemispheric immigration would be capped at 120,000 for the fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968, and for every fiscal year thereafter. The cap was a default, not an immediate command, and the default came with a timer.

The timer reflected the ambivalence of the bargain. The reformers accepted the ceiling to get the bill through the Senate, but they softened its onset with the commission study and the delayed effective date, plainly hoping that a future Congress might revisit the question. The restrictionists accepted the delay because they had the commitment in statutory text, and statutory text is harder to ignore than a promise. Both sides got something: the reformers got their bill, the restrictionists got their number. Neither side got what it expected from the number, because the number leaked.

The leak was the immediate-relative exemption. Spouses, minor children, and parents of United States citizens stood outside the 120,000 count, which meant the cap constrained only the non-exempt flow. As the exempt flow grew through the compounding dynamics described earlier, the cap covered a shrinking share of the total. Ervin had demanded a safeguard for the hemisphere; he received a ceiling with a hole in it, and the hole widened every year. The design flaw was not a drafting accident. It reflected the humanitarian consensus that close family reunification should not be numerically limited, a consensus no bargainer in 1965 was prepared to challenge. The consensus was sincere. Its interaction with the cap was unexamined.

The transition provisions added a further delay between enactment and effect. The act took partial effect on December 1, 1965, and came fully into force on July 1, 1968. The thirty-one-month phase-in gave the bureaucracy time to build the new preference machinery and gave the commission time to study the hemisphere. It also means that analyses dating the law's consequences from the October 1965 signing compress a transition the statute deliberately stretched. The first full fiscal year under the new act was fiscal 1968, which is why the DHS figure of 454,450 for that year is the proper baseline for measuring the law's early effects against the 296,700 of fiscal 1965.

## Deception or Miscalculation

The prediction record has been put to two opposed uses, and an honest history must present both, attribute every interpretation to a named source, and distinguish scholarship from advocacy. One reading treats the quotations as proof of deception: the sponsors knew the bill would transform immigration and lied to get it passed. The other reading treats the quotations as irrelevant, or treats the predictions as sincere and wrong for identifiable structural reasons. The evidence supports the second reading, and the historians who have studied the question say so, but the first reading has a public life that deserves an accurate description rather than a caricature.

The scholarly position is the sincere-miscalculation reading, and it is carried by the historians who have done the archival work. Otis Graham, the immigration historian, wrote that the 1965 reform was an inside-the-beltway adjustment engineered by liberal elites who pledged, and by all evidence sincerely expected, that its important symbolic reforms would bring little change in the number and origin of immigrants. A later summary of Graham's work characterizes his view as legislative self-deception, which is error, not fraud. David Reimers, in his history of post-1965 immigration, concluded that the reassurances do seem to have been believed at the time. Michael Barone, reviewing the record, wrote that there was no intentional deception: the Kennedys were echoing the expert testimony of the time, and the mistake was to think that immigration and the American labor market in the future would look like immigration and the American labor market in the past. Roger Daniels, the historian of American immigration restriction, put the point as counterfactual: had Congress fully understood the consequences, it almost certainly would not have passed the bill, which is a claim about ignorance, not deceit. And the 2024 peer-reviewed economic history of the act, by Giovanni Facchini, Timothy Hatton, and Max Steinhardt in the Journal of Economic History, concludes that much of the subsequent increase was due to side effects that would have been hard to predict in 1965 or to later policies.

The evidence for sincerity is circumstantial but weighty. The sponsors' private behavior matched their public words: they accepted bargains, the family weighting and the hemispheric cap, that only made sense if they believed the mechanisms would hold. A deceiver does not pay for safeguards he knows are useless. The expert testimony of the period supported the predictions; Barone's point about the Kennedys echoing the experts is documented in the hearing record. And the mechanism of the error is identifiable after the fact, which is what the prediction-and-outcome table above attempts to show. Sincere people can be wrong in structured ways, and the structure here, the compounding dynamics of sponsorship rights, was genuinely hard to see in advance.

The deception reading exists, but it lives in advocacy commentary, not in peer-reviewed history. Jerry Kammer of the Center for Immigration Studies, in a 2015 report on the act, advances the charge that the sponsors misled the public, and contributors to restrictionist journals have pressed versions of it. These writers deserve to be named and their argument described fairly: they point to the gap between prediction and outcome and infer intent from the gap. But the inference is theirs, and no peer-reviewed historian located in the verification for this article makes the intentional-deception case. Presenting the deception charge as scholarly consensus would be false. It is commentary, and this history labels it as such.

There is a further reason to prefer the miscalculation reading, and it concerns the sponsors' own interests. The prediction that mattered most to the bargaining, the family-mirror premise, was supplied by the restrictionists themselves. Feighan's insistence on family weighting rested on the belief that it would preserve the ethnic profile. The reformers adopted a restrictionist's safeguard and then watched it do the opposite of what the restrictionist intended. A deception theory has to explain why the supposed deceivers built their bill around their opponents' theory of demographics. The miscalculation theory has no such difficulty: everyone in the room, reformers and restrictionists alike, shared the same flawed model.

For readers who want the broader field of myths and corrections around this statute, the companion piece at /2013/04/15/us-immigration-law-myths/ collects them. This history draws no conclusion about present-day policy. Its subject is 1965, and its verdict is confined to the record: the predictions were sincere, specific, and wrong, and they were wrong for reasons the table makes visible.

## The Historians' Verdict

The scholarly literature on the prediction record is unusually one-sided, and the one-sidedness itself is evidence. The historians who have examined the archives agree that the sponsors believed what they said. Otis Graham's formulation remains the classic statement: the reform was an inside-the-beltway adjustment engineered by liberal elites who pledged, and by all evidence sincerely expected, that its important symbolic reforms would bring little change in the number and origin of immigrants. Graham later characterized the episode as legislative self-deception, a phrase that captures the distinction at the heart of this history. Self-deception is not other-deception. The legislators fooled themselves, not the public.

David Reimers reached the same conclusion from the archival record, finding that the reassurances do seem to have been believed at the time. The qualifier matters: Reimers does not claim the sponsors were right, only that the evidence shows they believed it. Michael Barone added the mechanism of the error, arguing there was no intentional deception and that the Kennedys were echoing the expert testimony of the time. The mistake, in Barone's account, was temporal: assuming the future of immigration and labor markets would resemble the past. Roger Daniels supplied the counterfactual test: had Congress fully understood the consequences, it almost certainly would not have passed the bill. Ignorance, not fraud, is the theory that fits the votes.

The 2024 economic history by Facchini, Hatton, and Steinhardt brought quantitative discipline to the question, showing that much of the increase attributed to the act came from hard-to-predict side effects or later policies. Their work does not exonerate the sponsors' arithmetic, but it complicates the story the deception theorists tell, in which a straight line runs from the bill's text to the demographic transformation. The line was not straight. It bent around exemptions, compounding, and later legislation.

The deception charge, by contrast, has no peer-reviewed historian behind it. It is advanced by advocacy writers, principally Jerry Kammer of the Center for Immigration Studies in a 2015 report, and by contributors to restrictionist journals. Their argument deserves a fair statement: the gap between what sponsors promised and what followed is so large that it strains credulity to call it accident, and the sponsors had political motives to minimize the bill's apparent impact. That is a serious argument, and it is honestly held. But it is commentary, not scholarship, and this history will not promote it to a status its own authors' venues do not claim. When the deception reading appears in public debate, readers should ask for the historian. There is not one.

The deeper point is about how democracies legislate under uncertainty. The 1965 Congress voted on a model of the world, the family-mirror premise, that every faction in the room shared. Reformers and restrictionists disagreed about values but agreed about mechanics, and the mechanics were wrong. When the error emerged, each side rewrote the history to suit its politics: one side found villains, the other found irrelevance. The defensible reading, the one the historians support, is less dramatic and more instructive. Sincere people, acting on the best expert advice available, built a machine whose dynamics they did not understand, and the machine did what machines do. It ran.

## The Myth of the Master Plan

The deception theory, stripped to its essentials, is a master-plan theory: it holds that the sponsors secretly intended the demographic transformation, concealed their intent behind false predictions, and executed a plan the public would never have approved. The theory is worth refuting in detail, because its persistence distorts public understanding of how the act passed, and because the refutation illustrates the evidentiary standards this history has tried to observe.

The master-plan theory fails first on the evidence of the bargains. A group executing a secret plan to transform immigration does not build its bill around its opponents' demographic theory. Yet the family-weighted preference system was Feighan's design, adopted because Feighan believed it would preserve the ethnic profile, and the hemispheric ceiling was Ervin's price, accepted because Ervin believed it would restrain numbers. The master-plan theorist must explain why the supposed conspirators armed their enemies' safeguards and then watched those safeguards produce the opposite of their intended effect. The miscalculation theory has a simpler account: everyone shared the flawed model, so the safeguards everyone trusted failed everyone equally.

It fails second on the documentary record. Decades of archival research by Graham, Reimers, and others have uncovered no memorandum, no private correspondence, no diary entry in which a sponsor admits to expecting transformation while preaching stability. The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but in this case the archives have been thoroughly worked, and what they contain is consistent: private assessments matching public predictions. Reimers's finding that the reassurances do seem to have been believed at the time rests on exactly this kind of documentary comparison. A master plan that left no paper trail across half a century of scholarship is possible but increasingly implausible.

It fails third on the logic of political self-interest. The sponsors who predicted modest effects had every incentive to be right. Kennedy, Celler, Hart, and Johnson staked their credibility on the predictions; the predictions' failure damaged them and their cause. A deceiver benefits from deception. The sponsors of the 1965 act were harmed by the gap between their words and the outcome, which is precisely what one would expect if the words were sincere. The deception theory requires its villains to have acted against their own interests for no discernible gain, since the bill would likely have passed with honest uncertainty more easily than with elaborate lies.

What remains, after the master-plan theory is set aside, is the harder and more interesting question: how do sincere, well-advised legislators get the future so wrong? The answer this history has developed is structural. They modeled a snapshot instead of a film, confused stocks with flows, mistook screens for valves, and extrapolated a fixed demand that their own statute would enlarge. These are cognitive errors, not moral ones, and they are the errors to which legislative bodies are systematically prone, because legislatures optimize for coalition-building rather than systems analysis. The 1965 act is the clearest American case study of the phenomenon, which is why its passage history deserves the careful, unweaponized telling this article has attempted.

## The Signing on Liberty Island

On October 3, 1965, President Johnson signed H.R. 2580 on Liberty Island in New York Harbor, at the base of the Statue of Liberty, and the law became Public Law 89-236. The choice of venue was deliberate stagecraft. Johnson's advance team understood that the immigration bill needed a symbolic setting equal to its moral claims, and no site in America carried more immigration symbolism than the island in the harbor. The ceremony linked the statute to the national narrative of welcome, and the photographs fixed that linkage in public memory.

Johnson's remarks leaned into the symbolism and away from the substance. He presented the bill as a correction to an embarrassing anachronism, praised the congressional managers by name, and predicted modest effects. His assurance that the measure was not revolutionary and would not reshape daily life echoed the sponsors' predictions and shared their fate. The signing remarks are sometimes quoted as evidence of deception, but in context they read as the standard presidential rhetoric of reassurance, the kind of language chief executives use when they want a controversial bill to land softly.

The ceremony's guest list and staging also served a political purpose: associating the bill with the Statue of Liberty made opposition sound un-American in a way that floor debate could not. Symbolism does not pass bills, but it protects them afterward, and the Liberty Island signing gave the 1965 act a patriotic aura that complicated later criticism. The law's text was dry; its christening was vivid.

## The Architects and the Gatekeepers

Legislative histories often read as if bills move themselves. They do not. H.R. 2580 moved because particular people occupied particular positions at a particular moment, and the 1965 story is incomprehensible without them. The cast divides into architects, who designed the reform, and gatekeepers, who could have stopped it and were instead converted or bought.

Emanuel Celler of Brooklyn was the reformers' institutional memory. He had entered Congress in the 1920s, had denounced the quota system in his maiden speech, and had carried the liberal alternative to the McCarran-Walter Act in 1952. By 1965 he chaired the House Judiciary Committee, which gave him control of the legislative vehicle itself. Celler's long tenure meant he had watched every previous reform effort die, and it showed in his tactics: he did not lead with moral thunder but with a bill engineered to survive. When Johnson recalled at the signing that Celler had criticized the quota system almost forty years earlier, the president was acknowledging something the House already knew, that the man holding the gavel had been waiting for this vote for most of his adult life.

Philip Hart of Michigan supplied the Senate's moral weight. Hart had sponsored the 1962 quota-revision bill and had introduced the Kennedy administration's 1963 bill in the Senate, so his name was already attached to the cause when 1965 arrived. As the Senate manager of H.R. 2580, he carried the chamber responsibilities: guiding the measure through committee, holding the coalition together, and lending the effort the credibility of a senator known for careful, unshowy liberalism. The bill's Senate sponsorship is sometimes described as Kennedy's alone, but the record assigns Hart the manager's role, with Kennedy as floor leader.

Ted Kennedy of Massachusetts, at thirty-three the youngest member of the Senate, chaired the Judiciary Subcommittee on Immigration and Naturalization and served as the bill's floor leader. His chairmanship gave him the hearings, and the hearings gave him the prediction record. Kennedy's opening statement of February 10, 1965, with its assurances about cities, ethnic mix, and jobs, became the most quoted text of the entire debate, which is why the misattribution of those words to the Senate floor has done so much damage. Kennedy also served as the negotiator who accepted the Western Hemisphere ceiling, the concession that bought the Senate's restrictionists. Youth, name recognition, and a chairman's gavel made him the public face of a campaign whose substance had been assembled by older hands.

Michael Feighan of Ohio was the gatekeeper who changed sides. As chairman of the House Judiciary immigration subcommittee and a longtime restrictionist, Feighan held the classic choke point: nothing reached the House floor without passing through his subcommittee. He had succeeded Francis Walter, the coauthor of the 1952 act and the embodiment of restrictionism, after Walter's death in 1963. Observers expected Feighan to continue Walter's blockade. Instead he became a convert to reform, and his alternative bill, negotiated with the Johnson White House, supplied the seven-preference system and the enlarged family share that defined the House version. Feighan's conversion is the single most important procedural event in the House story, because it turned the subcommittee from a graveyard into an engine. His premise, that family weighting would preserve the ethnic profile, was the restrictionist theory embedded at the heart of a reform bill.

Sam Ervin of North Carolina was the Senate's gatekeeper. A Southern Democrat and a skilled parliamentarian, Ervin led the restrictionist effort in the Senate immigration subcommittee and threatened to block action unless concessions were made. His first preference was to preserve the national origins quotas outright, but he could count votes as well as anyone, and he switched to a fallback demand: a ceiling on Western Hemisphere immigration. The switch is revealing. Ervin did not fight to the death for the old formula; he bargained for a new control he believed would do similar work. His demand, joined by Senate Minority Leader Everett Dirksen, became the 120,000 cap.

Dirksen's role deserves emphasis because it crossed party lines. As the Republican leader in the Senate, his support for the cap gave the restrictionist demand bipartisan cover and made it politically costly for the reformers to refuse. The Kennedy team's acceptance of the Ervin-Dirksen demand is corroborated from both scholarly and advocacy sources, which agree on the shape of the deal even as they disagree about the act's legacy. Dirksen did not oppose the bill; he priced his acquiescence, and the price was the ceiling.

Lyndon Johnson operated above the chamber fray and inside the negotiations simultaneously. His White House dealt directly with Feighan on the House compromise, trading the family-weighted architecture for the subcommittee chairman's support. He backed the Senate bargaining that produced the hemispheric ceiling. And he used the presidency's ceremonial power to frame the outcome, choosing Liberty Island for the signing and publicly crediting the congressional managers, Feighan among them. Johnson's legislative genius is often described in terms of vote counting, but the 1965 immigration fight shows the other half of the craft: identifying which gatekeeper to convert, which to buy, and what currency each one accepted.

## The 89th Congress: Votes and Framing

The architects and gatekeepers operated inside a chamber transformed by the election of 1964. The Democratic landslide produced the most liberal Congress in a generation, with majorities large enough to absorb defections and still pass controversial measures. Size alone did not guarantee action, but it changed the calculations of everyone in the building. Committee chairmen who had ruled by seniority suddenly faced majorities that could override them. Southern Democrats who had anchored the restrictionist coalition found themselves a minority inside their own party's caucus. The arithmetic of 1965 made the previously impossible merely difficult.

The framing mattered as much as the arithmetic. For decades the quota formula had been debated as a technical question about labor supply and national security. In 1965 the reformers recast it as a question of discrimination, and the civil rights movement supplied the vocabulary. The formula did not simply limit numbers; it ranked the world's peoples by ancestry, assigning full quotas to some nations and token quotas to others. Once the country had committed itself, through the civil rights legislation of 1964, to the principle that government should not classify citizens by race, the reformers asked why the government should classify would-be immigrants by ancestry. The parallel was direct, deliberate, and devastating to the old defense of the system.

The voting rights legislation of the same year reinforced the point. While the immigration bill moved through committee, the nation watched the campaign for voting rights, and the two efforts drew on the same moral reservoir. Members who voted to protect the franchise of Black citizens in the South could hardly defend a quota system that told Asian and African applicants their ancestry disqualified them. The reformers pressed this connection in debate, and it gave wavering members a way to understand their vote: not as an immigration decision but as a civil rights decision. Procedure and framing reinforced each other. The majorities made the vote possible; the framing made the vote honorable.

Truman's 1952 veto message, quoted earlier, had supplied the moral language thirteen years before the votes existed to act on it. The 1965 reformers inherited that language and sharpened it. Where Truman had called the country-by-country pattern insulting and foreign to American ideals, the 1965 advocates added the institutional weight of the civil rights consensus. The argument was old. The audience was new.

None of this should be read as suggesting the outcome was automatic. Large majorities have buried controversial bills before, and the restrictionists of 1965 were experienced parliamentary fighters. Ervin's subcommittee threat was real, Feighan's conversion was not foreordained, and the MacGregor amendment showed that a hemispheric cap commanded substantial House support even in defeat. What the 89th Congress provided was opportunity, not certainty. The bargains converted the opportunity into law.

## The Votes in Detail

The three roll calls of 1965 repay close examination, because their details carry the story's quantitative signature. The House vote of August 25, 1965, on final passage of H.R. 2580 was 318 yeas to 95 nays. Democrats voted 209 to 71, with 13 not voting; Republicans voted 109 to 24, with 6 not voting. The Democratic majority supplied roughly two-thirds of the yes votes, and the Republican minority supplied a comfortable supplement. The opposition's 95 votes, concentrated among Southern Democrats and conservative Republicans, represented the remnant of the coalition that had preserved the formula in 1952, reduced to less than a quarter of the chamber.

The Senate vote of September 22, 1965, was 76 to 18. Democrats voted 52 to 15, Republicans 24 to 3, with 6 senators not voting. The regional breakdown within the Democratic vote is the most revealing number in the entire legislative history: non-Southern Democrats voted 43 to 2, Southern Democrats 9 to 13. The bill was, in voting terms, a civil rights measure: the South against, the rest of the country for, with Republicans joining the majority. The 18 nay votes look small against the 76 yeas, but they included the chamber's most experienced parliamentary fighters, which is why the reformers paid the ceiling to neutralize them rather than rolling them.

The conference report vote in the House on September 30, 1965, was 320 to 70, with Democrats at 202 to 60, Republicans at 118 to 10, and 23 members not voting. The conference margin was slightly wider than the original House passage margin, which tells its own story: the Senate's ceiling, far from costing the bill votes, may have reassured wavering members that a safeguard was in place. The House accepted a concession its floor had rejected a month earlier, because the alternative was to kill the bill over a provision the Senate considered non-negotiable.

A persistent source of confusion deserves a final note. Some later accounts cite House and Senate tallies of 324 to 101 and 78 to 20. Those figures include paired votes, the practice by which absent members record their positions through colleagues. The official announced tallies, the ones entered into the record and the ones this history uses throughout, are 318 to 95 and 76 to 18. Mixing the paired-vote variants into the official counts inflates both sides and confuses the record. Precision about the numbers is part of precision about the history.

## The Opposition's Case

The restrictionists who fought the 1965 bill were not cartoon villains, and their case deserves a fair hearing, because the bargains only make sense if the opposition was serious. The Southern Democrats who voted 9 to 13 against the bill in the Senate, the House members who backed the MacGregor amendment 189 strong, and the senators who demanded the hemispheric ceiling shared a coherent position. They believed the national origins formula had preserved the country's ethnic composition, they believed that composition was worth preserving, and they believed the reformers' assurances about modest effects were either naive or false. Events proved them wrong about the assurances and right to be skeptical, which is an uncomfortable combination for the reformers' memory.

Their strongest argument was the one the reformers never refuted but absorbed. The restrictionists said, in effect: you claim this bill will not change who comes, but you cannot know that, and the burden of proof is on those who would dismantle a system that has worked. Feighan's premise, that family weighting would preserve the ethnic profile, was the restrictionist answer to the restrictionist objection, a safeguard designed by a restrictionist for restrictionists. The tragedy of their position is that the safeguard they trusted was the mechanism that defeated them. They voted for the bill because they believed the family preferences would do their work. The preferences did the opposite.

Their weakest argument was the cultural one stated baldly. Few restrictionists in 1965 were willing to say outright that the law should prefer Europeans, because the civil rights consensus had made that language toxic. Instead they spoke of stability, tradition, and the dangers of rapid change, euphemisms that the reformers easily translated back into the ancestry ranking everyone understood. The civil rights framing beat the cultural argument before the immigration debate began. By 1965, in the wake of the 1964 legislation, a defense of ethnic quotas sounded like a defense of segregation, and most members did not want to be heard making it.

The labor and security arguments fared no better. Celler's floor speech answered them directly with the labor certification requirement and the screening provisions, and the reformers could point to expert testimony supporting the claim that the bill would not displace American workers. The experts were wrong about the dynamics, as Barone later noted, but in 1965 their testimony gave the reformers cover and left the opposition arguing against the specialists. An opposition that must dispute both the moral framing and the expert consensus is an opposition losing on two fronts.

What the opposition got right was the direction of the error. They doubted the predictions, and the predictions failed. But they doubted them for the wrong reason: they feared the reformers were understating deliberate effects, when the reformers were actually misjudging unintended ones. And having secured their safeguards, the family weighting and the ceiling, the restrictionists voted yes, which means the final responsibility for the outcome is shared. The bill passed with restrictionist votes purchased by restrictionist-designed safeguards. Everyone in the room owned the result.

## The Experts Behind the Predictions

Barone's observation that the Kennedys were echoing the expert testimony of the time points to a neglected part of the story: the predictions did not originate with the politicians. They originated with the specialists who testified that immigration and labor markets would behave in the future as they had behaved in the past. The politicians repeated what the experts told them, and the experts were working from models that could not see the compounding dynamics of the new preference system.

The expert consensus of 1965 rested on two assumptions. The first was demographic: that the pool of potential immigrants was fixed and known, so reallocating quotas would reallocate a stable flow. The second was economic: that the American labor market would absorb newcomers in the patterns observed in the postwar decades, without the bill altering the incentives to come. Both assumptions treated the statute as a passive reallocation device. Neither modeled the statute as an active incentive system that would summon new demand and compound through sponsorship rights.

Why did the experts miss the dynamics? Partly because the dynamics were genuinely novel. No previous American immigration law had combined numerically limited family preferences with an uncapped immediate-relative channel on this scale, so there was no historical dataset from which to extrapolate the compounding effects. Partly because the disciplines that might have caught the error, demography and economics, were not in the habit of modeling kinship networks as growth processes. And partly because the experts, like the legislators, shared the cultural assumption that family ties mirror populations rather than multiply them. The error was systematic, not individual, which is why it survived peer review in the loose sense of professional consensus.

This matters for the deception debate. If the sponsors had invented the predictions against expert advice, the deception theory would gain plausibility. The record shows the opposite: the sponsors' predictions aligned with what the specialists told them. A conspiracy to deceive would have required the experts' cooperation or the sponsors' defiance of the experts, and there is no evidence of either. What the record shows is a professional consensus that was wrong, transmitted faithfully by politicians who had no reason to doubt it. The miscalculation reading does not merely excuse the sponsors. It indicts the consensus, which is a larger and more useful lesson.

## The Labor Certification and the Screening Machinery

Celler's House floor speech rested his reassurance on three brakes: the family-tie premise, the labor certification requirement, and the cost of travel. The first brake has been examined at length. The second and third deserve their own autopsy, because they illustrate a different kind of forecasting error: the confusion of a screen with a valve.

The labor certification requirement provided that no employment-based immigrant could enter without an individual certificate from the Secretary of Labor guaranteeing that American workers would not be displaced. On its face this was a powerful safeguard, and Celler invoked it as one. But the certification screened the employment channel, not the family channel, and the family channel was where the growth occurred. A control that binds one pipe does not limit the flow through another. As family sponsorship compounded, the employment-based categories, with their certifications and caps, became a smaller share of a larger total. The safeguard worked as designed on the flow it governed. The flow it governed was not the flow that grew.

The screening provisions more broadly, the health, security, and admissibility checks that Katzenbach and others cited, operated the same way. They determined who among the applicants could enter, not how many applicants there would be. "It will not relax the standards of admission," Kennedy said, and he was right: the standards were not relaxed. But standards are filters, and filters do not set volume. A finer filter on a larger stream still passes more water. The sponsors repeatedly conflated selectivity with scale, treating assurances about the strictness of screening as assurances about the size of the flow. The distinction is elementary in retrospect and was invisible in prospect, which is why it belongs in the catalog of structural errors rather than the catalog of deceptions.

The travel-cost brake dated fastest of all. Celler told the House that few Africans and Asians could even pay the cost of a ticket, as if the economics of 1965 were permanent. Within a decade, rising incomes abroad, cheaper air travel, and the remittance networks built by earlier immigrants had eroded the barrier. More importantly, the premise misunderstood migration economics: the decision to migrate is financed against expected future earnings, not current savings, and kinship networks routinely pool resources to pay passage. The ticket-price argument treated immigrants as isolated consumers. They were members of families, which was the point of the bill's own preference system. The left hand of the reassurance did not know what the right hand of the statute was doing.

## What the Sponsors Got Right

A history focused on failed predictions risks implying that the sponsors failed at everything. They did not. The 1965 reformers got the most important thing right: the national origins formula was indefensible, and ending it was a genuine achievement. Truman had said the country-by-country pattern insulted citizens and contradicted American ideals. The 89th Congress acted on that judgment, and the action stands regardless of what followed. A law can be morally right and technically miscalculated at the same time. The 1965 act was both.

The sponsors were also right that family reunification was a defensible organizing principle for immigration law. Whatever the demographic consequences, the idea that citizens and residents should be able to bring their closest kin to America commands broad support, and the preference system's emphasis on family reflected a humane instinct. The error lay not in valuing family unity but in predicting what valuing it would produce. A principle can be sound while its implementation surprises its authors. The distinction matters because later debates sometimes treat the unintended consequences as proof that the principle was corrupt. It was not. It was miscalculated.

They were right, finally, about the symbolism, even as they were wrong about the scale. Johnson's Liberty Island ceremony framed the act as America's recommitment to welcome, and that framing described something real. The statute did repudiate ancestry as a basis for allocating immigration opportunity, and the repudiation mattered to the Americans whose kin the old formula had disfavored. The symbolic correction was not empty. It was one of the genuine civil rights achievements of the 1960s, and it belongs in the same historical sequence as the legislation of 1964 and the voting rights measures of 1965.

None of this erases the forecasting failure. The sponsors promised modest effects and produced a transformation, and the transformation deserves the unflinching accounting this history has given it. But a complete verdict keeps both halves in view: the moral achievement was real, and the technical failure was real, and they were the same bill. That is why the Hart-Celler Act remains the most instructive legislative history in American immigration law. It shows what principled reform can accomplish and what it can unleash, in the same text, through the same bargains, voted by the same sincere and mistaken majorities.

## The Quota System the Bill Replaced

The national origins formula that H.R. 2580 abolished had governed American immigration since 1924, and its mechanics explain why the reformers cared so intensely about ending it. The system assigned each country an annual quota derived from the national origins of the American population as recorded in an earlier census. In practice the formula reserved the overwhelming share of quota numbers for northern and western Europe, while countries in Asia and Africa received small fractions that in some cases amounted to only a handful of visas per year. The discrimination was not incidental to the design. It was the design.

Truman's veto message of 1952 had said exactly this, in the moral language the 1965 reformers would inherit. The country-by-country pattern, Truman wrote, insulted large numbers of American citizens, irritated allies abroad, and contradicted the country's ideals. The insult fell heaviest on Americans whose ancestry traced to the disfavored regions. A citizen of Asian descent lived under an immigration law that declared, in effect, that people like his parents were less welcome than people like his neighbors. The reformers of 1965 understood the quota system as a domestic civil rights issue for this reason: it classified Americans by bloodline and told some of them their kin counted for less.

The formula also distorted the politics of reform. Because the quotas determined who could come, they determined who had relatives in America, which determined who could testify, organize, and lobby for change. The system starved its own opposition. Communities from the disfavored regions were small precisely because the quotas kept them small, and their smallness made it harder to build the political pressure needed to change the quotas. The reformers faced a circular trap: the law's effects prevented the formation of the coalition that might repeal the law. Breaking the circle required outside force, and the outside force arrived in the form of the civil rights movement and the 1964 election.

Defenders of the formula made two arguments. The first was cultural: the quota system preserved the nation's ethnic composition, and altering it would alter the country. The second was economic and security-based: unrestricted or reallocated immigration would displace workers and admit subversives. The 1965 reformers answered the first argument by embracing its premise and reversing its conclusion. They did not deny that the formula shaped composition; they denied that government should shape composition by ancestry. The family-preference compromise then neutralized the argument politically by promising, sincerely, that composition would barely change. The reformers answered the second argument with labor certification and screening provisions, the machinery Celler described on the House floor. Both answers were designed to reassure. Both reassurances failed, for reasons this history has traced.

## Procedure Made the Text

Step back and consider what the passage history demonstrates about how laws are made. The 1965 act's most consequential features were not chosen for their consequences. The family-weighted preference system was chosen because Feighan controlled the House subcommittee and demanded it. The Western Hemisphere ceiling was chosen because Ervin and Dirksen controlled the Senate's chokepoints and priced their acquiescence. The delayed effective date and the commission study were chosen to make the ceiling palatable. The immediate-relative exemption was chosen because no coalition in 1965 would vote to cap the reunification of citizens with their closest kin. Every one of these design elements was a procedural artifact, the residue of a bargain struck to move a bill through a particular room at a particular moment.

Then the artifacts interacted. The family weighting compounded through sponsorship rights. The exemption removed the cap from the fastest-growing channel. The ceiling arrived late and leaked. The phase-in delayed the reckoning. None of the bargainers modeled these interactions, because bargainers model votes, not dynamics. They asked whether each concession would secure the next vote, and the answer was yes in every case. They did not ask what the concessions would do when assembled into a working system, because that question belonged to a future nobody in the room was paid to consider.

This is the thesis thread of the history: procedure and bargaining determined the text, and the enacted text behaved in ways its authors did not model. The thread runs from the long failure through the trades, the predictions, and the aftermath without a break. The long failure was procedural, a story of gatekeepers and chokepoints. The breakthrough was procedural, a story of converted gatekeepers and purchased acquiescence. The predictions were procedural cover, sincere reassurances built on a static model. And the aftermath was the revenge of dynamics on procedure, the system doing what systems do when their designers optimize for the next vote instead of the next decade.

There is a temptation to moralize this story, to cast the sponsors as villains or fools. Resist it. The legislators of 1965 faced a real choice between an indefensible quota system and a reform whose consequences they could not fully foresee, and they chose reform. The choice was honorable. The forecasting was poor. Democracies routinely legislate under uncertainty, and the 1965 act is the textbook case of what uncertainty costs. The bill passed because the bargains worked. The bargains worked because everyone shared the same flawed model. And the model was flawed in ways that only became visible when the machine started running.

## Closing Assessment

Return to the One Test. A reader should be able to explain why a proposal that had failed for two decades passed by lopsided bipartisan margins in 1965. It passed because the 89th Congress supplied the votes and the civil rights framing, because Feighan's conversion and the family-weighted preference system bought the House restrictionists, because the Western Hemisphere ceiling bought the Senate restrictionists, and because the sponsors' predictions, sincere and specific, gave wavering members cover to vote yes. The House voted 318 to 95 on August 25, the Senate 76 to 18 on September 22, the conference report passed the House 320 to 70 on September 30, and Johnson signed Public Law 89-236 on Liberty Island on October 3.

The thesis thread runs through every stage: procedure and bargaining determined the text, and the enacted text behaved in ways its authors did not model. The preference system was a procedural artifact of the Feighan compromise, not a demographic forecast, and it compounded. The hemispheric ceiling was a Senate price, not a control that bit, since it arrived with a delayed effective date and an immediate-relative exemption. The predictions were the cover the bargains needed, and the cover rested on a static model of a dynamic system.

There is a final irony worth stating plainly. The restrictionists who voted yes believed they were buying safeguards. The reformers who voted yes believed they were buying fairness. Both got less than they expected and more than they feared, because the mechanism belonged to neither camp's theory. That is the legislative history of the Hart-Celler Act: a bill passed by people who understood its politics and misunderstood its arithmetic.

## A Coda on the One Test

The One Test posed at the outset asked for three things, and the history supplies them in compact form. First, why did the proposal pass in 1965 after failing for two decades? Because the 89th Congress combined liberal majorities with a civil rights framing that made ancestry-based quotas morally indefensible, and because the bargains converted or purchased the gatekeepers who had blocked every previous attempt. Feighan's conversion unlocked the House subcommittee, the family-weighted preferences bought the House restrictionists, and the Western Hemisphere ceiling bought the Senate restrictionists. The votes followed the bargains: 318 to 95 in the House, 76 to 18 in the Senate, 320 to 70 on the conference report.

Second, what did the sponsors predict, in their own words? Kennedy told the February 1965 subcommittee hearings that cities would not be flooded with a million immigrants a year and the ethnic mix would not be upset. Celler told the House floor there was no danger of an influx from Africa and Asia, citing family ties, labor certification, and travel costs. Robert Kennedy estimated a net increase of at most 50,000 a year. Fong said Asian immigration would never reach one percent of the population. Katzenbach said the bill was not designed to increase numbers. Johnson called it not a revolutionary bill. Each statement is quoted in this history with its venue and context, and the venue correction for Kennedy's words is the single most important factual repair the record needs.

Third, what actually happened? Legal immigration rose from 296,700 in fiscal 1965 to 454,450 in fiscal 1968, then to 524,300 in fiscal 1980 and 841,000 in fiscal 2000. The European share of immigration fell from more than half in the 1950s to 16 percent in the 1990s while the Asian share rose from 6 percent to 31 percent. More than 18 million legal immigrants entered in the three decades after passage, over three times the preceding thirty years' total. The sponsors' mechanisms failed in the four ways catalogued here: the mirror was a multiplier, the small base compounded fastest, the uncapped immediate-relative channel grew without limit, and the new incentives summoned new demand. The historians' verdict, from Graham and Reimers through Barone, Daniels, and the 2024 economic history literature, is sincere miscalculation, not deception. The advocacy-side deception charge is named, sourced to its actual proponents, and left where the evidence puts it: outside the scholarly consensus.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did the Hart-Celler Act pass Congress?

The bill, H.R. 2580, was introduced by Representative Emanuel Celler on January 13, 1965, and moved through the House Judiciary Committee under an immigration subcommittee chaired by the converted restrictionist Michael Feighan. The House passed it on August 25, 1965, by 318 to 95. In the Senate, Philip Hart managed the bill and Ted Kennedy served as floor leader; the Senate added a 120,000 Western Hemisphere ceiling as the price demanded by Sam Ervin and Everett Dirksen, then passed it on September 22 by 76 to 18. The House accepted the conference report on September 30 by 320 to 70, and President Lyndon Johnson signed it on October 3, 1965, as Public Law 89-236.

### Q: Who wrote the Hart-Celler Act?

Representative Emanuel Celler of New York introduced H.R. 2580 in the House and is its principal House author, a role he had played for reform efforts going back to the 1952 fight. In the Senate, Philip Hart of Michigan managed the bill, while Ted Kennedy, chairman of the immigration subcommittee, served as floor leader. The distinctive family-weighted preference system was shaped by Representative Michael Feighan of Ohio, the restrictionist subcommittee chairman whose alternative bill, negotiated with the Johnson White House, supplied the seven-preference architecture. The Western Hemisphere ceiling was a Senate addition extracted by restrictionist senators. Authorship was thus collective and bargained.

### Q: What did supporters predict the Hart-Celler Act would do?

Supporters predicted modest effects. At Senate subcommittee hearings on February 10, 1965, Ted Kennedy said cities would not be flooded with a million immigrants annually, the present level would remain substantially the same, and the ethnic mix would not be upset. On the House floor, Emanuel Celler said there was no danger of an influx from Africa and Asia, citing few family ties, labor certification, and travel costs. Robert Kennedy predicted a net increase of at most 50,000 a year. Hiram Fong said Asian immigrants would never reach one percent of the population. At the signing, Lyndon Johnson called it not a revolutionary bill. Every prediction proved wrong.

### Q: Why did Congress end national origins quotas in the Hart-Celler Act?

Congress ended the formula because the 89th Congress combined liberal majorities with a civil rights framing that made ancestry-based allocation morally indefensible. The national origins system ranked countries by bloodline, granting generous quotas to northern and western Europe and token quotas to Asia and Africa. After the civil rights movement discredited racial classification in domestic law, reformers argued the same classification could not stand in immigration law. Earlier reform attempts had failed for lack of votes and friendly committee leadership; the 1964 elections supplied both. Restrictionist objections were then neutralized through bargains, family-weighted preferences and a hemispheric cap, that were expected to preserve the status quo.

### Q: Was the Hart-Celler Act bipartisan?

Yes, by the roll calls. In the House on August 25, 1965, Democrats voted 209 to 71 and Republicans 109 to 24, for a total of 318 to 95. In the Senate on September 22, Democrats voted 52 to 15 and Republicans 24 to 3, for 76 to 18. The conference report passed the House on September 30 by 320 to 70, with both parties in favor. Bipartisanship needs one qualification: the Democratic majority supplied the margin in each vote, and opposition concentrated among Southern Democrats, who split 9 to 13 in the Senate, plus a minority of conservative Republicans. The bill was a liberal-led measure with genuine Republican support, not a party-line fight.

### Q: What was the final vote tally on the Hart-Celler Act?

The official passage tallies are 318 to 95 in the House on August 25, 1965, and 76 to 18 in the Senate on September 22, 1965. Some later accounts cite 324 to 101 and 78 to 20, but those figures include paired votes; the announced tallies are the ones to use. The party breakdowns were Democrats 209 to 71 and Republicans 109 to 24 in the House, and Democrats 52 to 15 and Republicans 24 to 3 in the Senate. After the Senate added the Western Hemisphere ceiling, the House agreed to the conference report on September 30 by 320 to 70. Do not mix the paired-vote variants into the official counts.

### Q: Did the Hart-Celler Act relate to the civil rights movement?

It related closely in timing, framing, and votes. The 89th Congress that passed the immigration bill also passed the voting rights legislation of 1965, and the reformers deliberately framed the national origins formula as a civil rights question: a system that allocated immigration by ancestry was the immigration counterpart of the racial classifications the movement was dismantling at home. The civil rights legislation of 1964 had already weakened the Southern bloc that defended the quota system. Members who had voted to end racial discrimination in public life found it difficult to defend ethnic discrimination in immigration law. The connection was substantive strategy, not retrospective decoration.

### Q: Where did Lyndon Johnson sign the Hart-Celler Act?

Johnson signed the bill on October 3, 1965, on Liberty Island in New York Harbor, at the base of the Statue of Liberty. The location was chosen for its symbolism, linking the new law to the national narrative of immigration, and Johnson's signing remarks played to the setting. In those remarks he called the measure not a revolutionary bill and predicted it would not reshape daily life, a prediction the subsequent decades did not bear out. The signing site is sometimes misremembered as the White House or Ellis Island; the contemporary record, including the Lyndon Johnson Presidential Library materials, places the ceremony on Liberty Island.

### Q: What role did Michael Feighan play in the Hart-Celler Act's passage?

Representative Michael Feighan of Ohio, chairman of the House Judiciary immigration subcommittee, was the central House convert. A longtime restrictionist who succeeded the arch-restrictionist Francis Walter after Walter's death in 1963, Feighan surprised observers by becoming a supporter of reform. His alternative bill, negotiated with the Johnson White House, largely shaped the version that reached the House floor: it introduced the seven-preference system and increased the share of visas for relatives of American citizens. Feighan insisted family unification take priority over employability because he believed family weighting would preserve the existing ethnic profile of immigration. Johnson later named him among those who guided the bill to passage.

### Q: How did the Western Hemisphere ceiling get into the Hart-Celler Act?

The ceiling entered in the Senate as the price of passage. The House-passed bill contained no Western Hemisphere cap. In the Senate, restrictionists led by Sam Ervin of North Carolina threatened to block the bill in subcommittee unless concessions were made. Ervin had hoped to preserve the national origins quotas but lacked the votes, so he and Senate Minority Leader Everett Dirksen demanded a cap on Western Hemisphere immigration instead. The reformers accepted, and the Senate added a 120,000 annual ceiling. The mechanism was a default: under section 21(e), the cap would take effect for the fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968, unless Congress acted otherwise, with immediate relatives of citizens excluded.

### Q: What was the MacGregor amendment to the Hart-Celler Act?

On the House floor, Representative Clark MacGregor, a Minnesota Republican, offered an amendment to cap Western Hemisphere immigration at 115,000 per year. The House defeated the amendment 189 to 218 and then passed the bill with no hemispheric ceiling at all. The defeat is significant for two reasons. First, it shows the House majority preferred an uncapped bill. Second, it foreshadowed the Senate bargain: the restrictionists lost the amendment in the House but extracted a slightly higher cap, 120,000, in the Senate as their price for letting the bill through. The MacGregor vote is the clearest floor evidence that the ceiling was a concession, not a design preference.

### Q: When did the Western Hemisphere ceiling take effect?

The 120,000 Western Hemisphere ceiling took effect in the fiscal year beginning July 1, 1968, under Section 21(e) of Public Law 89-236. The statute first commissioned a select commission to study Western Hemisphere immigration, and provided that unless inconsistent legislation was enacted on or before June 30, 1968, special-immigrant numbers from the Western Hemisphere would not exceed 120,000 in that fiscal year or thereafter. Immediate relatives of United States citizens were excluded from the cap. The act as a whole was partially effective December 1, 1965, and fully effective July 1, 1968.

### Q: What did Senator Kennedy actually say in the 1965 hearings?

On February 10, 1965, opening the Senate Judiciary immigration subcommittee hearings, Chairman Edward Kennedy said the cities would not be flooded with a million immigrants annually, that the present level of immigration would remain substantially the same, and that the ethnic mix would not be upset. He added that the bill would not inundate America with immigrants from any one country or area, or from the most populated and deprived nations of Africa and Asia. His summary ran: the bill will not flood the cities, will not upset the ethnic mix, will not relax admission standards, and will not cost American workers their jobs. These were hearing statements, not floor debate.

### Q: What did Representative Celler say on the House floor?

During the August 25, 1965, House debate, Representative Emanuel Celler argued that since the people of Africa and Asia had very few relatives in the United States, comparatively few could immigrate from those countries for lack of family ties. He added that no one could come without a Labor Department certificate guaranteeing American workers would not be displaced, that few prospective immigrants could even pay the cost of the ticket, and concluded there was no danger whatsoever of an influx from Africa and Asia. The peer-reviewed literature cites the Congressional Record of August 25, 1965, for the passage. It is the fullest floor statement of the family-ties premise.

### Q: How did the conference report differ from the House bill?

The conference report essentially adopted the Senate version, which meant it carried the 120,000 Western Hemisphere ceiling that the House bill had lacked. The House had defeated MacGregor's 115,000 cap amendment 189 to 218 and passed H.R. 2580 without any Western Hemisphere limit, while the Senate added the 120,000 cap in committee at the demand of Ervin and Dirksen. The conference reconciled the two versions around the Senate text. The House approved the conference report on September 30, 1965, by 320 to 70, a wider margin than the original 318 to 95 passage vote.

### Q: What earlier bills tried to end the quota system?

Before 1965, reformers made several attempts. In 1952 Celler and Senator Herbert Lehman advanced a liberal alternative to the McCarran-Walter bill, which prevailed and was enacted over Truman's veto. President Eisenhower sought to abolish the quota system in 1956 and again in 1960, without success. In 1962 Senator Philip Hart sponsored a bill basing quotas on the previous fifteen years of immigration adjusted for source-country population. In 1963 President Kennedy sent an administration reform bill to Congress, introduced in the Senate by Hart. Each effort died against the restrictionist committee leadership and the absence of a favorable floor majority.

### Q: What did Truman's 1952 veto message say about immigration quotas?

President Harry Truman vetoed the McCarran-Walter Act on June 25, 1952, because it preserved the national origins quota system. His message said the bill would continue, practically without change, the quota system enacted in 1924. Of the country-by-country limitations he wrote that they create a pattern insulting to large numbers of American citizens, irritating to allies abroad, and foreign to the country's purposes and ideals. He added a careful distinction: with the idea of quotas in general there was no quarrel, since some numerical limitation must be set; his objection was to the country-by-country pattern. Congress overrode the veto, and the act took effect June 27, 1952. The message became the moral template for the 1965 reformers.

### Q: How did Southern Democrats vote on the Hart-Celler Act?

Southern Democrats were the core of the opposition. In the Senate on September 22, 1965, Southern Democrats voted 9 in favor to 13 against, while non-Southern Democrats voted 43 to 2, according to the Congressional Quarterly key-vote tables. Overall Senate Democrats split 52 to 15. In the House, Democrats as a whole voted 209 to 71 for passage, with the Southern contingent supplying most of the Democratic no votes. The regional split captures the bill's civil rights character: the same Southern bloc that resisted voting rights resisted immigration reform, while the rest of the party, joined by most Republicans, carried it. Restrictionism in 1965 was geographically concentrated.

### Q: What part did Lyndon Johnson play in passing the Hart-Celler Act?

Johnson's White House negotiated the decisive House compromise directly with Representative Feighan, trading the family-weighted preference structure for the subcommittee chairman's support and his credibility with restrictionists. Johnson also backed the Senate bargaining that produced the Western Hemisphere ceiling, accepting Ervin's and Dirksen's price to keep the bill moving. At the signing ceremony on Liberty Island on October 3, 1965, he publicly credited the congressional managers, naming Feighan among those who guided the bill to passage. His signing remarks downplayed the bill as not revolutionary, a prediction that proved as wrong as the sponsors'. Johnson supplied pressure, patronage, and cover; the votes were organized, not merely inspired.

### Q: Who argues the sponsors deliberately deceived Congress?

The deliberate-deception charge is advanced by advocacy commentators, not by peer-reviewed historians. Jerry Kammer's account for the Center for Immigration Studies presents the prediction record as evidence of intentional misrepresentation, and contributors to The Social Contract journal have made similar arguments. The fact record for this article located no historian-authored peer-reviewed work making the intentional-deception case. Scholars including Otis Graham, David Reimers, Roger Daniels, and the team of Facchini, Hatton, and Steinhardt instead support the sincere-miscalculation reading. The deception claim should therefore be described as commentary, not as scholarly consensus.
