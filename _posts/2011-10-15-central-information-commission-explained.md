The Central Information Commission is the final appellate authority for information disputes involving the Central Government and the union territories, and on paper it is a formidable body. It can summon witnesses and records with the powers of a civil court. It can order a public authority to disclose information, to reorganise its record keeping, and to compensate an applicant who has suffered loss. It can fine the responsible officer personally, at a daily rate, out of that officer's own pocket. Its decisions are declared binding by the statute that created it. Yet appellants before this Commission routinely wait a year or more for a hearing, commissions across the country carry backlogs counted in lakhs of cases, and the body has on occasion recorded, in its own order, that it can do nothing further when a respondent simply refuses to obey it. This article explains that gap between the paper and the record, and it locates the gap where it belongs: not in the character of the people appointed, but in three structural features of the Right to Information Act, 2005 itself, namely the composition of the committee that appoints the commissioners, the insecurity written into their term and service conditions since the amendment of 2019, and the absence of any machinery by which the Commission can execute its own orders.

![The Central Information Commission, showing its appointment process, adjudicatory powers and the structural limits on enforcement - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The statute that creates the Commission, and the rights it exists to protect, are treated in full in [the complete guide to the Right to Information Act 2005](/2011/09/01/right-to-information-act-2005-guide/). The concern here is narrower and more institutional: what this body was designed to be able to do, who chooses its members, what it can order, what it cannot make happen, and why the distance between those two lists explains most of what is written about it. A reader who works through the design will be able to predict, from the text of the Act alone, both the Commission's genuine achievements and the points at which it predictably fails, because both are consequences of choices Parliament made in 2005 and adjusted in 2019.

## A creature of the statute it polices

The Central Information Commission is a statutory body, not a constitutional one. Section 12(1) of the Right to Information Act provides that the Central Government shall, by notification in the Official Gazette, constitute a body to be known as the Central Information Commission, to exercise the powers conferred on it and to perform the functions assigned to it under the Act. It was constituted with effect from 12 October 2005, the day the Act itself came into force, and its headquarters is at Delhi, with the possibility of offices elsewhere in India with the previous approval of the Central Government under Section 12(7). Its jurisdiction runs to the public authorities of the Centre: ministries and departments of the Government of India, bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed by it, and the administrations of the union territories. The corresponding commissions for the states are separate bodies constituted under Section 15, and the division between the two tiers is a recurring source of misdirected appeals, because an applicant whose grievance lies against a state department gains nothing by filing before the Central Commission, which has no authority over that department at all.

The distinction between a statutory and a constitutional body is not pedantry, and it shapes everything that follows in this article. The Election Commission and the Union Public Service Commission draw their tenure protections from the Constitution itself, so a Parliament that wished to weaken them would have to amend the Constitution, with the special majorities and, for some provisions, the state ratification that amendment requires. The Central Information Commission exists because an ordinary statute says so, and the same ordinary legislative process that created it can reshape its composition, its term of office, and the conditions on which its members serve. That is precisely what happened in 2019, when an amendment carried by ordinary majorities rewrote the tenure provisions of the Act. The Commission's independence therefore rests on statute and on convention rather than on constitutional entrenchment, and any honest profile of the institution has to reckon with how much of its authority is held on that revocable basis.

Within the scheme of the Act, the Commission sits at the third tier of an information dispute. The first tier is the Public Information Officer designated by each public authority under Section 5, who receives the application and must answer it within the period Section 7 allows. The second tier is the first appellate authority, an officer senior in rank to the Public Information Officer within the same public authority, who hears the appeal under Section 19(1). Only after those two tiers have been exhausted, or have failed to act in time, does the dispute reach the Commission, by second appeal under Section 19(3) or by complaint under Section 18. The Commission is therefore a body that sees a dispute only after the administrative machine has already had two opportunities to resolve it, which means the cases that reach it are disproportionately the hard ones: the refusals a department is determined to defend, the exemptions it is determined to claim, and the silences it has allowed to become deemed refusals. A tribunal fed on the residue of two failed tiers will always look slower and more contentious than the system it caps, and some of the criticism directed at the Commission properly belongs to the tiers below it.

The Act also assigns the Commission a reporting function that is easy to overlook and that matters to its institutional character. Under Section 25, the Commission prepares an annual report on the implementation of the Act, which the Central Government causes to be laid before each House of Parliament. Ministries and departments are required to collect and supply the material the report needs, including the number of requests each public authority received, the decisions under which access was refused, and the particulars of any disciplinary action taken. The report is the one instrument by which the Commission addresses Parliament and the public directly rather than through individual cases, and its tables are the source from which much of the reliable evidence about the Act's working, including disposal and pendency patterns, is drawn. A commission that can only report, and cannot direct, on the systemic side of its mandate reveals its design twice over, which is a theme this article returns to when it reaches the proactive disclosure obligations of Section 4.

## Who sits on the Central Information Commission

Section 12(2) fixes the shape of the body. The Commission consists of the Chief Information Commissioner and such number of Information Commissioners, not exceeding ten, as may be deemed necessary. The ceiling is statutory; the working strength is not. How many of the ten permissible commissioner posts are actually filled at any given time is a matter of executive appointment, and the distance between the sanctioned ceiling of eleven members including the Chief and the number in office is one of the two variables, along with the volume of incoming appeals, that determines how long an appellant waits. The Act sets the maximum and then leaves the institution's real capacity to the appointing process, which is the first structural feature in embryo.

Section 12(5) prescribes who may be appointed. The Chief Information Commissioner and the Information Commissioners are to be persons of eminence in public life with wide knowledge and experience in law, science and technology, social service, management, journalism, mass media, or administration and governance. The list is deliberately wide. It contemplates a commission drawn from seven different fields of public life, so that the body deciding disputes about scientific establishments, police forces, universities, banks and ministries would itself contain people who understand those worlds from inside. Section 12(6) then states the disqualifications: a commissioner shall not be a Member of Parliament or a member of the legislature of any state or union territory, shall not hold any other office of profit, shall not be connected with any political party, and shall not carry on any business or pursue any profession. The office is meant to be full time, undivided, and detached from party and commerce.

Section 12(4) distributes authority inside the building. The general superintendence, direction and management of the affairs of the Commission vest in the Chief Information Commissioner, who is assisted by the Information Commissioners and may exercise all powers and do all acts the Commission itself may exercise or do. In practice this makes the Chief the administrative head of the institution as well as its senior adjudicator: the registry, the allocation of cases among commissioners, the constitution of benches for matters the Chief considers fit for more than one member, and the institution's dealings with the government that funds it all run through the Chief's office. Cases are heard overwhelmingly by individual commissioners sitting singly, each carrying a personal docket of appeals and complaints allocated by the registry under the Chief's direction, with full benches reserved for questions of general importance, such as the 2013 decision on political parties discussed later in this article. The single-member docket is the Commission's normal working form, and it explains both its disposal capacity and its vulnerability: when a commissioner's post falls vacant, that commissioner's entire docket stops with it, and the cases wait for a successor or for redistribution to colleagues whose own dockets are already full.

### Who was the first Chief Information Commissioner of India?

Wajahat Habibullah, a former officer of the Indian Administrative Service, was appointed the first Chief Information Commissioner and led it from 26 October 2005 until September 2010. The Commission had been constituted that month, on the day the Right to Information Act came into force, and Habibullah set up its registry, its hearing practice and its first body of precedent.

The first Commission began work with a small registry, borrowed premises and an inflow of appeals that grew from a trickle to several hundred a month within its first year, a pattern the first Chief described publicly while pressing the government for staff and office space. That founding experience fixed a habit of mind that has persisted in the institution: the Commission understands itself as an adjudicating body that must also build its own administrative capacity while deciding cases, because the Act creates the office and the jurisdiction but leaves the machinery, the budget line and the staffing to be supplied by the Central Government under the Act's service provisions. The Central Government provides the officers and employees the Commission needs, on terms it prescribes. A body whose adjudicators are independent by design but whose registry, budget and buildings arrive through a ministry is independent in a qualified sense, and the qualification is structural, not personal. It recurs in the discussion of tenure below, because the 2019 amendment extended the same logic from the Commission's support system to the commissioners' own terms of service.

## The committee that chooses the commissioners

Appointment to the Commission is made by the President of India, but the effective choice belongs to the committee named in Section 12(3). The Chief Information Commissioner and the Information Commissioners are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee consisting of the Prime Minister, who is its chairperson; the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha; and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister. Two of the three members of that committee therefore belong to the executive whose departments are the Commission's principal respondents. The government of the day commands a majority of the recommending body by the design of the section itself, and the opposition member, however senior, can always be outvoted two to one. This is the first of the three structural constraints named at the head of this article, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not establish. It does not establish that individual commissioners decide cases to please the government; the decided record contains many orders against ministries, and commissioners have directed disclosure of material the government of the day would plainly have preferred to withhold. What the composition establishes is a risk at the point of selection rather than at the point of decision: a committee structured in this way can, if it wishes, prefer candidates unlikely to press hard, need never record a reason for preferring them, and cannot be outvoted by the one member whose political interest runs the other way.

The contrast with other Indian appointment designs is instructive. The selection committee for the Lokpal, whose composition raises the same question in a different balance and is examined in [the account of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013](/2011/11/15/lokpal-lokayuktas-act-2013-explained/), includes the Chief Justice of India or a judge of the Supreme Court and an eminent jurist alongside the political members. The committee for the Director of the Central Bureau of Investigation, after statutory amendment, includes the Chief Justice of India or a nominee judge. The committee for the Chief Election Commissioner, under the legislation enacted in 2023, reproduces the two-to-one executive pattern and was contested on precisely that ground. The Right to Information Act, drafted in 2005, contains no judicial member at all, and no requirement that the committee's deliberations, the applications received, or the criteria applied be made public. Whatever transparency the process has acquired since has been put there by the Supreme Court, in litigation described below, rather than by the section.

Two features of the statutory process deserve emphasis because they determine how the vacancy problem, examined later in this article, develops. The first is that the Act prescribes no timetable. Nothing in Section 12 requires the committee to meet before a vacancy arises, requires the government to advertise a vacancy, or fixes a period within which an appointment must be made. A post can lie empty for months without any provision of the Act being breached, because the Act is silent on the point. The second is that the Act prescribes no search process. It does not require applications to be invited, a search committee to be constituted, or a shortlist to be drawn from the seven fields named in Section 12(5). The committee recommends; the President appoints; the section asks nothing about how names arrive before the committee. Into that statutory silence the courts have inserted, by direction, most of the procedural content the process possesses, requiring governments to advertise vacancies, to publish the criteria and the particulars of applicants and of any search committee, and to begin the process in advance of an anticipated vacancy. Those directions bind as court orders do, but they sit on top of a section that does not contain them, and their enforcement depends on further litigation by citizens, which is why the appointment process returns to court so regularly.

The opposition member's presence is not worthless, and the strongest version of the design's defence should be stated. A committee that includes the Leader of the Opposition gives the opposition notice of who is being appointed, a voice in the room, and a record on which to found public criticism; appointments made over a recorded dissent carry a political cost, and dissent within the committee has been recorded in selections to the Commission and become public. The executive-majority pattern is also the pattern Parliament chose for several bodies in the same period, on the reasoning that appointments to statutory offices are an executive function in which the opposition is consulted rather than given a veto. The honest verdict on Section 12(3) is therefore narrower than the usual rhetoric. The defect is not that the Prime Minister chairs the committee; it is that the committee is the only safeguard the Act provides, and it is composed so that it cannot, by itself, block a determined executive. Every other safeguard, the published criteria, the advance timetable, the open field of candidates, had to be supplied from outside the section.

## Term, salary and the settlement of 2019

As enacted in 2005, Section 13 gave the commissioners a tenure of a kind familiar from the Election Commission's design. The Chief Information Commissioner held office for a term of five years from the date on which he entered upon his office, was not eligible for reappointment, and could not hold office after attaining the age of sixty-five years. An Information Commissioner held office for five years or until sixty-five, whichever was earlier, was not eligible for reappointment to that office, but could be appointed Chief Information Commissioner, subject to a ceiling of five years in aggregate across both offices. The salaries and allowances were fixed by the statute at parity: the Chief Information Commissioner's, with those of the Chief Election Commissioner, and an Information Commissioner's, with those of an Election Commissioner, with a deduction where the appointee already drew a pension from prior government service, and with an express bar on varying the conditions of service to a commissioner's disadvantage after appointment. Security of tenure in this scheme rested on three legs: a fixed term stated in the Act, a salary stated in the Act and benchmarked to a constitutional office, and removal only by the process in Section 14.

That removal process is the protective half of the design, and it survives. Under Section 14(1), the Chief Information Commissioner or an Information Commissioner can be removed from office only by order of the President on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, after the Supreme Court, on a reference made by the President, has inquired and reported that the commissioner ought on such ground to be removed. Section 14(3) adds a shorter list of grounds on which the President may remove a commissioner without that inquiry: adjudged insolvency, conviction of an offence which in the President's opinion involves moral turpitude, engaging during the term of office in paid employment outside the duties of the office, unfitness to continue by reason of infirmity of mind or body in the President's opinion, and the acquisition of a financial or other interest likely to affect the commissioner's functions prejudicially. Resignation is by writing addressed to the President under Section 13(4), and the oath of office is administered by the President or a person appointed by the President for that purpose under Section 13(3). A commissioner is therefore hard to remove for deciding cases in a way the government dislikes, and that protection is genuine. What a commissioner cannot be protected against by Section 14 is the expiry of a short term and the need, if service is to continue in any public office afterwards, to remain in the good opinion of the appointing executive.

The Right to Information (Amendment) Act, 2019, Act 24 of 2019, which received the President's assent on 1 August 2019 and was brought into force on 24 October 2019, dismantled the first two legs of the tenure stool and left the third standing. It amended Sections 13, 16 and 27 of the principal Act. In Section 13, the words fixing a term of five years were replaced, for both the Chief Information Commissioner and the Information Commissioners, by the words "for such term as may be prescribed by the Central Government", and the sub-section that had fixed salaries at Election Commission parity was replaced by a provision that the salaries, allowances and other terms and conditions of service shall be such as may be prescribed by the Central Government, retaining only the safeguard that they shall not be varied to a commissioner's disadvantage after appointment. The age ceiling of sixty-five years was left in place, as was the bar on reappointment and the aggregate ceiling for a commissioner elevated to Chief. The identical change was made to Section 16 for the State Commissions, so that the terms of state commissioners too are prescribed by the Central Government, a feature of the amendment that drew its own federal objection, since a state legislature's commission had its members' tenure set by the Union. The full legislative history and effect of that amendment are traced in [the account of the RTI Amendment Act 2019](/2011/11/01/rti-amendment-act-2019-explained/), including the parliamentary opposition it faced and the select committee that was demanded and not granted.

What the Central Government prescribed, by the Right to Information (Term of Office, Salaries, Allowances and Other Terms and Conditions of Service) Rules, 2019, notified with the amendment's commencement, was a term of three years from the date a commissioner enters upon office, a fixed pay of two lakh and fifty thousand rupees a month for the Chief Information Commissioner, and two lakh and twenty-five thousand rupees a month for Information Commissioners and for the chiefs and members of the State Commissions, with the pay of a commissioner who draws a pension reduced by the pension amount. The numbers matter less than the location of the power to set them. A term of three years prescribed by rule can be altered by rule, by the same executive whose departments appear before the Commission, without returning to Parliament; a term of five years stated in the Act could be altered only by another Act. The amendment therefore changed not merely the length of the tenure but the legal character of the promise made to the officeholder, from a statutory entitlement to an executive prescription held at the pleasure of subordinate legislation, protected only against mid-term disadvantage to an individual incumbent.

The case for the amendment, as the government made it, should be given its full strength. The Statement of Objects and Reasons reasoned that the Election Commission is a constitutional body established under Article 324, while the Information Commissions are statutory bodies, that the functions of the two are entirely different, and that the parity of status and salary between them was therefore an anomaly to be rationalised. It reasoned further that the terms of state commissioners varied in effect across states under the parity formula, because the state-level benchmarks differed, and that prescribing terms centrally would produce uniformity. These are real arguments about drafting coherence, and the anomaly point is not trivial: the 2005 Act borrowed the Election Commission's service template wholesale, as Indian statutes creating commissions habitually do, without asking whether the borrowing fitted. The case against the amendment, made by former commissioners, by the transparency movement that had campaigned for the Act, and by the opposition in Parliament, is that the coherence argument proves too much and addresses the wrong question. On this view, parity was not an accident of drafting but the mechanism by which a statutory body was given constitutional-style insulation, and replacing a statutory term with a prescribed one converts every commissioner into a person whose continuation in public life after a three-year term depends on executive goodwill, which is a standing incentive toward caution in exactly the cases where caution costs the citizen most. Wajahat Habibullah, the Commission's first Chief, was among the former commissioners who criticised the change publicly on that ground. Petitions challenging the amendment were filed in the Supreme Court; the amended scheme has not been displaced by any judgment, and the three-year prescribed term is the tenure on which commissioners hold office. Where the evidence supports a verdict, this article gives one: whatever the merits of ending the Election Commission parity, the transfer of the term itself from statute to rule was a reduction in the institution's independence, because it lowered the cost to the executive of shortening the tenure of a body that sits in judgment on the executive, and no countervailing safeguard was put in its place.

## Two doors into the Commission: complaint and second appeal

Everything the Commission decides arrives through one of two doors, and the difference between them is the single most useful practical distinction an applicant can learn, because the two routes have different triggers, different procedures and, after a judgment of the Supreme Court, significantly different remedies at the end of them. The applicant's own groundwork for both routes, the application, the first appeal and the timelines that precede them, is set out step by step in [the guide to filing an RTI application and pursuing its appeals](/2011/10/01/how-to-file-rti-application-india/); what follows is the institutional side of the same doors, what each one empowers the Commission to do. The account in this article states the general statutory position; it is not advice on an individual case, and the limitation periods in this Act are applied strictly, so a person carrying a live grievance needs advice on their own facts and dates.

The second appeal, under Section 19(3), is the ordinary door. It lies to the Commission against the decision of the first appellate authority, or against the failure that stands in place of a decision, within ninety days from the date on which the decision should have been made or was actually received, and the Commission may admit a late appeal if it is satisfied that the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause from filing in time. The appeal is a continuation of the information dispute itself: the Commission re-examines whether the information should have been given, and in doing so it works with two advantages the statute builds in for the appellant. Section 19(5) places the burden of proof squarely on the Public Information Officer who denied the request to show that the denial was justified, reversing the ordinary instinct that the person asking must make the case. Section 19(6) directs that an appeal be disposed of within thirty days, extendable for reasons recorded in writing to a maximum of forty-five days, a timetable whose relationship to the Commission's actual disposal times is examined when this article reaches the backlog. Section 19(4) protects the third party whose information is in issue, requiring that the third party be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard before an order is made about it. Section 19(7) then declares the decision of the Commission to be binding, and Section 19(8) equips it with the remedial powers described in the next sections of this article.

The complaint, under Section 18(1), is the other door, and it is not a shortcut version of the appeal. A complaint may be made to the Commission by a person who has been unable to submit a request because no Public Information Officer has been appointed or an Assistant Public Information Officer has refused to accept and forward the application; who has been refused access to information requested; who has received no response within the time limits the Act specifies; who has been required to pay a fee that in the complainant's view is unreasonable; who has been given information that is incomplete, misleading or false; or who complains in respect of any other matter relating to requesting or obtaining access to records under the Act. Where the Commission is satisfied that there are reasonable grounds to inquire, Section 18(2) empowers it to initiate an inquiry into the matter. The complaint route is therefore aimed at the conduct of the system: the missing officer, the obstruction, the unreasonable fee, the misleading reply. It can be filed without having run the two appeal tiers, which is what makes it attractive, and it is the route by which systemic failures reach the Commission at all.

In Chief Information Commissioner v. State of Manipur, decided on 12 December 2011 and reported at (2011) 15 SCC 1, the Supreme Court held that the two doors lead to different rooms. The case arose because a State Commission, acting on complaints, had directed the public authority to furnish the information sought. The Court upheld the High Court's view that a Commission entertaining a complaint under Section 18 has no jurisdiction to pass an order providing for access to the information: Sections 18 and 19 serve two different purposes, lay down two different procedures, and provide two different remedies, and the remedy for a person denied information is the appeal under Section 19, with its safeguards, including the burden of proof in Section 19(5), which the complaint procedure lacks. On a complaint, the Commission's coercive instruments are the inquiry itself and the penalty under Section 20 where the officer's conduct is not bona fide. The practical consequence is sharp and still catches applicants out. A complainant who wants the document must appeal; a complainant who wants the default examined and punished may complain; and a person who files only a complaint about a refusal may win the argument about the officer's conduct and still not receive the information, because the order that would release it belongs to the other procedure. The Commission's own practice has softened the edge of the distinction in case management, since registries and benches frequently treat the substance of a grievance pragmatically, but the jurisdictional line the Supreme Court drew remains the law, and it is the clearest illustration in the Act of remedy following route.

## The powers of inquiry

For the conduct of its inquiries, Section 18(3) clothes the Commission with the powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 in respect of specified matters: summoning and enforcing the attendance of persons and compelling them to give oral or written evidence on oath and to produce documents or things; requiring the discovery and inspection of documents; receiving evidence on affidavit; requisitioning any public record or copies of it from any court or office; issuing summons for the examination of witnesses or documents; and any other matter that may be prescribed. A public authority that ignores a hearing is therefore not merely being uncooperative; it is defying a summons backed by the procedural powers of a court, and officers do attend, file replies and produce files in very large numbers because the summons machinery works. The Commission also regulates its own procedure, and its hearings, conducted in person in Delhi for central matters and increasingly by video conference, follow the pattern of a tribunal: the appellant states the grievance, the officer justifies the denial under the burden Section 19(5) casts, and the commissioner decides on the record.

Section 18(4) adds a provision of unusual breadth, and it is the provision that most distinguishes the Commission's inquiry from an ordinary appellate review. Notwithstanding anything in the Act or any other law, the Commission may, during the inquiry of any complaint, examine any record to which the Act applies and which is under the control of the public authority, and no such record may be withheld from it on any ground. The authority that has refused the citizen a file on the ground of an exemption cannot refuse the Commission the same file on the same ground. The Commission can look at the disputed record in order to test the exemption claim against its contents, which converts exemption adjudication from an argument about descriptions into an inspection of the thing itself. In practice this power to call for and read the record is one of the Commission's most effective instruments, because a claimed exemption that survives correspondence frequently does not survive the commissioner's reading of the document.

### What can the Commission do about a public authority that never appoints a Public Information Officer?

The failure to designate an officer is a ground of complaint under Section 18(1), since a person unable to submit a request for want of an appointed officer may complain directly. In an appeal, Section 19(8) empowers the Commission to direct the authority to appoint one, and default exposes the officers to the penalty and disciplinary machinery of Section 20.

One boundary of the inquiry jurisdiction must be marked, because it answers a question applicants ask constantly. The Act gives the Commission no general power to act of its own motion. Its jurisdiction is structured around the complaint and the appeal: Section 18(2) speaks of the Commission initiating an inquiry where it is satisfied that there are reasonable grounds, but that satisfaction arises on a complaint received, and the section's scheme is reactive. The Commission has no equivalent of the National Human Rights Commission's express power to inquire on its own motion into a violation, and it undertakes no inspections, audits or investigations across public authorities at large. Letters and representations sent to it are in practice registered as complaints where they disclose a grievance within Section 18, which gives the reactive scheme some flexibility at the intake stage, but a commission that cannot begin a case by itself cannot patrol the system it caps; it can only decide, case by case, the failures that citizens bring to it and can afford to pursue. That boundary is the second half of the design choice the brief for this article identifies: Parliament made the Commission an adjudicator of grievances rather than a regulator of the information regime, and the consequences of that choice reappear when this article reaches proactive disclosure.

## What an order of the Commission can contain

Section 19(8) is the remedial heart of the Act, and its contents repay a functional reading. In its decision on an appeal, the Commission has the power to require the public authority to take any steps necessary to secure compliance with the Act. The section then specifies what that includes: providing access to information in a particular form if so requested; directing the public authority to appoint a Public Information Officer where none exists; publishing certain information or categories of information; making necessary changes to its practices in relation to the maintenance, management and destruction of records; enhancing the provision of training on the right to information for its officials; and providing the Commission with an annual report on compliance with the proactive disclosure obligation. Two further powers sit alongside the compliance directions. Under Section 19(8)(b), the Commission may require the public authority to compensate the complainant for any loss or other detriment suffered. Under Section 19(8)(c) it may impose any of the penalties provided under the Act, and under Section 19(8)(d) it may reject the application, which is the power to end the matter against the appellant where the denial was justified.

### Can the Commission direct a public authority to reorganise how it keeps its records?

Yes. Among the compliance steps Section 19(8) names expressly is requiring necessary changes to a public authority's practices for the maintenance, management and destruction of records, together with enhanced training of its officials on the right to information. The direction can therefore reach the system itself, not only the single disclosure.

The compensation power deserves separate notice because it is the least mechanical of the Commission's remedies and the one whose limits are least understood. Section 19(8)(b) is directed at loss or other detriment suffered by the complainant, and the Commission has used it where an applicant was put to demonstrable loss by a wrongful denial or by delay, for example where information needed for a time-bound legal or service claim arrived after the claim had been defeated by the delay itself. The power is discretionary, it requires the loss to be shown rather than presumed, and the sums awarded have been modest, because the provision is framed as compensation for detriment and not as damages at large. Its significance in the design is that it is the one remedy in the Act addressed to the citizen's loss rather than to the officer's conduct or the authority's compliance; everything else in Section 19(8) and Section 20 is about making the system behave. Even so, an award of compensation is an order like any other, and it travels to the same enforcement ceiling examined below: if the authority does not pay, the Commission's own toolkit for extracting payment is the penalty process against officers and the pressure of its orders, not execution.

Beyond the individual case, the Act gives the Commission one expressly recommendatory instrument of general application. Section 25(5) provides that if it appears to the Commission that the practices of a public authority do not conform with the provisions or spirit of the Act, it may give the authority a recommendation specifying the steps that ought in its opinion to be taken to promote conformity. The recommendation is not a direction, and no consequence is prescribed for ignoring it. This is the statutory home of the Commission's role in relation to Section 4, the proactive disclosure chapter that obliges every public authority to publish the particulars of its organisation, functions, decision-making norms, budgets, and the rest of the seventeen heads of disclosure the section enumerates, and to keep its records catalogued, indexed and progressively computerised. Section 4 was designed to shrink the need for applications by making the ordinary facts of administration available without asking, and the Commission's instruments for enforcing it are case-specific directions under Section 19(8) in appeals that happen to raise record practices, and recommendations under Section 25(5) that an authority is free to leave unimplemented. There is no power to audit a department's Section 4 compliance on the Commission's own initiative, no power to certify or grade it, and no consequence for a department that publishes nothing. The single largest obligation in the Act, measured by the volume of information it was meant to move, is the one with the weakest enforcement design, and the Commission's profile cannot be understood without that asymmetry, because the flood of individual applications that produces the backlog is in substantial part the measure of Section 4's failure.

## The penalty and the officer who pays it

Section 20 contains the Act's only coercive sanction, and its design is the key to the claim this article was commissioned to test. Where the Commission, at the time of deciding any complaint or appeal, is of the opinion that the Central Public Information Officer has, without any reasonable cause, refused to receive an application for information, or has not furnished information within the time specified in Section 7, or has mala fidely denied a request, or has knowingly given incorrect, incomplete or misleading information, or has destroyed information that was the subject of a request, or has obstructed in any manner the furnishing of information, it shall impose a penalty of two hundred and fifty rupees for each day till the application is received or the information is furnished, provided that the total amount of the penalty does not exceed twenty-five thousand rupees. Before the penalty is imposed, the officer must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and the burden of proving that the officer acted reasonably and diligently lies on the officer. Section 20(2) adds the second sanction: where the Commission finds that the officer has, without reasonable cause and persistently, committed any of these defaults, it shall recommend disciplinary action against the officer under the service rules applicable to that officer.

The penalty is personal. It is imposed on the Public Information Officer, not on the public authority; it is recovered from the officer, typically through the salary, and the authority cannot lawfully absorb it as a departmental expense without defeating the provision's purpose. The ceiling of twenty-five thousand rupees has stood unchanged since 2005, and the Commission cannot exceed it however long the default or however deliberate the obstruction. The penalty proceeding is also separable from the disclosure itself: the Commission issues a show cause notice to the officer, hears the explanation, and decides the penalty question in the same order or in a later one, which is why appellants sometimes receive their information and a penalty order together, and sometimes receive the information with a penalty notice still to be answered. The design is deliberate and defensible. Parliament judged that the failure to disclose is usually the failure of an identifiable desk, and that a daily-accruing personal fine would concentrate that desk's attention as no departmental reprimand could. In the Act's early years the penalty jurisdiction was widely credited with changing the behaviour of officers who had treated information requests as optional correspondence; the fear of a personal penalty, recorded in a reasoned order, is a real feature of the administrative world the Act created.

The structural criticism is equally specific, and it is the claim at the centre of this article: the penalty is personal but the failure is institutional. The officer designated as Public Information Officer is commonly a middle-ranking or junior official nominated to hold the designation alongside other duties. The decision to refuse a sensitive request, to claim an exemption expansively, or to let a file travel between sections until the time limit expires is rarely that officer's decision in any real sense; it is taken at the level where the consequences of disclosure are weighed, which is the level of the officer's superiors, and the designated officer's role is to sign the reply the file carries. The Act's only sanction therefore lands, by design, on the one participant in the default who usually did not make the substantive decision, while the institution whose instincts produced the refusal pays nothing and the senior officers who directed it are outside the penalty's reach altogether. The disciplinary recommendation under Section 20(2) is addressed to the same designated officer. Nothing in Section 20 authorises the Commission to penalise the public authority as an institution, to fine the first appellate authority for rubber-stamping a refusal, or to reach the superior officer who instructed the denial. Commissioners have complained in their own orders about exactly this misalignment, directing penalty at the officer of record while recording that the delay was caused by the office through which the file moved, and the complainant's compensation, where it is awarded at all, comes from the authority under Section 19(8)(b) rather than from the penalty, which is a fine and not a fund.

The record on imposition should be stated as carefully as the record on delay. Successive report cards on the performance of information commissions, prepared by the Satark Nagrik Sangathan and fellow researchers, have analysed disposals and recorded that penalties follow in only a small fraction of the cases in which delay or default is established on the face of the order, because commissioners frequently accept explanations of reasonable cause, drop show cause proceedings once information is finally supplied, or treat the disclosure itself as closing the matter. The gap between the defaults visible in the orders and the penalties actually imposed is therefore not only a feature of the section's reach but of its exercise. The strongest defence of that leniency is also institutional: a penalty regime applied at full rigour to officers who inherit broken record systems and answer for decisions they did not take would be unjust at the level of the individual case, and commissioners deciding individual cases respond to that. The result is a sanction that is feared in the abstract, sparingly imposed in practice, capped at a figure fixed in 2005, and aimed at the desk rather than the institution. It is the sharpest instrument the Commission has, and the ceiling on its effectiveness is built into its targeting.

## The ceiling: orders the Commission cannot execute

Section 19(7) declares the decision of the Commission to be binding. What the Act does not provide is any machinery by which a binding decision is executed when the public authority declines to comply. The Commission has no power of contempt of its own orders, no power to attach property or salary, no execution jurisdiction of the kind a civil court exercises over its decrees, and no prosecution of its own for disobedience; its recourse, when an order is ignored, is to issue notices, to hold further hearings, to impose or pursue penalty against the officer, and to record the non-compliance. Section 23 bars the courts from entertaining suits or proceedings in respect of orders made under the Act, but that bar has never excluded the constitutional jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court under Articles 226 and 32, and it is through writ petitions that Commission orders are in practice both challenged by aggrieved authorities and applicants and, where an authority simply sits on an order, enforced by judicial direction. The ultimate enforcer of the Commission's binding decision is therefore a court the applicant must separately approach, with the cost and delay that approach entails, and the Act confers no power of review on the Commission either, so an error in its own order is likewise corrected, if at all, by a writ court rather than by the Commission itself.

### What happens if a public authority simply ignores an order of the Commission?

The authority faces further notices, penalty proceedings against its Public Information Officer under Section 20 and a recorded finding of non-compliance, but the Commission cannot punish the disobedience as contempt or execute the order itself. An appellant left with an unimplemented order must in the end invoke the writ jurisdiction of the High Court to compel compliance.

The fullest demonstration of this ceiling is the political parties case. In June 2013 a full bench of the Central Information Commission, deciding complaints brought by the Association for Democratic Reforms and by Subhash Chandra Agrawal, held that six national political parties, the Indian National Congress, the Bharatiya Janata Party, the Communist Party of India (Marxist), the Communist Party of India, the Nationalist Congress Party and the Bahujan Samaj Party, were public authorities within Section 2(h) of the Act, on the ground of the substantial financing they received indirectly from the government, including land and accommodation at concessional rates, exemption from income tax, and free airtime on state media. The bench directed the parties to designate Public Information Officers and first appellate authorities and to comply with the Act's obligations. No party complied. Notices went unanswered and hearings unattended, a Bill to amend the Act so as to take political parties outside it was introduced in Parliament and lapsed without enactment, and in March 2015 the Commission recorded the continuing non-compliance and its own inability to proceed further in the matter, leaving the question to the government and to the courts, where the Association for Democratic Reforms filed its own petition. The episode is the cleanest evidence available for the third structural constraint, because every element of the Commission's strength was present and insufficient: a reasoned order of a full bench, on a question squarely within the Act, against respondents of the highest public prominence, and the institution's toolkit ended at recording that it could do no more. A body that cannot proceed against the most visible defaulters in its jurisdiction teaches every lesser authority the practical value of its orders, and that lesson, once taught, is part of the explanation for the routine non-compliance that appellants report in smaller cases.

The ceiling should not be overstated, and the next section returns to the counter-evidence. Most orders are complied with, because most public authorities treat a Commission order as an instruction from a statutory appellate body and because the officer of record has a personal interest in closing the matter. The ceiling binds in the cases where an authority calculates that the political or institutional cost of disclosure exceeds the cost of defiance, and those cases are disproportionately the ones the Act was passed for. The design question is therefore not whether the Commission needs the full apparatus of a court, but whether a binding appellate order in an information statute should depend entirely on the respondent's willingness and on a writ petition the citizen must fund. Parliament in 2005 answered that question one way. The record since is the working out of that answer.

## The state tier and the shape of the whole system

The Central Commission is the apex of only the central half of the system. Section 15 requires every state government to constitute a State Information Commission, and Section 16 mirrors the central design at the state level: a State Chief Information Commissioner and up to ten State Information Commissioners, appointed by the Governor on the recommendation of a committee consisting of the Chief Minister as chairperson, the Leader of the Opposition in the Legislative Assembly, and a Cabinet Minister nominated by the Chief Minister, the same two-to-one executive composition. Eligibility mirrors Section 12(5), disqualifications mirror Section 12(6), and the removal protection mirrors Section 14, with the Governor and the state High Court in the roles the President and the Supreme Court occupy centrally. The design was a deliberate choice to run one institutional pattern at both levels of the federation, so that an applicant's appellate experience would be governed by the same rules whether the respondent was a ministry in Delhi or a district office in a state capital.

Two consequences of that mirroring are worth the reader's attention. The first is that every structural feature identified in the central institution reproduces itself twenty-nine times over, in commissions of very different sizes, capacities and political environments: the executive-majority committee, the dependence on the government of the day to fill posts, and the enforcement ceiling are not features of the central body but of the model, and the countrywide figures for backlog and vacancy that follow are figures about the model. The second is the change worked by the amendment of 2019 at the state level. Since the amendment of Section 16, the term of office and the salaries and service conditions of state commissioners are prescribed by the Central Government, the same prescription that fixes the central commissioners' three-year term and pay, so that a state government appoints its commissioners through its own committee but on tenure terms set in Delhi. Critics of the amendment treated that feature as the federal objection in its purest form, since the state commissions were created by state notifications under a central Act and their members' independence depends on central prescription in two directions at once. The practical working of the model also varies with state capacity in ways the central body cannot correct: the Central Commission has no supervisory jurisdiction over State Commissions, cannot transfer their cases, cannot fill their vacancies, and cannot hear an appeal that belongs to them, so the system is a federation of parallel tribunals united by a statute and a name, and the citizen's remedy depends entirely on which side of the Section 12 and Section 15 line the respondent authority stands.

The union territories complete the map of the central jurisdiction. Public authorities under the control of the administrator of a union territory answer to the Central Information Commission, and the reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir in 2019 gave that position concrete effect: the former state commission of Jammu and Kashmir ceased to exist when the state was reorganised, and appeals and complaints relating to the public authorities of the successor union territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh lie to the Central Commission. The change is a tidy illustration of the model working as designed, since the line between the two tiers follows the constitutional line between the Centre and the states, and an authority's appellate forum moves with its constitutional ownership rather than with its geography.

## Vacancies, backlog and the arithmetic of waiting

If the enforcement ceiling is the constraint that binds at the end of a case, vacancy is the constraint that binds before the case begins. The Act permits a Commission of eleven members; it functions with however many the appointment process supplies, and the second appeal that Section 19(6) directs to be disposed of within thirty to forty-five days waits in a registry queue whose length is fixed by the ratio between the appeals filed and the commissioners sitting. The figures that follow are attributed as the brief for this article requires, because backlog numbers in this field circulate stripped of their sources and periods and acquire false precision in the retelling. The Report Card on the performance of information commissions prepared by the Satark Nagrik Sangathan and the Centre for Equity Studies in March 2018 recorded a backlog of more than 23,500 appeals and complaints before the Central Commission itself and waits exceeding a year in eight commissions. Writing in January 2020, Anjali Bhardwaj and Amrita Johri recorded the Central Commission's pendency at nearly 34,500 appeals and complaints on 15 January 2020, up from 27,364 on 1 January 2019, with five posts in the Commission, including that of the Chief, standing vacant. The Satark Nagrik Sangathan's Report Card for 2024 to 2025 recorded 4.13 lakh appeals and complaints pending across the twenty-nine information commissions on 30 June 2025, with Maharashtra carrying the largest backlog at more than 95,000, estimated disposal times for a fresh matter running to about twenty-nine years in Telangana and about twenty-three years in Tripura on the prevailing rate, the Central Commission's own estimated wait exceeding a year, six commissions having been left defunct for varying periods between July 2024 and October 2025 for want of appointments, and the Central Commission standing headless from 13 September 2025, the seventh occasion in eleven years on which the institution had been left without a Chief.

The litigation that made vacancy a legal question rather than an administrative complaint is Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India, Writ Petition (Civil) No. 436 of 2018, decided by a bench of Justices A. K. Sikri and S. Abdul Nazeer on 15 February 2019. The petition, brought against the background of the 2018 Report Card, challenged the failure of the Union and state governments to fill vacancies and the opacity of the process by which they were filled. The Court directed that vacancies be filled without delay, that the process of selection be initiated one to two months before an anticipated vacancy arises rather than after it, and that the process be made transparent: the advertisement inviting applications, the particulars of the applicants, the composition of any search committee, and the criteria adopted were all to be placed in the public domain, and appointments were to respect the breadth of Section 12(5), with the committee recording the field from which each recommended candidate came and the eminence on which the recommendation rested, in line with the directions the Court had issued earlier in Union of India v. Namit Sharma. In later proceedings in the same matter, in 2023, a bench led by the Chief Justice warned that leaving commissions unstaffed would defeat the object of the Act and render it a dead letter, directed the states to begin selection processes for vacant posts forthwith, and pressed the commissions toward hybrid hearings and electronic filing. The pattern across a decade is consistent: the statute prescribes no timetable, the executive's performance supplies the grievance, and the Court supplies the timetable by direction, in litigation that citizens must repeatedly bring because a direction in one round of appointments does not bind the conduct of the next.

The administrative counter-argument deserves to be put at its strongest, because it is the argument every government makes in reply and it contains a real point. Appeals and complaints are filed in very large numbers, hearings take the time they take, and no commission of eleven members, however diligent, could dispose of the inflow at the rate Indian citizens generate it; the true driver of delay, on this view, is the failure of the tiers below, the Public Information Officers who refuse or delay and the first appellate authorities who endorse them, together with the failure of Section 4, which was meant to make most applications unnecessary. The argument is substantially correct about causation and substantially beside the point about responsibility. A government that points to the inflow as the cause of the backlog is the same government that controls the three variables the Act leaves to it: how many of the eleven posts are filled, how promptly, and whether the departments under it comply proactively with Section 4. The backlog is best read as a ratio, and the executive holds the denominator.

A further distinction matters for the applicant's prospects. Pendency is not evenly spread. A commission with a full bench and a registry that lists cases in turn offers a wait; a commission left defunct, with no commissioner appointed at all, offers no forum, and appeals filed before it accumulate unheard until appointments are made, which is the position Jharkhand and Himachal Pradesh reached in the period the 2024 to 2025 Report Card records. For a right whose value decays with time, since information about a tender, a selection, an examination or an ongoing illegality is useful chiefly while the underlying matter can still be corrected, a wait measured in years is not a delayed remedy but a substantially diminished one. The shelf life of information is the honest core of the backlog criticism: an order directing disclosure, delivered promptly and enforced completely, can still fail the applicant if it arrives after the decision the information was meant to inform has become irreversible.

## Who gets appointed: the civil services question

Section 12(5) opens the Commission to seven fields. The appointments actually made, at the Centre and in the states, have been drawn heavily from one of them, administration and governance, and in practice from a single service within it: retired officers of the Indian Administrative Service have held a large share of the chief commissionerships and commissionerships, with the remainder distributed among retired officers of other services, former judges, journalists, academics and civil society figures. The criticism this pattern attracts is not of individuals, several of whom have built formidable disclosure jurisprudence, but of the funnel. A selection process that does not advertise widely, does not publish its criteria, and is controlled by a committee with an executive majority will predictably recruit from the pool the executive knows, which is the pool of officers who have just retired from the executive. The appearance problem compounds the structural one: the body meant to sit in judgment on the administrative machine is staffed substantially by the machine's own recent leadership, and appellants are asked to trust that the habits of a service career dissolve on appointment.

The defenders of the pattern answer on competence. The Commission's work is the review of administrative files, exemption claims grounded in the working of government, and the conduct of officers within service rules; a commissioner who has run a district, a department and a secretariat knows where records live, which claims of administrative difficulty are genuine and which are theatre, and how a direction will be received by the office that must obey it. Judicial members bring a different discipline, and the Commission's history includes judges who supplied it. In Union of India v. Namit Sharma, decided in 2013 and reported at (2013) 10 SCC 359, the Supreme Court first declared that information commissions perform quasi-judicial functions and should be headed by persons with a judicial background, with benches pairing a judicial member with an expert member, and then, on review, withdrew that requirement, restoring the statute's open field while directing that the appointing committee record the candidate's eminence and the field of experience relied on. The review judgment is the reason the committee's reasons, such as they are, exist at all. The balanced conclusion is that the statute's breadth is a deliberate flexibility rather than an oversight, and that the grievance is properly directed not at the presence of administrators on the Commission but at the opacity that prevents anyone from knowing whether the other six fields were ever seriously searched. Transparency of process, the advertisement, the published shortlist, the recorded criteria, is the reform that addresses the grievance without surrendering the flexibility, and it is the reform the courts have repeatedly ordered and the section still does not contain.

## The comparator: a commission built the other way

The design choices become clearest against the one Indian commission built on nearly opposite principles. The National Human Rights Commission, whose powers and recommendatory character are examined in [the guide to the National Human Rights Commission's powers and functions](/2010/09/15/nhrc-powers-functions-explained/), is chosen by a committee of six that includes the Prime Minister, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Home Minister, the Leaders of the Opposition in both Houses and the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, a breadth of composition the three-member information committee does not approach. The human rights body may inquire into a violation on its own motion, a power the information commissions do not possess. Yet its findings issue as recommendations, and the government it investigates may lawfully receive them and stop there. The Central Information Commission is the mirror image: a narrowly chosen body with no power to begin a case by itself, whose orders within its cases are declared binding and backed by a personal penalty. India has thus built its two great rights commissions with the strengths distributed inversely, one strong at intake and weak at outcome, the other strong at outcome on paper and weak at intake, appointment breadth and execution. Neither design is a drafting accident, and the comparison disciplines the criticism of both: the question for a legislature is never whether a commission is strong or weak, but at which joints it has been made strong, and whether those are the joints at which its work actually fails.

Inside its own building, the Commission's working is governed by the Chief's general superintendence under Section 12(4) and by the management regulations the Commission has framed for the allocation and disposal of its business, which distribute appeals and complaints among the commissioners, regulate listing and hearing, and provide for benches where the Chief directs. Hearings are open in character, cause lists are published, and orders are reasoned and placed on the Commission's record, so that the body of its precedent, on exemptions, on severability, on third party information and on the public interest override in Section 8(2), is available to applicants and officers alike and functions as the working law of the Act between the statute and the writ courts.

### How are cases assigned among the Information Commissioners?

Under Section 12(4), the general superintendence, direction and management of the Commission's affairs vest in the Chief Information Commissioner. The registry allocates appeals and complaints among the commissioners under that superintendence and the Commission's own management regulations, each commissioner ordinarily hearing a personal docket singly, with benches constituted by the Chief for larger questions.


## How a case moves through the Commission

The Commission's adjudicatory work has an anatomy that the statute sketches and its own practice fills in, and following a single appeal through the building explains several features of the institution that the sections alone do not convey. A second appeal arrives at the registry, by post, by hand or through the online filing facility the Commission maintains, and is first examined for the papers on which its admissibility depends: the application made to the Public Information Officer, the reply if any, the first appeal and the first appellate authority's order, since the Commission's jurisdiction under Section 19(3) is jurisdiction over the failure of the two tiers below it. A defective filing draws a deficiency communication, and the cure of defects is a stage at which inexperienced appellants lose months without knowing it, because the queue position the case will eventually occupy is earned only when the file is complete. Once registered, the appeal is allocated to the docket of an individual commissioner under the allocation system the Chief superintends, notice issues to the public authority calling for its comments and the record, and the matter is listed for hearing in its turn, which is the point at which the institution's vacancy arithmetic becomes the appellant's personal wait.

The hearing itself is deliberately informal by the standards of Indian adjudication. There is no court fee culture to speak of, no requirement of counsel, and no pleadings beyond the documents of the tiers below and the parties' written submissions; appellants appear in person, through representatives, or by video conference, and officers appear to justify the denial under the burden Section 19(5) places on them. The commissioner tests the exemption claimed against the record, which Section 18(4) entitles the Commission to call for and read, hears the third party where Section 19(4) requires it, and dictates or reserves an order that must engage with the provisions relied on, because a reasoned order is both the Commission's obligation and its protection on writ review. The order then enters the stage the Act says least about: compliance. The public authority is expected to implement the direction and report compliance, the appellant who receives nothing files a non-compliance communication, and the registry lists the matter again before the commissioner, who may issue further directions, initiate penalty proceedings against the officer, or record the default in terms that arm the appellant for a writ petition. Each of those stages exists in the Commission's practice because an institution that cannot execute its orders must build the follow-up that execution would have made unnecessary, and the labour of that follow-up is a real and rarely counted part of its workload.

The Commission's own self-knowledge comes from the annual report under Section 25, and the report's preparation is part of the same anatomy. Ministries and departments collect from the public authorities under them the statistics the section prescribes, the numbers of requests received, the refusals and the provisions under which they were made, the appeals and their results, and the disciplinary action taken, and the Commission compiles them into the report it furnishes to the Central Government for laying before Parliament. The exercise is imperfect, since the material depends on the authorities reporting on themselves, and researchers who use the report's tables say so. Yet it remains the only system-wide account of the Act's operation produced from inside the system, and its year-on-year tables of receipts, disposals and pendency at the Commission itself are the figures from which the vacancy debate draws its evidence. An institution that must report on a system it cannot supervise produces, in its report, the standing indictment and the standing defence of that system in the same volume.

## What the Commission actually decides: the exemption docket

Strip away the procedural questions and the substance of the Commission's docket is exemption adjudication. The great majority of second appeals turn on Section 8, which lists the categories of information exempt from disclosure, from the sovereignty and security of India and relations with a foreign state, through information forbidden by a court, parliamentary privilege, commercial confidence and trade secrets, fiduciary information, personal information and the rest, qualified at several points by harm tests and by the public interest. The Commission's task in case after case is to decide whether the harm the exemption guards against is real on the facts, whether the public interest in disclosure outweighs it where Section 8(2) permits disclosure notwithstanding the exemption, and whether the exempt portion can be severed under Section 10 so that the remainder of the record is released, a provision the Commission applies routinely and which converts many blanket refusals into partial disclosures. Alongside the exemptions sits Section 11, under which information relating to a third party that has been treated as confidential requires notice to that party before disclosure, and the Commission's hearings are the forum in which those third party objections, from public servants opposing disclosure of their service records to organisations opposing the release of their regulatory filings, are argued and decided.

This is the work in which the Commission has built the working law of the Act. Its orders on the disclosure of examination answer books and evaluation processes, on file notings and the deliberative record of government, on the assets and liabilities of public servants, on the records of regulatory inspections, and on the boundary between personal information and public activity constitute the precedential environment inside which every Public Information Officer in the central system decides applications, usually without any appeal being filed at all. Two disciplines visible in that body of precedent deserve emphasis in a profile of the institution. The first is the insistence that exemptions be justified on the record rather than recited: an officer who asserts that disclosure would impede an investigation or invade privacy must show the impediment or the invasion, because Section 19(5) places the burden on the officer and the Commission reads the disputed record under Section 18(4) to test the assertion. The second is proportionality in remedy: where a record mixes exempt and disclosable material, the order severs; where a claim fails on a technicality of the authority's own making, the order discloses; where the denial was right, Section 19(8)(d) ends the matter against the appellant with reasons the writ courts can examine. The Commission's critics and its defenders agree, from opposite directions, that this case law is the institution's most durable product, because it survives the turnover of members and the fluctuation of the backlog, and it is the principal reason the design question matters: the body writing this jurisprudence is appointed, tenured and staffed under the three constraints this article has described.

## The counter-reading: a record that is not failure

The strongest counter-reading of the Commission's record should be faced whole, because the structural argument of this article proves less than polemic usually claims. The Commission's orders are binding, and in the ordinary run of cases they are obeyed: information is supplied in very large volume through the second appeal process, officers attend, exemptions are tested against the actual record under Section 18(4), and disclosures that no department volunteered have been extracted by order, across subjects from examination answer books to file notings to the evaluation of public examinations. Penalties are imposed, show cause proceedings concentrate the attention of officers who ignored the tiers below, and compensation has been awarded where delay destroyed the value of a claim. The institution's precedent disciplines the entire system below it, because Public Information Officers decide applications in the shadow of what the Commission will do with a refusal, and that shadow effect, impossible to count, is plausibly the largest single contribution the body makes to disclosure in India. A profile that counted only the political parties default and the backlog figures would misdescribe an institution through which a very large quantity of information has in fact reached citizens who would not otherwise have obtained it.

The counter-reading answers the wrong charge, though, and the reply can be made precisely. The criticism this article has built is not that the Commission does nothing, or that its orders are worthless, or that its members lack commitment. It is that the institution's failures cluster exactly where its design is weak: it cannot fill its own benches, cannot secure the tenure of its own members from executive prescription, cannot reach the institutional level at which refusals are decided, cannot audit the proactive disclosure whose failure generates its caseload, and cannot execute an order against a respondent who calculates that defiance is cheaper than compliance. Delay and vacancy are the honest core of the case against the system's working, and delay in information is not a lesser species of justice delayed, because information has a shelf life and an order that arrives after the shelf life has expired is a remedy in form only. The Commission is best understood as a well-armed appellate tribunal placed at the top of a system whose intake, staffing and enforcement it does not control, and judged, fairly, as a success within its jurisdiction and a hostage outside it.


## Staff, budget and the administration of independence

Below the commissioners sits the machinery on which their independence silently depends, and the Act's provision for it is brief. The Central Government is required to provide the Chief Information Commissioner and the Information Commissioners with such officers and staff as may be necessary for the efficient performance of their functions, on salaries and conditions prescribed by the government. The Commission's registry, its legal and administrative wings, its hearing rooms and its information systems are therefore staffed through the executive the Commission oversees, substantially by officers on deputation from government service, and its budget is carried within the demands of the nodal ministry for the Act. No commissioner has suggested that a registrar or a section officer decides cases, and the point is not one of interference in adjudication. It is that the institution's capacity, how many benches can sit, how fast notices issue, whether a hearing room has functioning video facilities, whether the cause list and the orders reach the website, is an executive supply decision, renewed annually, and cannot be commanded by the body whose output it governs.

The headquarters provision tells the same story in miniature. Section 12(7) places the headquarters at Delhi and permits other offices elsewhere in India only with the previous approval of the Central Government. A commission for a country of India's distances might have been given regional benches by right, with appellants heard in their own regions; the Act instead makes the institution single-sited by default and treats expansion as an executive concession. The practical substitute has been the hearing method rather than the building: video conference hearings, pressed on the commissions by the Supreme Court in the Anjali Bhardwaj proceedings and expanded as a matter of the Commission's own procedure, allow an appellant in a distant state to be heard without travelling to Delhi, and electronic filing reduces the registry's dependence on post. These adaptations matter to access, and the Commission deserves credit for them. They also demonstrate the pattern this article keeps meeting: the institution works around its design where it can, and the workarounds, however energetic, remain revocable practices rather than statutory entitlements, dependent on the incumbent Chief's priorities and the ministry's supply of staff and equipment.

## The reform agenda, tested against the design

Because the diagnosis in this article is structural, the reform proposals that circulate around the Commission can be sorted by the joint they intend to repair, and the sorting is a useful test of which proposals are serious. On appointment, the proposals are to introduce a judicial member into the Section 12(3) committee on the pattern Parliament adopted for the Lokpal, to widen the committee beyond three, and to place the process itself, advertisement, applications, criteria, recorded reasons, into the Act rather than leaving it to court directions that lapse from one selection round to the next. On tenure, the proposal is the restoration of a term and conditions fixed by the Act, whether at the earlier Election Commission parity or at some expressly legislated figure, so that altering the tenure of the adjudicator once more requires the publicity and the majorities of a parliamentary amendment. On enforcement, the proposals range from a contempt power for wilful disobedience of orders, through a statutory execution mechanism on the pattern of tribunals whose orders are executable as decrees, to the modest and achievable step of a public compliance record in which every unimplemented order stands named against its authority in the annual report until it is implemented. On capacity, the proposals are to fill vacancies on the timetable the Supreme Court directed, to raise the statutory ceiling on commissioners if the inflow justifies it, and to attack the inflow itself by giving Section 4 a supervisory mechanism, whether in the Commission or elsewhere, with consequences for defaulting authorities. Each proposal has been urged in some form by former commissioners, by the transparency organisations that litigate the vacancy question, or in parliamentary debate on the amendment of 2019, and none of them is exotic by the standards of comparable Indian tribunals.

Two cautions from the institution's own history discipline the agenda. The first is that process reforms adopted only as practice decay: the advertisement and publication discipline the Supreme Court ordered in 2019 had to be re-litigated in later rounds because it lived in judgments rather than in the section, and the same fate would meet a compliance record or a selection code that a future government found inconvenient if it rested on nothing more durable. The second is that strength at one joint can be traded away at another: the amendment of 2019 was defended as rationalisation, and a reform package that restored tenure while surrendering the breadth of Section 12(5), or that added enforcement power while narrowing the complaint route, would reproduce the Act's habit of distributing strength inversely. The test for any proposed change is therefore the one this article has applied throughout: at which joint does the institution actually fail, and does the change put strength at that joint in the statute itself. Measured by that test, the unfinished business of the Right to Information Act is not the Commission's adjudicatory equipment, which is strong, but the three provisions in which its dependence on the executive is written, and the reform literature at its best is simply the argument about those three provisions conducted by other means.



Sequencing matters as much as selection among these proposals. Filling vacancies on a directed timetable, publishing selection criteria and applications, and maintaining a public compliance record require no legislation and can begin under the law as it stands; restoring a statutory tenure requires an amendment bill; conferring enforcement power requires an amendment bill of a more contested kind. A reform effort that spends its energy in that order, administrative discipline first and statutory reconstruction behind it, would relieve the constraint that costs applicants the most time while building the public record on which the harder amendments can be argued, and it would leave the institution stronger at every stage even if the later stages stall.

## The Chief's office: adjudicator and administrator in one

One office inside the Commission concentrates more of the institution's character than any other, and it deserves a section of its own. Section 12(4) vests the general superintendence, direction and management of the Commission's affairs in the Chief Information Commissioner, which makes the Chief simultaneously the institution's senior adjudicator and its administrative head. As adjudicator, the Chief carries a personal docket like every other commissioner and presides when benches are constituted for questions of general importance. As administrator, the Chief controls the allocation of cases among the commissioners, the Commission's dealings with the government on staff, budget and accommodation, and the institution's public posture, including the annual report. The combination has real advantages: the person who answers for the institution's output to Parliament and the public is the person who controls its internal distribution of work, so accountability and authority meet in one office. It also has a structural weakness that vacancy exposes. The Act contains no provision for an acting Chief Information Commissioner. When the Chief's post falls vacant, the superintendence the section vests in that office must be exercised by seniority and practice rather than by statutory text, benches for the largest questions await a Chief to constitute them, and the institution loses its single voice in its dealings with the executive at exactly the moment when the vacancy itself is the subject in dispute. That the Central Commission has stood headless on seven occasions in eleven years, on the count recorded in the 2024 to 2025 Report Card, therefore signifies more than one empty chair among eleven. It signifies the periodic suspension of the only office in the building charged with running the building, and it is a consequence the Act's silence, rather than any actor's decision, makes possible.

## Testing the claims: how to read the Commission's record

A profile of an institution earns its keep by leaving the reader able to test the claims made about it, including the claims in the profile itself, so this section states the working method the evidence above was assembled by, as a set of checks any reader can repeat. The first check concerns strength. The Act's ceiling is eleven members; the Commission's working strength at any moment is a separate fact, published in the institution's annual reporting and stated in the government's answers to parliamentary questions on the Act, and the two numbers should never be confused, because commentary that treats the sanctioned ceiling as the sitting strength flatters the institution's capacity by exactly the number of its vacancies. The second check concerns pendency. A backlog figure is meaningless without its source and its date, since the commissions' pendency changes monthly and the research organisations that track it, principally the Satark Nagrik Sangathan's periodic Report Cards, state their reference dates precisely; a figure repeated without either is a rumour wearing a number's clothing, and the reader who meets one should ask which commission, which month and whose count before drawing any conclusion from it.

The third check concerns enforcement, and it is best run on the institution's hardest file rather than its average one. The political parties matter traced in this article is the standing test: a full bench order of June 2013, non-compliance recorded by the Commission itself in March 2015, and no provision in the Act by which the Commission could proceed further. Any account claiming that the Commission's binding orders carry execution machinery must answer that file, and any account claiming the orders are simply ignored must answer the ordinary run of compliance in which information is supplied on direction every working day. The fourth check concerns the shelf life argument, which should be stated precisely or not at all: information sought under the Act is typically sought for a decision, a selection, a tender, a proceeding or a correction that remains open for a limited time, so a disclosure that arrives after that window has closed is worth a fraction of a disclosure within it, and the cost of delay is therefore not measured by the wait alone but by the proportion of cases in which the window has closed by the hearing date. A commission can be disposing of cases at a respectable rate and still failing this test, which is why disposal statistics and justice statistics are different things in this field. The reader who applies these four checks, strength against ceiling, pendency against source and date, enforcement against the hardest file, and delay against the shelf life of the information, holds the whole argument of this article in portable form, and can grade governments, commissions and commentators, this one included, against the record rather than against the rhetoric.

A fifth check concerns the penalty, since Section 20 is the figure most often quoted loosely about the Commission. The section's numbers are fixed and small: two hundred and fifty rupees for each day of default, twenty-five thousand rupees as the ceiling, and a disciplinary recommendation as the only further step. What varies, and what any serious assessment must measure from the commissions' own orders and the periodic Report Cards rather than from assertion, is the proportion of established defaults in which the penalty is actually imposed after the show cause proceeding runs its course. That proportion, far more than the statutory ceiling, is the working measure of the sanction's deterrent value, because officers calibrate their conduct to the penalty they expect and not to the penalty the section recites.

## The three structural constraints

The table below states the article's argument in its most compressed form. Each row names one structural feature, the provision in which it sits, what that feature prevents the institution from doing, the reform most commonly proposed for it, and whether that reform requires Parliament to amend the Act or can be achieved by administrative action within the law as it stands. The pattern in the last column is itself informative: of the three constraints, only one can be relieved without legislation, and that is the one the courts have been relieving, by direction, for a decade without lasting effect, which is evidence about where the durable remedy lies.

| Structural feature | Provision | What it prevents | Reform proposed | Requires amending the Act |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Executive majority on the appointment committee | Section 12(3) | An appointment process the executive cannot control, with judicial and opposition weight sufficient to block a compliant shortlist | Add a judicial member on the Lokpal pattern and publish the criteria, the applications and the committee's reasons as a matter of law | Amending the Act for the composition; the publication practice alone can be adopted administratively and has been directed by the courts |
| Tenure and service conditions prescribed by the Central Government | Section 13 as amended in 2019 | A term and salary fixed by statute, insulated from alteration by the executive the Commission sits in judgment on | Restore a fixed term and statutory conditions in the Act itself, whether or not the Election Commission parity is restored | Amending the Act; no administrative action can restore what the amendment removed |
| No machinery to execute its own orders | Sections 19(7) and 20, with no contempt or execution provision | Enforcement of a binding order against a public authority that declines to comply, without the citizen funding a writ petition | Confer a defined enforcement power, on the pattern of execution or contempt available to comparable tribunals, with safeguards | Amending the Act; administrative action can improve compliance tracking but cannot create a coercive power |

The closing verdict follows from the table. The Central Information Commission is neither the toothless body of its harshest critics nor the completed design its text promises. It is an institution whose adjudicatory equipment, civil court powers of inquiry, a binding decision, a personal penalty, compensation, and the right to read the disputed record for itself, is genuinely strong, and whose constituting equipment, the committee that appoints it, the tenure on which it serves, the vacancies it cannot fill and the orders it cannot execute, is weak at exactly the points where strength would have to come from the executive's willingness to be supervised. Parliament designed a commission that could overrule a ministry in a case and could not protect itself as an institution, and twenty years of record is that design working itself out. A reader, student or researcher assembling this institution's history into a coherent account, for a classroom, an examination or a seminar, will find that the three rows of the table organise nearly everything written about it, and a reader presenting that account can [review the finished slides in a browser-based presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) before the session. The Act gave the citizen a right and an appellate authority to vindicate it. What it withheld, deliberately or by omission, was the authority's independence of the power it exists to check, and until that withholding is repaired in the statute, the Commission's history will continue to be written in the distance between its orders and their execution.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What powers does the Central Information Commission have?

The Commission hears second appeals under Section 19 and complaints under Section 18 of the Right to Information Act. For inquiries it holds the powers of a civil court under Section 18(3), including summoning witnesses, requiring discovery of documents and requisitioning public records, and under Section 18(4) it may examine any record under a public authority's control, which no authority may withhold from it on any ground. Deciding an appeal, it can under Section 19(8) direct disclosure in a particular form, order the appointment of a Public Information Officer, direct changes to record management, award compensation, and impose penalties under Section 20. It has no contempt or execution power and no power to act of its own motion.

### Q: Who appoints the Chief Information Commissioner?

The Chief Information Commissioner is appointed by the President of India on the recommendation of a committee constituted under Section 12(3) of the Act. The committee consists of the Prime Minister, who chairs it, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister. The same committee recommends the Information Commissioners. The composition gives the executive two of the three seats, which is why the appointment process is criticised as executive controlled. Following directions of the Supreme Court in Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India, vacancies are to be advertised, the criteria and the particulars of applicants published, and the selection process begun before a vacancy actually arises.

### Q: Who is eligible to be appointed as an Information Commissioner?

Section 12(5) requires the Chief Information Commissioner and Information Commissioners to be persons of eminence in public life with wide knowledge and experience in law, science and technology, social service, management, journalism, mass media, or administration and governance. Section 12(6) disqualifies anyone who is a Member of Parliament or of a state legislature, holds any other office of profit, is connected with a political party, or carries on any business or profession. There is no entrance examination, age qualification or prescribed career route, and no requirement of a judicial background after the Supreme Court's review judgment in the Namit Sharma litigation restored the statute's open field.

### Q: How many Information Commissioners can be appointed to the Commission?

Under Section 12(2), the Central Information Commission consists of the Chief Information Commissioner and such number of Information Commissioners, not exceeding ten, as may be deemed necessary. The ceiling of ten commissioners plus the Chief is fixed by the Act, but the number actually in office at any time depends entirely on appointments being made, and the Act fixes no timetable for filling a vacancy. The gap between the sanctioned strength of eleven members and the working strength is the single most direct cause of the Commission's pendency, because each commissioner carries a personal docket of appeals and complaints, and a vacant post means a docket that is not being heard at all.

### Q: How is a State Information Commission appointed?

Section 15 requires every state government to constitute a State Information Commission, and Section 16 mirrors the central pattern. The State Chief Information Commissioner and the State Information Commissioners are appointed by the Governor on the recommendation of a committee consisting of the Chief Minister, who chairs it, the Leader of the Opposition in the Legislative Assembly, and a Cabinet Minister nominated by the Chief Minister. Eligibility and disqualifications follow the central model of persons of eminence in the named fields, barred from legislative membership, offices of profit, political party connection, business and professional practice. Since the amendment of 2019, the term and service conditions of state commissioners are prescribed by the Central Government.

### Q: How can an Information Commissioner be removed from office?

Section 14 protects the tenure. The Chief Information Commissioner or an Information Commissioner can be removed by order of the President only on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, after the Supreme Court has inquired into the matter on a reference from the President and reported that removal is warranted. The President may remove a commissioner without that inquiry on specified grounds, namely adjudged insolvency, conviction of an offence involving moral turpitude in the President's opinion, engaging in paid employment outside the duties of the office during the term, unfitness through infirmity of mind or body, or acquiring a financial or other interest likely to affect the functions prejudicially. Resignation is by writing to the President.

### Q: Can an Information Commission act on its own without a complaint?

The Act confers no general power to act of its own motion. The Commission's jurisdiction is structured around the complaint under Section 18 and the second appeal under Section 19, and Section 18(2) permits it to initiate an inquiry where it is satisfied that reasonable grounds exist, which arises on a complaint received rather than on the Commission's own surveillance of public authorities. Letters and representations are in practice registered as complaints where they disclose a grievance within Section 18, which gives the intake some flexibility. Unlike the National Human Rights Commission, which may inquire into a violation on its own motion, the Information Commission cannot begin a case by itself or audit a department uninvited.

### Q: What is the difference between a complaint under Section 18 and a second appeal under Section 19?

A complaint under Section 18 attacks the conduct of the system: no Public Information Officer appointed, access refused, no response within the time limit, an unreasonable fee demanded, or false and misleading information given. A second appeal under Section 19 continues the information dispute itself after the first appellate authority has decided or failed to decide. In Chief Information Commissioner v. State of Manipur the Supreme Court held that the two routes serve different purposes and give different remedies: a Commission hearing a complaint cannot direct that the information be supplied, because the disclosure remedy belongs to the appeal, which also carries the safeguard of Section 19(5) placing the burden of proof on the officer who denied the request.

### Q: Are Information Commission orders binding on a public authority?

Yes, as a matter of law. Section 19(7) declares that the decision of the Commission shall be binding, and the Act provides no appeal against it. The qualification is enforcement rather than validity. The Commission has no contempt power, no execution machinery and no prosecution of its own for disobedience, so a public authority that declines to comply faces notices, penalty proceedings against its Public Information Officer under Section 20, and a recorded finding of non-compliance, but the order is ultimately enforced, where it is enforced, through the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts. Bindingness in the statute and enforceability in practice are therefore two different questions, and much of the criticism of the Commission concerns the second.

### Q: Can a public authority refuse to show its records to the Commission during an inquiry?

No. Section 18(4) provides that, notwithstanding anything in the Act or any other law for the time being in force, the Commission may during the inquiry of any complaint examine any record to which the Act applies and which is under the control of the public authority, and no such record may be withheld from it on any ground. An authority that has refused the applicant a file by claiming an exemption under Section 8 cannot refuse the Commission the same file for testing that claim. This inspection power is one of the Commission's most effective instruments, because exemption claims are decided against the actual contents of the disputed record rather than against the authority's description of them.

### Q: Can the Central Information Commission award compensation to an applicant?

Yes. Section 19(8)(b) empowers the Commission, in its decision on an appeal, to require the public authority to compensate the complainant for any loss or other detriment suffered. The power is discretionary and is exercised where a wrongful denial or a delay has caused demonstrable loss, for example where information needed for a time bound claim reached the applicant only after the claim itself had failed for want of it. The loss must be shown, the amounts awarded have been modest, and the provision operates as compensation for detriment rather than damages at large. The compensation is payable by the public authority and is distinct from the penalty under Section 20, which is a personal fine on the officer.

### Q: Can the Commission enforce proactive disclosure under Section 4 of the RTI Act?

Only indirectly. Section 4 obliges every public authority to publish extensive particulars about itself and to maintain its records catalogued, indexed and progressively computerised, but the Act gives the Commission no power to audit that compliance on its own initiative. Its instruments are case specific directions under Section 19(8) in an appeal that raises record practices, including directions to publish information and to change record management, and recommendations under Section 25(5) where an authority's practices do not conform with the provisions or spirit of the Act, which carry no prescribed consequence if ignored. This is why Section 4, the chapter meant to make most applications unnecessary, remains the least enforceable part of the design.

### Q: Can an Information Commission order be challenged in court?

There is no statutory appeal from a Commission order. Section 23 bars any court from entertaining a suit, application or other proceeding in respect of an order made under the Act, and the Commission itself has no power of review. The orders are nevertheless routinely challenged, and enforced, through the constitutional writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226 and of the Supreme Court under Article 32, which the bar in Section 23 does not exclude. Both sides use the route: a public authority aggrieved by a disclosure direction may petition against it, and an applicant faced with an order the authority will not implement may petition for compliance. The writ court is therefore the Commission's court of appeal in practice.

### Q: Does the Central Information Commission have contempt powers?

No. The Act gives the Commission the powers of a civil court for the conduct of its inquiries under Section 18(3), which include summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses and requisitioning records, but it does not give the Commission power to punish disobedience of its orders as contempt, nor any machinery to execute a decision as a court executes a decree. Non-compliance is met with further notices, penalty proceedings against the Public Information Officer under Section 20, and recorded findings, and its ultimate enforcement is the writ jurisdiction of the High Court. The absence of a contempt power is one of the three structural constraints on the institution, visible most starkly when a respondent of prominence simply declines to comply.

### Q: Can the Central Information Commission review its own order?

No. The Right to Information Act confers no power of review on the Commission, and the Commission, as a creature of the statute, cannot assume one by implication where the Act has not granted it. An order alleged to contain an error, or to have been passed without hearing an affected party, is corrected, if at all, by the High Court or the Supreme Court in writ proceedings under Articles 226 and 32, since Section 23 bars other proceedings in respect of orders under the Act while leaving the constitutional jurisdiction untouched. The absence of even a review power means the Commission cannot repair its own mistakes, a deliberate legislative choice that trades finality and speed for dependence on the writ courts.

### Q: Does the Central Information Commission hear appeals against state government bodies?

No. The two tiers of commissions have separate jurisdictions. The Central Information Commission hears second appeals and complaints concerning public authorities of the Central Government and the union territories, while appeals concerning state government departments, state public sector bodies and other state public authorities lie to the State Information Commission of that state under Sections 15 and 16. The Central Commission has no supervisory authority over State Commissions, cannot transfer their cases and cannot entertain an appeal that belongs to them. A misdirected appeal gains the appellant nothing except lost time, so identifying whether the respondent authority belongs to the Centre or to a state is the first question to settle before a second appeal is filed.

### Q: Why are Information Commissions so backlogged?

The backlog is a ratio between inflow and sitting strength, and both sides of it are structural. Appeals and complaints arrive in very large numbers because Public Information Officers refuse or delay and first appellate authorities routinely uphold them, and because the proactive disclosure required by Section 4, which was meant to make most applications unnecessary, has never been enforced. On the other side, the Act caps the Commission at eleven members, fixes no timetable for appointments, and leaves posts vacant for long periods, so dockets shrink while filings do not. The Satark Nagrik Sangathan's Report Card for 2024 to 2025 recorded 4.13 lakh appeals and complaints pending across the twenty-nine commissions on 30 June 2025.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court say about vacancies in Information Commissions?

In Anjali Bhardwaj v. Union of India, decided on 15 February 2019, the Supreme Court directed that vacancies in the information commissions be filled without delay and that the selection process be initiated one to two months before a vacancy arises. It required the process to be transparent: advertisements inviting applications, the particulars of applicants, the composition of any search committee and the criteria adopted were to be made public, and the appointing committee was to record each recommended candidate's eminence and field of experience, as the Court had directed earlier in Namit Sharma. In later proceedings in 2023 the Court warned that commissions left unstaffed would make the Act a dead letter and directed states to begin selections forthwith.

### Q: Why do so many Information Commissioners come from the civil services?

Section 12(5) opens the Commission to seven fields, from law and journalism to social service and administration, but appointments at the Centre and in the states have been drawn heavily from retired officers of the Indian Administrative Service and other services. The funnel is the process: vacancies were for years filled without advertisement or published criteria, by a committee with an executive majority choosing from the pool it knows, which is the pool of officers who have just retired from the executive. Defenders of the pattern answer that commissioners who have run departments know where records live and which exemption claims are genuine. The criticism is therefore aimed at the opacity of the search rather than at any individual appointee's competence.

### Q: Does an appellant have to appear in person before the Central Information Commission?

Personal appearance in Delhi is not the only mode of hearing. The Commission regulates its own procedure, and appellants may present their case in person, through a representative, or by video conference, a mode of hearing whose expansion the Supreme Court pressed in the Anjali Bhardwaj proceedings, together with electronic filing. An appellant who does not appear still has the appeal decided on the record, since the burden of justifying a denial rests on the Public Information Officer under Section 19(5) rather than on the appellant. What matters is that the written appeal, the orders of the tiers below and the grounds urged are complete, because the Commission decides the justification for the refusal and not the persuasiveness of an oral performance.
