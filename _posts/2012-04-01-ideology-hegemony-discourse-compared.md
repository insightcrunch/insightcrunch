Ideology, hegemony, and discourse are the three words a reader of political theory is most likely to meet used as though they were interchangeable, and the habit costs more than elegance. A seminar essay announces that a newspaper exercises hegemony through its ideology, which a second essay redescribes as the newspaper's discourse, and both essays proceed as if nothing had changed between the sentences. Something has changed, and it is not a nuance of vocabulary. Each of the three concepts was built inside a different theory of how societies hold together, each presupposes a different answer to the question of what lies behind a representation, and each entitles its user to make a different kind of claim at the end of the analysis. To swap one for another mid argument is to change the theory while keeping the tone, rather as if a surveyor exchanged a theodolite for a barometer because both are instruments with dials. This article separates the three, reconstructs each from its strongest statement, and then compares them on a single stated criterion: what each concept commits its user to, and what each permits its user to say.

The comparison has a destination, and it can be stated at the outset. Ideology, in its Marxist senses, presupposes a real relation that a representation distorts or partially reveals, so it licenses the claim that a belief is false and that its falsity serves an interest. Hegemony, as Antonio Gramsci developed the term in prison, names leadership secured through consent rather than imposed by force, a leadership that involves genuine concessions and the active agreement of subordinate groups, and it licenses claims about how consent is produced, maintained, and lost. Discourse, in the sense derived from Michel Foucault, refuses the presupposition the first concept requires: there is no standpoint outside discourse from which a representation can be measured against an undistorted real, power and knowledge constitute one another, and the subject who might have seen clearly is itself an effect of the field it inhabits. A comparison that ends with three definitions side by side has done only half its work. The sharper half is the recognition that choosing among these instruments is a decision about what kind of sentence the analyst wants to be entitled to write afterwards, and that some combinations of the three are not layers of one analysis but contradictions wearing a shared vocabulary.

## Three words treated as one

The synonym habit has identifiable sources, and naming them explains why the confusion is so persistent. The first source is the textbook glossary, which defines each term in a single line and thereby flattens the theories that give the terms their force. Ideology becomes a system of beliefs, hegemony becomes dominant ideas, discourse becomes language and ideas, and three one line definitions that all contain the word ideas will inevitably be read as three names for one thing. The second source is the genuine overlap of subject matter. All three concepts are used to talk about how meanings circulate in a society, how some meanings come to seem natural, and how that naturalness is connected to power. Shared territory is real, and it tempts writers to treat the concepts as rival labels for the territory rather than as rival maps of it, drawn to different projections and incapable of being overlaid without distortion. The third source is disciplinary drift. Cultural studies, media studies, and political theory each borrowed all three terms during the decades when Gramsci and Foucault were being absorbed into English language scholarship, and each discipline bent the borrowed words toward its own questions, so that a single term can now carry a Gramscian sense in one journal and a loose journalistic sense in the newspaper on the same morning.

Against the habit, this article sets a discipline of its own. Each concept will be stated first in the terms its originator and its serious defenders would recognise, with its presuppositions made explicit rather than left as atmosphere. Only after the three reconstructions are complete will the comparison be drawn, and the comparison will be conducted through a single device, introduced here and used throughout. The device is the entitlement test. Before adopting any of the three concepts, the analyst asks what sentence the analysis needs to be able to conclude with. If the concluding sentence must say that a representation distorts a reality that can be independently described, the instrument required is ideology, with all its presuppositions accepted. If the concluding sentence must say that consent to an order was organised, was paid for with concessions, and can be withdrawn, the instrument is hegemony. If the concluding sentence must describe how a field of statements made certain things sayable, thinkable, and actionable while excluding others, without pronouncing the field false, the instrument is discourse. Concepts chosen this way stop being decorations. They become commitments, and commitments can be examined, defended, and, where they fail, surrendered.

### What is the difference between ideology and hegemony?

Ideology concerns representations that distort or partially express real relations. Hegemony concerns leadership secured through consent, involving genuine concessions and the active agreement of subordinate groups. Ideology asks whether a belief is true and whose interest it serves; hegemony asks how consent is produced and maintained.

That short answer is the article in miniature, and its two sentences already contain the decision the rest of the comparison elaborates. Notice what the ideology sentence assumes: that there are real relations, that representations stand in a measurable relation to them, and that the measurement can come out negative, as distortion, or positive, as partial expression. Notice what the hegemony sentence assumes instead: that subordinate groups are not containers into which beliefs are poured but parties to an arrangement, that their agreement is active rather than merely absent resistance, and that the arrangement has a price, paid in concessions that cost the leading group something real. A theory of distortion and a theory of negotiated leadership are not two descriptions of one mechanism. They locate the action in different places, one in the relation between representation and reality, the other in the relation between groups. Keeping that difference in view is the precondition for everything that follows, including the third term, which disputes the ground on which both of the first two stand.

## Ideology before Marx: the career of a coinage

The word ideology began its life as the name of a science, and the irony of its later career is that it came to name the opposite of scientific clarity. It was coined in the 1790s by Antoine Destutt de Tracy, a French philosopher working in the aftermath of the Revolution, to designate a proposed science of ideas that would trace every idea back to the sensations from which it was composed and thereby put reasoning about morality and politics on a secure foundation. The ideologues, as Tracy and his associates were called, were reformers with institutional ambitions, members of the Institut de France, and for a brief period their science of ideas was a semi official philosophy of the revolutionary settlement. The turn came with Napoleon, who blamed the ideologues for the failures of his Russian campaign and for resistance to his rule more generally, and who used the word as a term of abuse for impractical metaphysicians whose abstractions obstructed the business of government. In Napoleon's mouth the word already carried the charge it would never quite lose: ideas detached from reality, spun by men who mistake their systems for the world.

Marx and Engels inherited the word in its Napoleonic sense and gave it a theoretical job. In The German Ideology, written in 1845 and 1846 but unpublished in their lifetimes, the word names the process by which the ideas of an epoch present themselves as independent of the material life that produced them. The ruling ideas of an age, they argued, are the ideas of its ruling class, not because rulers cynically invent doctrines in committee, but because the class that controls material production also controls the means of mental production, the institutions through which ideas are made, circulated, and certified. The famous image of that text is the camera obscura, in which men and their circumstances appear upside down, an inversion that arises from the historical life process just as the inversion of objects on the retina arises from the physical one. The image matters for the comparison because it fixes the concept's presupposition in place at the moment of its founding. An inverted image is an image of something. The camera obscura does not hallucinate; it transposes a real scene. Ideology, from its first Marxist use, is a distorted representation of a reality that exists independently of the distortion and against which the distortion can in principle be measured.

Two further senses accumulated around that founding one as the tradition developed, and a careful comparison has to keep them distinct because they carry different commitments. In one sense, ideology is a neutral term for the belief systems of classes and movements, the worldview through which any group, including a revolutionary one, understands its situation; in this sense a socialist ideology is not a contradiction but a description. In the other, sharper sense, ideology is specifically the distortion that serves domination, the set of representations through which an unequal order presents itself as natural, eternal, or universally beneficial. The two senses coexist in the literature, sometimes in the same author, and much confusion in the three way comparison comes from opponents attacking the sharp sense while defenders retreat to the neutral one. The fuller reconstruction of these senses, with the textual history that separates them, is given in this series in the [marxist theory of ideology guide](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/), and the present article draws on that reconstruction where precision about the first term is required.

What all the Marxist senses share, and what the comparison turns on, is the anchoring of representation in a reality that is not itself a representation. The anchor takes different forms. For the early work it is material life and the relations of production. For later formulations it is class interest, the objective position of a group within those relations, against which the group's self understanding can be checked and found adequate or wanting. Even Louis Althusser, who rebuilt the concept in the most radical way the Marxist tradition produced, kept the anchor in a transposed form, since his ideology represents the imaginary relationship of individuals to their real conditions of existence, a formula in which the real conditions remain obstinately real. The tradition disagrees about almost everything else: about whether distortion is deliberate or structural, about whether science stands outside ideology or is itself a practice with ideological effects, about how directly interests explain beliefs. It does not disagree that there is something for ideology to be wrong about. That shared presupposition is the first line of the commitment table this article will build, and it is exactly the presupposition the third concept refuses.

## Ideology in the Marxist tradition: representation tied to a real relation

To see what the concept of ideology actually does in a Marxist analysis, consider the operations it performs rather than the definitions it attracts. The first operation is unmasking. A representation that presents a historically specific arrangement as natural or universal is returned to the conditions that produced it: the wage form presents the sale of labour power as an exchange of equivalents, and the analysis shows the surplus extracted inside the formally equal exchange; the legal form presents citizens as abstract equals, and the analysis shows the unequal owners who meet beneath the abstraction. The second operation is explanation by interest. A belief's persistence, despite evidence against it, is explained by the work it does for the order that hosts it, so that the question shifts from whether believers are stupid or dishonest, which the serious tradition rarely claims, to what function the belief performs. The third operation is prognosis. If a representation distorts a real relation, then changes in the relation, above all crises that make the relation visible in experience, should crack the representation, and the tradition's political expectations have always leaned on that predicted cracking.

Each operation depends on the presupposition identified in the previous section, and each fails without it. Unmasking requires a reality behind the mask that the analyst can describe more accurately than the mask does. Explanation by interest requires that interests be identifiable independently of the beliefs they are summoned to explain, otherwise the reasoning circles: the belief is held because it serves the interest, and the interest is inferred from the belief. Prognosis requires that experience can correct representation, that the real can push back against its distorted image with enough force to break it. These dependencies are not defects in themselves. Every concept has presuppositions, and a concept whose presuppositions are explicit is more honest than one whose are hidden. They become the centre of the comparison because Foucault's objection to ideology is precisely an objection to these three dependencies, stated as reasons rather than as rhetoric, and because Gramsci's hegemony, developed inside the same tradition, quietly relocates the analysis so that less weight rests on them.

### Does ideology simply mean false ideas?

No. False ideas are part of the sharp sense of the term, but the concept in its developed Marxist uses also covers representations that are partly true, systematically selective, or accurate about everything except their own origins and limits, and it extends beyond ideas to practices, rituals, and institutions.

The correction matters because the crude equation of ideology with lying produces a crude politics and a cruder scholarship. If ideology were simply falsehood propagated by the powerful, then refutation would be a sufficient politics and fact checking a sufficient method, and the tradition's own history shows both failing. Beliefs that serve an order are frequently true in their particulars. A meritocratic creed can point to real cases of advancement through effort; its ideological work lies in what the cases are made to stand for, in the conversion of exceptions into a description of the whole, and in the silence about the starting positions from which effort is made. Selectivity, framing, and naturalisation do more ideological work than invention does, because they are harder to refute point by point. The mature concept is therefore a theory of the relation between representation and reality, of which outright falsity is only the limiting case, and any comparison that defines ideology as false ideas has defined a position none of its serious holders occupies.

## The depth model: what the concept of ideology presupposes

Philosophers of the tradition sometimes describe ideology as resting on a depth model, and the phrase is worth keeping because it organises the comparison economically. On a depth model, appearances are a surface and reality is a depth: the surface may conceal the depth, invert it, or reveal it in fragments, but the depth exists, has a structure, and can be known by methods that penetrate the surface. Marxism's depth is the ensemble of production relations and the interests they generate. Ideology critique is the disciplined movement from surface to depth and back, showing how a representation is produced by the depth it misrepresents. The model gives critique its distinctive confidence. The critic is not exchanging one opinion for another but correcting a representation against the thing represented, and the correction can claim the standing of knowledge rather than preference.

The depth model also generates the tradition's most persistent embarrassment, which the objection section of this article will state at full strength and which belongs here as part of an honest reconstruction. If consciousness is formed by social position, the critic's consciousness is formed too, and the standpoint from which distortion is identified needs its own account. Karl Mannheim pressed the point in the 1920s, generalising the concept of ideology until it applied to every standpoint including the critic's, and then struggled to exempt a free floating intelligentsia from the general determination. Marxist replies have taken several forms: that the standpoint of the class with no interest in concealment is epistemically privileged, that practice tests representations in ways that break the symmetry between standpoints, that the depth is discovered by a science whose procedures are checkable rather than by a standpoint whose purity is asserted. Each reply has able defenders and able critics, and the dispute has never been closed to general satisfaction. What matters for the comparison is that the dispute is internal to the depth model. It asks where the undistorting standpoint is located. Foucault's move, examined later, is to deny that the question has an answer because the model itself is the problem, and Gramsci's move, examined next, is to change the subject from standpoints to organisations, from who can see truly to who can lead durably.

There is a second presupposition worth isolating before leaving the first concept, because it will reappear in the commitment table as a column. Ideology in the Marxist senses treats the subject, the bearer of beliefs, as given in outline before the beliefs arrive. Classes exist as positions in a structure, individuals occupy the positions, and ideology is what they come to think, or fail to think, within them. The direction of explanation runs from position to consciousness, with all the complications the tradition adds about mediation, lag, and unevenness. Althusser reversed the direction for the individual subject, arguing that ideology constitutes subjects rather than merely addressing them, and that reversal is the hinge on which the whole three way comparison turns, since Foucault took the constitution of subjects as his starting point while refusing the structure that, for Althusser, did the constituting. Hold that hinge in mind: who or what comes first, the subject or the formation that speaks through it, is the second question on which the three concepts divide, and it divides them as sharply as the question of truth.

## Hegemony before Gramsci: a word for leadership

Hegemony is an old word with a plain meaning that its theoretical career should not be allowed to obscure. In Greek usage a hegemon was the leader of a league of cities, the state whose authority the others acknowledged, and the word carried the sense of leadership exercised over nominal equals rather than the dominion of a master over subjects. That residue of acknowledged leadership, of authority that runs through the consent of the led, survived in the word's later uses and is the thread Gramsci picked up. In the decades before his work, the term circulated in Russian social democracy, where it named the question of which class would lead the democratic revolution in a country whose bourgeoisie seemed too weak and too frightened to lead its own: the proletariat, in alliance with the peasantry, was to exercise hegemony over the coming revolution, leading forces larger than itself toward aims it did not solely own. Lenin used the vocabulary in that strategic sense, and the Communist International debated hegemony as a problem of alliances and leadership. The word, at the moment Gramsci took it up, therefore already joined leadership to alliance, and already implied that the leading element leads others who have their own interests and must be won rather than commanded.

The international relations usage, which students meet in other courses and which the brief for this article flags as a misreading to correct, is a separate branch. There hegemony names the preponderance of one state in an international system, its capacity to set rules and supply order, and the adjective hegemonic attaches to wars, stability, and decline. That usage descends from the Greek root by a different path and owes little to Gramsci, for whom the consent of subordinate groups inside a society, organised through its institutions and its culture, is the entire point. A power that rules only by preponderance is, in Gramsci's vocabulary, merely dominant. Hegemony begins where preponderance is supplemented, and eventually steadied, by leadership that the led experience as in some measure their own. Keeping the two usages apart is not pedantry. The international relations sense names a fact about capability, while the Gramscian sense names a process of organisation, and importing the first into the second dissolves the second's analytical content into a synonym for being on top.

## Hegemony in the Prison Notebooks: consent armoured by coercion

Gramsci wrote the Prison Notebooks between 1929 and 1935, in a fascist prison, under censorship, in thirty three notebooks whose fragments were published only after the war and whose organisation generations of editors and scholars have worked to reconstruct. The circumstances are not decoration. He was analysing, from a cell, the defeat of the revolutionary movements he had served and the durability of the order that defeated them, and the question that organises the notebooks is why the capitalist West, unlike Russia in 1917, did not crack when its crisis came. His answer turned on the structure of civil society. In the East, he wrote in a famous contrast, the state was everything and civil society was primordial and gelatinous; in the West, between the state and the individual stood a dense fabric of churches, schools, newspapers, associations, professions, and parties, a system of trenches and fortifications that absorbed shocks and had to be conquered position by position. Hegemony is the name for command of that fabric: the capacity of a class to lead a society not only by holding the coercive apparatus but by organising the consent of the governed through the institutions in which they live their ordinary lives.

The definition that the secondary literature repeats, and that survives contact with the notebooks, has three components that must be held together. First, hegemony is leadership rather than domination: a hegemonic class presents its interests as the general interest and succeeds in having them accepted as such, so that its rule appears as the rule of a national project rather than of a faction. Second, the leadership is bought and maintained with real concessions. Gramsci insisted that hegemony presupposes taking account of the interests of the groups over which it is exercised, and that the leading group makes sacrifices of an economic corporate kind, compromises that cost it something, though they cannot touch the essential core of its position. Consent obtained without cost is not hegemony but its counterfeit, and it fails at the first crisis because nothing was ever given that the subordinate group would lose by defecting. Third, hegemony is an unstable equilibrium that must be continually rewon. It is not a possession acquired once and held by inertia. The balance of forces shifts, concessions generate new demands, allied groups develop their own leaderships, and the hegemonic project must be renewed, renegotiated, and at times rebuilt from a weaker position. A fuller treatment of the notebooks, their categories of the integral state and the war of position, and the editorial problems of the text is given in the [gramsci hegemony complete guide](/2015/01/15/gramsci-hegemony-complete-guide/), and what follows here concentrates on the points the comparison requires.

Those points can be sharpened through the machinery Gramsci built around the central concept. The integral state, in his expanded usage, is political society plus civil society, the coercive apparatus together with the institutions of consent, and the formula insists that modern rule is never either one alone: it is hegemony protected by the armour of coercion, leadership with force in reserve. Organic intellectuals are the organisers of hegemony, the functionaries, teachers, journalists, priests, engineers, and party workers who elaborate a class's conception of the world and wire it into institutions; every class creates such strata, and a class that cannot is not ready to lead. The war of position is the strategy the concept dictates in societies with dense civil societies: a long molecular struggle for the trenches of consent, as against the war of movement, the frontal assault that succeeded only where civil society was gelatinous. And the national popular names the form a hegemonic project must take, speaking to a people's real traditions and demands rather than importing a doctrine in the abstract. Each of these categories describes an activity, something done by identifiable agents through identifiable institutions, and that is the concept's signature. Where ideology asks what is believed and whether it is true, hegemony asks who is organising, through which institutions, at what price, and with what margin of security.

### Can consent be genuine inside a hegemonic order?

Yes, on Gramsci's account it not only can be but must be, because hegemony is defined against mere deception: the subordinate group receives real concessions, accepts a leadership it experiences as partly its own, and would notice the difference if the order ruled by fraud and force alone.

The point deserves emphasis because the most common corruption of the concept turns hegemony into a smarter name for brainwashing. On the corrupted reading, consent is manufactured by controlling the channels of information, and the led are dupes whose agreement proves the efficiency of the apparatus. Gramsci's own texts block that reading at several points. A hegemony that rests on deception is brittle in exactly the way his theory predicts, because deception, once suspected, converts consent into cynicism and cynicism into availability for a counter hegemony. The concessions requirement does the real work: a leading group must deliver, to allied and subordinate groups, goods they genuinely want, security, rising living standards, national dignity, educational access, legal protections, and the delivery binds the leader as well as the led, since the goods cannot be withdrawn without spending the authority they purchased. Nor is the traffic one way. Subordinate groups press their own intellectuals, their own institutions, and their own readings of the common project into the settlement, which is why Gramsci called the resulting order an equilibrium rather than a victory. Consent in his sense is neither the free agreement of contract theory nor the false consciousness of the crude ideology model. It is a practical, interested, revocable allegiance, and its analysis requires the analyst to find the concessions, price them, and watch for the moment their price rises beyond what the leading group can pay.

## What hegemony explains that ideology does not

Set the two Marxist concepts side by side and their division of labour becomes visible. Ideology is strong where the question is why a demonstrably partial representation is believed, and what its partiality hides. Hegemony is strong where the question is why an order endures despite the partiality being widely known. The second question is not rhetorical; it describes the normal condition of durable orders, in which subordinate populations are rarely ignorant of the arrangements that disadvantage them and yet organise their lives within those arrangements for generations. Explanations by deception fail that condition, because knowledge and compliance coexist. Explanations by hegemony begin there. They point to the concessions that make compliance reasonable, to the institutions that make alternatives hard to assemble, to the absence of a counter organisation capable of converting private discontent into a rival project, and to the genuine satisfactions, national, material, and symbolic, that the order delivers. The analyst of hegemony studies trade unions that negotiate rather than revolt and asks what the negotiating bought; studies school curricula as settlements between projects rather than as injections of doctrine; studies popular culture as a battlefield of incorporation and resistance rather than as a delivery system for ruling ideas.

Hegemony also explains change within stability in a way the ideology concept handles awkwardly. Because hegemony is an equilibrium, its analysis has native categories for erosion: the leading group that can no longer afford its concessions, the allied group whose organic intellectuals defect, the crisis of authority in which the old is dying and the new cannot be born, to borrow the notebooks' most quoted diagnosis, itself a description of a hegemonic interregnum rather than of a sudden enlightenment. Ideology critique expects false beliefs to shatter against experience; hegemonic analysis expects settlements to unravel at their seams, institution by institution, and looks for the rival organisation assembling in the gap. The two expectations generate different research and different politics, and neither is a refinement of the other. They are answers to different questions that happen to share a tradition, a vocabulary of class and interest, and a founding problem, the stability of an order that distributes its goods unequally.

One further contrast completes this part of the comparison. Ideology, in the sharp sense, is something an order has; hegemony is something a class does, and can fail to do. The grammar matters. Classes in Gramsci's writing exercise hegemony, lose it, contest it, and build counter hegemonies, and the concept's centre of gravity is therefore strategic and historical: it directs attention to campaigns, institutions, alliances, and moments. The concept of ideology directs attention to contents, to propositions and images and their truth values. An analyst can catalogue the ideology of an order from its texts without knowing anything about the organisations that sustain it, and can map the hegemonic organisation of an order without settling the truth of a single belief held inside it. That independence is precisely what makes the two concepts combinable in careful hands and confusable in careless ones, a question taken up in full later, after the third concept has been given the same treatment.

## Althusser: ideology as material practice and the making of subjects

Between the classical concept of ideology and Foucault's refusal of it stands Louis Althusser, whose 1970 essay on ideological state apparatuses rebuilt the concept so thoroughly that the comparison changes shape around him. Althusser matters here for three reasons. He moved ideology out of the head and into institutions and rituals. He made the subject an effect of ideology rather than its bearer. And he did both while retaining, in a deliberately paradoxical formula, the real conditions that ideology misrecognises, which makes his version the strongest form of the concept Foucault had to refuse and therefore the right form in which to state what was refused.

The institutional move is the essay's most durable contribution. Alongside the repressive state apparatus, the army, police, courts, and prisons that function by violence, Althusser placed the ideological state apparatuses: churches, schools, families, law, parties, trade unions, communications, and culture, institutions that function predominantly by ideology and mostly sit in what liberal vocabulary calls private life. Ideology on this account is not a set of propositions a state propagates but the lived medium of these institutions, materialised in their practices: the timetable and the examination, the ritual and the ceremony, the forms of address and the distribution of praise. The school, he argued, had replaced the church as the dominant apparatus in capitalist societies, reproducing not only skills but submission to the rules of the established order, delivered in the apparently neutral currency of knowledge. The fuller anatomy of the apparatuses, and of the distinction between the one repressive apparatus and the plurality of ideological ones, is set out in [althusser ideology state apparatuses](/2012/03/01/althusser-ideology-state-apparatuses/); the comparison needs the essay's two conceptual turns, which are stranger and more consequential than its institutional list.

The first turn is interpellation. Ideology, Althusser wrote, interpellates individuals as subjects: it hails them, as a policeman's call hails a passer by, and in turning to answer the call the individual is constituted as a subject who recognises himself in the position the hail assigns. The scene is deliberately theatrical and easily mocked, but the claim underneath it is precise. Subjectivity, the experience of being a self who chooses, believes, and answers, is not the origin of ideology but its product, reproduced whenever individuals recognise themselves in the positions institutions prepare for them: pupil, believer, citizen, worker, mother. The second turn is the formula already quoted in this article's reconstruction of the tradition, that ideology represents the imaginary relationship of individuals to their real conditions of existence. Imaginary here is a technical term for a lived relation that is experienced rather than known, and the formula's architecture is the depth model's architecture retained under new management: real conditions below, imaginary relation above, the subject constituted in the space between. Althusser even insisted that ideology has no outside for the subjects it constitutes, while reserving to science, and specifically to historical materialism, a knowledge of the structure that ideology misrecognises. That reservation is the seam Foucault pressed.

The comparison gains from stating Althusser's position at full strength, because at full strength it already concedes much of what casual users of the word ideology deny. Ideology for Althusser is not falsehood but a necessary structure, eternal in form, present in every society including a classless one, since individuals will always live their relation to their conditions through imaginary forms. It is not imposed by conspirators but reproduced by rituals its bearers experience as freedom. And it does not merely hide reality; it constitutes the subjects for whom reality is lived at all. What it retains, against Foucault, is the hierarchy of knowledge: a science of the structure stands outside the imaginary, and the distinction between science and ideology does critical work throughout. It also retains determination by the economy in the last instance, a phrase Althusser himself surrounded with qualifications about the lonely hour of the last instance never arriving, but a retention nonetheless. Foucault's three objections, when they arrive, map exactly onto these retentions: the truth against which ideology is set, the subject the concept presupposes, and the base to which it is secondary. The objections were aimed at the strongest version of the concept, which is why they still have to be answered by anyone who keeps the word.

### What did Althusser change in the concept of ideology?

He relocated it from beliefs to institutions and rituals, argued that ideology constitutes the subjects who bear it through interpellation, and recast misrecognition as a permanent structure of social life rather than an error that enlightenment or revolution would simply abolish.

It is worth pausing on the political temperature of that change, since concepts carry the marks of their intended uses. Althusser wrote as a philosopher inside a communist party whose official humanism he opposed, and his anti humanist reconstruction, in which subjects are effects and history is a process without a subject, was an intervention in that party's debates as much as in philosophy. The concept he built is austere by design: it offers no standpoint of authentic consciousness from which the interpellated might speak, and it treats humanist talk of alienated man as ideology in his technical sense. Foucault, who had been Althusser's student at the École Normale and who placed himself outside party orthodoxies entirely, inherited the austerity and dropped the science. That lineage is the shortest route through the comparison: ideology in Althusser constitutes subjects but knows the structure that does it; discourse in Foucault constitutes subjects and declines the knowledge claim. The distance between the two positions is one step, and the step is the whole dispute.

## The commitment table

The reconstructions are now complete enough for the comparison to be drawn in its findable form. The commitment table sets the three concepts side by side, not by their dictionary definitions but by their commitments: what each assumes exists behind a representation, where each locates the origin of the subject, whether each treats class as prior to politics or as constructed within it, what kind of critique each licenses its user to perform, and what each forbids its user to say. The last column is the one comparison tables usually omit, and it is the most informative, because a concept is known by its prohibitions as surely as by its permissions. Read down any row and the price of the concept is stated in its own currency.

Three features of the table deserve a reader's attention before the argument moves on. The first is that hegemony's row is the only one whose second column names something practical rather than metaphysical. Gramsci's concept does not need a theory of what lies behind representation, because its object is not representation but organisation; asking what hegemony assumes exists behind appearances is slightly the wrong question, and the table records that wrongness deliberately, since it explains why hegemony combines with other concepts more easily than its rivals do. The second feature is the symmetry of the prohibitions. Each concept's forbidden sentence is another concept's founding sentence: discourse forbids the falsity verdict that ideology exists to deliver, and ideology's presupposition of an accessible real forbids the Foucauldian description of truth as internal to a regime. These are not quarrels about emphasis. They are architectural incompatibilities, and any writer who layers the three concepts as though they were storeys of one building has built on a fault line. The third feature is the class column, which quietly contains the fate of post-Marxism. Whether class positions precede their political articulation or are created by it is the question on which Laclau and Mouffe broke with the tradition, and the table shows why the break was necessary for them: they took the hegemony row and revised its class cell, and the revision propagated through every other cell until the framework was no longer Gramsci's. The sections on Foucault will now supply the third row with its full justification, since discourse has so far been present mainly as a refusal, and a refusal deserves its positive doctrine.

## Discourse before Foucault: a word that means talk

Discourse is an ordinary word that Foucault conscripted, and the conscription is the second misreading the brief for this article instructs us to correct. In ordinary usage a discourse is a stretch of connected speech or writing, a sermon, a lecture, a formal treatment of a subject, and in linguistics the word marks the level above the sentence, the analysis of how utterances hang together in conversation and text. Neither usage prepares a reader for Foucault's, in which a discourse is neither a speech nor a style of speech but a regulated system of statements that constructs the objects it appears to describe. When a media scholar writes that a newspaper's discourse on crime constructs the criminal as a type, the Foucauldian claim, if it is meant seriously, is not that the newspaper talks about criminals in a slanted way, which would be an ideology claim in journalistic dress. It is that the ensemble of statements, legal, medical, journalistic, statistical, within which crime is discussed at a given period determines what a criminal can be: which acts count as crimes of which kind, which explanations of criminality are available, which interventions appear rational, and which speakers are authorised to pronounce. The object is an effect of the system that speaks about it. That is a claim about the constitution of knowledge, and it cannot be translated into the vocabulary of bias without losing exactly what makes it Foucauldian.

The looseness of the word's passage into general scholarship explains much of the three way confusion this article exists to repair. Because discourse can mean talk, any analysis of talk can call itself discourse analysis, and because ideology can mean ideas, any analysis of ideas can borrow the older word's critical force while using the newer word's method. The result is a hybrid literature in which newspapers are said to construct reality through discourse and to distort it through ideology in adjacent paragraphs, the two verbs contradicting each other unnoticed. Construction and distortion are not synonyms. Distortion implies a prior object bent out of shape by its representation; construction implies the object as we can know and handle it is made in the representing. A disciplined writer chooses the verb that matches the theory actually in use, and the choice is auditable in a finished text by exactly that verb test: search for where the argument says a representation is false, and discourse has been abandoned, or search for where it says an object is produced, and ideology has been.

## Foucault: discourse as rules of formation

Foucault's positive doctrine was built across two phases of his work, and the comparison needs both, since the later phase supplies the account of power that makes the refusal of ideology intelligible. The first phase, the archaeology of knowledge, studied discourses as systems with rules of formation: rules governing which objects can appear, which positions a speaker must occupy to speak with authority, which concepts can be formed, and which strategies of argument are available. His histories of medicine, of madness, and of the human sciences traced how such systems changed at their joints, how the medical gaze that reads symptoms on a body's surface gave way to an anatomical gaze that reads lesions in its depths, how madness was successively an error, a sin, an unreason to be confined, and an illness to be treated. The method deliberately suspended the questions other historians asked. It did not ask whether a period's medicine was true, nor whose interest its errors served, nor what great thinker authored its concepts. It asked what had to be the case, at the level of the archive of statements, for those things to be said at all. Truth, on this method, is not denied; it is relocated, from a property of statements measured against the world to a property of regimes, the systems of rules within which statements count as true or false in the first place.

The second phase, the genealogy of power, connected the regimes of truth to the institutions and techniques that produce subjects. In the study of the prison, disciplinary power works through surveillance, examination, and normalisation, training bodies into docility and usefulness and producing the delinquent as a knowable type. In the first volume of the history of sexuality, power over life operates through confessional techniques and medical and demographic knowledges that produce sexuality itself as an object of administration and an identity to be inhabited. The famous reversal of that book is that power is productive: it does not primarily repress a pre existing subject with pre existing desires, it fabricates the categories, the pleasures, and the selves through which life is lived. Power and knowledge, in the formula that names the whole phase, directly imply one another; no power relation exists without a field of knowledge, and no knowledge leaves power relations untouched. With that, the architecture that distinguishes the third concept from the first two is complete. Ideology presupposes a subject who might see truly and a reality to be seen; Althusser's ideology constitutes the subject but preserves a science of the constitution; Foucault's discourse constitutes subject and object together and preserves nothing outside the field from which a verdict on the field could be delivered.

### What does discourse mean in Foucault's technical sense?

A discourse is a regulated system of statements whose rules determine which objects can appear, who may speak with authority, which concepts can be formed, and what can count as true, so that the discourse constructs its objects rather than merely describing them.

Two clarifications protect the doctrine from its vulgarisations, one on each side. Against the charge that Foucault dissolved the world into words, the accurate statement is that his analyses are relentlessly material: prisons, clinics, timetables, architectures, examinations, filing systems. Discourse in his work is a practice, embodied in institutions and techniques, and the claim is about the conditions under which things become objects of knowledge and intervention, not about the nonexistence of bodies, pain, or death, which his pages describe with a clinician's concreteness. Against the opposite vulgarisation, which recruits Foucault as a theorist of ideology with better examples, the accurate statement is that the refusal was principled and repeated: the vocabulary of ideology, of consciousness and its distortions, of a truth held in reserve by the analyst, is exactly what the archaeology and the genealogy were built to work without. A writer may judge the refusal mistaken; the objection section of this article will give that judgement its strongest form and its answer. What a writer may not do, without ceasing to describe Foucault's position, is soften the refusal into a renaming.

## Power without a headquarters: the third concept's wager

The depth model locates power as ideology locates distortion, in a structure that can be named: a ruling class, a state, a base that determines in the last instance. Foucault's analytics of power refuses the location. Power, in his repeated formulation, is not a thing possessed by a class or an institution and wielded downward; it is a relation, exercised from innumerable points, capillary in its reach, present in the family, the clinic, the school, and the confessional as surely as in the ministry, and productive of the subjects between whom it circulates. The state, on this view, is not the headquarters of power but a composite effect of many power relations, codified and supported by them, important without being originary. The wager of the third concept is that this dispersed analytics sees what the located one misses: the micro techniques by which conduct is conducted, the knowledges that make populations legible, the self surveillance by which modern subjects govern themselves in the name of health, normality, and success. Its analyses of discipline and of government have been taken up by historians and sociologists with no allegiance to the refusal that generated them, which is evidence of the analytics' fertility and, simultaneously, of how rarely its full commitments are accepted even by its borrowers.

The wager has a price, and the commitment table has already entered it in the ledger: a framework that treats every truth claim as internal to a regime cannot exempt its own. The genealogist's descriptions are statements too, produced within the human sciences whose formation the genealogist studies, and their claim on a reader cannot be that they are true in the way the depth model means truth, undistorted by the field they describe. Foucault's own answers to this difficulty shifted across his career and were characteristically oblique: his books were offered as toolboxes, as fictions that might induce effects of truth, as histories of the present whose warrant was their capacity to make the familiar strange and the necessary contingent. Whether those answers suffice is part of the objection to come. What the comparison records here is the symmetry of the difficulty. Mannheim asked the ideology critic where he stood; the same question, addressed to the genealogist, receives the answer that standing outside is the one move the method forbids itself. Both frameworks, pressed at their foundations, face a version of the problem of their own location. They differ in what they do next: the Marxist tradition tries to solve it, the Foucauldian tradition tries to make a method out of declining to.

## Why Foucault refused the word: the three objections

Foucault stated his reasons for abandoning the concept of ideology explicitly, in an interview from the second half of the 1970s that has been widely anthologised, most accessibly in the collection of his interviews on power and knowledge. The statement is short, deliberate, and structured as three distinct difficulties, and because comparison writing so often replaces it with paraphrases about postmodern suspicion, the three deserve to be set out one by one and in his order of importance. He said he had difficulty using the concept, and gave the difficulties as follows.

The first difficulty is that the concept of ideology stands in virtual opposition to something else that is supposed to count as truth. To call a representation ideological is to measure it against a truth that the analyst possesses, or could possess, by some route that escapes the condition being diagnosed in everyone else. For Foucault the interesting problem lay one step earlier: not whether statements were true, but how effects of truth are produced within discourses that are in themselves neither true nor false, how a regime comes to sort statements into the true and the false and to attach power to the sorting. The concept of ideology, on this objection, presupposes the very thing that needs explaining, the availability of a truth outside the play of regimes, and thereby exempts the analyst's own discourse from the analysis. The second difficulty is that the concept of ideology refers, necessarily in his view, to a subject: to a consciousness that bears the distortion, a human reality whose representations can be set against its conditions. His whole archaeology had been built to dispense with the constituent subject, to treat subject positions as places within discourses and subjects as effects of power, and a concept that reinstalled the subject as the bearer of error asked him to rebuild the foundation he had spent two decades removing. The third difficulty is positional. Ideology, in the Marxist architecture, stands in a secondary position relative to an infrastructure that determines it: ideas are superstructural, the economy is the base, and however sophisticated the mediations, the concept carries that ordering within it. Foucault's histories had consistently refused the ordering, treating the asylum, the clinic, and the prison not as reflections of an economic base but as sites where power and knowledge were made, and a concept that assigned his objects to a derivative storey of a building he did not believe in was unusable to him.

Read together, the three objections are a compressed statement of the third row of the commitment table, and their precision is their value to the comparison. They are not complaints that ideology is an old fashioned word or a Marxist word or a word with a bad history. They identify the three load bearing presuppositions of the concept, truth outside the field, a subject who bears the error, and a base that does the determining, and they decline all three on stated methodological grounds. Anyone who continues to use the concept after reading them owes the three answers, and the tradition has supplied candidates: truth as a practical relation to a real that resists us, the subject as a position rather than an origin, determination as a constraint rather than a command. Whether the answers repair the concept or merely rename its difficulties is the live dispute, and it cannot be conducted at all by writers who do not know the objections exist. That, more than any verdict, is the justification for treating the interview as the centre of this comparison.

### How do the three objections fit together?

They are one objection at three depths: the concept of ideology requires a truth outside discourse, a subject to be wrong about it, and a base that explains why the error arose, and Foucault's method consists precisely in declining all three requirements at once.

It is equally important to record what Foucault put in the concept's place, since a refusal with no replacement would be a gesture rather than a method. The replacement is the analysis of regimes of truth: the types of discourse a society accepts and makes function as true, the mechanisms that distinguish true from false statements, the techniques accorded value in acquiring truth, and the status of those charged with saying what counts as true. Every item on that list is researchable without presupposing the analyst's exemption, since the analyst's own regime can in principle be submitted to the same inventory. The political question also changes its shape. Where ideology critique asks how false beliefs sustain domination and expects enlightenment to weaken it, regime analysis asks how truth is produced and circulated as a system of ordered procedures, and expects change to come from altering the procedures, the institutions, and the authorised speakers. Readers who find the second question thinner than the first have located the genuine cost of the exchange, and the cost will be weighed in the objection and reply that form the centre of this article's second half. Readers who find it more honest have located its appeal. The comparison does not require the reader to pick a side at this point; it requires the reader to see that a side is being picked whenever the vocabulary is chosen, including by writers who believe they are merely describing.

## The entitlement test stated in full

The device introduced at the outset can now be stated with the precision the reconstructions make possible. The entitlement test says: choose the concept by the sentence you need to be entitled to write at the end of the analysis, and accept the presuppositions that sentence carries. Three families of concluding sentences cover the great majority of work in this area, and each family maps to one concept with its price attached.

Suppose the analysis must conclude that a widely held representation is wrong, that the world is not as the representation says, and that the misdescription is not random but patterned in the interest of an order that the misdescription protects. That sentence is the glory of ideology critique and it is unavailable to the other two frameworks. Hegemony cannot deliver it, because consent organised around a true representation is still hegemony, and the concept is silent on truth. Discourse cannot deliver it, because falsity is a verdict from a standpoint the method refuses. To write the sentence with ideology is to accept the depth model, the located standpoint, and the determining structure, and to owe answers to Mannheim and to Foucault. Suppose instead the analysis must conclude that an order persists because a leading group has organised the allegiance of others through institutions and concessions, that the allegiance is real, interested, and revocable, and that it is failing or holding at identifiable points. That sentence belongs to hegemony, and it is unavailable to ideology, which can register the beliefs of the allied groups but not the price paid for them, and to discourse, which can map the statements in circulation but has no native category for negotiation, concession, or alliance. To write it with Gramsci is to accept classes and their organisations as the parties to the settlement, and a state whose coercion waits behind the consent. Suppose finally the analysis must conclude that a field of knowledge made a kind of person, a kind of problem, and a kind of intervention possible, that before the field existed these things could not be thought in their present form, and that the field's emergence rearranged what power could do. That sentence belongs to discourse, and it is unavailable to the first two frameworks, which must treat the kinds as pre existing objects that representations then describe well or badly. To write it with Foucault is to surrender the falsity verdict, the interest explanation in its classical form, and the subject who might have known better.

The test has a disciplinary consequence worth stating as this article's namable claim in its operational form: most confused writing in this area is confused because it wants a concluding sentence whose presuppositions its chosen concept does not supply. The media study that maps a field of statements beautifully and then concludes that the field distorts reality has spent Foucault's method and banked Marx's verdict, and the exchange is illegitimate in both currencies. The political essay that documents concessions and alliances and then concludes that the led were deceived has done Gramsci's research and drawn the crude ideology model's moral. The test does not forbid combining concepts; the combination question is real and receives its own section. It forbids unpriced combinations, in which the analyst draws on whichever concept supplies the desired sentence at each paragraph break while accepting none of the presuppositions that make any of the sentences good. Entitlement, in short, is earned at the level of the framework or not at all, and a reader armed with the test can audit any text in this literature, including this article, by matching its conclusions against the concepts it actually deployed.

## The strongest objection: the standpoint that was never found

Part Six of this series' method requires the strongest objection to be stated as its holders would state it, and the objection to the concept of ideology, from within the discourse tradition and its philosophical allies, is genuinely formidable. It runs as follows. Ideology critique promises a distinction between distorted and undistorted apprehension of the social world. That distinction requires a position from which the undistorted apprehension is available, a standpoint not itself deformed by the interests and positions that deform everyone else's. In the century since the concept was generalised, no such standpoint has ever been successfully specified. Mannheim looked for it in a free floating intelligentsia and produced a sociological hope rather than an epistemology. The classical Marxist candidate, the standpoint of the proletariat, either means the views workers actually hold, which are as mixed and as penetrated by the dominant representations as anyone's, or it means the views workers would hold if they apprehended their position correctly, which defines correctness by the theory and the theory by correctness, in a circle no amount of dialectical vocabulary has squared. Lukács's imputed consciousness, the most sophisticated version, asks the analyst to calculate what a class ought to think given its position, and then to treat actual disagreement by the class as evidence of its false consciousness, a procedure that cannot lose and therefore cannot win either, since a distinction that no evidence can overturn is not a distinction but a loyalty.

The objection then generalises. Every proposed escape route from the circle reimports the problem at a higher level. Practice as a test of representations tests them against purposes, and purposes are themselves framed within regimes of what counts as a problem and a success. Science as an exempt practice has its own institutions, its own exclusions, its own history of confident error, and its own entanglement with the powers that fund and use it, as the sociology of science spent decades demonstrating. The appeal to a real that resists our representations proves too little: a real that resists tells us when we are wrong about particulars, but the ideology critic needs a real that instructs, a structured totality whose interests and tendencies can be read off and used to grade whole systems of belief, and resistance at the level of the broken tool or the failed harvest does not deliver a grading of worldviews. Add the historical evidence. The discourse theorist notes that the confident unmaskings of one generation read, to the next, as period pieces saturated with the unmasker's own unexamined common sense, and that the critic's chair has been occupied in succession by theologians, philosophes, party philosophers, and professors, each certain that the distortion ended at their own door. A distinction that has failed specification for a hundred years, through the efforts of some of the strongest minds in the tradition, should be suspected of being unspecifiable, and a method built on an unspecifiable distinction should be abandoned rather than repaired once more. On the far side of the abandonment, the objector concludes, lies not silence but a different and more honest labour: describing how fields of knowledge and power are organised, without pretending to grade them from outside. That is the objection at full strength, and nothing in what follows should be read as diminishing it before the reply begins.

## The reply, and the concession it must carry

The reply begins by granting the objector his historical evidence and then disputing his inference. That the standpoint has never been specified to general satisfaction is true; that no specification is possible does not follow, unless the standard for success is set where no concept in social theory could meet it. The concept of a class standpoint was always better understood, by its most careful defenders, not as a location of guaranteed truth but as a hypothesis about the distribution of epistemic advantage: that a position in a structure makes some features of the structure easier to see and others harder, as a position in a building makes some of its rooms visible and others hidden, without making any occupant omniscient. On that modest reading the circle dissolves, because the claim is comparative and corrigible: the analyst assembles evidence from multiple positions, weights the testimony by what each position discloses and conceals, and treats the depth, the structure of production and interest, as itself a finding open to revision rather than a premise beyond appeal. Ideology critique conducted in that spirit is ordinary fallible inquiry with a theory of error attached, and the theory of error, the claim that mistakes are patterned by interest rather than random, remains the best explanation anyone has given of why some false beliefs are socially indestructible while others die on contact with a fact sheet.

The reply then presses the price of the alternative, which the commitment table recorded and which the objector must be made to pay in the open. A purely descriptive account of discursive fields cannot say that anything is wrong. It can show that a regime of statements emerged, that it authorised certain speakers and produced certain subjects, that it displaced an earlier regime whose rules differed. It cannot say that the regime's descriptions of the world are false, that the suffering it administers is unjustified, or that the interests it serves are served at others' expense, because falsity, justification, and interest are depth model vocabulary, and the method has struck them from its lexicon. Yet the critical literature written in the discourse idiom is saturated with exactly those verdicts, delivered in a tone of exposure that promises an unmasking the framework cannot underwrite. The prison study that cannot say the delinquent is a fabricated category used to justify a machinery of control says it on every page in effect while denying itself the right in principle. The normative smuggling is not a lapse of individual writers; it is structural, because readers and writers alike want the critical payoff, and the framework's honesty about its limits lasts exactly as long as its conclusions remain merely descriptive, which is to say, not long. A method that must borrow its conclusions from the framework it refused has not obviously won the exchange.

Then the concession, which this article's method also requires to be paid plainly. The specification problem is genuinely unsolved. There is no agreed procedure, inside or outside the Marxist tradition, for certifying that a given representation distorts a social reality rather than contestably describing it, and the strongest versions of ideology critique, those that claim a science of the social totality, claim more than anyone has delivered. The honest settlement between the frameworks is therefore asymmetrical. Ideology retains its entitlement to the falsity verdict on the condition that the verdict is issued fallibly, with the evidence and the standpoint declared, as a claim within inquiry rather than above it. Discourse retains its entitlement to describe regimes without verdicts, on the condition that the description forgoes the borrowed rhetoric of exposure. What neither may do is advertise the other's payoff at its own price. Stated as a rule for readers: trust the ideology critique that shows its standpoint, and trust the discourse analysis that resists its peroration, and treat with suspicion any text that offers a verdict with no standpoint or a description with a verdict smuggled into its adjectives.

## Althusser against Foucault: the fork inside the refusal

Because Foucault's formation ran through Althusser's seminar, the sharpest statement of their difference is a family quarrel, and its details repay attention. The two agree on more than either agreed with the humanist Marxism of their period. Both rejected the expressive subject of classical philosophy, the self transparent consciousness that ideology was supposed to cloud. Both treated ideas as inseparable from institutions, rituals, and techniques. Both described their object as a system that produces the individuals who live inside it. A reader who stopped there would file them as allies, which is how much survey writing files them, under a joint heading of French structuralism and its aftermath. The quarrel is over what remains standing when the subject is removed.

For Althusser, what remains is a structure known by science: the mode of production, its instances, its determination in the last instance by the economy, and a theoretical practice capable of producing knowledge of that structure by breaking with the imaginary in which it is lived. Ideology is the name of the break's other side, the eternal medium of lived relation, and the science ideology distinction is the load bearing wall of the whole edifice. For Foucault, nothing of that kind remains, and the science ideology distinction is itself an artefact of a regime: the claim to stand outside ideology in a place called science is one more move within the game of truth, historically datable to the centuries that made knowledge a profession and a power. Remove the wall and the edifice becomes a field: not structure and its representations but practices, knowledges, and powers in reciprocal formation, with no last instance and no outside from which the field could be graded. The class column of the commitment table registers the consequence. Althusser's classes are positions in a structure that precedes their consciousness, and his politics, such as it was, addressed parties as the bearers of the science that comprehends the structure. Foucault's analytics has no positions of that kind to address, and his politics, famously, addressed specific struggles at specific sites, the prison, the asylum, the clinic, without a general staff and without a theory of the whole that would tell the struggles how they sum.

### How do Althusser and Foucault differ over the subject?

Both deny that the subject is the origin of meaning, but Althusser's ideology constitutes subjects inside a structure that science can know from outside, while Foucault treats subject and structure alike as effects within fields of power and knowledge that have no outside.

The fork matters beyond the history of French theory because it recurs wherever the vocabulary is used. A writer who says that an institution interpellates its members and then proposes to describe the process from a standpoint the institution cannot reach is an Althusserian, whether or not the name is known to them, and owes the defence of the science claim. A writer who says that a discourse produces its subjects and then declines, on principle, to grade the discourse against the world is a Foucauldian, and owes the renunciation of the verdict. The two debts are different, both are real, and the literature's standing confusion is to contract the first debt in the analysis and pay the second in the conclusion, or the reverse. The entitlement test is simply the bookkeeping that prevents the swap.

## Laclau and Mouffe: hegemony cut loose from its anchoring

The third act of the comparison belongs to Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe, whose Hegemony and Socialist Strategy, published in 1985, performed the most consequential operation any of the three concepts has undergone: they kept Gramsci's word and removed its foundation. Their argument begins inside the Marxist tradition, as a genealogy of its central difficulty. Classical Marxism, they contend, assigned class positions by the economy and then expected politics to express them, and the whole subsequent history of the tradition, from the debates on hegemony in Russian social democracy onward, is the record of that expectation failing and of concepts being invented to manage the failure. Hegemony was the greatest of those inventions, because it named the practice by which a class leads beyond itself, constructing a collective will out of elements that its economic position did not supply. Gramsci, on their reading, took the logic as far as it could go inside the tradition and still stopped at its border: his collective will remained anchored, in the end, to a fundamental class, and his historic bloc gathered its elements around a centre the economy had appointed.

Laclau and Mouffe crossed the border. Drawing on the theory of discourse, and on a reading of language in which elements acquire identity only within an articulated whole, they argued that social positions, class positions included, are not given prior to their articulation but are constituted within it. There is no working class with a unified interest waiting beneath its representations, because the unity itself is a political construction, assembled, when it is assembled, by a hegemonic practice that links demands and identities into a chain no economy dictated. The social, on this account, is discursive through and through: not because everything is language in the thin sense, a claim they explicitly refused, but because every object is constituted as an object of knowledge and practice within systems of differences that no extra discursive foundation underwrites. Post-Marxism is the name this position took, and the fuller map of its alliances and its critics is drawn in [post marxism explained](/2022/03/01/post-marxism-explained/); the comparison needs the operation's effect on the commitment table, which is total. Take the hegemony row and revise its class cell from prior to constructed, and the revision propagates: behind the representation there is no longer an anchoring class interest, the subject of hegemony is no longer a class and its intellectuals but any collective will that articulation succeeds in composing, and the critique licensed is no longer a strategic anatomy of a settlement between given parties but an analysis of how the parties themselves were made.

### What does articulation mean in this theory?

Articulation is the practice of linking elements, demands, grievances, identities, symbols, into a whole that gives each element a changed meaning, so that the linked whole and its parts alike are products of the linking rather than its pre existing ingredients.

The operation clarifies, by contrast, what Gramsci's version commits its user to, and why the two share a word rather than a theory. Gramsci's hegemony presupposes the parties: classes formed in production, with interests that can be taken account of, compromised with, and betrayed, and its concessions are priced in a currency, material advantage, that exists independently of the discourse that negotiates it. Laclau and Mouffe's hegemony presupposes only the elements and the practice, and its concessions are redefinitions, the absorption of a demand into a chain of equivalences at the price of the chain's own coherence. Both are theories of how majorities are made. Only the first can say that a majority was bought at the expense of the interest it was assembled to serve, since only the first possesses interests outside the assembly by which the purchase could be judged. Critics of post-Marxism, including formidable ones from inside the Marxist tradition, have pressed exactly that point, arguing that a framework without prior interests cannot distinguish emancipation from incorporation, and that its celebration of contingency quietly retires the question of who owns what. Defenders reply that the prior interests were always the tradition's metaphysical comfort, posited to guarantee outcomes that history declined to deliver. The dispute is the three way comparison continued by other means, with the entitlement test as its scorecard: ask what sentence each side needs to write about a defeated movement, betrayal or failed articulation, and the frameworks declare themselves.

## Can you use hegemony without Marxism?

The question is asked constantly, in seminars and in print, and the honest answer has two parts that must not be merged. The first part concerns the word's travels. Hegemony is used, legitimately within its own discourse, by international relations scholars for the preponderance of a leading state, by historians for the cultural ascendancy of a class or a creed, and by political commentators for any dominance they wish to sound theoretical about. Those usages answer to their own literatures and are not errors merely because Gramsci would not have recognised them. The second part concerns the concept's content when it is Gramsci's concept that is meant. Used with its content intact, hegemony drags its anchoring with it: a class or an alliance capable of leadership, institutions of civil society through which consent is organised, concessions that cost the leader something, an integral state whose coercion stands behind the settlement, and the standing possibility of a counter hegemony organised by the led. A writer who keeps the word and drops the anchoring has not detached the concept from Marxism so much as detached it from its meaning, leaving a prestige synonym for influence. The middle position, occupied by serious scholars, is to borrow the mechanism while declaring the modification: to analyse, for instance, the hegemony of a professional orthodoxy within a discipline, where the parties are not classes but schools, the concessions are appointments and citations, and the civil society is a republic of letters. That borrowing works because the mechanism, leadership through organised consent purchased with real concessions, survives the change of parties, and because the borrower says what was changed. The test is always the same: can the writer name the leader, the led, the concessions, and the institutions? A hegemony claim that cannot fill those four slots is a metaphor wearing a citation.

Journalism deserves its own paragraph in this section, because the word's journalistic career is where the loss of content is most complete. In political commentary, hegemony usually means that a party, a faction, or an idea is winning: a headline announces one camp's hegemony over a debate, and the word adds nothing to dominance except syllables and the borrowed gravity of a prison notebook. Gramsci's concept, applied to the same events, would ask the questions the headline exists to avoid. Whose consent, exactly, has been organised, and through which institutions? What was conceded to obtain it, and what did the concession cost? Where is the coercion in reserve, and what would its use do to the consent it protects? How is the settlement being rewon, week by week, and where is the counter organisation forming? A dominance that cannot answer those questions may be real, but it is not hegemony in any sense that helps a reader think, and the journalist layer of this article returns to the point with the seriousness it deserves, since the word's misuse in commentary is now the main route by which new readers meet it.

## Media studies and the three vocabularies

No field uses all three concepts more promiscuously, or has better reasons for trying, than the study of media, and its history with them is a compact version of the whole comparison. The first wave of Marxist media analysis worked with ideology in its sharp sense: news and entertainment were examined for representations that naturalised an unequal order, and the method was unmasking, matching the content of bulletins and serials against the realities they were said to distort. The wave produced durable findings about selection and framing, and it accumulated the depth model's characteristic difficulties in an especially visible form, since the undistorted coverage against which distortion was measured had itself to be described by the analyst, whose newsroom nobody had ever seen. The second wave took up hegemony, partly through the Birmingham school of cultural studies and its reading of Gramsci, and the questions changed accordingly: from what the media inject to what settlements they negotiate, how popular pleasures are incorporated and resisted, how moral panics assemble temporary majorities, how the definitions of a situation are struggled over by police, politicians, editors, and publics rather than imposed by any one of them. Stuart Hall's work is the landmark here, encoding and decoding, primary definers, the analysis of policing a crisis, and its signature is the refusal of the passive audience that the crude ideology model required.

The third wave arrived with discourse, and with it a second refusal, this time of the residual depth model that even the Gramscian wave had retained. Discourse analytic work on news asks how the categories themselves, the terrorist and the commuter, the taxpayer and the scrounger, the expert and the vox pop voice, are constituted by the routines of their representation, and declines to grade the resulting field against a coverage that would finally be adequate to its object. Each wave survives inside the field as a live option, and examination answers in media studies are, whether candidates know it or not, wagers on one of the three rows of the commitment table. The wager can be made consciously. A question about bias in coverage is an ideology question and demands the described reality against which bias is measured; a question about why a definition of a crisis held for a decade is a hegemony question and demands the institutions and concessions that held it; a question about how a category of person came to be thinkable is a discourse question and demands the rules of the field that made it available. The candidates who lose marks are reliably those who answer a question of one type with the machinery of another, mapping a field when a verdict was requested, or delivering a verdict when only a map was earned.

The same discipline applies to the concept of ideology critique as a method in its own right, which extends beyond media to the study of political argument generally. The method's steps, reconstructing the representation, identifying its erasures, locating the interest the erasures serve, and testing the representation against the record, are codified in this series in [ideology critique method](/2012/03/15/ideology-critique-method/), and a reader of the present comparison will notice that every step presupposes the first row of the commitment table. That is not a criticism of the method; it is the method's honesty about its foundations, and it is exactly the honesty the entitlement test exists to demand from every framework, including the two that define themselves against the first.

## Rivals or complements: the layering temptation

Every teacher of this material has met the synthesising essay, the one that proposes to use all three concepts together, ideology for the content of beliefs, hegemony for their social organisation, and discourse for their linguistic texture, arranged as layers of a complete analysis. The proposal feels generous and mature, and it collapses on inspection, because the layers are not layers. They are answers to the same questions, the questions in the commitment table's columns, and at each question the three answers exclude one another. Either a representation can be measured against a real it distorts, or truth is internal to regimes and there is no such measurement; the analysis must choose at the moment it issues or withholds the falsity verdict, and it cannot do both in the same breath. Either class positions precede their articulation and constrain it, or they are constituted within it; a history of a movement must choose at the moment it explains why these people, and not others, were available to be organised. Either the subject is an effect all the way down, or there is a bearer of interests and experience to whom representations happen; the choice surfaces the first time the analysis says that a group recognised, or failed to recognise, anything.

Yet the complementarity intuition is not worthless, and dismissing it entirely would waste something the comparison has earned. The concepts are rivals as foundations and complements as instruments, and the distinction can be made operational. Foundations rival because each is a total account of the relation between meaning and power, and total accounts exclude. Instruments complement because an inquiry moves through phases, and different phases raise different questions: a study might begin by mapping a discursive field, proceed to examine the hegemonic settlement that stabilised one region of it, and end by judging one representation within the settlement against the evidence, issuing an ideology verdict with its standpoint declared. Nothing in that sequence contradicts itself, provided the transitions are marked and the presuppositions of each phase are accepted only for that phase's work and not smuggled into the next. What the entitlement test forbids is the unmarked slide, in which a Foucauldian map acquires a Marxist verdict in its final paragraph without the standpoint ever being argued for, or a Gramscian anatomy dissolves into construction talk whenever interests become inconvenient to specify. Marked transitions are scholarship; unmarked slides are the synonym habit with a methodology section.

The neighbouring debate with postcolonial theory, treated in this series in [marxism vs postcolonial theory](/2017/05/15/marxism-vs-postcolonial-theory/), runs on the same fault line and can be used as a check on one's fluency. There too a discourse derived framework, the analysis of colonial discourse and the constitution of the colonised subject within it, confronts a Marxist framework that wants to speak of material interests misrepresented and misrecognised, and there too the dispute is misdescribed whenever it is presented as a disagreement about emphasis or about which region of the world deserves study. It is a disagreement about what exists behind a representation and about who is entitled to describe it, which is to say it is the commitment table's first and second columns argued over a different archive. A reader who can state that has understood the comparison in its general form; the three concepts are one local instance of a division that runs through the whole modern theory of meaning and power.

## Choosing by question: the entitlement test at work

The comparison now pays its promised dividend, a decision procedure stated plainly enough to be used. The procedure has three steps. First, identify the concluding sentence the inquiry needs: a verdict on truth, an anatomy of consent, or a map of a field's organisation. Second, adopt the concept that entitles that sentence, and write its presuppositions into the inquiry's foundations where critics can see them. Third, audit the finished text for sentences belonging to the other families, and either price them, by supplying the presuppositions they need, or strike them. The procedure does not choose for the analyst; different inquiries into the same material will legitimately choose differently. It ensures that the choice is made once, knowingly, and at the foundations, rather than repeatedly, unknowingly, and at the level of adjectives.

### How does the entitlement test guide the choice of concept?

State the sentence the analysis must be able to conclude with, then adopt the framework that entitles that sentence: ideology for a verdict of distortion, hegemony for an account of consent organised and priced, discourse for a description of how a field constitutes its objects.

Worked briefly against the standard question types, the procedure sorts the territory cleanly. Examination questions asking whether a belief system serves the powerful, and how, are ideology questions, and the strong answer reconstructs the representation, names its erasures, and defends the reality against which the erasure is measured. Questions asking why an order is stable, why the disadvantaged consent, why a settlement endures or cracks, are hegemony questions, and the strong answer finds the leading group, the led, the institutions, and the concessions, and prices the equilibrium. Questions asking how a category, a problem, or a kind of person became thinkable, and what the thinking made possible, are discourse questions, and the strong answer reconstructs the rules of formation and the techniques of power that the category carries, while declining the verdict its examiners may be baiting it to deliver. Research questions follow the same sorting, with the addition that the researcher must also choose a relation to the archive: the ideology critic treats texts as evidence about a world beyond them, the analyst of hegemony treats them as moves in an organisational struggle, the genealogist treats them as the surviving surface of a regime. Even the archive changes with the concept, which is the final proof that the three are instruments and not synonyms: they do not merely interpret the same material differently, they constitute different material as relevant.

## A worked case: one order, three analyses

Abstractions in this area are best audited against a single case treated three ways, and the case chosen here is deliberately structural rather than partisan: the long stability, across the middle of the twentieth century in the industrial democracies, of the settlement between organised labour, large employers, and the state, the settlement whose elements included collective bargaining, welfare provision, full employment commitments, and progressive taxation. The case is historical, its archives are open, and each of the three frameworks has something characteristic to say about it, which makes the divergences legible.

The ideology analysis begins from the settlement's self description: a partnership of capital and labour under a neutral state, distributing the gains of growth by negotiation among equals. The analysis reconstructs that representation and measures it against the relations it describes. Ownership of the means of production did not change hands; the wage relation and the extraction of surplus within it continued beneath the partnership's language; the equality of the bargaining table abstracted from the inequality of the parties' reserves, the worker selling labour power under the compulsion of needing to live, the employer purchasing it from a position of stored wealth. The verdict follows: the representation was ideological in the precise sense, partly true in its particulars, since living standards rose and bargaining was real, and distorting in its framing, since it presented a compromise within an unchanged structure as the supersession of the structure's conflict. The analysis closes by locating the interest the framing served and by predicting, correctly as the sequel showed, that the framing would crack when the growth that funded it faltered. Every strength of the first row is on display, and so is its presupposition: the undistorted description of the wage relation, against which the partnership language was measured, is the theory's own, and a critic is entitled to ask from where it was measured.

The hegemony analysis of the same settlement asks a different question and gets a different, compatible at the factual level, rival at the foundational level, answer. It sees a historic bloc: a leading fraction of capital, organised through employers' associations, parties, and a sympathetic press, constructing a national popular project of reconstruction and rising consumption, and purchasing the allegiance of organised labour with concessions that were neither trivial nor fake: social insurance, public housing and health provision, legal recognition of unions, a share in the management of demand. Organised labour's own intellectuals staffed the settlement's institutions and elaborated its common sense, while the coercive armour remained visible at the settlement's edges, in the policing of unofficial strikes and the exclusion of the settlement's radical critics from its councils. On this reading the settlement's end, when it came, was not an enlightenment but a re-pricing: the concessions grew expensive relative to profits, a rival project organised its own intellectuals and its own common sense of crisis, and the equilibrium was contested trench by trench until a new hegemony, with different concessions to different allies, replaced it. The analysis never needs to call the partnership language false. It needs only to show what the language organised, what it cost, and when the bill exceeded the payer's willingness, which is the second row working exactly as designed.

The discourse analysis declines both starting points and begins from the categories the settlement made thinkable: the economy as a manageable object with measurable aggregates, the worker as a bearer of skills and a unit of manpower, the citizen as a client of welfare with needs that administration could classify, unemployment as a rate to be tuned rather than a condition to be abolished or a discipline to be imposed. It traces the knowledges that produced these objects, national accounting, Keynesian demand management, personnel science, social work, and the institutions that made the knowledges effective, treasuries, exchanges, clinics, and inspectorates. Power in this telling is not held by the bloc and exercised over the led; it circulates through the techniques by which populations were rendered legible and governable, and the subjects of the settlement, the responsible union leader, the deserving claimant, the prudent housewife of the consumption statistics, are its products as much as its parties. The analysis concludes, characteristically, without a verdict on the settlement's truth or its fairness, and its silence on those questions is either its integrity or its evasion, depending on the standpoint the reader brings, which is itself the third row's lesson about standpoints. Three accounts, one archive, and no possibility of merging the conclusions without choosing among the foundations: the case has done the work the comparison promised.

## Consent that survives open disagreement

One phenomenon deserves separate treatment because it is the hegemony concept's home ground and the other two concepts' hardest test: consent that survives open disagreement. In settled orders it is common to find subordinate populations who describe their arrangements critically, sometimes bitterly, in interviews and in private talk, and who nonetheless reproduce those arrangements daily, defend them against outsiders, and punish defectors from them socially. The ideology model must treat the critical talk as a crack in the false consciousness and the compliance as its residue, a split it can name but not order, since the same heads hold both. Discourse analysis describes the field within which complaint itself has licensed forms, the grumble, the satire, the ritual protest, that leave the field's rules intact, and its description is powerful precisely where the complaint is part of the machinery. Hegemony orders the phenomenon by asking what the compliance buys. The critic who reproduces the settlement is usually not deceived and not merely discursively positioned; he is party to an exchange whose terms he knows, security against disruption, dignity within a recognised role, goods delivered and remembered, and his criticism is the sound of a party monitoring the exchange, not of a dupe awakening or failing to. When the exchange deteriorates, the same criticism becomes the raw material of counter organisation, which is why hegemonic analysis watches the price of concessions the way meteorology watches pressure.

The phenomenon also disciplines the concept's enthusiasts, and the discipline should be recorded. Not all compliance is consent, and hegemony analysis degenerates when it forgets the armour in its own formula. Populations reproduce arrangements from exhaustion, from fear, from the absence of any organisation through which refusal could be coordinated, and from calculations of personal survival that no theory of allegiance should be allowed to rename as agreement. Gramsci, who wrote under a regime that jailed him, never made that mistake: his consent is always consent within a field patrolled by coercion, and the analyst's task is to find the proportion in each case, not to assume it. A comparison that ends with hegemony romanticised would have failed its own standard, and the standard is worth restating at this point in the article because it governs the persona layers and the verdicts still to come: each concept at its strongest, each price stated, and the choice between them referred to the question being asked rather than to the sympathies of the asker.

## The subject in the three frameworks

The commitment table's second column, the origin of the subject, rewards a slower treatment, because it is the column students find most abstract and the one that decides the most in practice. Begin with the plainest question an analyst can ask of any population: when its members speak, whose voice is it? The first framework answers that the voice belongs to subjects formed by their positions, speaking, with distortions, about a world they inhabit; the analyst's task is to correct for the distortion as an astronomer corrects for the atmosphere, and the correction presupposes both the star and the telescope's owner. The Althusserian refinement answers that the voice is the structure's, ventriloquised: the subject experiences itself as the author of its beliefs precisely because ideology's function is to produce that experience, and the analyst, armed with the science of the structure, hears the apparatus in the utterance. Gramsci's answer differs from both, and the difference is easy to miss because his vocabulary of consciousness sounds like the first framework's. For Gramsci the subject of politics is collective and made, not found: a class becomes a subject, a collective will, through the work of its intellectuals and its organisations, and the making is a historical achievement that can fail, as it failed catastrophically in his own Italy. The voice, when it comes, belongs to an organisation that built the throat it speaks with. Foucault's answer completes the set: the subject is a position within discourse and a fold of power, the interiority that power produces as its relay, and the question whose voice is malformed, since voice and owner alike are effects of the field the question treats as their container.

These are not four ways of saying that people are influenced by society. They generate four different relations between the analyst and the analysed, and the relations have ethical as well as methodological weight. The first framework's analyst corrects the subject, and must carry the charge of condescension that Foucault's objection pressed. The second's analyst decodes the subject, and must carry the charge of treating persons as symptoms. The third's analyst joins or opposes the subject's organisation, and carries the responsibilities of a participant. The fourth's analyst describes the conditions under which subjectivity of that kind became possible, and must carry the charge of refusing solidarity with the subjects described, prisoners, patients, claimants, whose own accounts of their situation the method brackets. No framework escapes a charge; each chooses which charge to answer. In the classroom this is the point at which the comparison stops being a taxonomy and becomes a formation in judgement, and it is why the student layer of this article is expanded: a student who can state the four relations and their charges owns the comparison in a way that no memorised triad of definitions can supply.

## What each concept cannot say

A framework is partly defined by its silences, and collecting the three silences in one place sharpens the comparison more than another round of definitions would. The ideology framework cannot comfortably describe the production of its objects. Its categories, the worker, the citizen, the nation, arrive in the analysis as the furniture of the world, and its histories therefore begin in the middle of things, with representations of objects whose constitution it does not interrogate. When the object itself is the historical novelty, when a kind of person or problem has just been assembled by new knowledges and institutions, ideology critique finds itself describing the drapes of a room whose building it missed. The hegemony framework cannot comfortably grade truth. Its settlements are made of representations it treats as moves rather than as claims, and a hegemonic analysis of a demonstrably false belief, a fabricated atrocity story, a doctored statistic around which consent was organised, must either borrow the ideology framework's verdict or describe the fabrication's career in a tone of neutrality that readers experience as evasion. Nor can it see far below organisation: the unorganised, the inarticulate, the practices that never reach the level of a project, appear in its pages mainly as absences, as the led whose intellectuals have not yet arrived. The discourse framework cannot comfortably say that an arrangement is worse than its predecessor, only that it is different in its rules and its products; it cannot name a beneficiary in the strong sense, since interest presupposes a subject with a good of its own that the field might serve or thwart; and it cannot explain, from its own resources, why one regime yields to another at a particular moment rather than another, since the ruptures it records are redescribed more readily than they are caused. Each silence is the shadow of a strength catalogued earlier in this article. The point of the catalogue is not that the analyst needs a fourth framework without silences, which does not exist, but that the choice among the three is also a choice among silences, and an inquiry whose central phenomenon falls inside its framework's silence will fail no matter how skilfully it is conducted.

## Class: prior or constructed, the dividing line restated

Return, near the end of the comparison, to the column of the commitment table where the stakes are most concrete. If class positions are prior, given by the structure of production before any politics begins, then a movement's failure to unite its class is a failure of organisation or of consciousness, and the analyst's categories of betrayal, false consciousness, and objective interest retain their sense. The political consequence is a discipline: strategies are judged by their correspondence to interests that exist independently of the judging. If class positions are constructed, articulated within discourse from elements that carry no necessary belonging, then a movement's disunity is not a failure to recognise a pre existing whole but the normal condition of the social, and unity, when achieved, is an achievement of articulation with no underlying guarantee. The political consequence is a different discipline: strategies are judged by their success in composing chains of equivalence, and the charge of betrayal loses its tribunal, since there is no interest outside the composition by which a composition could be convicted. Gramsci stands on the first side with his own complications, since his collective will is made but made by classes whose existence he never doubted; Laclau and Mouffe crossed to the second side while keeping his vocabulary, which is why their book remains the comparison's most instructive document, the record of a single cell of the table being revised until the whole framework changed its identity. Foucault stands off the axis in one sense, since class is not his organising category at all, and on the second side in another, since for his analytics every collective identity, class included, is an effect of the practices that name and administer it. A reader deciding among the three concepts is, in the end, deciding where to stand on this line, and the decision will outlast any single inquiry: it determines what kind of political disappointment the analyst is prepared to describe, and in what vocabulary.

## A short history of the comparison itself

The three concepts did not arrive as a triad. They were assembled into one by the institutions that teach theory, and the assembly has a history that explains the synonym habit better than carelessness does. Gramsci entered English language scholarship slowly, through selections from the notebooks published from the late 1950s and the fuller selection of 1971, and his hegemony was taken up in the 1970s by historians and by the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies as a tool against the economism of the Marxism then dominant. Althusser's influence crested in the same decade, in film theory, in literary studies, and in the journals of the New Left, bringing interpellation and the apparatuses into the same seminar rooms that were reading Gramsci. Foucault arrived in force slightly later, in the translations of the late 1970s, and his refusal of ideology landed in a field already dissatisfied with the depth model for its own reasons, supplying that dissatisfaction with a method and a licence. By the time a unified theory curriculum existed, in cultural studies and the newer sociology programmes, the three were being taught in adjacent weeks of the same module, by lecturers who had often been formed by one of them and borrowed the others as supplements. The triad is a curriculum artefact before it is a philosophical one, and the confusions this article has been correcting are the predictable result of teaching three foundations as three topics. Laclau and Mouffe's intervention of 1985 is best read against that background: it was an attempt, from inside the seminar room's own materials, to force the choice the curriculum had been avoiding, by showing that hegemony radicalised consistently becomes discourse theory and ceases to be Marxism in any sense its founders would have claimed. Whether one applauds or resists the attempt, it had the virtue of refusing the layering temptation at the moment of its maximum attractiveness.

Transmission also imposed quieter distortions that a careful reader should price in. Gramsci's notebooks are fragments, censored in composition and reconstructed by editors whose ordering of the material has been revised by successive critical editions; the hegemony of the textbooks is a systematisation of notes its author never systematised, and divergent readings of the concept, some stressing consent, some stressing its coercive armour, can each cite genuine fragments. Foucault's three objections survive in an interview, a genre he used for provocations he sometimes qualified in his books, though in this case the books corroborate the interview at every point. Althusser's essay was a party intervention whose later author himself subjected its theoreticism to criticism. None of this invalidates the concepts; it instructs the reader to hold them as reconstructions, argued from difficult texts, rather than as quotations with page numbers, and to prefer, wherever the comparison tightens, the formulations their authors returned to rather than the phrases their popularisers found portable.

## Three sightings: the vocabulary at work in ordinary argument

The comparison can be carried out of the seminar room, and three ordinary sightings show the audit working at street level. The first sighting is a ministerial speech, reported in a newspaper, in which a government describes its welfare reforms as a restoration of fairness between those who work and those who depend. An ideology reading asks what the fairness representation erases: the distribution of the reforms' costs, the labour market that produces dependence, the history by which the categories of worker and dependant were attached to desert. A hegemony reading asks what settlement the speech is servicing: which groups' allegiance is being purchased by the distinction, which institutions will administer it, and what has been conceded elsewhere to hold the coalition the speech addresses. A discourse reading asks how the speech's categories became available at all: by what administrative and journalistic routines a population came to be sorted into the working and the dependent, and what forms of self understanding that sorting supplies to the sorted. All three readings can be printed about the same column of newsprint, and they contradict one another only at the foundations, which the newspaper's readers will never see debated, because the newspaper will use the three vocabularies interchangeably inside a single leader column.

The second sighting is a corporate training programme, of the kind through which large employers teach their values to new staff. The ideology reading examines the programme's representations of the firm as a family and of advancement as the reward of attitude, and measures them against the firm's actual distribution of security and reward. The hegemony reading examines the programme as one trench among many: the induction rituals, the internal honours, the grievance channels, the share schemes and staff councils that price the staff's allegiance, and the quiet presence, behind the values, of the contract's termination clauses. The discourse reading examines the programme's production of a subject: the employable self, enterprising, resilient, fluent in the vocabulary of its own development, a self that experiences the firm's requirements as its own aspirations. The third sighting is smaller and sadder: a dismissed worker telling an interviewer that he blames himself for lacking the skills the market wanted. Ideology hears an internalised representation that converts a structural event into a personal failure. Hegemony hears the settlement's common sense working at the level of a single biography, and asks what institutions taught the conversion and what the man's allegiance, while it lasted, was paid. Discourse hears a subject position speaking, the deficient self that a whole apparatus of training, assessment, and advice exists to produce and to offer remedies to. The three hearings are not three verses of one song. They are three theories of what kind of event a man's self blame is, and the entitlement test is the only honest way to hold them in one head: in sequence, with the transitions marked, and never in a blend.

## Auditing a text: the commitment table in use

The comparison closes its analytical work with a usable instrument, the audit that the commitment table makes possible, stated as a sequence of questions a reader can put to any text that uses this vocabulary, including the texts set on courses and the drafts of one's own essays. Ask first what the text treats as existing behind its representations: a structure of interests it can describe independently, an organisation it can name, or nothing it is willing to specify. Ask second where its subjects come from and what its grammar does with them: are they corrected, decoded, organised, or produced? Ask third what its concluding sentences claim: a verdict, an anatomy, or a map, and whether the concept named in its introduction is the concept that entitles its conclusion. Ask fourth what it cannot say, and whether the unsaid is the thing the reader most needed to know. A text that survives the four questions with its commitments visible is a text whose framework can be argued with, which is the highest condition to which writing in this area can aspire. A text that dissolves under the questions, revealing a verdict with no described reality beneath it, an organisation with no concessions in it, or a map with a smuggled peroration, has been caught in the synonym habit, and the audit has done in four questions what a semester of definitional teaching often fails to do.

There is a final application of the audit, reflexive and brief. This article has itself chosen a framework for its comparisons, and the choice should be visible enough to be audited. Its method has been to reconstruct each concept's commitments and to allocate questions among them by the entitlement test, which is a Gramscian courtesy extended to rivals: it treats the frameworks as organisations of thought with territories and prices, and it has deliberately issued no verdict on which framework's picture of the world is true, because that verdict, by the article's own test, would require a standpoint argument that a comparison article is not the place to conduct. Readers who finish the article persuaded that one row of the table is simply correct have gone beyond its argument, and are invited to notice the moment of going, since noticing it is the skill the whole comparison exists to teach.

## Hegemony and the state: the integral state revisited

One element of Gramsci's construction deserves a section of its own in a comparison of this kind, because it is the element that most clearly separates his concept from the other two and the one most often dropped in borrowings: the state. Gramsci's integral state, political society plus civil society, was a deliberate expansion of the concept of the state beyond the government and its coercive arms to include the whole organisation of consent, and it carries a consequence that users of the word hegemony rarely register. If the institutions of consent are part of the state in the integral sense, then hegemony is not an alternative to state power but the state's normal mode of operation in a developed society, and the distinction between ruling by consent and ruling by force becomes a distinction between the state's two registers rather than between the state and something outside it. A school, on this accounting, is not a private institution that happens to support the state; it is one of the state's trenches. A newspaper is not an external commentator on power; it is part of the fortification system within which power is exercised and contested. That is why the war of position is a war for the state in its integral sense, and why its victories are institutional and cumulative rather than rhetorical.

Set the other two concepts beside that construction and their silences about the state become audible. The classical ideology concept has a state theory available to it, the state as the executive committee of a class or as the condensate of class relations, but the concept of ideology itself does its work at the level of representations wherever they circulate, and its analyses can proceed indefinitely without deciding what the state is. Foucault's analytics, by design, dissolves the state into an effect of governmental practices, a composite reality and a mythicised abstraction whose unity is produced by the very techniques that invoke it; the move liberates analysis from a false centre and simultaneously makes it hard to say why some practices command armies and others do not, a difficulty his later lectures on governmentality worked to reduce by treating the state as a practice of governing rather than a thing. Gramsci alone of the three makes the state the explicit stake: hegemony is won or lost over the integral state, and the counter hegemony he hoped for was a rival organisation of the whole apparatus, coercion and consent together. A borrower who takes his concept without his state takes the word's warmth and leaves its war behind, and the commitment table's hegemony row should be read with the integral state as its silent sixth column.

## Ideology after the objections: what a chastened critique looks like

The concept of ideology did not retire when the objections were filed, and the forms in which it survives are instructive for the comparison, since survival under criticism is evidence about which of its commitments were load bearing. The philosopher Raymond Geuss, in a study that became a standard point of entry, sorted the concept's senses into descriptive, pejorative, and positive families, and the sorting alone dissolves a shelf of confusions: a descriptive ideology is any group's worldview, a pejorative one is a worldview criticised for the distortions or the domination it involves, and a positive one is a worldview a movement needs in order to act. Much polemic in this area consists of defending the descriptive sense against attacks aimed at the pejorative, or attacking the pejorative sense with arguments that only touch the positive. Terry Eagleton's survey of the concept catalogued the definitions in circulation and defended a version of the pejorative sense with the depth model's commitments made unusually explicit: representations are ideological, on his account, not merely when they are false but when their falsity, partiality, or naturalising work functions to sustain relations of domination, and the reality against which they are measured is available to a fallible, historically situated inquiry rather than to a science enthroned above history.

What survives, in these defences, is the falsity verdict with its standpoint declared and its claims moderated; what is surrendered is the guarantee. The chastened ideology critic no longer promises an exit from ideology into undistorted apprehension, only better and worse apprehensions, checked against evidence, against the testimony of those positioned differently, and against the recalcitrance of events. Whether the chastened version still deserves the old name, or has become a general theory of motivated error with a Marxist accent, is a fair question, and Foucault's defenders ask it with some relish. But the comparison should record the symmetry: the discourse framework has undergone its own chastening, as its practitioners quietly reintroduced interests, beneficiaries, and verdicts under the cover of describing regimes, and its popular form is no more faithful to its founder's renunciations than the popular form of ideology critique is to its founder's system. The concepts as actually used, in journals and in classrooms, are truces between their founders' architectures and their users' needs, and the entitlement test audits the truces: it asks of any actual text not which founder it cites but which sentences it writes and whether it has paid for them.

A worked miniature may fix the chastened procedure in memory. Take the representation, common in the settlement examined earlier, of the housewife's unpaid labour as a natural expression of her role rather than as work. The unchastened critique denounces the representation as false consciousness implanted by a patriarchal capitalism and stops. The chastened critique does more work and claims less magic. It reconstructs the representation's career through advice literature, housing design, wage structures built on a family wage assumption, and the testimony of the women who lived it, including the testimony of those who affirmed it and the satisfactions they reported. It measures the representation against the describable reality of hours worked, output produced, and dependence created, states the interest the naturalisation served in keeping that labour unwaged and its performer without an independent income, and declares its own standpoint, an inquiry committed to counting unwaged labour as labour and prepared to defend the counting as a finding rather than assume it as an axiom. The verdict it finally issues, that the representation distorted by naturalising, is the ideology framework's sentence, purchased at the chastened price: fallible, evidenced, and owned. Nothing in the procedure requires the analyst to deny that the category of housewife was also discursively produced, or that consent to the arrangement was organised through institutions that delivered real goods. It requires only that the transitions between those registers be marked, which is the whole of this article's practical teaching.

## Discourse in practice: synthesis attempts and their tensions

The strongest evidence that the three frameworks are rivals at the foundation is the fate of the attempts to fuse them, and the most instructive attempt is critical discourse analysis, the school associated with Norman Fairclough and a wide network of linguists and social scientists. The school's ambition was exactly the layering proposal given methodological rigour: texts analysed with linguistic precision, discursive practices of production and consumption analysed institutionally, and the whole set within a social practice analysed with the help of Marxist categories, ideology and hegemony among them. Its analyses of political language, of the marketisation of public institutions, of the conversational style of modern leadership, are among the most concrete achievements in this entire field, and its practitioners are explicit that they use discourse in a sense modified by the company it keeps: for Fairclough a discourse is a way of representing some part of the world, one representation among possible rivals, which reintroduces precisely the plurality against a background that the Foucauldian concept was built to refuse, since rival representations imply a represented world about which they can be partial. The fusion works, where it works, by quietly revising the third concept back toward the first, keeping Foucault's attention to the constitutive force of categories while surrendering his renunciation of the verdict. That is a legitimate intellectual operation and often a fruitful one. What it is not is a demonstration that the original three were compatible all along; it is a demonstration that a fourth position can be built from their materials at the price of one founder's central refusal, which is what the commitment table predicts every synthesis will cost.

Other syntheses pay at other tills. Analysts of hegemony working on climate politics or on European integration have borrowed the discourse framework's account of articulation wholesale, through Laclau, and paid with the class anchoring, gaining a supple account of how coalitions of demands are composed and losing the ability to say what the composition cost the composed. Foucauldian historians of the welfare state have borrowed the ideology framework's beneficiaries, speaking of interests served by regimes of truth, and paid with the renunciation, gaining a politics and losing the methodological purity that made the descriptions startling. The pattern across the cases is stable enough to state as a finding of this comparison: the frameworks combine the way currencies exchange, at rates, with something surrendered in every transaction, and the scholarly virtue lies not in refusing the exchanges but in publishing the rate. A literature that published its rates would be unmistakable: its introductions would state, in the first pages, which sentences the work intends to be entitled to and which founder's refusal it has declined to honour. Some of the best work in the field already does exactly that, and it is disproportionately the work that survives its decade.

## Hegemony on the shop floor: consent manufactured in the labour process

The hegemony concept's reach is best tested at small scale, and its most famous small scale application is Michael Burawoy's study of a Chicago machine shop, published in 1979 as Manufacturing Consent, whose title's echo of the media phrase was deliberate. Burawoy, working as a machine operator, asked why workers who were nobody's dupes, and who could describe their exploitation with precision, nevertheless worked with an intensity that served the firm's accumulation and obscured the surplus being extracted from them. His answer was neither deception nor discourse but organisation at the point of production: the labour process was arranged as a game, making out, in which operators pursued targets, beat rates, and competed for the small satisfactions of craft and seniority, and playing the game generated consent to the rules within which it was played, including the rule that the surplus went elsewhere. The game delivered real goods, relative autonomy, relief from boredom, a ladder of informal status, and those goods were the concessions in miniature, priced in effort and paid in the currency of the working day. Around the game stood the firm's internal state and internal labour market, the grievance procedures and promotion ladders that absorbed conflict into channels and made the settlement's institutions part of the workers' own biography.

The study matters to the comparison because it shows the three frameworks competing on a single shop floor with unusual clarity. An ideology reading of the same material would reconstruct the representations of fair rates and natural targets and grade them against the extraction they conceal, and its finding would be true as far as it went while missing the mechanism, since the operators often held the critical representations and the compliant conduct simultaneously. A discourse reading would map the categories the game made available, the good operator, the rate buster, the old hand, and the selves they offered, and its map would be illuminating while declining to say who the arrangement served. Burawoy's hegemony reading alone explains the conjunction the others split: knowledge without rebellion, criticism inside consent, because the consent was not to representations at all but to a game whose playing delivered goods and whose rules, once played under, reproduced the relations that made the game necessary. Critics have pressed the study on its generalisability, and its author's own later work revised its account of the firm's concessions under changed conditions, which is exactly the fate the framework predicts for settlements: repriced, renegotiated, and in places withdrawn.

## Ideology and science: the break, and the historian's counterattack

One inheritance from Althusser kept the ideology concept at war with itself for a generation, and a comparison that omits the quarrel understates what the first concept's users must defend. Althusser's architecture required a science of history standing outside ideology, and he named the passage from one to the other an epistemological break, borrowing the notion from the philosophy of science: Marx, on this account, broke with the ideological problematic of his youth and founded a new continent of knowledge, as chemistry had broken with alchemy. The claim gave ideology critique its strongest possible warrant, a certified outside, and its most vulnerable flank, since the certification procedure was the theory's own. If the break is vouched for by historical materialism and historical materialism is the break's product, the circle Foucault's first objection describes has closed with an audible click. Althusser himself, in later self criticism, conceded that the formulation had been theoreticist, an overvaluation of theory's autonomy, and softened the break into a tendency; the concession is a measure of the objection's force from inside the tradition.

The counterattack from the other wing of Marxism was as notable as the objection from outside it. E. P. Thompson, the historian of the English working class, devoted a long polemical book of 1978 to Althusser's system, defending experience, agency, and the historian's craft against what he saw as a structuralism that abolished subjects in theory while depending on them in politics. The quarrel matters here because it shows the subject question dividing the first framework against itself: Thompson's ideology is borne by experiencing subjects who test it against their lives and sometimes break it in struggle, while Althusser's subjects are the ideology's effects and can break nothing, the break being reserved to science. Foucault read the quarrel from outside both camps and drew the conclusion his method required, that the outside the two wings fought over, whether named experience or science, was the depth model's mirage in its two favourite costumes. A student who can stage that three cornered fight, Thompson's experience against Althusser's science against Foucault's refusal of both, has the comparison in its most concentrated form, and an analyst choosing the ideology concept in full knowledge of the fight will choose the chastened version described in this article, with its standpoint declared and its guarantees surrendered, because the unchastened versions are precisely the ones the fight destroyed.

## For students and exam candidates

This comparison appears across politics, sociology, media, and cultural studies syllabuses, and it is marked, everywhere, by the same standard: the candidate who treats the three terms as synonyms has failed before the argument begins. Learn the commitment table as five questions rather than as three definitions. What does the concept assume exists behind a representation? Where does its subject come from? Is class prior or constructed? What critique does it license? What can its user no longer say? Five questions, asked of each concept, will reconstruct any of the three under examination conditions more reliably than a memorised paragraph, because the questions generate the definitions instead of recalling them. Memorise Foucault's three objections as a numbered structure in prose: ideology presupposes a truth outside the field, refers to a subject, and stands secondary to a determining base. Then learn the entitlement test as your essay plan: state the sentence your answer needs to conclude with, choose the concept that entitles it, and declare the presupposition you have accepted. Examiners reward that declaration disproportionately, because it converts a comparison from a list into a judgement. For revision, keep the three frameworks on facing pages with their forbidden sentences written in the margin, and file your examples by question type rather than by topic: verdict examples with ideology, consent examples with hegemony, category examples with discourse. A structured notebook for the comparison, such as the [Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) companion in this series, is built for exactly that filing, and the habit of sorting by entitlement rather than by theme is the single most transferable skill this topic teaches.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach the triad as a dispute, not as a sequence. The standard progression, ideology first as the crude ancestor, hegemony as its refinement, discourse as its sophisticated successor, smuggles in a verdict and deprives students of the only exercise that matters, which is choosing. Open instead with the concluding sentences: put a falsity verdict, an anatomy of consent, and a map of a discursive field on the board, and let the seminar discover that no single concept on the syllabus entitles all three. The commitment table then arrives as the record of a problem the class has already felt. Two exercises repay their time reliably. The first gives groups the same short archive, a ministerial speech, a union bulletin, a advice column, and requires each group to analyse it under an assigned framework and then to list what its framework forbade it to say; the lists teach faster than the analyses. The second takes a published article that slides between frameworks and sets the audit as a hunt: find the sentence the introduction did not pay for. On Gramsci, resist the abridged consent only version and put the concessions requirement and the armour of coercion on the table in the first session, since a hegemony without a price and without a prison is the journalistic word with footnotes. On Foucault, teach the interview's three objections as the primary text; they are short, numbered in effect, and they end the gavotte in which students attribute to him a general suspicion of ideas.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, the comparison is a matter of declaring foundations before the archive chooses them for you. The three frameworks constitute different evidence: texts as testimony about a world beyond them, texts as moves in an organisational struggle, texts as the surface of a regime. A research design that has not decided which of those its sources are will decide accidentally, paragraph by paragraph, and its conclusions will inherit the incoherence. State the entitlement in the design: the sentence the project exists to earn, and the presupposition it accepts, and let referees argue with a visible commitment rather than a shifting one. Historians face the same choice in period dress, since the sources of any episode will support a verdict, an anatomy, or a map, and the historiography of most episodes contains all three competing under the flags of rival schools. Journalists meet the vocabulary at its most degraded and can perform a public service by declining the degradation: hegemony in commentary almost always means dominance with footnotes removed, and a reporter who asks the four Gramscian questions, whose consent, which institutions, what concessions, where is the coercion in reserve, will produce a better story than the headline the word was borrowed to decorate. When the four questions have no answers, print dominance, which is an honest word, and leave the notebooks to those prepared to do their work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between ideology and hegemony?

Ideology concerns representations and their relation to reality: whether a belief distorts or partially expresses the real relations in which its holders stand, and whose interest the distortion serves. Hegemony concerns leadership and its organisation: how a class or alliance secures the active consent of subordinate groups through institutions and genuine concessions, and how that consent is maintained or lost. The two concepts belong to one tradition and share a vocabulary of class and interest, but they answer different questions. Ideology looks at contents and grades them; hegemony looks at settlements and prices them. An order can therefore be hegemonic while its representations are largely accurate, and a representation can be ideological in a society where no stable hegemony exists, which is why neither term can do the other's work.

### Q: What is the difference between ideology and discourse?

Ideology presupposes a reality independent of its representations, against which a representation can be measured and found distorting; the concept therefore licenses verdicts of falsity and explanations by interest. Discourse, in Foucault's sense, refuses that presupposition: a discourse is a regulated system of statements that constitutes its objects and its subjects, and truth is an effect produced inside such systems rather than a standard outside them. The practical consequence is a difference in what an analysis may conclude. The ideology analyst can say that a field of statements misdescribes the world and serves an order by doing so. The discourse analyst can show how the field organises what can be said, known, and done, but cannot call the field false without abandoning the method. Construction and distortion are the two verbs, and choosing between them is choosing between the concepts.

### Q: Why did Foucault reject the concept of ideology?

Foucault gave three explicit reasons in an interview from the second half of the 1970s. First, the concept of ideology stands in necessary opposition to something else that is supposed to count as truth, and his interest lay in how effects of truth are produced within discourses rather than in measuring statements against a truth held in reserve. Second, the concept refers to a subject, a consciousness that bears the distortion, while his work treated subjects as effects of discourse and power rather than as their origin. Third, ideology stands in a secondary position relative to a determining infrastructure, an ordering of base and superstructure that his histories of the clinic, the asylum, and the prison had consistently refused. The three objections are methodological, not rhetorical: each names a presupposition his archaeology and genealogy were built to work without.

### Q: Is hegemony a form of ideology?

Only in the loosest usage, and the looseness costs precision. In Gramsci's construction, a hegemonic project certainly includes a conception of the world, elaborated by organic intellectuals and diffused through the institutions of civil society, and that component resembles what the tradition calls ideology. But hegemony names the whole organisation of leadership: the conception, the institutions that carry it, the concessions that purchase allegiance to it, and the coercion held in reserve behind it. Reducing hegemony to a form of ideology keeps the component and drops the organisation, the price, and the armour, which are the parts of the concept that explain stability and collapse. The more accurate statement is that ideology, in the sense of a lived conception of the world, is one element inside a hegemonic formation, and that Gramsci's own usage treats the two terms at different levels of analysis.

### Q: Which of these concepts is most useful for analysing politics?

The question has no framework free answer, and the entitlement test explains why: usefulness is relative to the sentence the analysis needs to conclude with. For a verdict on whether a representation distorts a describable reality in the service of an interest, ideology is the instrument and the others cannot substitute. For an account of why an order endures, how its consent is organised and priced, and where it might crack, hegemony does work neither rival attempts. For an account of how a field of knowledge made a kind of person, problem, or intervention possible, discourse is unmatched. Politics contains all three kinds of question, often about the same events, which is why the concepts persist side by side in the literature. The disciplined answer is therefore procedural: choose by the question, declare the presupposition, and resist the prestige of whichever term is fashionable in the discipline asking.

### Q: Do these three concepts assume a truth behind appearances?

Ideology does: its whole operation depends on a real relation behind the representation, which the representation distorts or partially reveals, and which the analyst claims to describe more adequately. Hegemony, in Gramsci's version, does not require that assumption in the same form; what stands behind consent is not a hidden truth but an organisation, its institutions, and the concessions it pays, all of which are empirically investigable without a depth model of truth. Discourse refuses the assumption outright: for Foucault there is no truth behind the field of appearances that the analyst could occupy, since truth is produced within regimes of statements and every standpoint, the analyst's included, is internal to some regime. The three answers to this single question, yes, not necessarily, and no, are the fastest reliable way to sort the concepts, and they generate most of the other rows of the commitment table as consequences.

### Q: What did Laclau and Mouffe change?

In Hegemony and Socialist Strategy, published in 1985, Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe kept Gramsci's concept of hegemony and removed its anchoring in class. Gramsci's collective will remained tied, in the last analysis, to a fundamental class whose position in production preceded its politics. Laclau and Mouffe argued that social positions, class positions included, are not given prior to politics but are constituted within discursive articulation, as demands and identities are linked into chains that no economy dictated. The change propagates through the whole framework: behind representations there is no longer an anchoring interest, the subject of hegemony is any collective will that articulation succeeds in composing, and betrayal loses its meaning since there is no prior interest to betray. The position, named post-Marxism, is the point at which the hegemony tradition and the discourse tradition merge, at the price of the class foundation both had inherited.

### Q: Can you use hegemony without Marxism?

The word travels freely and is used in international relations for the preponderance of a leading state and in commentary for any dominance a writer wishes to dignify; those usages answer to their own literatures. Gramsci's concept, used with its content intact, cannot be detached so easily, because its content includes the anchoring: classes or alliances capable of leadership, the institutions of civil society, concessions that cost the leader something real, and the coercive state standing behind the settlement. A borrowing that keeps the mechanism and declares the modification, analysing the hegemony of an orthodoxy within a profession, say, with schools substituted for classes and appointments for concessions, is legitimate and often illuminating. A borrowing that keeps only the word is not a use of the concept but of its prestige. The test is whether the writer can name the leader, the led, the concessions, and the institutions.

### Q: Which concept do media scholars prefer?

The field has preferred each in turn, and all three remain in live use because media research asks all three kinds of question. The earliest Marxist media analysis worked with ideology, unmasking representations against the realities they were held to distort, and produced durable work on selection and framing alongside the depth model's difficulties about the analyst's own standpoint. The cultural studies wave associated with Birmingham and with Stuart Hall worked with hegemony, replacing the passive audience with negotiated settlements over the definitions of situations. Discourse analysis then reframed the object itself, asking how news routines constitute the categories, the criminal, the taxpayer, the expert, within which reporting becomes possible. A media scholar's preference is therefore best read as a declaration of which question the scholar takes to be primary, and examination answers gain marks by making that declaration explicit.

### Q: Are these three concepts rivals or complements?

They are rivals as foundations and complements as instruments, and confusing the two relations is the commonest error in the literature. As foundations they are total accounts of the relation between meaning and power, and their answers to the commitment table's questions exclude one another: a representation either can be measured against an independent real or it cannot, and class positions either precede their articulation or are constituted within it. As instruments they can serve successive phases of one inquiry, a map of a field, an anatomy of the settlement that stabilised part of it, a verdict on one representation within the settlement, provided the transitions are marked and each phase's presuppositions are accepted for that phase's work. What is not available is the layered synthesis in which all three operate simultaneously as aspects of a single analysis, since at every load bearing question the layers contradict.

### Q: How does hegemony differ from ideology in what it asks the analyst to look for?

Ideology directs the analyst toward contents and their credentials: the propositions, images, and narratives in circulation, the evidence for and against them, the erasures and naturalisations they perform, and the interests those performances serve. Its characteristic documents are texts read against a described reality. Hegemony directs the analyst toward organisations and their transactions: the institutions through which consent is built, the intellectuals who staff them, the concessions recorded in budgets, statutes, and settlements, the coercion held in reserve, and the points where the equilibrium strains. Its characteristic documents are archives of negotiation, membership, and expenditure read as the price list of allegiance. An analyst following the first direction can complete the work without knowing who paid for anything; an analyst following the second can complete it without grading a single belief as true or false. The objects of attention barely overlap, which is the practical meaning of the claim that the concepts are instruments rather than synonyms.

### Q: What should a student check before treating discourse as a synonym for ideology?

Four checks, each quick and each decisive. First, the truth check: does the intended analysis need to call a representation false? If so, discourse will not carry the conclusion, since the method renounces the standpoint a falsity verdict requires. Second, the object check: is the claim that an object is described badly, or that it is constituted by the field that describes it? The first is ideology's claim, the second discourse's, and they cannot both be the thesis. Third, the subject check: does the analysis presuppose bearers of belief whose interests explain their beliefs, or does it treat those bearers as products of the field? Fourth, the vocabulary check: scan the draft for the verbs. Distorts, masks, and naturalises belong to one framework; constitutes, produces, and renders thinkable belong to the other. A draft that mixes the verb sets has mixed the frameworks, whatever its introduction declares.

### Q: Why does Foucault resist the concept of ideology?

Behind the three stated objections lies a single intellectual commitment that explains the resistance's depth: Foucault's histories were built to show how what counts as truth, knowledge, and even the human subject are made within practices, and the concept of ideology presupposes at every point exactly what those histories set out to make questionable. A truth exempt from the analysis, a subject prior to its formation, an economy prior to its representations: accept any of the three and the histories lose their object, since the asylum's knowledge, the clinic's gaze, and the prison's disciplines become secondary reflections of something more real elsewhere. The resistance is therefore not a preference for a fresher word but a defence of a research programme, and it is consistently maintained across the interviews and the books. Critics may judge the programme's price too high; the reply section of this article weighs their case. What cannot be maintained is that the refusal was casual.

### Q: Which concept best explains consent that survives open disagreement?

Hegemony, and the case is its home ground. Where subordinate groups criticise an order fluently and reproduce it daily, the ideology model must split the phenomenon into a residue of deception and a crack of awakening, without a principle for ordering the two, while discourse analysis describes the licensed forms the criticism itself takes without explaining the allegiance underneath. Hegemony orders the case by treating consent as an exchange: the led receive concessions they value, security, income, recognition, goods delivered through institutions they partly staff, and their criticism functions as monitoring of the exchange rather than as its repudiation. The explanation generates a prediction the others do not: when the concessions deteriorate or their price rises beyond the leading group's capacity, the same criticism becomes available for counter organisation. Consent that survives disagreement is thus neither false consciousness nor discursive positioning but a priced and revocable allegiance, watched at its seams.

### Q: What does each framework lose when the three are merged into one word?

Each loses the entitlement that made it worth having. Ideology loses its verdict: merged into a general word for meaning and power, it can no longer say that a representation is false and that its falsity serves an interest, because the merged term carries discourse's renunciation of the standpoint such a verdict needs. Hegemony loses its price list: merged, it becomes a synonym for dominance or influence, and the concessions, institutions, and coercive reserve that explain why consent holds and when it fails drop out of the analysis. Discourse loses its refusal: merged, it becomes a fashionable word for talk or ideas, and its claim that fields of statements constitute their objects and subjects dissolves into the claim that language is slanted, which nobody needed Foucault to learn. The merger therefore does not add the three strengths; it subtracts them, leaving a term vague enough to conclude nothing in particular with great theoretical confidence.
