## A contract that asked for nothing in return

In the early months of 1809, in a Vienna living under the shadow of another French invasion, three aristocrats put their names to a document unlike any other in the history of European music. Between them they promised a composer four thousand florins a year, payable in quarterly instalments, for as long as he remained in Vienna or in the Austrian hereditary lands. The contract named no duties. It required no symphonies, no operas, no teaching, no attendance at any court, no dedication of any score. It did not even require him to compose at all. It simply undertook to pay him to exist where he was, so that a rival offer from a foreign court would lose its power over him, and it stands at the centre of the Beethoven life and career because it converted the oldest bargain in music, service in exchange for bread, into something new: a settlement negotiated between a working musician and his patrons, in which the musician held the stronger hand.

The three signatories were Archduke Rudolph, the youngest brother of the Emperor and a pupil of the composer, who undertook fifteen hundred florins; Prince Ferdinand Kinsky, who undertook eighteen hundred; and Prince Franz Joseph Lobkowitz, who undertook seven hundred. The composer was Ludwig van Beethoven, thirty eight years old, already losing his hearing, and already in receipt of an offer from Kassel that would have made him a salaried Kapellmeister in the ordinary way. He chose the annuity instead, and the choice defines the career this article follows. The thesis of the account is that Beethoven was the first composer to make a living from a negotiated settlement with the aristocracy rather than employment by it, and that the independence produced by that settlement is the precondition for everything later described as his heroism. The heroism has been written about exhaustively. The negotiation has not, and it came first.

That order matters because the familiar story runs the other way. In the familiar story a titanic personality bursts the bonds of patronage by force of character, and the money is an embarrassment to be hurried past. The documents tell a slower and more interesting story. A boy raised inside a court chapel learned exactly what service cost and what it paid. A young pianist in Vienna learned what the aristocracy would compete for and how to make it compete. A man in his thirties, facing the loss of the sense his trade depended on, converted reputation into a written income with the care of a lawyer. None of this diminishes the music. It explains the conditions under which the music became possible, and it explains why the career, examined closely, looks less like an escape from the patronage system than like its most sophisticated product. Beethoven did not leave the old world of courts and chapels behind him. He renegotiated his place inside it, clause by clause, until the place no longer resembled a post.

## Bonn, the court chapel and a working musician's household

Bonn in 1770 was the residence of the Elector of Cologne, and its court maintained a chapel and an orchestra of respectable quality for a small capital. Music there was not an ornament laid over the machinery of government. It was part of the machinery, performed daily in the chapel and regularly in the court theatre, and the families who supplied it lived in a recognised stratum of court servants: salaried, housed near their work, and dependent on the Elector's favour for every improvement in their condition. Into that stratum the van Beethoven family had risen one generation before the composer's birth. His grandfather, also named Ludwig, had come from the Austrian Netherlands as a young singer, risen to the post of Kapellmeister at the Bonn court, and died at the end of 1773 with a solid local reputation and a modest property. His son Johann was a tenor in the court chapel, a competent musician whose gifts were narrower than his father's and whose career stalled at the level of the rank and file. The wider world of such courts, in which a chapel appointment was at once a livelihood, a status and a ceiling, is mapped in the series survey of [the history of classical composers](/2010/03/07/history-of-classical-composers/), and Bonn was a small but complete example of it.

The household into which Ludwig van Beethoven was baptised on 17 December 1770 was therefore a working musician's household in the precise sense. Income arrived as salary and as the small extras of court service. Status depended on continued favour. The mother, Maria Magdalena, came from a family of court servants at Ehrenbreitstein and brought steadiness to a home that her husband's drinking increasingly undermined. Seven children were born, of whom three sons survived infancy: Ludwig, the eldest surviving child, Caspar Carl, born in 1774, and Nikolaus Johann, born in 1776. The rooms the family occupied were ordinary court lodgings, and the instruments in them were tools of the trade rather than furniture. A child in that house learned music the way a child in a baker's house learns bread: by proximity, by routine, and by being put to work early.

His first teachers were his father and the court's own personnel. Johann van Beethoven taught him the clavier and the violin with an insistence that contemporaries remembered as severity, and the boy was also placed with court musicians including the organist Giles van den Eeden and the singer and actor Tobias Pfeiffer, who lodged for a time in the household. The pattern of these lessons matters more than any single anecdote about them. He was being trained by practitioners, inside a working institution, to professional standards and professional deadlines: feast days did not wait for inspiration, and neither did the Elector's theatre. When the boy later astonished Vienna, what he carried from Bonn was not only talent. It was the craftsman's assumption that music is a job done on schedule, an assumption his father's household and his grandfather's chapel had in common, and one he never lost even when his income ceased to depend on schedules at all.

## The father, the lessons and the prodigy attempt that failed

Johann van Beethoven had watched the Mozart children tour Europe a few years before his own son's birth, and the lesson he drew from their success was commercial. A prodigy was a marketable wonder. A court musician with a stalled career and a growing family could reasonably see in a gifted son the family's route to a better income, and Johann set out to manufacture the wonder on the Mozart model. The attempt produced the first documented fact of the composer's career, and it is a falsification. In March 1778 a concert bill in Cologne advertised the seven year old Ludwig van Beethoven as a child of six. The age printed on that bill was wrong, wrong in the direction that made the performance more remarkable, and wrong with the father's knowledge. Beethoven himself carried uncertainty about his birth year into adult life as a consequence, at times believing himself younger than he was. The career that later generations would load with meanings about truthfulness and moral stature begins, in the surviving record, with a small printed lie told to sell tickets.

The first revelation of this biography is worth holding plainly: the prodigy attempt failed. The boy did not tour. No foreign court showered the family with gifts, no publisher rushed his juvenilia into print across Europe, and within a few years the enterprise was quietly redirected from exhibition to apprenticeship. The failure was partly a matter of timing, since the market for child wonders had cooled since the Mozart tours, and partly a matter of the boy himself, whose gifts were real but whose temperament, even as a child, was remembered as stubborn and inward rather than charming. What replaced the exhibition tour was better. Christian Gottlob Neefe, the court organist and the most intellectually serious musician in Bonn, became his teacher in composition and his advocate in print. It was Neefe who reported in a musical journal in 1783 that his pupil, then described as eleven, played the clavier with skill and power and might become a second Mozart if he continued as he had begun. The comparison was a teacher's promotional kindness, and Beethoven remembered the debt accurately all his life, writing to Neefe years later to say that if he became a great man, Neefe would share the credit.

The father's severity, meanwhile, left evidence of a kind the legend rarely quotes. Members of the Bonn circle who knew the household, including the baker's son Gottfried Fischer, whose family lived in the same building, recalled practice enforced late into the night and a child weeping at the keyboard. Such recollections were written down long afterwards and carry the softening and sharpening of memory, and the article treats them as the testimony they are rather than as transcript. Even discounted, they establish the household's emotional economy: the son's talent was the family's capital, and capital was not allowed to rest. Here the first of this article's open questions takes hold. A musician formed in a household where his gift was treated as a trade asset, who learned in childhood exactly what patrons pay for and what they expect in return, would one day be handed a contract that demanded nothing at all: no duties, no deliveries, no attendance. What does a composer do with a contract that demands nothing? The question opens here, in the Bonn childhood that taught him the price of everything, and it does not close until the annuity years themselves, sixteen sections on, when the uses he found for unconditional income can be weighed against the conditions that produced him.

## What the Bonn orchestra taught him

In 1784 the fourteen year old Beethoven was appointed assistant court organist, a salaried post under Neefe, and he was soon playing viola in the electoral orchestra as well. The appointment is easy to pass over as a biographical milestone, but its real significance is educational. A court orchestra in a residence city was a school with a punishing timetable. It played opera in the theatre, sacred music in the chapel, and concert music at court, and a boy seated inside it absorbed the whole working repertory of the late eighteenth century from the inside: Italian opera buffa and opera seria, French works in translation, symphonies by Haydn and by the Mannheim generation, concertos in which he might one evening accompany and another evening listen. He learned what an oboe can carry and what it cannot, what a horn section does to the balance of a hall, how a singer breathes and where a string section runs out of bow. These are lessons no treatise teaches, and they are audible in everything he later wrote.

The orchestra also taught him the social grammar of the profession. Its members were servants of the Elector, ranked, uniformed and paid by the treasury, and its best players doubled as the city's teachers, copyists and concert promoters. Around the institution stood families like the Ries family, whose head Franz Ries led the orchestra, and the Romberg cousins, violinist and cellist, who were near contemporaries of Beethoven and remained benchmarks for him. The young Anton Reicha, later a theorist of consequence, was a flautist in the same band and a friend. A boy does not need to be told, in such company, that music is an economy of favours, reputations and obligations. He watches it work.

The orchestra's teaching was also, in the strictest sense, compositional. A violist sits inside the harmony, playing the inner voices that carry the logic of a movement while carrying none of its tunes, and a boy who spent his formative years on that desk learned harmony as a physical sensation in the fingers and the bow arm before he learned it as theory. He also learned the theatre's repertory from the pit upward: the Bonn company under its ambitious Elector performed Mozart's operas within a few years of their premieres, together with works by Dittersdorf, Paisiello and the French school, and the assistant organist who rehearsed singers at the keyboard absorbed the craft of dramatic pacing, of the accompanied recitative and the ensemble finale, at the age when absorption is permanent. When his own operatic ambitions surfaced in Vienna, they were built on pit experience no conservatory of the day could have supplied, because no conservatory of the day had an opera house attached.

Bonn added one more influence that the chapel could not supply. The household of the widow von Breuning took the awkward adolescent in as a piano teacher to her children and gave him something his own home lacked: books, conversation, and a family in which learning was valued for its own sake. Through the von Breunings he met Franz Wegeler, a medical student who became a lifelong friend and, decades later, one of the two authors of the first biographical notices of him. Through the same circle he encountered the ideas of the German Enlightenment in their Bonn form, serious, moral and literary. When he left for Vienna he carried two educations, the orchestra's and the salon's, and the tension between them, craftsman and intellectual, servant's son and votary of Schiller, never resolved itself in him. It powered him instead.

## The patrons who sent him to Vienna

The decision that Beethoven should study in Vienna was made by Bonn, not by Beethoven. The Elector Maximilian Franz, youngest brother of the Emperor Joseph the Second and a genuine musical amateur, had reorganised the Bonn court theatre and chapel with unusual ambition, and he understood that his most promising young musician needed a larger stage and a greater teacher. In 1787 he sent the sixteen year old to Vienna, where the boy is reported to have played for Mozart and received a guarded encouragement that later retelling inflated beyond recognition. The journey collapsed within weeks. His mother was dying of consumption, and he hurried home to find the household disintegrating: Maria Magdalena died in July 1787, an infant sister followed, and Johann van Beethoven's drinking, already a problem, became the family's governing catastrophe. The son who should have been studying counterpoint in Vienna spent the next five years holding a Bonn household together, petitioning the Elector, successfully, for half of his incapacitated father's salary to be paid directly to himself for the care of his younger brothers. The future composer of heroic individualism was formed as a teenage head of household, in paperwork, debt and negotiation with authority. The pattern is worth naming early, because the rest of the career repeats it.

Those five years in Bonn, from 1787 to 1792, were not a waiting room. He played viola in the orchestra through its seasons, taught piano in the von Breuning house, composed the two cantatas on the death of Joseph the Second and the accession of Leopold the Second that were Bonn's official musical responses to imperial events, and was sent in 1790 to meet Haydn when the older master passed through the city on his way to London, an encounter arranged by the court as plainly as a job interview. The Elector's investment had a portfolio logic any modern institution would recognise: retain the asset's salary line, fund its finishing abroad, and bind it by gratitude and by the family it leaves behind. That the asset's family was disintegrating, that the father's decline required the son's remittances and the son's vigilance in roughly equal measure, only tightened the binding. He left Bonn as its orchestra's product and its chapel's pensioner, with a widowed household's debts behind him and its expectations on his back.

The second departure, in November 1792, was Bonn's considered investment. The Elector granted leave with continued partial salary arrangements behind him, Haydn, passing through Bonn on his way back from London that summer, had seen the young man's cantata work and agreed to take him as a pupil, and the circle of friends marked the leaving with a farewell album. Count Ferdinand Waldstein, the most musical of his Bonn patrons and the one who had done most to press his case, wrote in that album the famous prediction that through unremitting diligence he would receive the spirit of Mozart from the hands of Haydn. The sentence has been quoted ever since as prophecy. In context it is something plainer and more useful: a patron's memorandum of what the investment was for. Bonn was not exporting a prodigy. Bonn was sending its orchestra's most valuable product to the capital to be finished by the capital's leading master, with the network of Rhineland aristocracy commending him to the salons where Viennese reputations were made. Four days after he left, French troops were advancing on the Rhineland and the world that had produced him began to dissolve. His father died that December. Beethoven never lived in Bonn again, and the Bonn that had trained him ceased, within a few years, to exist as a court at all.

## Arrival in Vienna and the lessons with Haydn

The Vienna he entered in November 1792 was the musical capital of Europe and a city running on aristocratic music making. A small corps of great houses maintained private orchestras or quartet parties, subscribed to concerts, employed Kapellmeister, and treated a new pianist of brilliance as a social prize. The city Mozart had left the year before his death was still organised around his absence, and its salons were alert for a successor, which is the context in which [the Vienna decade of the Mozart life and career](/2010/10/21/wolfgang-amadeus-mozart-life-and-career/) should be read alongside this one: two careers in the same rooms, a generation apart, one ending as the other began. Beethoven arrived with a technique from the Bonn opera orchestra, a suitcase of unpublished manuscripts, and introductions to the households that mattered, among them Prince Karl Lichnowsky, an ardent Mozart partisan, who took him into his own house and treated him from the first as an artist to be protected rather than a servant to be used.

The Haydn lessons began at once and occupied the centre of his formal study. They were, by the standards of the time, expensive, and they were conducted on the older master's terms: exercises in counterpoint on the Fuxian model, corrected in sessions between Haydn's own composing and the preparations for his second London journey. The surviving exercise books show the pupil working through species counterpoint and fugue with uneven care, and show, too, corrections that were never made and errors that passed undetected. Beethoven drew his own conclusion and acted on it in a way that reveals the man entire: rather than confront the most powerful musician in Europe, he secretly took his exercises to Johann Schenk, a workmanlike Viennese composer and a gifted teacher, and had them corrected properly before Haydn saw them. The deception preserved the patronage and got the education. It also fixed a lifelong ambivalence. Haydn was the gate through which he had entered Vienna, the name on his first dedication, and the standard against which the city measured him, and Beethoven spent the rest of the decade alternately honouring that debt and chafing under it.

His other studies filled the gaps the Haydn arrangement left. When Haydn departed for London at the start of 1794, Beethoven went openly to Johann Georg Albrechtsberger, the city's leading counterpoint teacher, for a rigorous course that lasted into 1795, and he took instruction in vocal writing from Antonio Salieri, whose counsel on setting Italian text he sought intermittently for years. The list is instructive. He assembled his own education from the best specialist available for each component, paid for it himself, and owed no single master the whole of it. The Bonn apprentice who had been manufactured as a prodigy and then reprocessed as an orchestral craftsman ended his training as something neither place had planned: a self managing professional who had learned, among other things, never to let one patron, one teacher or one contract define the whole of his position.

## Why the Haydn lessons went badly

The lessons went badly for structural reasons before they went badly for personal ones. Haydn in 1793 was the most famous living composer, committed to London, composing the works of his last great period, and in no position to give a demanding pupil the slow daily attention that counterpoint exercises require. Beethoven, for his part, was twenty two, already formed by a decade of professional playing, and convinced, not without evidence, that the exercises he submitted came back with their faults uncorrected. Each man behaved as his situation dictated. The failure was in the fit between a master whose teaching was a sideline and a pupil who needed a craftsman instructor and had been promised a monument. Later biographers, eager for a drama of succession, sharpened the story into a rivalry between generations. The documentary record supports something cooler: a business arrangement that under delivered, quietly supplemented by both parties, since Haydn continued to present his pupil to the public and Beethoven continued to advertise the connection, most visibly in the dedication of his first opus.

One episode is usually made to bear more meaning than it can carry. Ferdinand Ries, who became Beethoven's pupil and assistant in the following decade and published his recollections jointly with Wegeler in 1838, reports that Haydn advised against publishing the third of the Opus 1 trios, the C minor, which he thought would not be understood or well received, and that Beethoven suspected the advice of jealousy. Ries is a valuable witness and a loyal one, and the anecdote should be read as his account rather than as a transcript of the room. What is certain is narrower. The trio was published, it was understood, and it became one of the most admired of the set. The young composer had begun to trust his own judgement of an audience over the judgement of the most experienced composer alive, and the audience proved him right. That pattern, deference in form and self reliance in substance, describes the Haydn relationship better than any story of a quarrel. There is no record of an open break. There are dedications offered and accepted, encounters in later years conducted with public respect, and a private reserve on the younger man's side that he extended, at one time or another, to nearly everyone who had ever taught him anything.

The deeper cause of the friction is worth stating, because it explains the shape of the career that followed. Beethoven required from a teacher something the eighteenth century rarely supplied: not a master to imitate but a technician to interrogate. His surviving exercises are covered with questions. He wanted to know why rules held, where they bent, and what a given prohibition was protecting, and Albrechtsberger, who gave him rules in abundance, satisfied him no better in the end than Haydn had. The education that actually formed him was completed in private, in the study of scores: Handel, Bach, Mozart and Haydn himself, copied, analysed and argued with in the sketchbooks. The lessons failed, insofar as they failed, because the student had already become his own institution, and the annuity contract of 1809, which paid him to answer to no one, merely gave that inner fact an outer legal form.

## The pianist first, the composer second

Vienna heard Beethoven at the keyboard for years before it formed an opinion of him as a composer, and the second revelation of this article is that the order of those reputations matters. He arrived as a pianist of the most formidable kind: a player whose improvisations, by the testimony of trained musicians who heard them, moved listeners in ways his written music had not yet attempted. Carl Czerny, who heard him as a child and later became his pupil, remembered playing of overwhelming power and of a singing legato that struck him as a new thing in the world. The salons competed for him. Prince Lichnowsky installed him in his household; the Friday morning quartet parties at the Lichnowsky palace, where the young Schuppanzigh played, put the newest chamber music in front of him weekly; Count Razumovsky and Prince Lobkowitz drew him into their musical establishments. His public debut as a soloist came in the spring of 1795, playing one of his own piano concertos at the Burgtheater, and by then his standing among the aristocracy was already secure enough that publishers were negotiating for works the public had barely heard.

The economics of that standing deserve precision, because they explain the annuity later. A fashionable pianist in 1790s Vienna earned from teaching the daughters of great houses, from dedications rewarded with gifts and annuities of favour, from salon appearances that were paid in kind and in credit, and from publication, which for most composers was the smallest item of the four. Beethoven played the system with a clear head. He accepted support and returned it in dedications and in music for named occasions. He also, from the beginning, refused the final implication of the system, which was residence and obedience. He quarrelled with Lichnowsky over a perceived slight to his status and walked out of the prince's country house in 1806, leaving behind the famous defiance, reported by Ries, that there were and would be thousands of princes but only one Beethoven. The sentence may be polished by its reporter. The walk was real, and so was its meaning: he had calculated, correctly, that his reputation was now a form of property that no single patron could withdraw.

As a pianist he belongs in the front rank of the instrument's history on the evidence of witnesses rather than of recordings, and the series survey of [the greatest pianists of all time](/2025/05/07/greatest-pianists-of-all-time/) weighs that evidence in its own terms. What matters for the life is the consequence. The pianist's reputation was the capital on which the composer's career was financed. It bought him the years in which the early masterpieces could be written without a Kapellmeister's deadlines, it gave him his leverage with publishers, and it made the threat to leave Vienna in 1808 credible enough to be worth four thousand florins a year to avert. When the hearing failed, it was this capital, and not any employer's loyalty, that he lived on.

## Improvisation contests and the aristocratic salon

The Viennese salon ran on contest. Pianists were measured against each other in improvisation as singers were measured in opera houses, and a young virtuoso arriving in the city was expected to defend himself. Beethoven's early years are punctuated by these trials, and each of them is a small document in the economics of reputation. The Abbé Joseph Gelinek, a fashionable pianist and a shrewd judge, was brought to meet the newcomer and reported to his patron that he had found a devil of a player who improvised on a given theme in a manner he had not heard even from Mozart. The compliment, preserved because it was worth repeating, did practical work: it priced the new pianist in the only currency the salons traded in. A decade later the stakes were formalised in his celebrated contest with Daniel Steibelt, a touring virtuoso of real gifts and theatrical habits, who challenged him at a soirée in the house of Count Fries in 1800. Ries, who was present in the circle if not in the room, describes Beethoven seizing a cello part from Steibelt's own music, placing it upside down on the desk, and improvising a devastating set of variations on its inverted theme. Steibelt left Vienna and would not perform in a city where Beethoven was present again.

Such stories survive because they were told by partisans, and the modern reader should discount the choreography. What does not need discounting is the institution they reveal. Improvisation was the one arena in which a composer pianist demonstrated capacities that notation could not convey and a rival could not fake: invention in real time, memory, structural command, and nerve. Beethoven's dominance in that arena was total enough that by the early 1800s the contests stopped, because there was no longer a challenger whose reputation could survive the attempt. His rivalry with Joseph Wölfl, a Mozart pupil of genuine brilliance, was conducted instead as a running comparison between salons, with partisans on each side and the honours, by general consent, divided between Wölfl's polish and Beethoven's power.

The salon also imposed obligations that the legend of the untamed genius prefers to omit. He taught, because teaching paid and because pupils in great houses were channels of influence; his pupils included Archduke Rudolph, who became a serious composer and his most durable patron, the young Ferdinand Ries, and, briefly and stormily, Carl Czerny. He played at command in houses whose politics he despised. He composed dances, variations on fashionable tunes, and settings of Scottish and Irish folk melodies for the Edinburgh publisher George Thomson, hackwork by any elevated standard, undertaken for fees and continued for years. The annuity did not free him from the market. It freed him from the worst terms the market offered, and the difference between those two conditions is the subject of his middle career.

## The first publications and the business of selling music

Beethoven understood print earlier than most composers because print had been attempted on him in childhood. The three piano sonatas published at Speyer in 1783 with a dedication to the Elector, the so called Kurfürsten sonatas, were a Bonn court's advertisement for its prodigy, and the boy who appeared on their title page as eleven years old, two years younger than the truth, learned what a title page could do. In Vienna he proceeded as a principal, not as a product. He withheld his first opus until 1795, though he had works ready sooner, and then issued the three piano trios as Opus 1 with a dedication to Lichnowsky and a subscription list that canvassed the aristocracy in advance. The manoeuvre announced that his music was an investment to be subscribed to, not a servant's offering to be rewarded. Haydn's reported misgiving about the C minor trio belongs to this moment: the set was a bid for standing, and it succeeded.

Publication, for him, was never merely income. A printed work fixed a text, staked a claim in the republic of musicians across Europe, and advertised the composer to patrons he would never meet. How that text was built, the layering of voices and the expansion of inherited forms that publishers were being asked to sell, is the territory of the companion article on [Beethoven's musical style explained](/2011/01/21/ludwig-van-beethoven-musical-style-explained/), and it is compressed here deliberately. The biographical point is the strategy. He learned the trade's geography, which houses commanded which markets: Artaria and later Haslinger in Vienna, Breitkopf und Härtel in Leipzig, Simrock in Bonn, Clementi and his successors in London, the Paris houses beyond them. He learned that copyright in music was a patchwork of local privileges rather than a European right, and that the same composition could therefore be sold several times in several countries to several publishers, each buying exclusivity only within his own territory. The apprentice of the Bonn chapel had become a rights holder, and he behaved like one: hard, litigious about terms, and perpetually convinced, sometimes rightly, that he was being cheated.

The early publications also built the platform on which his public career as a composer was launched. His benefit concert at the Burgtheater in April 1800, the first academy devoted to his own works, presented the First Symphony and the Septet to a Vienna that already knew him as a pianist and was now required to reclassify him. The Septet became so popular that its fame annoyed him for years. The reclassification is the hinge of the decade: the pianist first, the composer second, and then, as the hearing failed and the playing receded, the composer only, on terms he had arranged in advance. Few musicians in history have managed their own succession from performer to author with comparable foresight, and none of it was foresight of the mystical kind. It was business judgement, formed in Bonn, sharpened in the salons, and recorded in a correspondence that grows more commercial, and more irritable, with every year that passes.

## The onset of hearing loss

### When did Beethoven become deaf?

He was never deaf at a stroke, and the question only makes sense as a process. The first symptoms, ringing in the ears and difficulty with high and distant sounds, appear in his own account in his late twenties. The loss deepened across two decades, through ear trumpets and written conversation, toward near total silence.

The progression, not any single year, is the fact to hold: buzzing and roaring that he dated to about 1796 or 1798, ear trumpets from the middle of the 1810s, written conversation books from 1818, and at the end almost nothing of speech or music caught by ear at all.

The documentary anchor for the onset is his own correspondence. In letters written in the summer of 1801 to two friends, Franz Wegeler in Bonn and Karl Amenda, he confessed what he had hidden for years: his hearing had been weakening for about three years, his ears sang and roared day and night, and in the theatre he could no longer catch the high notes of instruments and voices unless he was close. He described the social consequence with precision. In company he appeared absent minded or misanthropic because he could not hear what was said to him, and he could not bring himself to announce to the world that the one sense a musician could least spare was failing him. The letters are remarkable less for their despair than for their bookkeeping. He lists doctors consulted, treatments attempted, small improvements and relapses, with the methodical frustration of a man auditing an asset that keeps depreciating.

Here the second open question of the article takes hold. If the hearing loss began in his late twenties, and was established and worsening by 1801, how did he keep performing in public for some fifteen more years? He gave concerts, premiered concertos, played chamber music with the finest players in Vienna, and conducted, through the whole of the period in which the legend prefers to picture him already sealed in silence. The answer is distributed across the working adaptations of the next sections, and its final instalment, the last public performance as a player, does not arrive until section 24. What can be said at the outset is that the loss, in its first decade, was a professional secret managed by professional means: he chose repertoire and rooms, watched lips and faces, leaned on colleagues who knew, and withdrew by stages from the improvisation contests and salon duties where an unpredictable exchange might expose him. The deafness of the legend is a wall that fell in a day. The deafness of the record is a tide, and he worked in front of it for as long as work was possible.

The medical cause was never established in his lifetime and has resisted confident retrospective diagnosis ever since; the theories offered across two centuries say more about their authors than about the patient, and this article declines to choose among them. What the record establishes is the course: gradual, fluctuating in its early years, accompanied by constant noise, and complicated by the digestive illnesses that run through his correspondence like a second theme. For the biography those facts are sufficient. The hearing loss matters here as a working condition that entered a working life at a datable point and had to be priced into every contract, every performance and every relationship that followed.

## The Heiligenstadt document of 1802

### What is the Heiligenstadt Testament?

It is a long letter written in October 1802, during a summer stay in the village of Heiligenstadt outside Vienna, and addressed to his brothers Caspar Carl and Johann. In it he confessed his concealed hearing loss, described the isolation it caused, and asked that a physician explain his condition after his death.

He also directed that his small property pass to his brothers and their children. The letter was never sent. He kept it among his papers for the remaining twenty five years of his life, and it was found only after his death.

The circumstances of its writing are recoverable in outline. His physician that year, Johann Adam Schmidt, a serious doctor with musical interests, had advised a summer in the country quiet of Heiligenstadt, away from the noise and demands of Vienna, in the hope that rest might help the ears. The hope failed. The six months in the village brought no improvement, and in the autumn, facing a return to a city in which he expected to grow steadily deafer among people who did not know, he wrote the letter. It is dated in two stages, 6 October and 10 October 1802, and it was folded and kept with a care that its contents explain: it was written for posthumous readers, a deposition to be opened when he could no longer be questioned about it. Its contents are therefore described in what follows rather than reproduced, since quotation torn from the letter's function has done much of the damage surveyed in the next section.

What it does, read whole, is threefold. It testifies: the author sets down, for a future he does not expect to argue with, the fact and the felt consequences of his condition, including the admission that only his art held him back from ending his life. It instructs: the brothers are told to live in harmony, to raise their children in virtue, and to share what little he leaves. And it arranges: the request that his doctor explain the deafness after his death is the act of a man managing his posthumous reputation with the same care he gave to a dedication or a contract, determined that the world should not be free to mistake his withdrawal for arrogance or his deafness for an eccentricity. Maynard Solomon, the most searching of his modern biographers, reads the document within the pattern of a crisis survived and converted into resolve, and the reading has worn well. The letter ends in a determination to master his fate rather than submit to it. That ending is not a mood. It is a programme, and the following decade is its execution.

## What the document is and what it is not

Because the letter was published within months of his death and has been reprinted ever since, it has acquired a public function its author never gave it, and the third revelation of this article is a correction of scale. The Heiligenstadt document is a private letter to his brothers that he never sent, and reading it as a public manifesto inverts what it is. A manifesto is written to persuade contemporaries. This text was written to be found, which is a different act with a different audience: posterity, addressed over the heads of the living. Its author concealed his deafness from Vienna with complete success for years after writing it. He did not read it aloud, circulate it, or trade on the sympathy it would have bought him. He filed it, and went back to work, and the work of the next years, the Eroica Symphony and the opera and the concertos of the middle period, was conducted in public without reference to it. Any account that treats the letter as the key he handed his contemporaries is using a document he deliberately withheld from them.

It is also not a suicide note, though it records that the thought had been entertained and renounced, and the distinction is more than pedantry. A suicide note closes a life. This document opens a negotiation with one: it sets terms, assigns duties, requests a medical explanation, and resolves on endurance. Nor is it a medical record, though it contains the most famous first person account of his symptoms; for the clinical course of the condition the letters of 1801 and the later testimony of doctors and conversation partners are the better evidence. Lewis Lockwood, whose study of the life and the music keeps the documents in strictest order, treats the letter as one source among many in the autumn of 1802, and the discipline is instructive. The letter's authority is emotional and testimonial. It proves what the deafness felt like to the man enduring it in that season. It does not prove what the deafness was, and it was never meant to.

What it legitimately is, and why it cannot be discarded either, is the single most important document of his inner life that he chose to preserve. He kept it through moves, purges of papers and the chaos of his lodgings for a quarter of a century, alongside the other unsent deposition of his life, the letter to the Immortal Beloved. Two unsent letters, filed together in his effects, each addressed and each withheld: the pattern is itself biographical evidence, of a man who conducted his deepest business on paper and then declined to post it. The brothers who were its nominal readers saw it only after the funeral, in the hands of the executors, and by then its instructions about harmony among them were already beyond fulfilment. The custody war of the next decade would see to that.

## Working through the loss rather than past it

The trap in every account of the deafness is to treat it as the explanation of the music, a wound from which masterpieces issued. The record supports a drier and more durable story: a working musician with a progressive disability renegotiated his working methods, year by year, for three decades. The adaptations were practical. He composed at the piano with his teeth against the instrument in his earlier years, by report, to catch vibration; he used pianos built with resonating attachments; he commissioned ear trumpets from Johann Nepomuk Mälzel, the metronome's inventor, whose acoustic devices fill the conversation of the middle 1810s. He shifted the centre of his labour from performance to the desk, and at the desk he already possessed the most powerful compensating technology in the history of composition: the sketchbook habit, a lifelong practice of drafting, discarding and revising on paper that let him conduct experiments in a medium he could see. How far that habit reaches back, into the discipline of a Bonn apprenticeship steeped in the scores of the past, can be traced in the companion account of [the Bach life and career](/2010/08/07/johann-sebastian-bach-life-and-career/), whose subject built a career on the same faith in paper.

The adaptation was also social, and it was managed with decreasing success. He husbanded the situations in which hearing mattered least and performance could be controlled: his own academies, where colleagues knew the situation; chamber music among intimates; conducting, where the beat was visible and the orchestra forgiving. He avoided, increasingly, the situations where it mattered most, the salon conversation, the rehearsal of other men's works, the negotiation conducted aloud. Friends and pupils learned to shout, to gesture, to write. Strangers concluded that he was proud, eccentric or mad, and the reputation for misanthropy that clings to him was in part the social price of a disability he refused to advertise. Barry Cooper, the editor and cataloguer whose work on the sketchbooks and the documentary record is a standard of care, has emphasised how much of the late behaviour reads differently once the logistics are understood: a man who cannot hear a question is not necessarily refusing to answer it.

Two disciplines held the structure together. The first was routine: he rose early, composed in the morning in intense bouts, walked for hours with sketchbook pages in his pockets, and organised his chaotic lodgings, his copyists and his correspondence around the desk work that remained fully within his powers. The second was candour in the right places. To doctors he was exact about symptoms. To his closest correspondents he was, after 1801, intermittently open about the ears. To the public he offered nothing, and the nothing was itself a strategy: the market was asked to judge the works, which arrived in print immaculate, rather than the worker, whose condition arrived in person in a state of alarming disrepair. Section 14 of this article has deliberately shown the deafness as a working condition because that is what it was for the man who had it. The heroic interpretation, in which deafness is the furnace of genius, belongs to the debate article of this cluster, and it will be tested there. Here it is enough to record that the furnace had a timetable, a set of tools, and a cost that he itemised in letters to his friends like bills.

## The annuity contract of 1809

### How did Beethoven earn money?

From more sources, in more currencies of obligation, than any composer before him: a court salary at Bonn, teaching and salon playing in his Vienna youth, sales of works to publishers in several countries at once, dedications, benefit concerts, commissions, and from 1809 the annuity described below. No single source ever sufficed.

The career is the portfolio, assembled and defended piece by piece across forty years, and this section takes the annuity, the strangest item in it, in detail.

The annuity's immediate cause was a rival bid. In 1808 Jerome Bonaparte, installed by his brother as King of Westphalia, offered Beethoven the post of Kapellmeister at Kassel at a salary of six hundred gold ducats a year, a real post with real duties in a real court. Beethoven let the offer circulate in Vienna, and Vienna answered. The contract of 1 March 1809, signed by Archduke Rudolph, Prince Kinsky and Prince Lobkowitz, promised him four thousand florins annually in quarterly payments, the Archduke contributing fifteen hundred, Kinsky eighteen hundred and Lobkowitz seven hundred, for as long as he remained in Vienna or the Austrian lands and until he obtained an equivalent appointment, which all parties understood he would not seek. The fourth revelation of this article is the one the thesis rests on: the annuity made him the first major composer paid to exist rather than to produce, and it changed what he was able to attempt, because the buyer of his time was no longer entitled to any of it.

The income table below sets the annuity in the portfolio it completed. Its reliability column is the column that matters: every source except the annuity arrived with a hook in it.

| Period | Source of income | Approximate reliability | What it required of him | What it allowed him to refuse |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1784 to 1792 | Electoral salary at Bonn as assistant organist and orchestral violist | Steady but small, and half of his father's salary was diverted to him by petition | Daily chapel and theatre service, teaching, obedience to court routine | Very little; a servant's post on a servant's terms |
| 1792 to 1802 | Teaching, salon performance and dedications in Vienna | Good while his playing lasted, and perishable with his hearing | Attendance, lessons to aristocratic pupils, music for occasions | Residence in any single patron's household |
| 1795 to 1809 | Publication in Vienna, Leipzig, London and Paris | Irregular, negotiated work by work, exposed to piracy and delay | Marketable genres on publishers' schedules, corrected proofs, dedications | Total dependence on performance income |
| 1809 to 1827 | The annuity of four thousand florins from Rudolph, Kinsky and Lobkowitz | Promised for life in Vienna, then eroded by devaluation, death and bankruptcy among the payers | Remaining in the Austrian lands; nothing else | Court office, weekly delivery of works, a Kapellmeister's duties |
| 1800 to 1824 | Benefit concerts and academies of his own works | Occasional and weather dependent, large in good years and absent in bad | Organisation, solo performance while he could, conducting | Nothing reliably; the income was windfall, not floor |
| 1819 to 1826 | Commissions and subscriptions, including the Missa solemnis offered to the courts of Europe | Slow, prestigious, and paid in instalments across years | Long works written on his own schedule, presentation copies, courtly correspondence | Short deadlines and small forms he had outgrown |

Read as a whole, the table describes an ascent from wages to rent: from being paid for hours, to being paid for objects, to being paid, in the annuity years, for nothing but presence. The ascent was never completed and never secure, as the following sections show. But its direction is the direction of the career, and it was travelled deliberately.

## What independence actually bought him

The question opened in the Bonn childhood can now be closed. What does a composer do with a contract that demands nothing? In Beethoven's hands the unconditional income bought, first, time of a specific kind: not leisure, but gestation. The works of his last fifteen years were slow in a way no employer would have financed. The Missa solemnis occupied him for some four years. The late quartets, each separately commissioned and separately paid, were written at a pace of months per movement, with sketchbooks accumulating like geological strata. A Kapellmeister at Kassel would have owed his king operas, cantatas and chapel music on a calendar. The annuity's four thousand florins purchased exemption from every calendar but his own, and the late style, whatever else it is, is the sound of a man who cannot be hurried. How completely that exemption was used is a matter for the catalogue of the works themselves, weighed in the companion article on [Beethoven's greatest compositions](/2011/02/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-greatest-compositions/), and the biographical claim made here is only the enabling one: no settlement, no such timescale.

It also bought the simple right to wait between works, a luxury no salaried Kapellmeister of the period enjoyed and few freelance musicians could afford. The catalogue of the annuity years is punctuated by long silences that are not failures of invention but decisions about price and occasion: years in which the symphonies stop while the quartets deepen, seasons given to teaching the Archduke Rudolph in composition, a systematic course of study conducted for a pupil who was also a payer of the annuity and who received, in the dedication of the Fourth Piano Concerto and a shelf of other works, a return on his fifteen hundred florins that no contract had specified. The relationship with Rudolph is the annuity in miniature and its best justification. What began as a pupil's gratitude and an emperor's brother's patronage became, across twenty years, the nearest thing Beethoven had to an institutional home: a standing connection, renewed by music rather than by duties, on terms the composer effectively set. Rudolph's payments, alone among the three shares, never lapsed, and the stability of that one relationship underwrote risks that the whole portfolio could not.

Second, it bought refusal. He refused pupils he did not want, dedications that did not pay or honour him, alterations demanded by singers and impresarios, and the social attendance that had been the tax on his youth. The famous rudeness of the later Beethoven, the broken engagements and insulted patrons, reads differently against the contract: it is the conduct of a man whose income no dinner invitation could enlarge or diminish. Third, and least noticed, it bought continuity of address. He remained in Vienna, in a succession of lodgings, a fixed point to which publishers, patrons, copyists, instrument makers and visiting musicians all knew to come. The freelance composers who followed him across the nineteenth century inherited the model as a matter of course, a career assembled from publishing, teaching, commissions and patronage with no post at its centre. He had to invent it, contract by contract, and the annuity was the keystone. Its failure, when it came, therefore threatened the whole arch, and the manner of its failure is the business of the war and inflation that fill the section after next. First, the other leg of the portfolio deserves its own account, because the annuity never stood alone. It stood beside the publishers, and the publishers had to be fought for every florin.

## Publishers, negotiation and simultaneous editions

Beethoven's correspondence with publishers is the least sentimental body of business writing in musical history, and the most instructive. He wrote to Breitkopf und Härtel, to Artaria, to Simrock, to Clementi, to Schott and to a dozen lesser houses in a tone that oscillates between courtship and open warfare, sometimes within a single letter. He offered works in batches and priced them like a man selling horses. He demanded payment in specified currencies and complained, with justice, about unauthorised reprints, sloppy engraving and proofs corrected by no one. He also, on documented occasions, sold the same work to more than one publisher in the same season, exploiting the territorial patchwork of musical copyright, and when the collisions surfaced he met them with a fluency of excuse that his correspondents learned to price in advance. The double dealing should be stated plainly, because the record states it: the composer who insisted on the sanctity of his texts did not always insist on the sanctity of his contracts, and publishers who call him difficult in their letters have evidence on their side.

The system he exploited deserves a defence he never bothered to make. A composer of his generation received no royalties. A work sold outright earned its fee once, however often it was reprinted, however far it travelled. Performance brought no payment beyond the concert itself. The annuity apart, his only defence against this arithmetic was to multiply first sales across territories, to time releases, to withhold desirable works until terms improved, and to cultivate competition among houses the way a patron of the old school had cultivated competition among composers. Seen from Leipzig or London, this was sharp practice. Seen from his desk, it was the construction, by the only means available, of something resembling an income from intellectual property before the law had invented one. Jan Swafford, whose biographies of Brahms and of Charles Ives set a modern standard for documentary patience, has written of the period's music business as a world in which composers were paid like tradesmen and expected to be grateful; Beethoven's letters are the sound of a tradesman declining gratitude.

The labour was real and it consumed him. Proofreading alone, for a composer whose autographs are thickets of correction and whose printed texts he supervised with ferocity, was a second career conducted by post. He employed copyists and supervised them badly and well by turns. He negotiated for the Missa solemnis by subscription among the courts of Europe at fifty ducats a copy, a scheme that required years of courtly letters and produced a trickle of payments and a mountain of presentation scores. None of this appears in the heroic portrait, and all of it is the career. The negotiation was not the distraction from the work. It was the mechanism by which the work's conditions, its time, its texts, its very existence in durable form, were secured.

## The Vienna of occupation, inflation and war

The annuity was signed into a collapsing currency. French armies occupied Vienna in 1805 and again in 1809, the year of the contract itself, when Beethoven stayed in the city through the bombardment and, by the testimony of his circle, sheltered in his brother's cellar with pillows over his damaged ears. War brought requisitioning, scarcity and a paper currency printed to pay for it all, and in 1811 the Austrian state answered its debts with the Finanzpatent, a forced devaluation that reduced paper money to a fifth of its face. The annuity, four thousand florins on paper, was suddenly worth eight hundred in the new reckoning, and the three payers responded as their circumstances allowed. Lobkowitz, whose estates were mortgaged to sustain his musical establishment, suspended payment and slid toward the bankruptcy that overtook him in 1813. Kinsky, the largest contributor, was killed in a riding accident in November 1812, and his heirs disputed the obligation. Rudolph alone paid steadily, and paid to the end.

Beethoven sued. The proceedings against the Kinsky estate and the negotiations with the Lobkowitz trustees ran across the middle 1810s, and they were resolved by settlements that restored the nominal sums in the devalued currency, with arrears partly recovered: a legal victory and an economic defeat, since four thousand florins in the new paper bought a fraction of what the contract's framers had meant. The episode is the dark twin of the thesis. The settlement with the aristocracy had made him independent of employment, and the same settlement left him exposed to everything that could befall aristocratic fortunes: war, inflation, death and debt. He had diversified out of service and into patronage, and patronage, in the decade of Napoleon, turned out to be a security like any other, denominated in a currency that could be devalued by decree.

His response was characteristic. He did not seek a post. He increased the other lines of the portfolio, teaching the Archduke, selling to more publishers in more countries, and mounting, in the Congress winter of 1814 and 1815, the most lucrative public campaign of his life, when Vienna filled with the sovereigns and ministers of Europe and his music of patriotic celebration was performed before them to immense applause. The fame of those months was real and so was the money, and both faded with the Congress. What remained was the pattern of the last decade: an income assembled annually from annuity remnants, publishing, subscriptions and favours, never sufficient to end anxiety and never so insufficient as to force him back into service. He died, as the estate section of this article will show, possessed of bank shares he had refused to touch. The anxiety and the shares belonged to the same man, and both were products of 1809.

## The Immortal Beloved letter

### Who was the Immortal Beloved?

Nobody knows, and the honest answer begins there. In July 1812 Beethoven wrote a passionate letter to a woman he addressed only as his immortal beloved, and the letter was never sent; it was found after his death in a secret drawer. The leading candidate in scholarship is Antonie Brentano, argued by Maynard Solomon. An older case names Josephine Brunsvik.

The letter was written in pencil, across three sittings on the sixth and seventh of the month, and it was found in the cabinet together with the Heiligenstadt letter. The evidence has never produced agreement, and this article presents the dispute rather than a winner.

The letter's circumstances can be reconstructed more firmly than its addressee. Beethoven had travelled to Prague at the start of July 1812 and gone on to the Bohemian spa town of Teplitz for his health, and the letter was written there in a state of evident agitation, beginning on a Monday morning and breaking off and resuming across two days. Its writer speaks of a meeting only days past, of obstacles that both parties acknowledge, of a resolve to live either together or not at all, and of a love that he treats as the central fact of his life. He also writes as a man of forty one whose other attachments are documented as a series of approaches and withdrawals: to Josephine Brunsvik in the middle 1800s, in letters that survive from his side and burn with an intensity her family circumstances could not accommodate; to Therese Malfatti, in a connection that family tradition connects with a marriage plan around 1810; to Bettina Brentano, who publicised their acquaintance in letters of doubtful fidelity. Against that background the Teplitz letter is not an anomaly. It is the peak of a pattern, the one attachment, whoever its object, that he appears to have regarded as mutual, serious and impossible.

Two features of the letter have guided every serious attempt at identification, and they deserve to be set down before the candidates are weighed. The first is its tone of established intimacy: this is not a seduction or a proposal but a reckoning within a bond both parties already treat as permanent, written by a man who expects to be understood in half sentences. The second is its calendar. The letter looks back to an encounter within the previous days, in Prague, and forward to a reunion the writer anticipates within the same Bohemian journey, and it is shadowed throughout by a third presence, an obstacle the letter never names, which both parties appear to regard as immovable. Any candidate must satisfy all three conditions at once, intimacy, itinerary and obstacle, and the history of the question is the history of candidates who satisfy two and strain at the third.

The letter was found by the executors among his most private papers, in the company of the Heiligenstadt deposition and a miniature portrait of a woman whose identity has itself been argued over. Its survival was his doing. He kept it for fifteen years, through dozens of removals, in lodgings from which papers vanished constantly. Like the earlier letter, it is evidence of the filing instinct that governed his inner life: the decisive documents were written, dated, and withheld. A biographer is entitled to find the pattern eloquent. He is not entitled to complete the address.

## Why the addressee is disputed

The dispute is a model of how biographical evidence fails, and it deserves its longevity. Begin with what is certain: the letter exists, it is in his hand, it is dated internally to July 1812, and it was written from Teplitz to a woman who had been near him in Prague days before and whom he expected to reach Karlsbad. Everything else is triangulation. The candidates are constructed from itineraries: which women of his acquaintance can be placed in Prague in the first days of July 1812, whose circumstances fit the letter's references to an established bond and an obstructing situation, and whose later conduct is consistent with the letter having been meant for her. The method is sound and its results are not, because the surviving itineraries are incomplete and because more than one woman can be made to fit them with effort.

Maynard Solomon built the case for Antonie Brentano with the patience of a prosecuting scholar: she was in Prague with her husband Franz Brentano in the relevant days, on the family's journey toward Karlsbad; the Brentanos became Beethoven's intimate friends in Vienna, handled his business affairs, and named him godfather to a child; and a pattern of later kindnesses and silences, Solomon argued, is best explained by a bond formed in 1812 that both parties then spent fifteen years not naming. Antonie was married, a mother, and a woman whose own letters show a mind of unusual seriousness, and Solomon's reading has persuaded many of the field's most careful readers without ever achieving proof. The older candidacy of Josephine Brunsvik rests on firmer paper of an earlier date: his letters to her from 1804 onward survive, declare a love she appears to have returned, and end only when her family's opposition and her second marriage closed the road. Josephine's advocates, among them the Hungarian scholarship that first published the correspondence, ask why the composer of those letters needs a new beloved in 1812 at all, and answer that the Teplitz letter is the last flare of the one great attachment of his life. Her itinerary in July 1812 can be argued toward Prague. It cannot be documented there.

The stance of this article follows its charter. Each case has been given at its strongest, with its scholar named, and the matter is closed structurally rather than by verdict. For Solomon to be right, Antonie Brentano's documented presence and the friendship's later shape must carry the weight he loads on them, and the absence of any letter between them must be read as the silence of discretion. For the Josephine case to be right, the letter's urgency must be continuous with a correspondence that had effectively ended years before, and her presence in Bohemia that July must be supplied by inference. A single document, a hotel register, a dated letter in either woman's hand, would end the argument. None has appeared. The Immortal Beloved remains what the evidence makes her: the most written about unnamed person in musical biography, and a standing lesson in the difference between a case and a conclusion.

## The brothers and the family business of being Beethoven

From the winter of 1792, when his father died in Bonn, Beethoven was the head of his family, and he never resigned the office. The role came to him by the route described earlier, a teenage petition for half a drunkard's salary, and it shaped his relations with his brothers for thirty five years. Caspar Carl, four years younger, settled in Vienna as a clerk in the finance administration, played the piano respectably, and made himself useful to the famous brother as copyist, negotiator and man of business, work for which he was intermittently paid and around which the two men quarrelled with the intimacy of partners who are also kin. Nikolaus Johann, the youngest, trained as a pharmacist, served in the French medical service during the occupation, prospered, and bought the estate of Gneixendorf near Krems, rising in the world by the conventional ladder his brother had declined to climb. Johann's pride in the property produced the family's most reliable joke, reliably documented because it survives in correspondence: congratulated in writing as a landowner, he signed himself a proprietor of land; Ludwig answered in kind, signing himself a proprietor of brains. The exchange is funny and it is also a map of the family business. Three sons of a failed court tenor had divided the available futures among them: office, land, and art.

The business of being Beethoven, as the brothers experienced it, was demanding. Ludwig treated family loyalty as a claim he could levy in both directions. He expected his brothers' services, their signatures, their credit and their attendance, and he gave in return money, influence, introductions and an absolutism about their marriages and arrangements that he would not have tolerated for an hour directed at himself. He opposed Caspar Carl's marriage to Johanna Reiss in 1806 with a bitterness the record does not soften, and the opposition matters beyond its unpleasantness, because Johanna became the antagonist of the custody case that consumed his middle years, and the enmity of 1806 was its seed. With Johann the friction was commercial and fraternal: loans, property, the disputed terms of the Gneixendorf hospitality at the end. Yet the family's solidarity was also real. Caspar Carl's business services in the years of the annuity lawsuits were substantial and are documented in the correspondence. Johann's estate sheltered the composer and his nephew in the crisis autumn of 1826. The brothers quarrelled as shareholders quarrel, over a single enterprise in which all three were invested and only one was the asset.

What the enterprise produced, besides music, was paperwork: powers of attorney, loan notes, disputed wills, custody petitions, and correspondence in quantities that make the Beethoven family one of the most documented in the history of music. That paperwork is the biographer's fortune and the family's misfortune, because it preserves the quarrels in the same detail as the devotion. A judgment on his conduct as a brother has to be built from it whole, the generosity and the tyranny on facing pages, and this article's stance is to build it so and let the proportions speak. He supported his family for forty years and tried to govern it for the same period, and the two facts are not in tension. They are the same fact, in a man whose understanding of love and of management had been formed in a household where a child's talent was the family's capital and capital was managed, closely, by whoever could hold the pen.

## The custody conflict over his nephew

Caspar Carl van Beethoven died of consumption in November 1815, leaving a son, Karl, aged nine, and a will that named the boy's mother Johanna and his uncle Ludwig as co guardians. Beethoven moved at once to have Johanna excluded. The campaign that followed ran for nearly five years through two court systems, consumed his health, his money and his creative output, and remains the most contested episode of his life, because the documents support two readings of his conduct and the charter of this series requires both to be stated at strength.

The case against his conduct, made most searchingly by Solomon and accepted in outline by much modern scholarship, runs as follows. His hatred of Johanna predated the will by a decade and had its roots in the opposed marriage of 1806. He pursued the custody not as a reluctant duty but as a possession, idealising the boy as the heir of his name and his art while subjecting him to a regime of surveillance, boarding schools chosen and changed abruptly, intercepted letters, and interrogations that the diary of Fanny Giannatasio, whose father's school Karl attended, records with mounting dismay. The proceedings exposed his own confusions: he allowed the court for nobles, the Landrecht, to assume a nobility his Dutch van did not confer, and when the pretence collapsed the case was transferred to the city magistrate, a humiliation he felt keenly. Karl's own statements to the courts shifted with the pressure applied to them, and the boy's flight to his mother at Christmas 1818, and his reported wish to be free of his uncle, stand in the record against every claim of rescue.

The case for his conduct, made by the older biographers and restated in part by Lockwood with proper caution, begins from the same file. Johanna's position was genuinely compromised: she had a conviction for embezzlement from 1811 in her record, her household was irregular, and the court that finally ruled in Beethoven's favour in 1820, after appeal, did so on evidence about her fitness that it examined in full. Guardianship law and custom of the period placed the father's family ahead of the mother in ways that make his claim conventional rather than monstrous by the standards of his own time, and the money he spent on the boy's schooling, clothes, medical care and musical education, itemised in his letters and accounts, was spent in fact and not in pretence. On this reading the tragedy is not of a villain and a victim but of a deaf, ageing, obsessive man and a child who needed ordinariness, each failing the other in a legal frame too rigid to notice.

This article does not resolve his motives by assertion in either direction, and it reports what the records contain: that the final judgment of 1820 gave him sole guardianship, administered jointly in practice with the boy's later guardian Karl Peters and then the court official Jakob Hotschevar; that Karl was educated at good schools at his uncle's expense; that relations between uncle and nephew oscillated between tenderness and coercion, documented in letters on both sides; and that the relationship's crisis, when it came in 1826, came with a suddenness that suggests how much strain the surviving correspondence politely declines to itemise.

## The 1826 family crisis

In the summer of 1826, Karl van Beethoven, then nineteen years old and facing decisions about his future that his uncle had largely made for him, attempted to take his own life. He survived. Those are the facts the record firmly supports, and the restraint of this section is deliberate: the episode belongs to a young man who did not choose fame, and it is stated here in the plainest terms and without detail of any kind.

What followed can be told more fully, because it is documented in the uncle's correspondence and it altered the last year of his life. Beethoven's response to the crisis combined anguish, shame before the authorities who investigated, and a practical reordering of everything. The plan of a military career for Karl, which the young man had resisted and which the events of the summer had poisoned, was abandoned; a cadetship was eventually arranged through other channels the following year. In the autumn, seeking quiet and economy, Beethoven took Karl to stay at his brother Johann's estate at Gneixendorf. The visit, which lasted from late September into December, was meant as convalescence for both of them and became instead a slow quarrel between the brothers over money, lodging and old scores, conducted in a house where the nephew's presence was a reproach to everyone's arrangements. Uncle and nephew left at the start of December, travelling back to Vienna in winter weather in an open carriage because no closed one could be hired. Beethoven arrived ill. He never properly recovered, and the final months begin with that journey.

The crisis is the point at which the custody case stops being a legal story. Whatever judgment a reader forms of the five year campaign, its end is not in dispute: a young man damaged, an old man shattered, and a family enterprise that had begun in a Bonn chapel closing in recrimination and grief. The article leaves the episode there, as the record does.

## The last public performance as a player

The question opened at the onset of the hearing loss can now be closed with a date. Beethoven's last public performance as a pianist took place on 11 April 1814, when he played the solo part in the first performance of the Archduke Trio, Opus 97, at a morning concert in Vienna. He was forty three. The loss that had begun in his late twenties was eighteen years old, and the fifth revelation of the article is the simple arithmetic of that span: he continued to perform in public for roughly a decade and a half after the hearing loss was established, so the deafness of his career was a long professional twilight and never a single extinguishing event.

The twilight had been managed in stages, and the stages are documented. Through the first years of the new century he still premiered his own concertos, playing the Third in 1803, the Fourth in private and then in public, and the Fifth, by 1809, he did not premiere at all: the Emperor Concerto was introduced by Carl Czerny in 1812, a delegation that announced the transition more plainly than any confession. His improvisation, once the wonder of the salons, withdrew from public view; witnesses of the middle 1800s already describe playing that depended on compensation. Louis Spohr, the violinist and composer, attended rehearsals in 1814 and left the most quoted account of the end: at a forte the playing was powerful still, but in soft passages the ageing pianist, unable to judge his own touch, struck notes so gently that whole groups of them did not sound, and Spohr, watching the greatest musician of the age guess at his own instrument, recorded that the spectacle moved him to a sadness he could not shake. The Archduke Trio performance of that spring was repeated a few weeks later. Then, as a soloist before the public, he stopped.

What continued was everything that could be done by eye and by proxy. He conducted, or stood where conductors stand, at performances of his works into the 1820s, with the orchestra in practice following its leader, Michael Umlauf, over his shoulder. He rehearsed singers and players by written instruction and by fury. He attended premieres, including the Ninth Symphony's, in a visible and honoured uselessness that audiences understood and he must have understood too. The withdrawal should be read as an economic event as well as a medical one. Performance had been his first capital, the asset that bought Vienna. Its staged liquidation across fifteen years, concerto premieres delegated, improvisation retired, the platform surrendered last, is the soundest evidence of the manager he remained inside the artist: he sold his playing down slowly, at the best prices the market of sympathy and reputation would bear, and converted the proceeds into the authority of the composer who no longer needed to be heard at a keyboard to be obeyed in a hall.

## The conversation books

From the spring of 1818, the written record of Beethoven's daily life changes its nature. His hearing had declined past the point at which ordinary conversation could reach him, and visitors began to write their side of the exchange in stout blank books kept for the purpose, while he answered aloud. Some one hundred and forty of these conversation books survive. The original number has been estimated at around four hundred, covering the last nine years of his life, and the gap between those two figures is the subject of the next section. First, the books themselves deserve description, because they are the strangest major source in musical biography: a vast archive of questions without answers, requests without replies, in which the central figure is present on nearly every page and audible on almost none.

Their contents are the unfiltered traffic of a working household. Visitors, pupils, copyists, publishers' clerks, tradesmen, relations and cranks wrote down what they needed from him: terms for a score, complaints about a copyist, arrangements for a walk, medical advice sought and given, prices, gossip about courts and singers, family business, and, threaded through everything, Karl, his schooling, his health, his future. Beethoven's spoken answers survive only where a visitor wrote down a summary or where his own drafts and letters parallel the exchange, and he also used the books himself on occasion, jotting memoranda and, in company he distrusted, writing rather than speaking when he did not wish to be overheard by servants. The books therefore flatter every reader's worst instinct, which is to treat them as a transcript. They are not a transcript. They are one side of thousands of conversations, preserved by accident and by theft, and the third open question of this article arises directly from that condition. How much of what we know about his character, the rages, the suspicions, the jokes, the tenderness, comes from this lopsided record, filtered through a witness who, the next section will show, tampered with the evidence itself? The question opens here, with the books on the table. It closes in section 33, when the whole primary record has been inventoried and the contaminated share of it can be weighed against the rest.

Used with discipline, the books are irreplaceable, and Theodore Albrecht, the American scholar whose life's work has been the editing and translation of Beethoven's documents, has shown in his editions of the correspondence how they should be handled: as evidence of what was put to Beethoven, of who had access to him, and of the material circumstances of the late years, with every inference about his replies marked as inference. Used without discipline, they are a ventriloquist's dummy. The difference between those two uses is, in large part, the difference between the modern biography of Beethoven and the nineteenth century one, and the hinge between them is a person: the amanuensis who attached himself to the deaf composer in the 1820s, took the books into his keeping after the death, and became, for a hundred years, the world's chief witness to what Beethoven was like.

## Schindler, the first biographer, and what he did to the record

### Why are Beethoven's conversation books unreliable?

Partly because they record only the visitors' written side of each conversation, and partly because of what happened to them after 1827. Anton Schindler, who had served Beethoven as a secretary and took the books into his keeping, destroyed a share of them and inserted fabricated entries into many that survive. Every quotation from them needs its provenance checked first.

The fabricated entries, manufactured conversations that never took place, can be detected by handwriting, by ink and by internal impossibility, and any use of the books that skips that check builds on sand.

Schindler's own position in the story must be fixed before his tampering can be weighed. Anton Schindler, born in 1795, a trained lawyer and a working violinist, attached himself to Beethoven in the early 1820s and served, in two periods separated by a quarrel and a reconciliation, as the composer's unpaid factotum: running errands, managing copyists, negotiating the practical business that deafness made laborious. The service was real and the intimacy was real, and both ended at the funeral in March 1827, after which Schindler took possession of the conversation books, by what authority has never been clear. In 1840 he published the first substantial biography of Beethoven, a book built on his access and his papers, and for the rest of the nineteenth century his portrait of the composer, irascible, suspicious, sublime and wronged, was the portrait, amplified by every subsequent life that leaned on his. Lecturers who still build classroom presentations from that inherited portrait, sometimes assembling the evidence visually through [the browser-based presentation viewer on the ReportMedic site](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), are handling a tradition whose foundations this section has to inspect, because the inspection changes what the foundations are.

The shareable fact of this article lands here, and it should be stated with the flatness it deserves. Anton Schindler, Beethoven's assistant and first major biographer, is now known to have destroyed many of the conversation books and forged entries into the surviving ones. Of the roughly four hundred books that once existed, about one hundred and forty remain, and Schindler boasted in writing of having destroyed a quantity he represented as compromising, a destruction he presented as loyalty. Worse, in the books he kept he inserted entries in a disguised hand, composed after the events, attributing to visitors remarks they never wrote and staging exchanges that flatter his own place in the composer's life and darken the reputations of people he disliked. The forgeries were suspected early and proved in the 1970s, when scholars including Peter Stadlen demonstrated, from the physical evidence of inks and hands and from entries recording conversations at which Schindler could not have been present, that the additions were systematic. The sixth revelation follows, and it is the most expensive claim in this article: his first biographer destroyed a large part of the conversation books and forged entries into what remained, so the familiar portrait of his character rests on a contaminated source, and every anecdote that descends from Schindler alone, including the most famous ones, has to be quarantined as the testimony of a witness with a documented record of fabrication.

The quarantine is the working rule of this article, and it has been applied from its first page: Schindler appears here as a subject, never as a source, and where his version is the only version of an event the text has said so or declined the event. The consequences reach further than one biography. The whole nineteenth century reception of Beethoven's personality, the stormy titan of a hundred essays, descends in significant part through Schindler's filter, and the wider question of how witnesses manufacture the reputations they claim only to report is taken up in the series study of [how critics shaped reputations](/2026/07/21/how-critics-shaped-reputations/). What can be salvaged is also worth stating. Schindler preserved what he did not destroy, including papers that would otherwise have perished, and his biography, read as a memoir of the biographer, is a document of the first importance about the cult of Beethoven. It is evidence about 1840. It is not, without corroboration, evidence about 1824.

## The late isolation and how much of it was chosen

The standard picture of the last decade shows a deaf composer sealed away from the world, visited by the faithful, producing the late quartets in a silence broken only by his nephew's troubles. The conversation books themselves disprove the sealing. Their pages record a traffic of visitors that never stopped: publishers and their agents, the quartet players of Ignaz Schuppanzigh's circle who premiered the late quartets and argued about them in his rooms, foreign musicians making pilgrimages, among them Rossini in 1822, who left an account of a meeting conducted partly in writing in rooms of famous disorder, old friends, tradesmen, doctors, relations, and the nephew whose schooling and prospects fill page after page. A hermit does not generate four hundred books of other people's handwriting. The isolation was real, but it was selective, and the selection was substantially his.

What deafness withdrew was not society but spontaneity. He could no longer attend a rehearsal and catch an aside, no longer improvise socially, no longer conduct a negotiation in a corridor. What remained available was the scheduled encounter, the written exchange, the walk, the desk, and within those forms he was formidably active: composing the defining works of his last decade, the Missa solemnis, the Ninth Symphony and the final quartets, supervising editions, quarrelling by post with half the publishers of Europe, and litigating his family's affairs. He chose his lodgings for quiet and changed them incessantly. He chose his hours and defended them with rudeness that the conversation books show to have been, often, a scheduling instrument rather than a temper. Barry Cooper's work on the documentary record is valuable here precisely for its refusal of atmosphere: entry by entry, the late years show a working professional with a disability, a business and a family crisis, not a statue in a garret.

His instruments chart the border between the two isolations. The pianos of his last years, including the English Broadwood grand sent to him by its maker in 1818 and the Viennese instruments fitted with resonating hoods and attachments by the Streicher firm, whose family were among his closest practical allies, were attempts to keep a physical channel open by engineering rather than by company. He played for visitors who stood close enough to feel the floor. He followed new music through scores sent by publishers and through the reports of the conversation books, a reader's knowledge of a world he could no longer hear, and his judgments of contemporaries in the late years have the sharpness of a man reading evidence he cannot test. The quartet performances of his own late works he attended as a reader attends, score in hand, watching the bows.

The costs were nonetheless severe, and chosen isolation shaded into the other kind without a defensible border. He heard almost nothing of the music of his contemporaries in his last years and little of his own except inwardly. Friendships thinned under the labour of written conversation. The suspicion that runs through the late correspondence, of servants, of relations, of the world conspiring about money and about Karl, grew in the silence where correction by casual talk should have operated. The picture the evidence supports is neither the hermit of the legend nor a merely busy professional. It is of a man rationing a diminished channel of contact with ferocious intelligence, and paying, in loneliness and mistrust, the exchange rate of the rationing.

## The Ninth, the premiere and the money it did not make

The Ninth Symphony reached the platform by a route that illustrates the whole late economy. Its origins lay in a commission from the Philharmonic Society of London, which in 1822 voted him fifty pounds for a new symphony manuscript, money that arrived and was banked while the work grew into something its commissioners had not imagined, a symphony with a choral finale that London had not ordered and Vienna would hear first. Through 1823 and into 1824 he negotiated the premiere as he negotiated everything, among competing venues and societies, with a petition from Viennese admirers urging him to present his recent great works in the city that was his home rather than abroad. The concert took place on 7 May 1824 in the Kärntnertortheater, and it placed the Missa solemnis's principal movements, billed discreetly as hymns, beside the new symphony, before a house that was full and, by the standards of his legend, adoring. He stood on the platform with the score, beating time to a performance actually directed by Michael Umlauf, and the most repeated story of the evening, that the contralto Caroline Unger turned him gently to face applause he could not hear, comes from the singer's own later telling and is given here as her account, which is what it is.

The road to that platform had consumed two years of committee work conducted largely on paper. Petitions circulated among the Viennese aristocracy and the musical public in the winter of 1824 to persuade him that the Missa and the symphony should be premiered at home rather than carried to Berlin, where he had been tempted to take them; the petition, signed by thirty of the city's leading patrons and musicians, is a document of civic anxiety as much as it is one of admiration, since Vienna understood that losing the premieres meant losing its claim on him. He let himself be persuaded, extracted terms from the theatre direction, fought over the censorship of the Mass movements, over the size of the orchestra, over the copying bills, and arrived at the evening of 7 May with the air of a man who had promoted a prize fight. The audience that filled the Kärntnertortheater included, by every account, the cream of musical Vienna, and its enthusiasm was the genuine article. What it was not was transferable into income beyond the night itself, because the age had not yet built the machinery, the long run, the repeat performance market, the printed parts hired across Europe, that turns a premiere into an annuity of its own.

The money did not follow the adoration. The receipts of the first concert, after the theatre, the copyists, the enlarged orchestra and chorus and the soloists had been paid, left him a sum he considered an insult; the accounts he kept of it survive in his papers in a hand of controlled fury. A repeat concert on 23 May, mounted in the open air hall of the Redoutensaal with additions to the programme, played to a half empty house in fine weather and lost money outright. The work that posterity would rank among the summits of Western music earned its composer, across its triumphant premiere and its immediate repetition, approximately the moral of his whole career: the prestige was permanent and the cash was not. The London fifty pounds, the Viennese box office, the subscriptions for the Missa, the annuity dwindling in devalued paper behind them all: add the columns together and the last decade's masterpieces were financed by a portfolio in visible decay, assembled by a deaf man in his fifties out of instruments invented in his thirties. That the music shows no trace of the arithmetic is the fact the arithmetic exists to frame.

## Health, treatment and the final months

His health was a second career of complaints running beneath the first. The digestive disorder that tormented him from his twenties onward appears in the correspondence with a regularity that makes it a kind of ground bass: abdominal pain, diarrhoea and constipation alternating, remedies attempted and abandoned, doctors consulted in procession. Episodes of jaundice and rheumatic fever interrupted the work in 1821. His eyes inflamed repeatedly in the middle 1820s, threatening the sense he worked by on paper. He drank more than his physicians allowed and less than his legend suggests, took the waters at Baden and Teplitz on medical orders that doubled as working holidays, and regarded every doctor after Schmidt with the scepticism of a man who had audited too many promises. The physicians of the final illness included Andreas Wawruch, whose clinical report on the case, written for professional readers after the death, is among the most valuable documents of the last months and among the most harrowing, and it is used here only for its outline.

The end came by the road from Gneixendorf. He reached Vienna at the start of December 1826 already ill from the winter journey, and the illness settled into the pattern his doctors recognised as the last: fluid gathering in the abdomen, relieved by procedures repeated across the winter, brief rallies in which he corrected proofs, received visitors, made plans for a tenth symphony and for an oratorio he would not write, and spoke, by the testimony of those present, with composure about the end. A priest was called and the rites of his church administered. He died in his lodgings in the Schwarzspanierhaus on 26 March 1827, in his fifty seventh year, during a thunderstorm that the earliest accounts made much of and that this account leaves to them. The official cause recorded by his physicians was the failure of a liver long diseased, with the dropsy that attended it. Nothing in the life requires a more elaborate ending, and the plain statement is the one the evidence buys: he died, worn out at fifty six by illnesses his doctors could name but not treat, with work unfinished on the desk and bank shares untouched in it.

His brother Johann and his friend Anselm Hüttenbrenner were among the few present at the death; Karl, the centre of his last decade, was away with his regiment's cadet corps and did not reach Vienna in time. The desk and its contents passed to the executors, and the two unsent letters, to the brothers of 1802 and to the unnamed beloved of 1812, began their second life as public documents almost at once.

## The death and the funeral crowd

The funeral, three days later on 29 March 1827, was the largest Vienna had given a musician, and its scale is the surest surviving measure of his public standing at the end. Estimates of the crowd run from ten thousand to twenty thousand people, figures reported by the newspapers of a city of some three hundred thousand, and the schools of Vienna were closed for the day. The coffin was carried from the Schwarzspanierhaus to the parish church of the Trinity in the Alsergrund, where Mozart's Requiem was sung, and eight Kapellmeister of the city held the pall cloths while the torchbearers, thirty six of them, included the young [Franz Schubert, whose own life and music](/2018/06/07/franz-schubert-life-and-music/) were lived in Beethoven's shadow and in his city. The actor Heinrich Anschütz delivered at the graveside in the Währing cemetery the oration written by Franz Grillparzer, Austria's leading dramatist, and the oration's restraint is notable: it praises the artist and warns the mourners, in terms, against mistaking the man for his works.

The ceremony was organised at speed by the circle that had served him, with the invitations printed and the order of the procession published, and its details were reported across the German press within the week, which made the funeral the first fully mediated event of his afterlife. Grillparzer's oration, spoken at the grave because the dramatist's own nerve failed him, contains the funeral's most durable sentence in paraphrase: that the mourners stood not at the end of a life but at the gate of a fame that would outlast them all, and that the artist had been a man, with a man's faults entire. The newspapers carried the crowd's size and the schools' closure as the story's facts, and both facts did quiet argumentative work in the months that followed, in the subscription appeals for a monument and in the first negotiations over a biography. A public that had buried him in those numbers had, in effect, already voted on the question his first biographer would spend a book answering.

The crowd requires interpretation, because a funeral measures something more complicated than sales. Vienna had not made him rich. It had, across thirty five years, made him its own: the deaf composer in the street with his manuscript paper and his ear trumpet was a civic institution, the last representative, as the obituaries put it, of an age of Viennese music that had begun with Haydn. The annuity of 1809 had been three aristocrats' wager that he belonged to the city. The funeral was the city's ratification of the wager, delivered in the only currency a crowd commands, which is presence. He was buried at Währing beside a modest stone, in a grave whose later history of exhumations and reburial belongs to the reception story rather than to the life, and the life, for the purposes of this biography, closes with the inventory that followed.

## The estate and the auction of the manuscripts

The settlement of the estate in the months after the funeral produced the two discoveries that frame everything since. The first was paper. In the dead man's cabinet the executors found the Heiligenstadt letter and the letter to the Immortal Beloved, neither ever sent, each preserved for decades; found the bank documents; and found, in the same repositories, the working archive of the career, sketchbooks, autograph scores, corrected copies and correspondence in quantities that no one had catalogued. The second discovery was financial, and it embarrassed the legend in its cradle. The composer who had complained of poverty in a thousand letters, who had bargained for every ducat and suspected every publisher, had died possessed of seven shares in the Austrian National Bank, worth in the region of ten thousand florins, which he had bought across the years and refused, through every crisis of the custody case and the final illness, to sell. His letters explain the refusal without contradicting the complaints: the shares were Karl's inheritance, fenced off in his mind from the operating accounts of a life that genuinely did run short of cash. Both things were true. He was cash poor and capital careful, and the heroic tradition, which prefers its geniuses unworldly, has been correcting for the bank shares ever since.

The executors' work was complicated by one piece of foresight that deserves recording. Beethoven had made a will in Karl's favour in 1823, after an illness he thought might kill him, and renewed its substance in the arrangements of his last days, so the nephew's claim on the bank shares and the residue was never seriously in doubt. The claims that did circulate were the trade's: publishers asserting rights in works they had bought, copyists with unpaid bills, the landlord, the doctors, the household. That the settlement closed within the year without litigation of consequence is a small monument to the bookkeeping habits of a man whose lodgings suggested chaos. The chaos was in the rooms. The papers were in order, tied, docketed and filed by the same instinct that had preserved two unsent letters for a quarter of a century.

The manuscripts themselves were dispersed at auction in November 1827, in a sale whose carelessness the subsequent history of Beethoven scholarship has had to repair item by item. Publishers and collectors bought autographs and sketchbooks for sums that now read as derisory. Haslinger acquired much; Artaria acquired much; lots travelled to Berlin, to London, to private hands, and the great modern labour of reassembly, conducted by the Beethoven Haus in Bonn and by scholars including Barry Cooper in his cataloguing of the sketches, has been an effort to undo the geography of a single afternoon's bidding. The conversation books, as the earlier sections recorded, passed into the hands of Anton Schindler, with consequences already described. An estate is a life's last self portrait, and this one is unusually legible: bank shares for the nephew, unsent letters for posterity, an archive scattered to the trade, and debts and claims small enough to settle without scandal. The negotiator of 1809 had kept his books, in every sense that matters, to the end.

## The nephew afterwards

Karl van Beethoven survived his uncle by thirty one years, and his afterlife in the record is mostly silence, which may have been the point. He passed first into the guardianship of Jakob Hotschevar, entered the army as a cadet in the year after the death, served without distinction or disgrace, left the service, and married Caroline Naske in 1832. He lived thereafter in Vienna on the income of his inheritance, the bank shares among it, raised a family, and died in 1858 at the age of fifty two. His son Ludwig, named for the uncle, later emigrated to America, and the direct line of the Bonn chapel family dissolves into the ordinary obscurity from which the composer's fame had briefly lifted it.

His relations with his mother belong to the part of the record the uncle's party wrote most of, and they should be read with that caution attached. Karl visited Johanna, corresponded with her, and in later life acknowledged her without drama; the reconciliation, such as it was, happened offstage and after the principals were dead. The guardianship files close, the inheritance was administered, and the young man who had been the most litigated child in Vienna became a rentier of his uncle's bank shares, which is perhaps the most Beethovenian outcome available to him: the annuity principle, independence purchased by capital held against the world, inherited by the boy in whose name the capital had been assembled.

The biographical tradition has not known what to do with Karl, casting him by turns as the ungrateful burden of a great man's last decade and as the victim of that decade's demands. The records support the second reading more often than the first, but the honest summary is narrower: he was a child fought over by adults with causes, educated expensively, pressured continuously, and damaged in ways the summer of 1826 made undeniable, and his unremarkable adulthood, neither musical nor disastrous, reads in retrospect like a refusal of the role the family business had written for him. He kept his uncle's bank shares and, so far as the record shows, his own counsel. It is a fittingly inconclusive end to the custody story, and this article leaves it, as it left the custody itself, unresolved by assertion.

## What the primary record actually consists of

The third open question can now be closed, because the inventory is complete. The primary record of this life consists of five great deposits. First, the correspondence: some sixteen hundred letters from his hand survive, beside a large body of letters to him, and they cover business, family, health and art with a candour that no contemporary filtered, since he wrote them; Theodore Albrecht's editions of the letters have made this the best ordered part of the whole archive. Second, the sketchbooks and autographs, tens of thousands of pages of drafting that Lockwood and Cooper have taught the field to read, in which the working mind is visible without any witness intervening at all. Third, the paper of institutions: court records in Bonn and Vienna, publishers' archives, the annuity contract, the custody files, the estate inventory, documents written by clerks with no interest in his legend. Fourth, the testimony of contemporaries who knew him and wrote independently, Ries and Wegeler in their notices of 1838, the Giannatasio diary, the letters and memoirs of visitors, Czerny's recollections, evidence that can be cross checked against the first three deposits. Fifth, and last in reliability though first in influence, the conversation books, one sided by design and, in the Schindler share of them, tampered with by the man who also wrote the first biography.

Weighed in that order, the answer to the question opened in section 25 is reassuring in a way the nineteenth century could not have expected. The core of what we know about his character does not rest on Schindler. It rests on sixteen hundred letters in his own hand, on court files, on publishers' ledgers and on the independent testimony of men and women who had no cult to serve. Schindler's contamination is real, and it has been quarantined in this article and should be everywhere, but it contaminates the anecdotes, the dinner table sayings and the staged scenes, not the skeleton of the documented life. Researchers who keep documentary notes of their own across an archive this scattered may take a practical hint from the digital turn in such work: [the download page for VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/download/), the offline encrypted notes app, is one way scholars organise private research files without surrendering them to a server. The larger point stands apart from any tool. The life survives its first biographer because the life was lived, unusually for its century, on paper that other people also kept. The argument about its meaning, the heroic construction and its critics, belongs to the companion article on [Beethoven's legacy and the debate around it](/2011/03/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-legacy-debate/), and is deliberately left there. This biography ends with the record itself, which is the only foundation either side of that argument is entitled to build on.

## A career built on a negotiation, not a post

The thesis can now be restated in its earned form. His grandfather held a post. His father held a post and watched it fail to feed his family. Beethoven, formed by both examples and by a childhood in which his talent was the family's capital, spent forty years ensuring that he would never hold one. He converted a court salary into a pianist's reputation, the reputation into publishing leverage, the leverage into a contract that paid him to remain and required nothing, and when that contract decayed under war and inflation he converted the residue into subscriptions, commissions and bank shares, dying with his independence intact and his nephew provided for. The heroic tradition described all this as the overflow of genius. It was also, and first, a negotiation conducted across a lifetime by a man who had learned in Bonn exactly what service costs, and the music's famous freedom, the long gestations, the refusal of occasions, the works that address posterity over the heads of their first audiences, is the dividend of the settlement he extracted. The downstream of that model, the freelance careers it made imaginable for Schumann, for Brahms and for the century that followed, is charted in the companion article on [Beethoven's influence on music](/2011/02/21/ludwig-van-beethoven-influence-on-music/), and one heir may stand here for the pattern: [Johannes Brahms, whose life and music](/2018/05/07/johannes-brahms-life-and-music/) were built, two generations later, on precisely the post free economy of publishing, patronage and performance that Beethoven had improvised into existence.

The negotiation also explains the feature of the career that purely musical accounts leave mysterious, which is its shape in time: the early blaze of the pianist, the heroic middle built on credit extended by patrons who expected association rather than service, the long late silence punctuated by works of a scale no market had ordered. Each phase corresponds to a funding instrument. Salon credit financed the first, subscription and dedication the second in part, and the annuity, that most modern of instruments, a payment for being rather than for doing, financed the third. When music historians describe the emancipation of the composer as an event in the history of ideas, they are describing, from a distance, a change in the payment schedule. Beethoven heard the change first because he negotiated it first, in rooms in Bonn and Vienna, with men whose names are on the contract of 1809 and whose great grandfathers had simply owned their Kapellmeister outright.

None of this requires the music to be smaller than its legend says. It requires the legend to be re founded on the documents, where it turns out to be more interesting: not a titan kicking against an order he despised, but the order's most gifted negotiator, who understood the aristocratic world from inside its smallest chapel and bent it, clause by clause, until it paid him to be left alone. The contract of 1809, signed by an archduke, two princes and nobody's servant, is the emblem of the career. It asked for nothing in return. He gave it, in return, everything, on his own schedule, which was the only term he had ever really been negotiating for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Ludwig van Beethoven?

Ludwig van Beethoven was a composer and pianist born in Bonn in 1770 and settled in Vienna from 1792 until his death in 1827. Trained inside the Bonn court chapel, where his grandfather had been Kapellmeister and his father sang tenor, he arrived in Vienna as a virtuoso pianist and became the first major composer to live without a court post, supporting himself through publishing, teaching, commissions, patronage and, from 1809, an annuity paid by three aristocrats for remaining in Austria. His hearing failed progressively from his late twenties, yet he performed publicly into his forties and composed for a decade after conversation with him had to be conducted in writing. The career matters beyond the works because he improvised into existence the freelance model on which composers after him built their lives, negotiating his independence contract by contract.

### Q: Where and when was Beethoven born?

He was born in Bonn, in the Electorate of Cologne, in 1770, and baptised there on 17 December of that year. No birth record survives, only the baptismal entry, and his birthday has traditionally been given as 16 December on the reasonable inference that baptism followed birth by about a day. The date of his birth was muddied early: his father presented him in concert as younger than he was, advertising the seven year old as a child of six in 1778, and Beethoven himself was unsure of his birth year for much of his life. The Bonn of his birth was a small electoral capital whose court maintained the chapel and orchestra in which his grandfather had risen to Kapellmeister and in which he himself was playing, and drawing a salary, by the age of fourteen.

### Q: Did Beethoven study with Haydn?

Yes. Haydn agreed to take him as a pupil when they met at Bonn in the summer of 1792, and the lessons began when Beethoven reached Vienna that November and ran until Haydn's departure for London at the start of 1794. The surviving exercise books show systematic work in counterpoint on the strict Fuxian model, with exercises corrected unevenly and some errors passing unnoticed. Dissatisfied, Beethoven secretly had his exercises corrected by Johann Schenk before Haydn saw them, a deception that preserved the patronage while supplying the education. After Haydn's departure he studied counterpoint openly with Johann Georg Albrechtsberger into 1795 and took counsel on vocal writing from Antonio Salieri over a longer period. The Haydn connection was advertised by both men for the rest of the decade, most publicly in the dedication of Beethoven's Opus 1 piano trios, and the relationship, though privately strained, was never openly broken.

### Q: Why did the Haydn lessons fail?

They failed, insofar as they failed, because of a mismatch of situation rather than a clash of genius. Haydn in 1793 was the most famous composer in Europe, preparing his second London journey and composing at full stretch; detailed daily correction of a pupil's counterpoint exercises was work he had neither the time nor, by then, the temperament to do thoroughly. Beethoven, already a professional of a decade's standing, detected the uncorrected errors and concluded, reasonably, that he was paying for a name. Ferdinand Ries later reported Haydn's advice against publishing the C minor trio of Opus 1, and Beethoven's suspicion that the advice was not disinterested; the anecdote comes from a loyal pupil writing decades later and should be held as his account. The deeper cause was temperamental: Beethoven wanted rules explained and tested, not transmitted, and in the end he educated himself from scores, with Haydn's own works among his principal textbooks.

### Q: Was Beethoven famous as a pianist before he was known as a composer?

Yes, and by a margin of years. Vienna's salons took him up on his arrival in 1792 as an improviser and keyboard player of overwhelming power, and his standing with the aristocracy, his teaching practice and his income all rested on the playing long before the publications accumulated into a reputation. Witnesses with standing of their own, among them Carl Czerny, remembered a legato and a force at the keyboard that they treated as new in the world. His public debut as soloist came in 1795, the year of Opus 1, and the great benefit concert of his own works followed in 1800, by which time the reclassification from pianist to composer was under way. The sequence mattered economically: the pianist's reputation was the capital that bought the composer his independence, made his threat to leave Vienna in 1808 worth buying off, and sustained him while the hearing loss closed the performing career by stages.

### Q: How did Beethoven's hearing loss progress?

Gradually, across roughly three decades, and never as a single event. In letters of 1801 to Franz Wegeler and Karl Amenda he dated the first weakness back about three years, to his late twenties: ringing and roaring in the ears, difficulty with high notes and with speech at a distance, and acute embarrassment in company, which he concealed because a musician could least afford the confession. Through the following decade he still premiered concertos and played chamber music, adapting rooms, repertoire and colleagues to the condition. Ear trumpets, made for him by Johann Nepomuk Mälzel, belong to the middle 1810s. His last public performance as a soloist came in April 1814. From 1818 his visitors wrote their side of conversations in the conversation books, and by his final years he could catch almost nothing by ear. The medical cause was never established in his lifetime.

### Q: Could Beethoven hear his own late works?

Almost nothing of them in performance, and the distinction between hearing and knowing is the one to keep. By the years of the Missa solemnis, the Ninth Symphony and the late quartets, his deafness was profound enough that conversation required writing and rehearsals required intermediaries. He attended performances, followed scores with his eyes, watched bows and faces, and corrected details that visitors reported to him in the conversation books. What he possessed, in compensation, was an inward command of sound built over fifty years of playing, conducting and revising, exercised through the sketchbooks in which the late works were drafted and redrafted on paper. Witnesses describe him at the Ninth's premiere standing with the score, beating time the orchestra was not taking from him. The late works were composed by a man who could verify every note on the page and almost none of them in the air, and that is the accurate form of the famous paradox.

### Q: What did the 1809 annuity contract actually say?

The contract, dated 1 March 1809, was signed by Archduke Rudolph, Prince Ferdinand Kinsky and Prince Franz Joseph Lobkowitz. Rudolph undertook fifteen hundred florins a year, Kinsky eighteen hundred and Lobkowitz seven hundred, a total of four thousand florins payable quarterly for as long as Beethoven remained in Vienna or the Austrian hereditary lands, and until he obtained a post of equivalent value, which nobody expected him to seek. It imposed no duties whatsoever: no compositions owed, no performances, no teaching, no attendance. Its stated purpose was to keep him in Austria against the rival offer of the Kapellmeister's post at Kassel from Jerome Bonaparte. Its later history was rougher than its terms: the Austrian devaluation of 1811 cut its real value, Lobkowitz suspended payment as his finances collapsed, Kinsky died in 1812 and his heirs disputed the obligation, and Beethoven recovered reduced settlements by lawsuit. Rudolph paid faithfully until the composer's death.

### Q: How did Beethoven negotiate with publishers?

Hard, continuously, and on several fronts at once. Because a composer of his period received a single fee and no royalties, he built his income by multiplying first sales: the same work could be offered to Artaria in Vienna, Breitkopf und Härtel in Leipzig, Clementi in London and a Paris house, each buying rights only for its own territory under the patchwork copyright of the day. He priced works in batches, demanded payment in named currencies, supervised proofs with ferocity, and complained in letters that publishers across Europe learned to expect, about piracy, delays and careless engraving. He also, on documented occasions, sold the same work twice within one market and excused himself fluently when the collision surfaced, conduct his correspondents priced into their dealings with him. For the Missa solemnis he devised a subscription scheme among the courts of Europe at fifty ducats a manuscript copy. The correspondence, thousands of letters, is the fullest surviving record of a composer constructing an income from intellectual property before the law recognised the thing.

### Q: Who was Beethoven's Immortal Beloved?

The identity of the woman addressed in the unsent letter of July 1812, found in his cabinet after his death, has never been established, and any answer that names her without qualification is overstating the evidence. The letter, written from Teplitz in pencil across the sixth and seventh of July, speaks of a recent meeting in Prague and of obstacles both parties accept, and the candidates are constructed by matching those clues against documented itineraries. Maynard Solomon argued the case for Antonie Brentano, who was in Prague with her husband in the relevant days and whose family became Beethoven's closest Viennese friends, and his case is the most fully documented. An older tradition names Josephine Brunsvik, the recipient of impassioned surviving letters from the middle 1800s, whose presence in Bohemia that July must be inferred rather than proved. Other names have been advanced and have faded. The dispute is best understood as a lesson in biographical method: the letter is certain, the addressee is a triangulation, and the triangulation has never closed.

### Q: Why did Beethoven fight for custody of his nephew?

His brother Caspar Carl died in November 1815 leaving a will that named Beethoven and the boy's mother Johanna as co guardians of Karl, then nine years old, and Beethoven petitioned at once to have Johanna excluded. The motives remain contested and the record supports elements of several. His hostility to Johanna dated from her marriage, which he had opposed in 1806. Her position was genuinely vulnerable: she carried a conviction for embezzlement from 1811, and the courts that heard the case examined her fitness in full before awarding him sole guardianship in 1820. He spent heavily and documentedly on the boy's schools, clothes, doctors and music lessons, and described the duty in his letters as sacred. The opposing reading, pressed by Maynard Solomon and others, sees obsession rather than duty: surveillance, abrupt changes of school, intercepted correspondence and an idealisation of the boy as heir to the Beethoven name. The five year case ran through two court systems and ended, whatever its motives, in the damaged relationship whose crisis came in 1826.

### Q: What are the conversation books?

They are the blank books in which, from 1818 until his death, Beethoven's visitors wrote their side of conversations because his deafness had made speech unintelligible to him. He answered aloud, so the books preserve questions, requests, prices, gossip, family business and arrangements, while his own replies survive only in fragments and in his letters. Roughly one hundred and forty books survive of an estimated four hundred. They are an irreplaceable record of the late household, of who had access to him and of the material texture of his days, and they are treacherous in two specific ways: they record only one side of every exchange, and they passed after his death into the custody of Anton Schindler, who destroyed many and inserted fabricated entries into others. Modern editions, prepared with the physical evidence of hands and inks laid out, allow a careful reader to use them. Used innocently, as transcripts, they mislead.

### Q: How reliable is Schindler as a source on Beethoven?

As a source on himself, excellent; as a source on Beethoven, only with corroboration. Anton Schindler served Beethoven as an unpaid secretary in the 1820s, took the conversation books into his keeping after the death, and published the first major biography in 1840, which fixed the composer's portrait for a century. Research in the 1970s, led by Peter Stadlen, established from the evidence of handwriting, inks and entries describing events Schindler cannot have witnessed that he destroyed a large part of the conversation books and forged entries into many that survive, manufacturing conversations that flattered his own intimacy with the composer. His biography also preserves genuine material and documents the early cult of Beethoven. The working rule is the one this article applies: Schindler is a subject, never a source. Where his version of an anecdote is the only version, it is reported as his claim or set aside, however famous it has become.

### Q: How did Beethoven die?

He died in Vienna on 26 March 1827, in his fifty seventh year, after a final illness that began with the winter journey back from his brother's estate at Gneixendorf in December 1826. His physicians recorded the failure of a long diseased liver, with the dropsy that attended it, as the cause; abdominal fluid was drawn off repeatedly across the winter in procedures his doctor Andreas Wawruch described in a clinical report. His health had been poor for years, with a chronic digestive disorder running through the correspondence, an attack of jaundice in 1821 and recurring inflammation of the eyes. He received the last rites of his church, transacted business and corrected proofs during rallies, and died during a thunderstorm on the afternoon of 26 March, with his brother Johann and his friend Anselm Hüttenbrenner among the few present. His nephew Karl did not reach Vienna in time.

### Q: How did Beethoven's funeral reflect his public standing?

The funeral of 29 March 1827 was the largest Vienna had given a musician, and the newspapers treated its scale as the news: estimates of the crowd run from ten thousand to twenty thousand in a city of some three hundred thousand, and the schools were closed for the day. Eight Kapellmeister held the pall, thirty six torchbearers accompanied the coffin, among them Franz Schubert, and the actor Heinrich Anschütz delivered at the Währing cemetery the oration written by Franz Grillparzer, Austria's leading dramatist. Mozart's Requiem was sung at the funeral service. The standing measured by those numbers was civic rather than commercial. Vienna had not made him rich; the annuity behind him had been three men's wager. But across thirty five years the deaf composer had become the city's emblem of its own musical age, the last survivor of the line from Haydn, and the crowd's presence ratified in public what the obituaries said in print.

### Q: Which Beethoven biography should I read first?

Begin with Lewis Lockwood's Beethoven: The Music and the Life, which keeps the documentary record and the works in strict order and characterises its evidence with unusual care. Maynard Solomon's biography, the deepest study of the inner life and the source of the leading case for Antonie Brentano as the Immortal Beloved, rewards a second reading once the outline is secure; its psychological arguments should be weighed against its documents, which are impeccable. Barry Cooper's work, including the Beethoven Compendium he edited, is the reference standard for the sketchbooks, the chronology and the catalogue. The first notices by Franz Wegeler and Ferdinand Ries, published in 1838, are primary testimony rather than modern biography and are best read in that spirit, as evidence by two men who knew him. Theodore Albrecht's editions of the letters allow any reader to test every biography against the composer's own paperwork, which is where all of them begin.

### Q: Should I trust nineteenth century accounts of Beethoven's character?

In layers, and never whole. The century's portrait descends in large part through Anton Schindler's biography of 1840, and Schindler destroyed conversation books and forged entries in those he kept, so any anecdote sourced to him alone belongs in quarantine. Against that tainted channel stand independent witnesses who wrote from their own knowledge: Wegeler and Ries in their notices of 1838, the diary kept by Fanny Giannatasio during Karl's schooling, the letters and memoirs of visitors such as Czerny and the young Ferdinand Hiller, who visited in the final month. These accounts disagree with each other in emphasis, which is a sign of health. The test to apply to any nineteenth century story is the one this article applies throughout: ask who is telling it, what they stood to gain, and whether the letters, court files or publishers' archives, which had no legend to serve, agree. Character claims that survive that test can be trusted. The rest is reception history, valuable as such and false as fact.

### Q: How do I separate documented fact from legend about Beethoven?

Work from the deposits outward. The firmest ground is paper nobody created for posterity: the annuity contract, the custody files, publishers' ledgers, the estate inventory, and the correspondence, some sixteen hundred letters in his own hand, now available in Theodore Albrecht's editions. Next come the sketchbooks and autographs, where the working process is visible without a witness. Then the testimony of contemporaries, weighted by independence: a Bonn baker's son remembering the household, a pupil remembering lessons, a diarist recording a schoolboy's guardian, each checked against the documents. Last and least, the anecdotes that descend through Schindler, which require corroboration before use. A practical rule follows: any claim about Beethoven's character that can be traced to his own letters or to a court file is probably fact; any claim that exists only as a good story told by one later writer is probably reception. The legend is not worthless. It is evidence about the centuries that needed it, filed in the wrong drawer.

### Q: How do I follow Beethoven's career in chronological order?

Anchor the life in four dated blocks and let the detail attach to them. Bonn, 1770 to 1792: the court chapel childhood, the falsified prodigy bills, the salaried posts from 1784, the dead mother and the teenage head of household, the departure for Vienna in November 1792. The pianist's Vienna, 1792 to 1802: Haydn and Albrechtsberger, the salons and improvisation contests, Opus 1 in 1795, the first academy of his own works in 1800, and, closing the block, the letters of 1801 and the Heiligenstadt letter of 1802 that admit the deafness. The negotiated independence, 1802 to 1815: publication across Europe, the annuity of 1809, its erosion by devaluation and death, the Congress winter fame, the last public performance as a pianist in 1814. The final decade, 1815 to 1827: the custody case, the conversation books from 1818, the Missa and the Ninth, the premiere of May 1824, the crisis of 1826 and the death the following March.

### Q: Which recordings suit a listener new to Beethoven?

Choose performances that make the career audible as the sequence it was, and begin where the life itself turned. A first symphony set with lean forces and brisk tempos shows the Bonn craftsman inside the Viennese classicist; the Eroica from the same set then lands with its full weight as the work of the annuity's eve. For the piano, a complete sonata cycle by a single player pays compound interest, since the sonatas track the life from salon brilliance to the written conversations of the last decade, and the middle period concertos, the Fourth above all, preserve the pianist he was before the platform was surrendered. The late quartets should come last and be allowed to be difficult; they were written by a deaf man for posterity and they keep posterity's hours. Recordings change with every generation of players, and the durable advice is structural: one strong cycle per genre, heard in the order the works were written, with the life in mind.
