Between the first day of November 2000 and the fifteenth day of the same month, the reorganisation Acts 2000 brought three new states into the Indian Union: Chhattisgarh out of Madhya Pradesh, Uttaranchal out of Uttar Pradesh, and Jharkhand out of Bihar. No capital was shared, no joint capital was declared, no special status was promised to a city left behind, and no court was asked to decide whether the map itself was lawful. The three statutes were passed by Parliament within days of one another, they follow a single template so closely that whole parts correspond section by section in substance, and they settled into working administration faster than any reorganisation before or since. This article reads the three Acts as what they are: one instrument in three copies, with the variations marked, so that a reader can see what a reorganisation statute has to decide, what each of the three decided, and why the decisions held.

![The three reorganisation Acts of 2000 and the creation of Chhattisgarh, Uttaranchal and Jharkhand on a single statutory template - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The analytical value of the three Acts lies in the control they offer. When three legislatures-in-waiting are created by the same Parliament, in the same session season, on the same drafting pattern, the differences between the outcomes cannot be explained by the drafting. What differed was the territory: in each case the successor state took a region with an administrative centre of its own, and the parent state kept its capital, its High Court city in two of the three cases, and the larger share of population and seats. The single variable that the brief history of Indian reorganisation keeps testing, whether the capital is contested, was absent in all three. The statutes therefore show the template working under the most favourable conditions it is likely to meet, which is precisely why they repay close reading: they mark the baseline against which every harder reorganisation, including the one examined in this series for 2014, has to be measured.

## The reorganisation Acts 2000: one template in three copies

A reorganisation Act is a statute that has to perform, in a single text, a series of operations that the Constitution otherwise distributes across many instruments. It creates a state, which means it must describe the state's territory, amend the First Schedule to the Constitution so the new name and territory enter the constitutional text, and rewrite the Fourth Schedule so the new state has seats in the Council of States. It must constitute a legislature for a polity that has never voted as a unit, which means it must say who sits in the new Assembly on the first morning, how long they sit, and how the first real election will be organised. It must give the new state a judiciary, which means constituting a High Court, moving its judges, transferring its pending cases, and preserving the rights of the lawyers who practise in its courts. It must divide a going concern: the money in the treasury, the public debt, the land, buildings and stores, the corporations and boards that operate across the old boundary, the pension promises made to retired servants, and the serving officers whose cadres were built for one state and must serve two. And it must do all of this without a legal vacuum, which means it must declare that the laws in force continue, give governments a limited power to adapt those laws by order, give courts a power to construe them so they work, and give the President a limited power to remove difficulties that nobody foresaw.

Each of the three Acts of 2000 performs every one of those operations, and each performs them in the same order. Part I is preliminary in each Act: a short title, and a set of definitions that includes the appointed day, the population ratio between the successor states computed from the 1991 census, the sitting member, the successor state and the transferred territory. Part II effects the reorganisation itself: a formation section that names the new state and lists the districts of which it consists, a section preserving the residue as the continuing parent state, an amendment of the First Schedule, and a saving of the state governments' power to alter district boundaries afterwards. Part III deals with representation in the legislatures: the Council of States, the House of the People, the Legislative Assembly, and the delimitation of constituencies. Then come parts on the High Court, on authorisation of expenditure and distribution of revenues, on the apportionment of assets and liabilities, on services, and a closing part of legal and miscellaneous provisions: continuance and adaptation of laws, transfer of pending proceedings, the overriding effect of the Act, and the power to remove difficulties. The Bihar Act adds one part that the other two do not carry, a part on the management and development of water resources, and that single addition is itself instructive, because it marks the one subject on which the Bihar division could not be left to the general template.

### What does a reorganisation Act actually do?

A reorganisation Act creates a new state by ordinary legislation under Article 3, and in one text it fixes the territory, seats the new state in Parliament and in its own Assembly, constitutes its High Court, divides assets, liabilities and government servants between the successor states, and keeps every existing law in force until the new legislatures change it.

The three Acts are the Madhya Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000, Act 28 of 2000; the Uttar Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000, Act 29 of 2000; and the Bihar Reorganisation Act, 2000, Act 30 of 2000. Their numbers are consecutive because their passage was consecutive: the three bills moved through Parliament in the monsoon session of 2000, and the Bihar Act, to take the one whose dates are most often recorded, received the President's assent on 25 August 2000. None of them commenced on assent. Each Act defines the appointed day as the day the Central Government appoints by notification, and each was brought into force on a different November date chosen in advance: the Madhya Pradesh Act by notification S.O. 827(E) of 14 September 2000, appointing 1 November 2000; the Bihar Act by notification S.O. 829(E) of the same date, appointing 15 November 2000; and the Uttar Pradesh Act by notification S.O. 950(E) of 20 October 2000, appointing 9 November 2000. The gap between enactment in August and commencement in November was deliberate working time, and what filled it, the preparation of allocation lists, the identification of judges, the printing of new seals and the thousand smaller acts of separation, is the unglamorous substance of a successful reorganisation.

The template was not invented in 2000. It descends from [the States Reorganisation Act 1956](/2018/05/15/states-reorganisation-act-1956-explained/) and the succession of single-state Acts that followed it, and the three Acts acknowledge the descent on their face: the Madhya Pradesh Act, by its section 77, amends section 15 of the 1956 Act to seat Chhattisgarh in the zonal council scheme, and the Bihar Act, by its section 83, makes the corresponding amendment of the same 1956 Act. What 2000 added was simultaneity and scale: three applications of the template at once, covering the three largest undivided states of the Hindi-speaking interior, each of which had resisted division for decades. The reader who wants the constitutional mechanism and the standard heads set out in general terms, before watching them applied, should begin with [the complete guide to state reorganisation in India](/2018/05/01/state-reorganisation-india-guide/), which owns that general account; this article owns the three texts and the record they produced.

## The anatomy of the three Acts

Because the three texts correspond so closely, the anatomy of one is the anatomy of all, and the Bihar Act, whose arrangement is the most fully documented of the three, can serve as the type. It runs to ninety-two sections in ten parts, followed by ten schedules. Part I, sections 1 and 2, gives the short title and the definitions, including the population ratio of 645.30 to 218.44 that governs the financial divisions. Part II, sections 3 to 6, effects the reorganisation: formation of Jharkhand, the residue as Bihar, the First Schedule amendment, and the saving of district-altering powers. Part III carries representation in the legislatures, from the Fourth Schedule amendment for the Council of States through the Legislative Assembly and the Legislative Council of Bihar to delimitation and the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes provisions. The parts that follow deal in turn with the High Court, with authorisation of expenditure and distribution of revenues, with the apportionment of assets and liabilities, and, in Part VIII, with services, where the provisions run through allocation in section 72, protection of conditions of service in section 73, the deeming of appointment in section 74, advisory committees in section 75, the Central Government's power to give directions in section 76 and the State Public Service Commission in section 77. Part IX, sections 78 to 82, is the water resources part peculiar to this Act. Part X, sections 83 to 92, closes with the legal and miscellaneous provisions: the amendment of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956, territorial extent of laws in section 84, the power to adapt laws in section 85, the power to construe laws, the power to name authorities, legal proceedings, transfer of pending proceedings in section 89, the right of pleaders to practise, the overriding effect of the Act, and the power to remove difficulties in section 92. The ten schedules then do the work that cannot be done in sections: the allocation of sitting members of the Council of States between the successor states by term, the lists of parliamentary and assembly constituencies assigned to each state, and the corresponding detail for the reserved seats.

The Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh Acts follow the same plan with the section numbers their own length dictates: in the Madhya Pradesh Act the legal provisions fall near the end of a slightly shorter text, with territorial extent in section 78, adaptation in section 79, construction in section 80, the naming of authorities in section 81, legal proceedings and transfer of pending proceedings in sections 82 and 83, pleaders in section 84, overriding effect in section 85 and removal of difficulties in section 86. The correspondence is close enough that a reader who has mastered the Bihar Act's arrangement can find any provision in either of the others within a section or two, and that drafting discipline is itself a policy choice: it made the three settlements teachable as one subject, examinable as one subject, and administrable by officers who might serve any of the six successor states in a career and meet the same instrument in each.

Two features of the anatomy deserve emphasis before the detailed sections that follow. The first is the place of the schedules. In most statutes the schedules are appendages; in a reorganisation Act they are where the settlement becomes personal, because they name, or make identifiable, the individual members of the Council of States assigned to each successor state and the individual constituencies that compose each Assembly. The First Schedule to the Madhya Pradesh Act, excerpted in the reported judgments, works through the sitting members of the Council of States term by term, directing that of the five members whose terms expire on 9 April 2002, two named members shall be deemed elected for Chhattisgarh and three for Madhya Pradesh, and so on through the cycle, a device that preserves the staggered retirement of the Council without a single special election. The second feature is the position of the definitions. The population ratio sits in section 2, before any operative provision, because it is the settlement's unit of account: every financial clause in the Act spends it, and placing it in the definitions makes it a fact of the Act rather than a conclusion to be argued afresh in each part.

A word on the drafting vocabulary, because the Acts' defined terms do quiet work throughout. Each Act speaks of the existing state, meaning the undivided state as it stood immediately before the appointed day, of the successor states, meaning both products of the division indifferently, and of the transferred territory, meaning the districts that move. The vocabulary matters because the Acts' provisions constantly need to address the two products together and severally: a provision about successor states allocates between both; a provision about the transferred territory locates a thing or a person; and a provision about the existing state fixes the baseline of law, membership and employment from which the division proceeds. The definitions also fix the reference dates with care, a sitting member being a person who is a member immediately before the appointed day, and a law being an instrument having the force of law immediately before that day, so that the whole settlement is photographed at a single instant, the midnight before the appointed day, and every deeming provision develops that photograph.

## Three movements behind the three Acts

The statutes are parallel; the histories are not. Each of the three territories arrived at 2000 by a different road, and the differences explain both the order in which the appointed days fell and the small variations in the drafting.

The Jharkhand demand is the oldest of the three as an organised politics. A distinct political claim for the tribal plateau of southern Bihar was voiced from the early decades of the twentieth century, and it acquired a party when Jaipal Singh Munda, the Oxford-educated captain of the Indian hockey team that won gold at Amsterdam in 1928, founded the Jharkhand Party in 1949 and carried the demand into the first general elections. The States Reorganisation Commission of 1953 to 1955 considered the claim and declined to recommend a separate Jharkhand state, so the plateau stayed in Bihar through the 1956 settlement. The demand acquired a second life with the founding of the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha in 1972 by Shibu Soren, Binod Bihari Mahato and A. K. Roy, which fused the tribal identity claim with the grievances of the coal belt's industrial workforce. Through the 1990s the demand widened beyond its tribal base, and a half-way institution, the Jharkhand Area Autonomous Council, was created under a Bihar statute of 1994 and set up in 1995. The appointed day chosen for Jharkhand, 15 November 2000, was the birth anniversary of Birsa Munda, the nineteenth-century Munda leader whose rising against the British and the moneylender-landlord order made him the movement's presiding symbol; the choice of date announced, in the calendar itself, whose state this was understood to be.

The Chhattisgarh demand has a quieter record. A separate Chhattisgarh was urged in the Raipur Congress as early as 1924, on the ground that the Chhattisgarhi-speaking south-eastern districts of the Central Provinces, and later of Madhya Pradesh, formed a region with its own language, its own agrarian structure of rice cultivation, and a population with a very large tribal share in its southern and northern reaches. The States Reorganisation Commission did not recommend a separate state, and in 1956 the region entered the new Madhya Pradesh, where it remained one of the state's recognised regions alongside Mahakoshal, Bundelkhand, Baghelkhand and Malwa. The demand revived in strength in the 1990s, this time with support that crossed party lines within the region and, critically, without determined resistance in Bhopal: the government of Madhya Pradesh in the years of decision was led by Digvijaya Singh of the Congress, in office from December 1993 to December 2003, and the creation of Chhattisgarh was treated in the state capital as a settlement to be managed rather than a loss to be fought. Ajit Jogi became the first Chief Minister of the new state, whose capital was Raipur, the region's own largest city and its historical administrative centre.

The hill demand in Uttar Pradesh was the most turbulent of the three in its final decade. The Uttarakhand Kranti Dal had carried the claim for a separate hill state from 1979, arguing that the Garhwal and Kumaon hill districts, together with the hill population of the Terai, could not be governed well from Lucknow across a distance that was cultural and economic as much as physical. The demand turned into mass agitation in 1994, after the state government of the day coupled the statehood question with a reservation policy the hills rejected, and the agitation met force: firing on demonstrators at Khatima on 1 September 1994, and, on 2 October 1994, firing at Rampur Tiraha in Muzaffarnagar district on activists travelling to a rally in Delhi, in which protesters were killed and women marchers were assaulted. Those two days fixed the moral terms of the demand; after 1994 a separate hill state was a question of when and in what borders, not whether. The borders were themselves argued over, in particular whether the plains districts of Hardwar and Udham Singh Nagar belonged in the new state, and the Act as passed included both. The new state's name in the Act was Uttaranchal, a name the hill movement had never marched under, and its replacement by Uttarakhand six years later is treated below as part of this article's record.

One thread runs through all three histories and matters for the legal analysis that follows: the territories were, in the language of the brief these Acts answer to, geographically, economically and, in the Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh cases, substantially tribally distinct from the remainder of the parent state. The plateau, the rice plain and the hills each had a centre of their own (Ranchi, Raipur, Dehradun) that already functioned as the region's capital in waiting. The treatment of the tribal territories in the successor states connects this article to the land and forest rights cluster, and the reader following that thread should read [the Forest Rights Act 2006 explained](/2017/03/15/forest-rights-act-2006-explained/) alongside, because the Fifth Schedule areas in Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand are among the principal theatres of that later statute. What the movements had not settled, in any of the three cases, was the housekeeping: which officer would serve which state, which share of the debt would follow which population, and which court would hear a case filed in October 2000 about land in a district that changed states in November. That is the work the Acts exist to do, and it is work of a kind that movements cannot perform for themselves.


## The middle decades: from the 1956 refusal to the 2000 settlement

The three demands share a biography: each was placed before the States Reorganisation Commission in the middle 1950s, in substance if not in identical form, and each was refused or deferred, and the forty-five years between the refusal and the settlement are the real legislative history of the three Acts. The Commission, chaired by Fazl Ali with H. N. Kunzru and K. M. Panikkar as members, reported in 1955 and supplied the reasoning of the 1956 settlement. For the Jharkhand plateau it recorded the demand and the tribal distinctness behind it, and declined the separate state, reasoning in part from the plateau's mixed population and its economic integration with the Bihar plain; the plateau's representatives in the Bihar Assembly were left to work through the politics of the undivided state, which they did, with diminishing returns, for two generations. For the Chhattisgarh region the Commission's settlement folded the Chhattisgarhi-speaking districts into the great central state it assembled from the Central Provinces and Berar, Madhya Bharat, Vindhya Pradesh and Bhopal, judging a viable Madhya Pradesh to need the region's agricultural surplus and its minerals. For the hills of Uttar Pradesh the Commission was not the forum, since the 1956 settlement barely touched that state's boundaries, and the hill districts' grievance continued to be prosecuted inside the largest state politics in the country, where eight or ten hill legislators counted for little against the hundreds returned by the plain.

The decades after 1956 gave each demand a second education. The Jharkhand movement learned organisation in the coal belt and in the Santhal Parganas, and its parliamentary wing learned the limits of a regional party inside a state Assembly dominated by the plain; the Jharkhand Party's merger into the Congress in the 1960s dissolved the demand's first vehicle, and the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha rebuilt it from 1972 on a programme that joined tribal land and forest grievances to the wages and housing of mine and steel workers, which is why the movement of the 1990s could mobilise beyond the tribal districts into the industrial towns the Act would transfer. The Chhattisgarh demand learned patience inside the Congress and later inside both national parties, its regional leadership arguing the case in the language of development administration rather than identity, until by the late 1990s the demand had the rare property of being nobody's enemy in the state that would lose the territory. The hill movement learned the street: the Uttarakhand Kranti Dal's foundation in 1979 gave the demand a permanent party, the reservation agitation of 1994 gave it martyrs, and the shootings at Khatima and Rampur Tiraha gave it a moral claim that no subsequent government in Lucknow or Delhi could answer with delay. By the time a government at the Centre held all three promises in its programme, the three demands had each, in different registers, exhausted the alternatives to statehood: autonomous councils had been tried in Jharkhand, regional development boards and assurances had been tried in Chhattisgarh's case in substance, and in the hills the argument itself had been settled by 1994. The Acts of 2000 are short because the arguments of 1955 to 2000 were long.

## The appointed days in practice

The three appointed days were working days in the fullest sense, and the record of who took office where fixes the human content of the deeming provisions. In Raipur on 1 November 2000, Ajit Jogi was sworn in as the first Chief Minister of Chhattisgarh, at the head of a Congress government formed out of the transferred members of the undivided Assembly, and Dinesh Nandan Sahay took office as the state's first Governor; the High Court at Bilaspur was inaugurated the same day. In Dehradun, seated as the interim capital of Uttaranchal, Surjit Singh Barnala became the new state's first Governor and Nityanand Swami of the Bharatiya Janata Party its first Chief Minister on 9 November 2000, governing with the provisional Assembly of thirty until the elections of February 2002 replaced it with a House of the intended strength. In Ranchi on 15 November 2000, Prabhat Kumar became Jharkhand's first Governor and Babulal Marandi its first Chief Minister, at the head of a coalition government of the alliance that had carried the reorganisation at the Centre. Three features of these arrangements repay notice. Each first government was formed from the transferred legislature without an election, exactly as the Acts contemplated, and each therefore began with a majority assembled by party affiliation rather than by a fresh verdict, a legitimacy the template accepts as the price of continuity. Each first Chief Minister came from the region and its movement in the broad sense, Jogi a former district collector of the region turned politician, Marandi a leader of the statehood alliance in the plateau, Swami a hill politician of long standing. And each state began with its Congress or coalition arithmetic already complicated by the allocation of its officers, so that the first administrative act of every new government was the reception of lists: of members, of judges' files, of pending cases, and of the thousands of servants whose final state was still to be determined.

## The constitutional door: Article 3 and three different answers from the parents

None of the three Acts is a constitutional amendment, and none needed to be. Article 3 of the Constitution empowers Parliament by law to form a new state by separation of territory from any state, to increase or diminish the area of any state, and to alter the boundaries or the name of any state. Two conditions guard the power, and both are procedural. A bill for the purpose may be introduced in either House only on the recommendation of the President. And where the proposal affects the area, boundaries or name of an existing state, the bill must be referred by the President to the legislature of that state for expressing its views within a period the President may fix and extend. Article 4 then supplies the machinery that makes the scheme workable: a law under Article 3 may itself amend the First and Fourth Schedules and contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions, and no such law is deemed an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368. A reorganisation is therefore carried by ordinary legislative majority, and the three Acts of 2000 were carried exactly so.

The reference to the state legislature is the point on which the three cases diverge, and the divergence has to be described accurately, because it is often flattened into a claim that all three parents consented. The Supreme Court settled the legal meaning of the proviso early, in Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay in 1960: the reference is for the expression of views, the views are not a veto, and Parliament may proceed on the expiry of the period whether the legislature has answered favourably, unfavourably or not at all. What the proviso secures is that the affected state is heard on the record, not that it agrees. Measured against that standard, the three parents of 2000 gave three different answers, and the article's comparative point depends on keeping them distinct.

Uttar Pradesh answered in favour, repeatedly and in advance. The Uttar Pradesh legislature had recommended a separate hill state years before the bill existed: resolutions recommending the establishment of Uttaranchal were adopted by the Legislative Assembly in 1991 and by both Houses in August 1994, the Assembly on 24 August and the Legislative Council on 25 August, and the Allahabad High Court recorded those resolutions when the question of the region's distinct identity came before it in the Manvendra Shah litigation. Successive state governments in Lucknow forwarded the recommendation to the Centre. When the bill of 2000 came, the reference under the proviso was a formality on a question the legislature had already answered four times over.

Madhya Pradesh answered with acquiescence that amounted to support. The demand for Chhattisgarh had advocates in both national parties inside the state, the state government led by Digvijaya Singh did not obstruct the proposal, and no resolution of resistance was placed in Parliament's way. The political economy of the case helps explain the temper: Madhya Pradesh after division remained one of the largest states in the Union, retained its capital at Bhopal, retained the seat of its High Court at Jabalpur, and shed a region whose development demands its own politicians had long said Bhopal could not meet. Division was presented, and received, as administrative rationalisation.

Bihar did not consent, and the record should not be rewritten to say it did. The state government of the period was led by the Rashtriya Janata Dal, first under Lalu Prasad Yadav and then under Rabri Devi, and Lalu Prasad Yadav had opposed the bifurcation in the most quoted sentence of the whole episode, declaring that Jharkhand would be created over his dead body. The objections were economic and stated as such: the undivided state's mineral wealth, its industrial towns and a large part of its revenue base lay in the southern districts, and a truncated Bihar would be left with the flood-prone northern plain and the weaker exchequer. Nitish Kumar, then in a different party formation, supported the creation of Jharkhand in principle while pressing the claim that the Centre owed the truncated Bihar compensation and special status, a claim that outlived the reorganisation by decades. Parliament heard the views, as the proviso requires, and proceeded regardless, as Babulal Parate permits. The legal significance of the contrast is the point to hold: the one reorganisation of the three that lacked the parent legislature's support produced no constitutional litigation about the creation itself, because there was nothing to litigate. Consent is politics under Article 3, not law; and that is a large part of why all three creations, consented or not, were legally unassailable from the day the notifications issued.

### Did Parliament need the parent states to agree?

No. Article 3 requires the President to refer the bill to the affected state legislature for its views, and the Supreme Court held in Babulal Parate that the views do not bind Parliament. Uttar Pradesh had resolved in favour, Madhya Pradesh acquiesced, and Bihar objected; all three reorganisations were equally valid in law.

A further detail of the Bihar passage deserves recording because it shows the same point from the drafting side. In the debates of the period the alternative name Vananchal was canvassed for the new state, a name carrying a forestry rather than a tribal association, and the movement's own name, Jharkhand, is the name the enacted Act carries in its formation section. The name in an Act is chosen by Parliament, not by the parent state and not by the movement, and the survival of the movement's name in the text is a political fact about 2000, not a legal entitlement.

## The passage of the three bills

The legislative history of the three Acts is compressed, and the compression is itself a finding. The demands were decades old; the bills took a single session. The government of the day, the National Democratic Alliance led by Atal Bihari Vajpayee, had carried commitments to all three states into office, and the three bills were moved in the monsoon session of 2000, debated in the last days of July and the first days of August, and passed by both Houses within days of one another. The Bihar bill's passage dates are the most fully recorded of the three: the Lok Sabha passed it on 2 August 2000, the Rajya Sabha on 11 August 2000, and the President's assent followed on 25 August 2000. The Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh bills travelled the same route in the same weeks. Nothing in the passage resembled the long parliamentary sieges of earlier federal questions: no joint committee, no reference to a select committee that rewrote the text, no constitutional point of order that threatened the enterprise. The debates divided, as the Article 3 discussion above records, on the economics and the politics, not on the drafting.

The interval between assent in August and the appointed days in November was bridged by the commencement device each Act carries: the Act comes into force on the appointed day, and the appointed day is the day the Central Government notifies. The device does two kinds of work. It lets Parliament enact a complete settlement while leaving the executive a controlled window in which to prepare the settlement's operation, the allocation of officers being the largest single task, since the interim rule of the services part runs from the posting held on the appointed day and the posting pattern had therefore to be frozen and known in advance. And it lets the three creations be staggered across a fortnight, 1, 9 and 15 November, so that the administrative machinery of the Centre, which had to issue three sets of notifications, allocation orders and consequential directions, dealt with one appointed day at a time. The notifications themselves, S.O. 827(E) and S.O. 829(E) of 14 September 2000 for Madhya Pradesh and Bihar and S.O. 950(E) of 20 October 2000 for Uttar Pradesh, were issued weeks ahead of the days they appointed, which gave every affected office a dated certainty to work toward.

The preparation window also discloses something about the template's assumptions. A reorganisation Act on this pattern presumes a cooperative administrative machine: the two successor governments do not exist until the appointed day, so the preparation is necessarily done by the undivided state's administration and the Centre together, and the Act's own advisory and consultative machinery for services and assets only fully engages once there are two governments to consult. In 2000 the presumption held in all three cases. Even in Bihar, where the state government opposed the creation politically, the administration executed the division, because the Act left it no lawful alternative and because the professional services, whose members were themselves being allocated, had every incentive to make the machine work. The distinction is worth generalising: political opposition to a reorganisation expresses itself in the legislature and the press; administrative obstruction would express itself in the files, and the template is built so that the files move by force of the Act itself, through deeming provisions and continuance clauses that operate without any official having to choose to cooperate.

## Drawing the map: the formation sections

Part II of each Act is short, and its brevity is a design decision worth noticing. Section 3 of the Madhya Pradesh Act provides that on and from the appointed day there shall be formed a new state to be known as the State of Chhattisgarh, comprising sixteen named districts of the existing state: Bastar, Bilaspur, Dantewada, Dhamtari, Durg, Janjgir-Champa, Jashpur, Kanker, Kawardha, Korba, Koria, Mahasamund, Raigarh, Raipur, Rajnandgaon and Surguja. Section 3 of the Uttar Pradesh Act forms Uttaranchal from thirteen named districts: Pauri Garhwal, Tehri Garhwal, Uttarkashi, Chamoli, Dehradun, Nainital, Almora, Pithoragarh, Udham Singh Nagar, Bageshwar, Champawat, Rudraprayag and Hardwar. Section 3 of the Bihar Act forms Jharkhand from eighteen named districts: Bokaro, Chatra, Deoghar, Dhanbad, Dumka, Garhwa, Giridih, Godda, Gumla, Hazaribagh, Kodarma, Lohardaga, Pakur, Palamau, Ranchi, Sahebganj, Singhbhum (East) and Singhbhum (West). In each Act the companion section provides that the parent state shall comprise the territories other than those specified, and the amendment section then writes the change into the First Schedule: Chhattisgarh entered as entry 26, Uttaranchal as entry 27 and Jharkhand as entry 28 in the list of states, each defined by reference back to the formation section of its Act.

Three features of this drafting carry the analysis. The first is that the territory is defined by whole districts as they stood on the appointed day. No village-level boundary commission was needed, no district was split between the successor states, and the reference in the definitions to a district is construed as the area comprised within that district on the appointed day, which freezes the map at the moment of transfer even though both governments remained free, under the saving section each Act carries, to alter district names, areas and boundaries within their own states afterwards. A boundary drawn on whole districts cannot generate a boundary dispute of the kind that a line drawn through districts generates; there is no enclave problem, no river thalweg problem, no village claimed by both sides. The second feature is that the districts chosen were, in each case, districts that already sat in recognisable regional groupings with their own commissioners' divisions and administrative habits, so the new state inherited a working district administration wholesale rather than assembling one. The third feature is what the formation sections do not contain: no provision for a shared capital, no provision for a joint institution of governance, no condition subsequent, and no referendum. The creation is absolute on the appointed day, and everything else in the Act is machinery for consequences.

The three Acts side by side make the template and its variations visible at a glance, and the comparison is the findable artifact of this article. ## The district lists read closely

A formation section is a list, and lists repay reading. The sixteen districts of the Madhya Pradesh Act compose a region with two distinct characters: the rice-growing plain of the Mahanadi basin around Raipur, Durg, Rajnandgaon and Bilaspur, and the forested highlands of Bastar in the south and Surguja in the north, among the most heavily tribal districts in India, with the coal-bearing districts of Korba and Raigarh between. The Act takes the whole of the old Chhattisgarh region and nothing outside it; no district of the Mahakoshal or Bundelkhand regions was claimed or transferred, and the line the Act draws is the line the region's own administrative history had already drawn. The thirteen districts of the Uttar Pradesh Act are the eight hill districts of Garhwal and Kumaon in their post-1990s subdivided form, Pauri Garhwal, Tehri Garhwal, Uttarkashi, Chamoli, Rudraprayag, Dehradun, Nainital, Almora, Bageshwar, Pithoragarh and Champawat, together with two districts that are not hill districts at all: Hardwar, in the plain at the foot of the Shivaliks, and Udham Singh Nagar, in the Terai, whose inclusion was resisted in the parliamentary debates by members who argued that a hill state should be made of hills, and whose retention in the Act reflects the Terai's economic and demographic entanglement with the hill economy and the strength of the statehood movement in the Terai towns themselves. The eighteen districts of the Bihar Act compose the Chota Nagpur plateau and the Santhal Parganas, the whole of Bihar south of the Ganga plain with its coalfields at Dhanbad and Bokaro, its steel city at Jamshedpur in Singhbhum (East), and its administrative capital in waiting at Ranchi; and the list is notable for what the older, larger Jharkhand idea would have added and the Act excludes, the districts of the movement's maximal map in Bengal, Orissa and Madhya Pradesh, which no Article 3 bill confined to the reorganisation of Bihar could have reached.

The lists also record the state of district administration in 2000, frozen at the moment of transfer by the definitions clause. Several of the named districts were themselves young: Dantewada and Kabirdham (Kawardha) among Chhattisgarh's, Bageshwar, Champawat and Rudraprayag among Uttaranchal's, carved from older districts in the 1990s as the undivided states subdivided their remoter regions. The reorganisation inherited that subdivision and then accelerated it, since each successor government found in the saving power to alter districts the readiest instrument for bringing administration closer, and the district counts of all three states have grown steadily from the transferred base. For the reader of the Acts, the lesson of the lists is that a reorganisation by whole districts is only as clean as the district map it inherits, and that the draftsmen of 2000 were fortunate in a map whose districts had been redrawn in the preceding decade along the very regional lines the Acts would constitutionalise.

## Representation: seating two states where one sat before

Part III of each Act solves a problem that is easy to state and delicate to solve: on the appointed day, every parliamentary and assembly constituency in the undivided state has a sitting member, the Houses cannot be left unrepresentative of either successor state for a day, and no election can be held in advance for a state that does not yet exist. The template's solution has three limbs. For the Council of States, the Act amends the Fourth Schedule to divide the parent state's seats between the successor states and then, by schedules to the Act itself, allocates the sitting members between the two states by name and by the expiry dates of their terms, so that each successor state's contingent retires in the ordinary biennial rhythm without a special election. For the House of the People, the Act fixes the number of seats allotted to each successor state and provides that the sitting members elected from constituencies falling in the transferred territory become members for the corresponding constituencies of the new state, the existing delimitation of constituencies continuing to operate until the next delimitation. For the Legislative Assembly, the Act fixes the strength of the new Assembly and constitutes it, in the first instance, out of the sitting members of the parent Assembly who represent constituencies in the transferred territory, those members being deemed to have been elected to the new Assembly and serving the remainder of the term for which the parent Assembly was elected.

The numbers, applied, are these. Madhya Pradesh had forty seats in the House of the People; eleven went to Chhattisgarh and twenty-nine remained. Its sixteen seats in the Council of States divided into five for Chhattisgarh and eleven for Madhya Pradesh. Its Assembly of 320 members divided into an Assembly of ninety for Chhattisgarh, formed of the members from the transferred constituencies, and 230 for the residue. Uttar Pradesh had eighty-five seats in the House of the People; five went to Uttaranchal and eighty remained. Its thirty-four seats in the Council of States divided into three for Uttaranchal and thirty-one for Uttar Pradesh. Bihar had fifty-four seats in the House of the People; fourteen went to Jharkhand and forty remained. Its twenty-two seats in the Council of States divided into six for Jharkhand and sixteen for Bihar, and its Assembly of 324 divided into eighty-one members for Jharkhand and 243 for the residue. In each case the new state's share tracks its share of the undivided state's population, the same proportionality the definitions section encodes in the population ratio, and the Acts carry the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes reservation of constituencies across the division by continuing the delimitation orders in force, so that the reserved constituencies lying in the transferred territory simply became reserved constituencies of the new state. The longer story of how those constituency boundaries were drawn, frozen on the 1971 census and later readjusted within states, belongs to [the Delimitation Acts explained](/2018/04/01/delimitation-acts-india-explained/), which this article links at this point because the representation settlement of 2000 cannot be understood without the freeze that made it arithmetic rather than politics.

One case broke the pattern, and the exception is among the most instructive devices in the three Acts. The hill and Terai constituencies of Uttar Pradesh returned too few members to the undivided Assembly to constitute a plausible legislature for Uttaranchal at the strength the Act intended. The Uttar Pradesh Act therefore constituted a provisional Legislative Assembly for the new state of thirty members: twenty-two drawn from the members of the Uttar Pradesh Legislative Assembly representing the transferred constituencies, and eight drawn from the members of the Uttar Pradesh Legislative Council representing the region, a borrowing from the upper House that only a bicameral parent could supply. That provisional body carried the state until the first general election, held in February 2002, filled the seventy seats the Act had fixed for the Assembly, with one further member nominated from the Anglo-Indian community in the manner the Constitution then allowed, making a House of seventy-one. The device deserves its place in the template's history: it shows the draftsmen preferring an admitted stopgap, openly labelled provisional, to the pretence that a transferred rump could simply be renamed a legislature.

### How were the new Assemblies filled without an election?

By deeming. The sitting members of the parent Assembly elected from constituencies in the transferred territory became members of the new state's Assembly for the remainder of the term. Only Uttaranchal needed a different device, a provisional Assembly of thirty members drawn from both Houses of the Uttar Pradesh legislature, because its transferred constituencies returned too few members.

The representation part of each Act also disposes of the parent state's upper House where one existed. Bihar and Uttar Pradesh were bicameral; Madhya Pradesh was not. For Bihar, the Legislative Council continued as the Council of the truncated state, with the Acts' provisions adjusting its composition to the reduced territory; for Uttar Pradesh, the Council likewise continued at Lucknow. No new Legislative Council was created for any of the three successor states, a silence worth registering: the template constitutes the institutions a new state cannot function without, the Assembly, the government, the High Court and the services, and leaves optional institutions to the new state's own later choices under the Constitution.

## The constituency settlement in detail

The representation part rewards a closer look, because it performs a feat of constitutional joinery that is easy to miss. The total strength of the House of the People and of each Assembly was, in 2000, frozen by the constitutional amendments that had suspended readjustment of seats between states until the first census after 2026. A reorganisation that created new states inside that freeze had therefore to divide existing seats, not create new ones, and to do so without disturbing the inter-state balance the freeze protected. The three Acts do exactly this: the successor states' seat totals sum, in every case, to the parent state's frozen total, forty for Madhya Pradesh dividing into twenty-nine and eleven, eighty-five for Uttar Pradesh into eighty and five, fifty-four for Bihar into forty and fourteen, with the Council of States totals dividing on the same principle. Within each state, the Acts avoid any fresh delimitation of constituency boundaries. The delimitation orders in force continue to apply, the constituencies that fall in the transferred territory are assigned to the new state as they stand, and the Election Commission is empowered to make the consequential adjustments of description and extent that a change of state boundary requires, without redrawing the electoral map. The first elections in the new states were therefore fought, in Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand in the ordinary cycle and in Uttaranchal in February 2002, on the same constituency boundaries the undivided states had used, a continuity that removed election administration from the list of things a new state had to invent.

Reserved seats crossed the boundary by the same continuity. The Acts preserve the reservation of seats for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes in the Houses and Assemblies as the delimitation orders had determined them, so that the new Assemblies began with their reserved complement fixed by the transferred constituencies: in the Assembly of Chhattisgarh, ten seats reserved for the Scheduled Castes and twenty-nine for the Scheduled Tribes out of ninety; in Jharkhand, nine and twenty-eight out of eighty-one; in Uttaranchal, thirteen and two out of seventy. The Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh figures describe the demographic fact that drove the politics: assemblies in which well over a third, and in Chhattisgarh well over two fifths, of the seats are tribal-reserved are legislatures of a different character from the parent Houses they left, and the reorganisation changed the internal politics of all six legislatures, the parents' included, by concentrating the transferred regions' representatives in Houses of their own. In the House of the People the same pattern appears in smaller numbers: of Chhattisgarh's eleven seats, four are reserved for the Scheduled Tribes and one for the Scheduled Castes; of Jharkhand's fourteen, five for the Scheduled Tribes and one for the Scheduled Castes; of Uttaranchal's five, one for the Scheduled Castes.

A further provision of the representation parts deals with a contingency the reader might not anticipate: the sitting member whose constituency straddled the new boundary, or whose constituency was altered in its state affiliation by the transfer. Because the Acts transfer whole districts and constituencies were delimited within districts, the contingency barely arises, and the template's deeming provisions, which attach the member to the constituency he was elected from wherever that constituency has gone, dispose of it without residue. The near-absence of the problem is a consequence of the district-based map praised in the formation sections above: an electoral geography inherited intact is one of the dividends of drawing the line on district boundaries.

## The tribal dimension: Fifth Schedule areas and a constitutional echo

Two of the three transferred territories carried a constitutional character that the Acts themselves barely mention and that shaped the successor states profoundly: they contained extensive Scheduled Areas under the Fifth Schedule to the Constitution. The southern and northern districts of Chhattisgarh and the greater part of the Jharkhand plateau are Fifth Schedule country, in which the Governor carries special responsibilities for the administration of tribal areas, regulations may be made for the peace and good government of those areas and for restricting the transfer of tribal land, and a Tribes Advisory Council advises on the welfare of the Scheduled Tribes. Reorganisation did not alter the Fifth Schedule's text, which speaks of states generally, but it altered the Schedule's operation: the advisory councils, the Governors' annual reports on Scheduled Area administration, and the land-protection regimes of the successor states answered to Raipur and Ranchi rather than to Bhopal and Patna, and the political weight of the tribal interest inside the smaller state was transformed, since the Scheduled Tribes form a far larger share of Chhattisgarh's and Jharkhand's population than they had formed of the undivided states. A provision of the Constitution registered the change six years later. The proviso to Article 164(1) had long required the states of Bihar, Madhya Pradesh and Orissa to have a Minister in charge of tribal welfare; after the reorganisation the requirement no longer fitted the map, since truncated Bihar's tribal population had largely passed to Jharkhand, and the Constitution (Ninety-fourth Amendment) Act, 2006 rewrote the proviso to name Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Orissa, removing Bihar and seating the two new tribal-majority-interest states in its place. The amendment is a useful specimen of reorganisation's long tail: the Acts of 2000 amended the First and Fourth Schedules themselves, as Article 4 permits, but consequential amendments elsewhere in the constitutional text, which Article 4 does not reach, waited for the ordinary amendment process and arrived in their own time.

The point connects the 2000 Acts to the larger series of which they form a part. The social justice legislation of the plateau and the rice plain, the land alienation protections, the working of the panchayats extension statute in the Scheduled Areas, all operate differently inside states where the tribal population is a quarter or more of the whole than they did inside the large undivided states, and the reorganisation is therefore part of the prehistory of those subjects even though its text never addresses them. The Acts are silent on the Fifth Schedule because the Schedule needed no amendment; the silence is a reminder that a reorganisation statute's effects are not confined to its four corners.

## A High Court for each new state

The judicial part of each Act performs for the courts what the representation part performs for the legislatures. From the appointed day, each Act constitutes a separate High Court for the new state: the High Court of Chhattisgarh, seated at Bilaspur, which began functioning on 1 November 2000 as the nineteenth High Court of India; the High Court of Uttaranchal, seated at Nainital, from 9 November 2000; and the High Court of Jharkhand, seated at Ranchi, from 15 November 2000, where the Patna High Court had long maintained its Ranchi bench, so that the new court inherited a working seat, a bar and a body of pending business at a known address. From the same day, the parent High Court, at Jabalpur, at Allahabad and at Patna respectively, ceased to have jurisdiction over the transferred territory, save as the Acts preserve for proceedings in transit.

The personnel provisions follow the same deeming logic as the Assemblies. Such of the judges of the parent High Court as were identified for the purpose under the Act's machinery became judges of the new High Court on the appointed day, carrying their seniority and conditions with them, and the new court's Chief Justice was appointed under the Constitution in the ordinary way. The alternative, a new court with no judges until the appointment process ran, would have left the new state without a constitutional court for months; the template instead treats the judiciary, like the services, as a cadre to be divided rather than a body to be founded. Subordinate courts required no division at all in their own right, since a district court sits in a district and the district itself had been assigned entire: the courts of the transferred districts simply became courts of the new state, under its High Court, from the appointed day.

The transitional provisions then handle the untidy residue. Proceedings pending before the parent High Court that arose within the transferred territory stand transferred to the new High Court, which is given the files, the record and the jurisdiction to dispose of them as if they had been instituted before it; the Madhya Pradesh Act carries this in its section 83 and the Bihar Act in its section 89, with definitions of the corresponding court in the successor state to resolve cases of doubt. Orders already passed by the parent High Court before the appointed day retain their force and are enforceable by the corresponding court of the new state. And the Acts preserve the profession's continuity in a small clause that examination candidates rarely notice and practitioners of the time valued: a pleader enrolled and entitled to practise in the subordinate courts of the undivided state remained entitled to practise in those courts for one year from the appointed day, notwithstanding the transfer of the territory, as the Madhya Pradesh Act provides in its section 84. The general statement in this article is legal information about a settled statutory scheme and is no substitute for advice on any individual proceeding, whose forum and limitation questions turn on its own facts.

Below the High Courts, the subordinate judiciary divided itself by the same territorial logic, with one refinement. The district and subordinate courts of the transferred districts became courts of the new state on the appointed day, and the judicial officers manning them, who belonged to the state judicial service of the undivided state, were allocated between the successor states under the services machinery like other state servants, subject to the constitutional position that control over district courts and the officers of the subordinate judiciary vests in the High Court: from the appointed day, the new High Court at Bilaspur, Nainital or Ranchi exercised that control over the courts and officers within its territory, and appointments of district judges in the new state were thereafter made by its Governor in consultation with its own High Court under Article 233. The Chhattisgarh court's first working day illustrates the division's completeness: it was inaugurated at Bilaspur on 1 November 2000 with Justice R. S. Garg, a judge of the Madhya Pradesh High Court identified for the new court, as its first acting Chief Justice, in the presence of the Chief Justice of the parent court whose judges had been divided to staff it.

What did not happen is as significant as what did. There was no litigation over the constitution of the three courts, no challenge to the transfer of judges, and no period in which any of the three states lacked a functioning High Court. The contrast with the judicial arrangements of the 2014 reorganisation, where a common High Court served two states for years while its eventual division was debated, belongs to the contrast section below; here it is enough to mark that the 2000 template treats a separate High Court from the first morning as a non-negotiable part of statehood, and delivers it by the simple expedient of dividing an existing court rather than designing a new one.

## Assets and liabilities: the location rule, the ratio, and the reference to the Centre

The apportionment part of each Act is where a reorganisation stops being constitutional symbolism and becomes accountancy, and the template resolves it by applying two different principles to two different kinds of item. Assets that are located in a territory pass to the state in which they are located: land, buildings, stores, and the fixed works of government follow the map, because any other rule would produce a state administering another state's secretariat. Liabilities, and those assets that are not located anywhere in particular, are apportioned between the successor states in the population ratio that the definitions section fixes from the 1991 census: for Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh, 485.7 to 176.2; for Bihar and Jharkhand, 645.30 to 218.44. The public debt of the undivided state, its guarantees, and its obligations in respect of pensions and provident funds are worked through on that ratio basis, with machinery for adjustment where an asset or a liability is later found to have been misdescribed, and for the accounts of the transition period itself, since revenue collected and expenditure incurred around the appointed day does not divide as cleanly as a balance sheet date suggests.

Between the two clean principles lies the untidy middle: the institutions of the undivided state that served both territories and could not be cut along the boundary. Each Act therefore carries provisions for the corporations, boards and companies of the undivided state: their assets and liabilities are to be apportioned between the successor states, in the first instance by agreement between the two governments, and in default of agreement by order of the Central Government, whose determination the Acts make the operative settlement. The state electricity boards were the largest of these undertakings and generated the most consequential litigation of the apportionment. In the dispute over the division of the Madhya Pradesh electricity board's balances, the Supreme Court worked with an apportionment of current assets and liabilities keyed to the ratio of power consumption between the two states, roughly seventy-seven to twenty-three on the figures before the Court, and declined to treat that basis as arbitrary: the episode illustrates the general pattern, which is that the courts did not reopen the principle of division but policed the choice of measure within it, asking whether the ratio adopted bore a rational relation to the thing divided. A consumption ratio for an electricity board, a population ratio for general debt, a location rule for a building: the template's realism lies in refusing to force one measure onto unlike items.

A second line of litigation tested not the division between the states but the continuity of benefits promised by the undivided state to private parties. In Commissioner of Commercial Tax, Ranchi v. Swarn Rekha Cokes and Coals, the Supreme Court considered units in the transferred territory that held exemption from sales tax under the undivided Bihar's industrial policy of 1995 and a notification under the Bihar Finance Act, 1981, and the question was whether benefits that had crystallised before the appointed day survived the extinction of the state that granted them. Reading the continuance and adaptation provisions of the Bihar Act, sections 84 and 85 in that Act's numbering, the Court treated the benefit as surviving in the successor state: the reorganisation had continued the laws in force rather than wiping the slate, and an entitlement earned under the old law within the transferred territory was enforceable against the successor that inherited the territory. The proposition generalises, and it is one of the template's quiet strengths: reorganisation changes the sovereign address of a territory, not the legal position of the people in it, unless the Act or a later competent legislature says otherwise.

Pensions deserve a separate sentence because they produced the longest tail of inter-state accounting. The Acts place the liability for pensions of servants who retired before the appointed day, and for the pensionable service of those who continued, on a shared basis worked out between the successor states, and the settlement between Bihar and Jharkhand on pension liabilities was still being quantified in public controversy years afterwards, with figures in the thousands of crores asserted on the Bihar side. The pattern repeats the article's larger finding: the template's principles were accepted, and the disputes were about measurement and money, fought through correspondence, committees and occasional litigation, not about the legitimacy of the division itself.

## The finance clauses: expenditure, revenue and the first budgets

Between the apportionment part and the services part, each Act carries the financial machinery that keeps two governments funded across the appointed day, and the machinery deserves attention because a state that cannot lawfully spend money on 2 November is not yet a state in any sense that matters to its citizens. The template's provisions run on several tracks. Each Act authorises expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of the successor state for the remainder of the financial year, on the strength of the appropriation made for the undivided state apportioned between the successors, so that salaries, pensions and works in progress continued to be paid without waiting for the new Assembly to pass its first budget. Each Act provides for the distribution between the successor states of the net proceeds of taxes and duties for the transition period, following the Finance Commission's devolution pattern and the population basis the Act fixes, with the Comptroller and Auditor General's certification machinery adapted to the divided accounts. And each Act handles the unglamorous tail of public finance: the deposits, provident funds, and treasury balances standing in the name of the undivided state on the appointed day, which are apportioned between the successors, and the guarantees given by the undivided state for loans raised by boards and corporations, which follow the undertakings they secured into whichever successor inherited the undertaking.

The revenue side of the division carried a structural asymmetry that the politics of the period noticed at once and that no drafting could have removed. The transferred territories were not average slices of their parents. Jharkhand took the greater part of undivided Bihar's industrial base, its coal and mineral royalties and its large central public sector plants, which is why the truncated Bihar's complaint of impoverishment had substance and why its demand for compensatory treatment persisted. Chhattisgarh likewise took a share of Madhya Pradesh's mineral wealth and power generation disproportionate to its population share, while contributing a smaller share of the parent state's commercial tax base; and Uttaranchal took a territory whose revenue base was thin in relation to the cost of governing mountain districts, a structural deficit its finances have carried since. The Acts apportion what exists; they do not equalise what the division creates. The equalisation question belongs to the Finance Commission and to the politics of special assistance, and the template's refusal to write fiscal promises into the reorganisation statute, promises whose performance would depend on future budgets and future governments, is of a piece with its refusal, noted in the verdict below, to promise what a statute cannot perform. The later history of the Andhra Pradesh settlement, where expectations of fiscal commitment became the substance of the grievance, shows the cost of the opposite choice.

One consequence of the debt apportionment deserves separate notice, because it greeted each new finance minister on the first morning. A successor state inherits its population-ratio share of the undivided state's liabilities but not a corresponding share of its revenue history, and its own borrowing programme begins from that inherited stock: the market loans, the loans from the Centre, and the small savings and provident fund liabilities that the ratio assigns. The new states of 2000 therefore began life with debt stocks they had not contracted, serviced from revenues they were still assembling, and their early budgets were framed under that discipline. The template accepts the consequence as the price of the ratio's simplicity, and the alternative, tracing each loan to the works it financed and assigning it to the state that inherited the works, was considered in the drafting tradition and rejected as unworkable across thousands of instruments; where a loan was tied to a specific undertaking that passed entire to one successor, the liability followed the undertaking under the apportionment of that undertaking rather than the general ratio. The distinction, ratio for the general debt, following for the tied debt, is a small instance of the template's governing habit of matching the measure to the thing measured, the same habit the courts later approved in the electricity board litigation.

## Services: the allocation that outlasted every other question

If the map was settled in a day, the men and women who administered it were settled over years, and the services part of each Act is the part whose consequences ran longest. The starting rule is continuity: every person serving in connection with the affairs of the undivided state immediately before the appointed day continued to serve, from that day, on an interim basis, in the successor state in whose territory he was posted, until the Central Government determined his final allocation. Allocation itself was made by the Central Government by general or special order, after consultation with the governments of the successor states, and in the Bihar Act the operative provisions can be followed in sections 72 to 77: allocation in section 72, protection of conditions of service in section 73, the deeming of appointment in section 74, advisory committees in section 75, the Central Government's power to give directions in section 76, and provision for the State Public Service Commission in section 77. The other two Acts carry the same machinery in the same order, because the problem is identical: a cadre is a single ranked list, and splitting it requires a rule for every name on it.

The protective provisions matter as much as the allocating ones. No officer's conditions of service could be varied to his disadvantage by reason of the allocation without the previous approval the Acts require, a safeguard carried from the constitutional protection of civil servants into the transition itself. Officers were invited to express preferences, and the advisory committees, on which the successor states were represented, worked through the cadres service by service, weighing domicile, seniority, the needs of the smaller cadre, and the option forms, before the Centre issued its orders. For the All India Services, the Acts and the rules under the All India Services Act produced separate cadres for each successor state, so that the Indian Administrative Service and Indian Police Service cadres of the undivided states were split into cadres for the parent and the new state, with serving officers allocated between them and future recruitment feeding each cadre separately.

### Why did final allocation take years when the states took a day?

Because a cadre is a single ranked list and every name on it had to be placed without damaging seniority or conditions. Options had to be invited, advisory committees constituted for each service, two governments consulted, and the arithmetic of each cadre made to balance, while provisional allocation kept every post filled in the interval.

The litigation this machinery generated had a characteristic shape, and it is the shape the brief for this article predicts: not challenges to the reorganisation, but disputes about the incidents of allocation. The Patna High Court, in litigation arising from the Bihar division such as the proceedings concerning officers Arvind Vijay Bilung and V. N. Mishra, had to work out which successor state could suspend or discipline an officer in the interval between provisional and final allocation, and it resolved the question by the categories the Act creates: an officer finally allotted to a state answers to that state; an officer provisionally allotted and posted in a state is, during the provisional period, under that state's control and deemed appointed by it for disciplinary purposes; and an officer in respect of whom no order has been made remains in the residual category the statute contemplates. Other disputes ran to seniority within the split cadres, to the treatment of officers who had opted for one state and been allotted to the other, and to the position of employees of boards and corporations whose undertakings were themselves being apportioned. Suits and writ petitions on allocation were still being decided years after the appointed days, and final allocation orders in some cadres took correspondingly long to settle. None of this touched the validity of the states; all of it touched the lives of the officers concerned, and the honest summary is that the template performed exactly as designed at the level of principle and slowly at the level of the individual file.

The mechanics of allocation, as they worked on individual files, followed a policy pattern common to the three divisions and visible in the decided cases. An officer's option was invited and recorded, and options were honoured where the cadre's arithmetic allowed; domicile in the successor state was a weighty consideration; and where a cadre had to be divided beyond what options supplied, the burden fell, under the Government of India's allocation policy as the courts recorded it, on those with least to lose in service terms, including officers junior in the cadre's seniority, who could be called upon to serve the new state whether or not they had opted for it. Provisional allocation came first, so that no post stood vacant while the committees worked, and final allocation followed when the cadre lists were settled, which in the larger cadres and the contentious ones took years rather than months. The two-stage design explains the otherwise puzzling shape of the litigation, in which courts in 2001 and after were still being asked which state could discipline an officer: the Acts had deliberately left a class of officers in an interim condition, and the interim lasted longer than the draftsmen can have intended, because the committees' work was consensual in method and therefore slow where the two successor governments' interests in retaining senior or specialist officers diverged.

The State Public Service Commission provisions complete the institutional picture. The undivided state's Commission continued for the parent; for the new state, the Acts provide for its own Commission, with transitional arrangements under which the existing Commission could serve the new state's needs until its own body was constituted, and with the members of the existing Commission allocated in the same spirit as the judges and the officers. Recruitment in progress at the appointed day, examinations announced and selections pending, were carried across by the same continuance logic that governed the rest of the transition.

## The employees the services part does not reach

The services part governs persons serving in connection with the affairs of the state, and its limits define a further class the settlement had to place: the employees of the undivided state's public undertakings, statutory corporations, boards and local bodies, who are not members of the state services and whose allocation follows not the cadre lists but the fate of their employer. Where an undertaking's area of operation lay wholly in one successor state, the undertaking and its staff passed to that state with the territory. Where it served both, the Acts' apportionment machinery for corporations applied: the undertaking continued to function for both states for an interim, and its assets, liabilities and staff were then divided, by agreement or by central order, or the undertaking was reconstituted for each state separately, as happened with the electricity boards and the forest development corporations whose affairs reached the courts. The staff of such undertakings had no option forms and no advisory committees of the services pattern; their security lay in the continuity of the undertaking and in the general principle, which the courts enforced in the continuity litigation, that reorganisation does not extinguish accrued service benefits. Employees of municipalities and panchayats in the transferred districts presented the simplest case of all: their employer was a local body seated in a district, the district changed its state, and the employment continued untouched, a reminder that the greater part of public employment in a transferred territory is entirely undisturbed by a reorganisation, and that the celebrated difficulties of allocation concern the state-level cadres and the head-office staffs of the joint undertakings, a small and senior fraction of the whole.

The audit and accounts machinery divided on the same principle. The offices of the Accountant General serving the undivided state were reorganised so that each successor state had its own audit establishment, the accounts of the transition year were rendered for the undivided state to the appointed day and for each successor thereafter, and the Comptroller and Auditor General's reports for the divided year speak in the divided form. The detail is clerical, and it completes the picture the finance clauses began: by the close of the transition, every rupee of the six states could be appropriated, spent, accounted and audited within its own constitutional machinery, which is the operational definition of a completed reorganisation.

## Continuance of laws, and the safety valves at the end of each Act

The closing part of each Act is a set of devices for preventing the legal vacuum that a change of sovereignty over territory would otherwise threaten, and the devices are worth stating plainly because they, more than any other part of the template, are what a citizen of the transferred territory actually experienced. Nothing changed on the ground on the appointed day except the name of the state. The territorial extent provision, section 78 in the Madhya Pradesh Act and section 84 in the Bihar Act, declares that the reorganisation does not itself alter the territories to which any existing law extends, and that references in any law to the undivided state are to be read, until a competent legislature provides otherwise, as references to that state's territories as they stood before the appointed day, which in practice means that every Act, rule, notification and licence in force in the transferred territory continued in force there as law of the new state. The power to adapt laws then gives the appropriate government, the Centre for Union subjects and the state government for the rest, a period of two years from the appointed day to make by order such adaptations and modifications of pre-existing laws, by repeal or amendment, as the change of state requires: the Madhya Pradesh Act states the period in its section 79, and the Adaptation of Laws Orders actually made under it, such as the order of 2001 that substituted Chhattisgarh for Madhya Pradesh through the scheduled laws, show the power doing its clerical work. Behind the adaptation power stands a judicial safety valve, the power to construe laws, under which a court or authority enforcing an unadapted law may construe it, without affecting its substance, in the manner necessary to make it work in the successor state. Behind that stands the power to name authorities, so that a function the old law assigned to an officer of the undivided state can be assigned by notification to the corresponding officer of the new state. And at the end of each Act stands the power to remove difficulties: if any difficulty arises in giving effect to the Act, the President may by order do anything not inconsistent with the Act to remove it, every such order is laid before Parliament, and, as the Madhya Pradesh Act provides in its section 86, no such order may be made after three years from the appointed day. The sunset is the point. The template grants extraordinary tidying powers freely and then withdraws them on a stated date, leaving the successor legislatures, by then elected and sitting, in sole charge of their statute books.

## Water: the one addition to the template

The Bihar Act's Part IX, sections 78 to 82, on the management and development of water resources, with provision for a management board, its staff, its jurisdiction and its regulations, is the single structural addition any of the three Acts makes to the common pattern, and it is there because the Bihar division divided a river system as well as a state. The Sone and its tributaries water the agricultural heartland of the truncated Bihar from works and catchments lying in or shared with Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Uttar Pradesh, and a division of territory that ignored the hydrology would have stored up the most damaging class of inter-state dispute there is. The drafting answer was to carry the management of the shared water resources into the Act itself and to institutionalise it in a board, rather than leaving the two successor states to improvise cooperation afterwards. Even so, river questions between the successor states and their neighbours outlasted the settlement, and litigation over the Sone system in later years reached the Supreme Court with five riparian states asserting positions where the undivided map had shown fewer. The lesson generalises beyond Bihar: a reorganisation Act can divide everything that sits still, land, buildings, cadres, debt, and it still has to make special provision for the things that flow.

## What changed in the Constitution's text on each appointed day

The constitutional footprint of the three Acts is precise and can be stated completely, which is itself a consequence of the Article 3 and 4 scheme. On 1 November 2000 the First Schedule gained entry 26, Chhattisgarh, defined as the territories specified in section 3 of the Madhya Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000, and the entry for Madhya Pradesh acquired its excluding words; on 9 November entry 27, Uttaranchal, followed on the same pattern; on 15 November, entry 28, Jharkhand. On the same three days the Fourth Schedule's table of Council of States seats was rewritten for the six states concerned: Madhya Pradesh's sixteen seats became eleven for the residue and five for Chhattisgarh, Uttar Pradesh's thirty-four became thirty-one and three, and Bihar's twenty-two became sixteen and six. No other article of the Constitution required amendment by the Acts. Article 168, which lists the states having Legislative Councils, was untouched, because no new Council was created and the Councils of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh continued for the truncated states without textual change. No special provision of the Article 371 family was enacted for any of the three states, a silence that distinguishes them from several earlier and later creations and reflects the settlement's premise: the new states were ordinary states of the Union from their first day, with no transitional constitutional status, no special responsibilities cast on their Governors beyond the Fifth Schedule's standing scheme, and no asymmetry to be negotiated away later. The Governor of each new state was appointed under the ordinary power, separate appointees being named for Raipur, Dehradun and Ranchi rather than the undivided states' Governors being continued in a dual charge, although the Constitution permits one person to hold two governorships and the device has been used in other transitions.

The absence of ratification deserves its sentence. An amendment of the First or Fourth Schedule by the ordinary amendment route would have engaged Article 368 and, for the Fourth Schedule's representation of states in Parliament, the ratification of half the state legislatures; Article 4(2) disapplies that route for laws made under Articles 2 and 3, and the Supreme Court's jurisprudence from Babulal Parate onward has treated the disapplication as settled. The three reorganisations therefore rest on the votes of Parliament alone, carried in a single session, and their constitutional validity has never been questioned in any court, a stability that contrasts with the litigation that attended earlier exercises of the power and that confirms the 2000 Acts as the template's most legally uneventful application.

## The variations catalogued: where the three texts part company

Reading the three Acts against one another, the departures from the common text are few enough to list, and each answers to a fact about its territory. The Bihar Act alone carries the water resources part described above, because the Bihar division alone divided a major inter-state river system in a way that demanded a standing institution from the first day. The Uttar Pradesh Act alone carries the provisional Assembly, because the Uttar Pradesh division alone produced a transferred territory whose sitting members could not plausibly constitute its legislature, and it alone had a Legislative Council from which the deficiency could be made good. The Bihar and Uttar Pradesh Acts both had to deal with the parent Legislative Council, which the unicameral Madhya Pradesh Act had no occasion to mention. The Bihar Act alone had to carry the memory of a contested name into its text: the formation section's choice of Jharkhand over the canvassed Vananchal is a variation not of machinery but of identity, and it was settled in Parliament rather than in the drafting. The Madhya Pradesh Act's appointed day fell on 1 November, the anniversary of the formation of Madhya Pradesh itself in 1956, a calendrical symmetry the notifications do not explain and the record leaves as coincidence or quiet design. And the three Acts divide, in their apportionment parts, on the ratios their populations dictate, the only figures in the template that are different in every copy because they are measurements rather than choices.

The catalogue matters for a practical reason. A reader advising on, or examining, a future reorganisation can treat the common text as given and concentrate entirely on the variations: the subjects on which a new case will need its own Part IX are the subjects peculiar to its territory, a river system, a shared capital, a common institution, a disputed district, and the 2000 Acts teach that the draftsmen of a sound reorganisation identify those subjects in advance and legislate for them inside the Act, rather than leaving them to be discovered by litigation afterwards.

## The successor states in the federal machinery

A new state must be seated not only in Parliament but in the standing machinery of Indian federalism, and the three Acts attend to the seating. The zonal councils constituted under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 are the clearest instance: the Madhya Pradesh Act, by its section 77, substitutes the names in section 15 of the 1956 Act so that Chhattisgarh enters the Central Zonal Council beside Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh, and the Bihar Act's corresponding amendment of the same 1956 Act seats Jharkhand in the Eastern Zonal Council beside Bihar, while Uttaranchal took its place in the Central Zonal Council with its parent. The amendments are two lines long and easily overlooked, and they matter because the zonal councils, chaired by the Union Home Minister with the chief ministers of the member states, are among the few standing forums in which the disputes a reorganisation leaves behind, over water, over assets not yet divided, over the cadre files still open, can be raised at political level with the Centre in the chair. The Acts' authors, having created six states out of three, took care to leave each pair of successors in a room with a referee.

The same instinct appears in the treatment of the inter-state river questions and of the corporations whose operations crossed the new boundaries. Where an undertaking of the undivided state served both successors, the Acts contemplate its continuance as a joint undertaking until apportioned or reconstituted, rather than its instant division on the appointed day, a recognition that some institutions, an electricity board with an integrated grid, a transport undertaking with through routes, a marketing federation with stocks in both territories, cannot be split by a date in a notification without interrupting the service they exist to provide. The continuance is temporary and the apportionment duty is mandatory, but the sequencing, continuity first, division second, is the template's signature in the institutional as in the legal field, and it is the opposite of the clean-break model that a less experienced drafting might have preferred.

## The renaming of Uttaranchal

The Uttar Pradesh Act created a state whose name its own movement had not chosen. The hill movement had marched for Uttarakhand, the traditional name for the northern region of Garhwal and Kumaon, and the name enacted in 2000, Uttaranchal, was a coinage of the drafting process that never acquired affection. The correction came by the same constitutional route as the creation, a short central Act under the name-altering limb of Article 3: the Uttaranchal (Alteration of Name) Act, 2006, whose section 3 provides that as from the appointed day under that Act the State of Uttaranchal shall be known as the State of Uttarakhand, whose following sections substitute the new name in the First and Fourth Schedules, and which was brought into force on 1 January 2007 by notification GSR 779(E) of 29 December 2006. The renaming Act even reproduces, in miniature, the parent template's housekeeping: a power in the appropriate government to adapt laws for the change of name, exercisable within one year, and a power in courts to construe unadapted references. Two practical consequences followed. Every reference to Uttaranchal in the 2000 Act, in the schedules of members it carries, and in six years of state legislation had to be read, and where necessary adapted, as a reference to Uttarakhand; and the episode entered the stock of examination traps in this field, since the state created in 2000 and the state renamed in 2007 are the same state, and any account that dates Uttarakhand's creation to 2007 is wrong.

## Why these three settled, and what the smoothness does and does not prove

The namable claim of this article can be stated with its evidence. The template works when the capital is not in dispute. In each of the three cases the successor state's capital was a city inside the transferred territory that already served as the region's centre, Raipur, Dehradun and Ranchi, and in each case the parent state kept its own capital, Bhopal, Lucknow and Patna, together with the secretariat, the assembly building, and the administrative establishment seated there. Nothing had to be shared, divided, held in trust, or promised for a future date. The reader can test the claim against the counter-examples the series covers: the 2014 division of Andhra Pradesh, in which one capital city was made the common capital of two states for a period of years and the successor state's own capital became the central grievance of the settlement, generated the litigation, the protest and the sense of unfinished business that the 2000 round wholly escaped. Where the capital is not in dispute, a reorganisation is an exercise in conveyancing and cadre management, difficult but soluble; where it is in dispute, the same template acquires a wound at its centre that no drafting of the other parts can heal.

The claim needs its counter-reading stated at full strength, because the smoothness of the legal transition is easily misread as smoothness of settlement. The movements that preceded the three Acts were long and, in the hill case, bloodied: the dead of Khatima and Rampur Tiraha are not a footnote to the Uttar Pradesh Act but part of the price at which it arrived, and the Jharkhand movement's dead and displaced across decades of agitation in the plateau stand in the same relation to the Bihar Act. Within the new states, grievances of a different order persisted and persist in politics: the complaint in Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh that mineral-bearing territory contributes revenue out of proportion to what returns to it, a complaint with a long pedigree in the plateau's politics; the hardship of officers and employees allocated against their preference, some of whom litigated their files for years; and the unfinished argument, inherited by every successor state, about whether smaller states in fact govern better, an argument on which the record of the three states since 2000 offers material to both sides and a verdict to neither. The statutes governed the first of these matters, the legal transition, and governed it well. They did not govern the politics, and no statute could have. A smooth legal transition and a contested political settlement are different things, and the three Acts are evidence for the possibility of the first, not proof of the second.

For completeness, the fiscal aftermath should be recorded in the same even temper. The truncated states' claims for compensation, Bihar's demand for a special package and special category status being the most prominent, were pressed on the Centre for years and belong to the politics of federal finance rather than to the law of reorganisation; the Acts themselves made no promise of compensation, and none was enforceable. What the Acts did promise, a division of assets, liabilities and personnel on stated principles with a central referee in default of agreement, was delivered, and the delivery is the measure of the template.

## What the Acts left open

A statute that settles everything it touches still leaves a penumbra of questions it does not touch, and the three Acts' penumbra is instructive. The permanent capital of Uttaranchal was the largest open question: Dehradun was the seat of government from the appointed day and was understood on all sides to be interim, and the claim of Gairsain, a small town in the hills nearer the new state's geographical and emotional centre, was pressed from the movement years onward, gathering force whenever the complaint was heard that the plains-adjacent capital reproduced the distance the state was created to abolish. The state government eventually declared Gairsain the summer capital in 2020, a political settlement of a question the 2000 Act had been content to leave to politics. Chhattisgarh's capital question took the opposite form: Raipur was never in doubt as the seat, and the open question was the building of a new administrative city beside it, the project that became Naya Raipur, a planned capital complex raised on greenfield land in the years after statehood. Jharkhand's Ranchi was likewise never in doubt, its position as the plateau's centre having been established across a century of administration and a summer-capital past.

Below the capitals, the internal map of each new state continued to move under the saving power each Act preserves, as the successor governments created new districts out of the transferred ones: the sixteen districts of Chhattisgarh, the thirteen of Uttaranchal and the eighteen of Jharkhand have all multiplied since the appointed days, as new states characteristically bring administration closer by subdivision. The Acts also left open, because they had to, the question whether the new states would develop the administrative depth their small cadres required: the division of an All India Services cadre produces two cadres each thinner at the senior levels than the parent, and the successor states' dependence on the Centre for officers, on deputation and on accelerated promotion in the early years is a documented feature of every reorganisation, felt in 2000 as in 1956. And the Acts left open the largest question of all, whether statehood would deliver the development the movements had demanded, a question no statute can answer and on which the evidence of two decades is mixed in ways this article has already recorded from both sides.

## The argument about smaller states, as the 2000 round framed it

Because the three creations were uncontested in law, they became the standing exhibit in the general argument about the size of Indian states, and the stance charter of this series requires the argument to be given in its strongest forms on both sides. The case for division, in the form its most distinguished early exponent gave it, is B. R. Ambedkar's. In his 1955 memorandum Thoughts on Linguistic States, Ambedkar argued that very large states were a danger to democratic control and to the minorities within them, and he proposed, among other divisions, that Uttar Pradesh be split into three states and Bihar into two, on the express ground that a state too large to be governed accountably would be governed badly and would dominate the federation by its parliamentary weight. The 2000 round enacted, forty-five years later, divisions of both states on his list, and its advocates added the administrative argument he would have recognised: that a district officer's tour, a citizen's journey to the capital and a file's journey to a decision are all shortened when the state is smaller, and that the hill districts' neglect from Lucknow and the plateau's from Patna were facts of distance before they were facts of politics.

The case against, in its strongest form, is fiscal and institutional rather than sentimental. A new state duplicates the fixed costs of statehood, a governor, a legislature, a High Court, a secretariat, a police headquarters, and must fund them from a revenue base that division does not enlarge; it begins life with a thinner senior administration than either parent possessed; its politics, conducted in a smaller arena, may be more exposed to the capture of the state's principal industry, a charge laid against mineral-rich Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh in particular; and the multiplication of states multiplies the occasions of inter-state dispute over water, boundaries and assets of the very kind the apportionment parts exist to settle. On this reading the smoothness of 2000 proves little about statehood's benefits, because the legal transition is the cheap part and the expensive part is the decades of governance that follow. Both readings are honestly held and the evidence of the three states supports elements of each, which is why this article has kept its verdict where the evidence is firmest: on the statutes. Whether India should have more states is a political question that the 2000 Acts inform but do not answer; how a state should be made, when the political question is answered in favour, is a legal question they answer completely.

## The comparison the template invites: 1956 and 1966

The claim that the capital is the load-bearing variable can be tested within the template's own history, because the template had been used before under different conditions. The reorganisations of 1956 were commission-made and language-led, and they divided capitals as a matter of course: Bombay city was the prize of the bilingual Bombay state's dissolution in 1960, and the contest over it between Maharashtra and Gujarat was the central drama of that reorganisation, settled only by assigning the city to Maharashtra and leaving a grievance that Gujarati politics nursed for years. The Punjab reorganisation of 1966 is the harder case and the more instructive one. There the capital, Chandigarh, was itself part of the disputed territory: the Punjabi Suba movement's claim to the city met the counter-claim of the Hindi-speaking areas that became Haryana, and the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966 answered by making Chandigarh a Union territory and the common capital of both successor states, with the city's ultimate assignment left to a future decision that politics has never taken. The result is the standing counter-example to the 2000 experience: a reorganisation whose legal transition was completed in 1966 and whose political settlement remains open at its capital more than half a century later, generating commissions, accords and grievances in every decade since. The 2000 draftsmen did not choose their capitals; the geography of the three demands chose for them, since Raipur, Dehradun and Ranchi were inside the transferred territories and Bhopal, Lucknow and Patna were outside them. But the template's good fortune in 2000 is precisely the point: the variable was set favourably by the map, and the Acts' clean record is evidence of what the template can do when the map sets it so, and, by the Chandigarh contrast, of what no template can do when it does not.

The 1956 comparison yields a second, quieter lesson about scale. The States Reorganisation Act, 1956 redrew the whole Union in one statute; the Acts of 2000 each divided one state. The single-statute method of 1956 forced every region's claim to be traded against every other's in one negotiation, and its settlements carried the marks of the trading. The one-state-at-a-time method that the Union adopted after 1960, and that 2000 used three times in parallel, confines each settlement to its own merits and its own ratios, which is a further reason the three Acts could be so nearly identical: each was answering the same closed list of questions about one division, undistracted by the claims of the division in the next state. Simultaneity without consolidation, three statutes on one template rather than one statute for three states, is itself part of the design this article commends to the reader's attention.

## The litigation record, taken as a whole

Collected, the litigation the three Acts produced falls into four classes, and the classification is the fairest summary of the settlement's legal quality. The first class is the allocation litigation described in the services section: individual officers contesting their state, their seniority, or the disciplinary authority over them, decided on the Acts' own categories and closed without residue once the final allocation orders were made. The second class is the apportionment litigation described in the assets section: successor states and their boards disputing the measure of division, the electricity balances being the largest instance, in which the courts accepted the Acts' framework and corrected, where they corrected at all, the choice of ratio within it. The third class is the continuity litigation, of which the Swarn Rekha Cokes decision is the type: private parties and successor states disputing whether rights and obligations incurred under the undivided state's law survived in the transferred territory, answered by the courts from the continuance provisions in favour of survival, a body of decisions that runs from industrial-policy exemptions to the affairs of the undivided state's corporations, as when the Patna High Court in 2004 upheld the competence of each successor state to constitute its own forest development corporation for its territory when the undivided state's corporation was wound up. The fourth class is the class that does not exist: no challenge to the formation of any of the three states, to the constitution of their legislatures or High Courts, to the allocation of their parliamentary seats, or to the validity of the Acts themselves was entertained by any court. A reorganisation statute should be judged by the litigation it fails to generate as much as by the litigation it survives, and on that measure the three Acts of 2000 stand with the cleanest records in the template's history. The explanation returns, for the last time, to the same two causes: a map drawn on whole districts with uncontested capitals left nothing constitutional to fight about, and the housekeeping the Acts did leave open belonged to a class the courts could resolve from the text without remaking the settlement.

## The parents after division, and the demands the settlement left behind

A reorganisation has six products, not three, and the truncated parents deserve a paragraph each in any honest account. Madhya Pradesh emerged still one of the largest states of the Union by area, with its capital, its High Court and four fifths of its Assembly intact, and its politics continued across the appointed day with the least disturbance of any of the six: the same government in Bhopal that had acquiesced in the division continued in office, and the state's subsequent political history shows no scar tissue attributable to the loss of Chhattisgarh. Uttar Pradesh remained the most populous state in the country by a wide margin, its Assembly still the largest, its weight in the House of the People reduced by five seats of eighty-five; the hill districts' departure removed from its politics a region whose representatives had always been a small and often disregarded bloc, and the state's politics proceeded on the plain's own terms. Bihar's truncation was the deepest cut of the three, a third of its population, its mineral and industrial base, and its southern identity removed together, and its politics after 2000 reorganised themselves around the fact: the demand for special category status and a special package, pressed by successive governments in Patna as compensation for the division, became a standing feature of the truncated state's relations with the Centre, and the pension and asset accounts with Jharkhand remained a live file between the two secretariats for years. The three experiences confirm the asymmetry the finance clauses recorded: division costs the parent in proportion to what the transferred territory contributed, and the Acts' clean settlement of the legal questions did nothing to redistribute that cost.

The settlement also left a residue of unsatisfied geography. The Jharkhand that the movement's founders imagined had at times embraced adjoining tribal districts of Bengal, Orissa and the Central Provinces, and the Act's confinement of the new state to Bihar's districts left that larger idea as a sentiment rather than a claim, occasionally voiced and never seriously pressed, since no government of Jharkhand has sought to reopen the map the Act drew. Within Uttaranchal, the inclusion of Hardwar and Udham Singh Nagar continued to be debated after 2000 by those who had opposed it, without gathering the force to reopen the formation section. And beyond the three territories, the settlement's success became the argument of every unsatisfied region in the large states: Vidarbha's advocates in Maharashtra, Bundelkhand's across Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh, and others besides, cite the fortnight of November 2000 as proof that the Union can divide a great state without disorder when it chooses, and the citation is accurate as far as it goes. What the citation omits, and what this article has laboured to record, is the condition the three cases happened to satisfy: regions with centres of their own, capitals uncontested, and maps drawn on whole districts. A demand that cannot satisfy that condition will not be settled by the precedent it cites, because the precedent's lesson is the condition itself.

The political record of the new states in their first years also belongs to the honest account, since the movements had promised not merely statehood but better government. The delivery was uneven in the way the counter-reading in the smaller-states argument predicts. Jharkhand passed through repeated changes of chief minister and spells of President's rule in its first decade and a half, and the politics of its mineral economy drew the charges of capture that the sceptics of small states had forecast. Uttarakhand likewise saw frequent changes of chief minister and a short spell of central intervention in its Assembly's affairs. Chhattisgarh, by contrast, settled early into long tenures and stable alternation between the national parties. The variation among the three is itself the finding: the same statute, the same template and the same appointed-day machinery produced three different political trajectories, because a reorganisation Act creates the arena and cannot script the play. The Acts of 2000 are therefore best understood as a complete answer to the question they address, how to make a state lawfully, cleanly and without a vacuum, and as no answer at all to the question that animated the movements, how the new state would be governed. Both halves of that sentence are the verdict the record supports.

## Errors in the common accounts, corrected from the texts

Because the three creations are examination territory and a standing reference in statehood debates, the errors that circulate about them are worth correcting in one place from the Acts themselves. The first error gives the three states a single birthday. They have none: the appointed days are 1 November 2000 for Chhattisgarh, 9 November 2000 for Uttaranchal and 15 November 2000 for Jharkhand, and the stagger was a deliberate administrative choice recorded in three separate notifications, not an accident of the calendar. The second error dates the states to the passage of the Acts in August 2000. The Acts were enacted in August, but a reorganisation Act creates nothing until its appointed day, and between assent and appointed day the undivided states continued in law exactly as before; the states' own foundation observances keep the November dates, correctly. The third error treats Uttarakhand as a state created in 2007. The state was created on 9 November 2000 as Uttaranchal and renamed by the Uttaranchal (Alteration of Name) Act, 2006 with effect from 1 January 2007; the 2007 event is a change of name under the same Article 3 power, and every institution of the state, its Assembly's numbering of sessions included, runs from 2000. The fourth error supposes that the three Acts were one Act, or that a single States Reorganisation (Amendment) Act did the work. There are three Acts, numbered consecutively, 28, 29 and 30 of 2000, each complete in itself, and the near-identity of their texts is a drafting achievement rather than a single instrument. The fifth error attributes the creations to a commission. No commission sat on the 2000 round; the States Reorganisation Commission had reported in 1955 against or past these demands, and the settlements of 2000 were political decisions of the Union government and Parliament, taken on the accumulated record of the movements, which is why their legislative history is a history of movements and resolutions rather than of a report and its acceptance. Each error is small, and each dissolves the feature of the episode from which the lesson is drawn, the stagger, the commencement device, the renaming, the parallel drafting and the political route respectively, which is why a statute pillar exists to correct them.

## The template after 2000, and the verdict

Every later statehood demand in India has been argued in the shadow of the three Acts of 2000, and every later reorganisation has been drafted from their text. The demands that cite them, for Vidarbha, for Bundelkhand, for the further division of large states, invoke them as proof that division can be accomplished without disorder, and the invocation is fair so far as the statutory mechanics go. When Parliament next used the template at full scale, in [the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014](/2018/06/15/andhra-pradesh-reorganisation-act-2014/), it departed from the 2000 pattern at exactly the points this article has identified as load-bearing: a shared capital for a term of years, a High Court that remained common while its division was deferred, and assurances about a new capital and special arrangements that were written as political commitments rather than self-executing provisions, with the result that the settlement's contested residue is located precisely where the departures lie. The 2019 reorganisation in the north raised different questions again, outside the template's assumptions altogether. The comparison is the strongest available evidence for the template's soundness within its conditions: used whole, on a territory with its own centre and a parent that keeps its capital, it has never failed; departed from, it fails at the points of departure.

For the reader who examines or advises on a future bill, the three Acts supply a working method that can be stated in prose. Begin with the formation section and ask whether the territory is described in whole districts and whether any district, town or institution is claimed by both sides. Turn to the capital and ask the article's central question: is the successor's capital inside the transferred territory, is the parent's capital outside it, and if either answer is no, what does the bill do about the sharing, for how long, and with what exit? Turn to the representation part and check that the seats divided sum to the frozen totals and that the first Assembly is constituted by a device honest about its own provisional character. Turn to the High Court part and ask whether the new state has its own court from the appointed day or a common court for a term, and if a term, what happens when it expires. Turn to the apportionment part and identify the unit of account, the ratio, the treatment of the undertakings that cross the boundary, and the referee in default of agreement. Turn to the services part and ask who allocates, on what advice, with what protection, and how long the interim may run. And turn finally to the closing part and check the sunsets: an adaptation power with a date, a removal-of-difficulties power with a date, and no open-ended executive authority surviving the transition. A bill that answers those questions as the Acts of 2000 answer them will settle; a bill that defers any of them has located its future litigation, and the reader can say where.

The verdict on the three Acts themselves follows. As drafting, they are models of their kind: complete in their coverage of what a reorganisation must decide, economical in their language, honest in their sunsets, and disciplined in refusing to promise what a statute cannot perform. As settlements, they are complete in law and incomplete in politics, and the distinction is not a criticism of the Acts but a description of what Acts are. The reader who has followed the three texts in parallel can do the thing the One Test for this article requires: take any future reorganisation bill, lay it beside the template of 2000, and identify, part by part, what it settles, what it defers, and where its disputes will be found. They will be found, as they were in 2014, in the departures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which three states were carved out in the 2000 reorganisation?

Chhattisgarh was carved out of Madhya Pradesh by the Madhya Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000 (Act 28 of 2000), Uttaranchal out of Uttar Pradesh by the Uttar Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2000 (Act 29 of 2000), and Jharkhand out of Bihar by the Bihar Reorganisation Act, 2000 (Act 30 of 2000). The three became the twenty-sixth, twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth states of the Union. Each Act transferred whole districts: sixteen to Chhattisgarh, thirteen to Uttaranchal and eighteen to Jharkhand. Uttaranchal was renamed Uttarakhand with effect from 1 January 2007.

### Q: What were the appointed days for the three new states?

The appointed day for Chhattisgarh was 1 November 2000, notified by S.O. 827(E) of 14 September 2000. The appointed day for Uttaranchal was 9 November 2000, notified by S.O. 950(E) of 20 October 2000. The appointed day for Jharkhand was 15 November 2000, notified by S.O. 829(E) of 14 September 2000, a date that was also the birth anniversary of Birsa Munda. Each Act defines the appointed day as the day the Central Government appoints by notification, and the stagger across the fortnight let the administrative machinery handle one separation at a time.

### Q: How were High Courts provided for the new states?

Each Act constituted a separate High Court for the new state from its appointed day. The High Court of Chhattisgarh sat at Bilaspur from 1 November 2000, the High Court of Uttaranchal at Nainital from 9 November 2000, and the High Court of Jharkhand at Ranchi from 15 November 2000, where the Patna High Court had maintained a bench. Judges of the parent High Court identified under the Act's machinery became judges of the new court, and the parent court's jurisdiction over the transferred territory ended on the appointed day, subject to the transfer provisions for pending proceedings.

### Q: How were government employees allocated between successor states?

Every officer of the undivided state continued in service on the appointed day, provisionally, in the successor state where he was posted. Final allocation was made by the Central Government by general or special order, after consultation with the two successor governments and on the work of advisory committees that processed each cadre. Options and domicile were weighed, conditions of service were protected against disadvantage, and the All India Services were split into separate cadres for each successor state. In the Bihar Act the machinery runs through sections 72 to 77, covering allocation, protection, advisory committees and the Centre's direction power.

### Q: Why were the 2000 reorganisations relatively uncontested?

Three conditions coincided. Each transferred territory had a centre of its own, Raipur, Dehradun and Ranchi, so the new state's capital was inside its territory and the parent kept its own capital, leaving no capital to share or dispute. The boundaries ran on whole districts, so no boundary line had to be drawn through settled country. And the demands were decades old and politically matured, with Uttar Pradesh's legislature having resolved in favour and Madhya Pradesh acquiescing; even Bihar's political opposition could not translate into legal contest, because Article 3 requires only that the parent legislature's views be sought, not that it consent.

### Q: How was Uttaranchal renamed Uttarakhand?

Parliament enacted the Uttaranchal (Alteration of Name) Act, 2006, under the name-altering limb of Article 3. Its section 3 provides that as from the appointed day under that Act the State of Uttaranchal shall be known as the State of Uttarakhand, and its following sections substituted the new name in the First and Fourth Schedules to the Constitution. The Act was brought into force on 1 January 2007 by notification GSR 779(E) of 29 December 2006. It also gave the appropriate government one year to adapt existing laws to the new name. The state itself dates from 9 November 2000; only the name dates from 2007.

### Q: What happens to pending court cases after reorganisation?

Proceedings pending before the parent High Court that arose within the transferred territory stand transferred to the new state's High Court, which receives the record and disposes of the case as if it had been instituted before it; the Madhya Pradesh Act carries the provision in section 83 and the Bihar Act in section 89, with a definition of the corresponding court to resolve doubts. Orders passed before the appointed day remain effective and enforceable through the corresponding court. Cases in the subordinate courts of the transferred districts simply continue, under the new High Court's control, without any transfer at all.

### Q: How is representation in Parliament adjusted after a new state?

The reorganisation Act itself does the adjusting. It amends the Fourth Schedule to divide the parent state's Council of States seats between the successors, and schedules to the Act allocate the sitting members between the two states by name and term so the biennial retirement cycle continues undisturbed. For the House of the People, the Act fixes each successor's seats within the parent's frozen total, and sitting members elected from transferred constituencies become members for the new state. In 2000 the divisions were eleven of forty House seats to Chhattisgarh, five of eighty-five to Uttaranchal and fourteen of fifty-four to Jharkhand.

### Q: What is an appointed day and who fixes it?

The appointed day is the date on which a reorganisation takes legal effect: the new state is formed, the schedules change, the new High Court opens and the allocations begin. The Act does not fix the date in its text. It defines the appointed day as the day the Central Government may appoint by notification in the Official Gazette, which lets Parliament enact the complete settlement first and lets the executive choose the commencement date once the preparatory work, above all the interim postings of officers, is ready. In 2000 the device produced three different November dates from three Acts passed in August.

### Q: What role did the population ratio play in the settlements made by the 2000 Acts?

The population ratio, fixed in each Act's definitions from the 1991 census, was the unit of account for everything that could not be divided by location. Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh divided in the ratio 485.7 to 176.2, and Bihar and Jharkhand in the ratio 645.30 to 218.44. General public debt, guarantees, pension liabilities and unlocated balances were apportioned on the ratio, while assets located in a territory passed to the state containing them and liabilities tied to a specific undertaking followed that undertaking. The courts later accepted measures matched to the thing divided, upholding a power consumption ratio of roughly seventy-seven to twenty-three for an electricity board's current balances.

### Q: What happened to the Legislative Councils of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh?

They continued as the Councils of the truncated states, with their composition adjusted to the reduced territory under the representation parts of the two Acts. No new Legislative Council was created for Chhattisgarh, Uttaranchal or Jharkhand: the template constitutes only the institutions a new state cannot function without, and leaves optional second chambers to the new state's later choice under the Constitution. The Uttar Pradesh Council also played a transitional part in the settlement itself, since eight of its members representing the hill region were borrowed to complete Uttaranchal's provisional Assembly of thirty. Madhya Pradesh was unicameral, so its Act had no Council to provide for.

### Q: Why did Uttaranchal start with a provisional Assembly of thirty members?

The hill and Terai constituencies of the undivided Uttar Pradesh Assembly returned only twenty-two members, too few to constitute a working legislature for a state whose Assembly the Act fixed at seventy seats. The Uttar Pradesh Act therefore borrowed eight further members from the Uttar Pradesh Legislative Council to form a provisional Assembly of thirty, openly labelled provisional, which carried the state from 9 November 2000 to its first general election in February 2002. The device was possible only because Uttar Pradesh was bicameral, and it shows the template preferring an acknowledged stopgap to a transferred rump dressed up as a legislature.

### Q: How were the All India Services cadres split between the successor states?

Separate cadres of the Indian Administrative Service, the Indian Police Service and the Indian Forest Service were constituted for each successor state, and serving officers of the undivided state's cadre were allocated between the two cadres by the Central Government under the services part of each Act and the rules framed under the All India Services Act, 1951. Officers served provisionally where they were posted on the appointed day until their final allocation, and their conditions of service, including pay and pension expectations, were protected against variation to their disadvantage. Future recruitment thereafter fed each successor's cadre separately through the ordinary Union Public Service Commission cycle.

### Q: What happened to the State Public Service Commission of the undivided state?

The undivided state's Commission continued as the Commission of the parent state, and each Act provided for the new state to have its own State Public Service Commission, with transitional arrangements under which the existing Commission could discharge the new state's needs until its own body was constituted and its members appointed. Members of the existing Commission were dealt with on the same allocation principle as other constitutional and statutory office-holders affected by the division, and examinations and selections in progress at the appointed day were carried across by the Acts' continuance machinery, so that a recruitment begun under the undivided state could be completed under its successors.

### Q: How were the electricity boards of the undivided states divided?

The state electricity boards were the largest joint undertakings the apportionment parts had to divide. The boards continued to serve both successor states for an interim, and their assets and liabilities were then apportioned, by agreement between the two governments or, in default, under the Acts' machinery with central determination. The division produced the round's most consequential apportionment litigation, in which the Supreme Court considered the Madhya Pradesh board's balances and declined to disturb an apportionment of current assets and liabilities made on the ratio of power consumption between the two states, roughly seventy-seven to twenty-three, holding that the measure bore a rational relation to the thing divided.

### Q: For how long could laws be adapted by executive order after an appointed day?

Two years. Each Act gives the appropriate government, the Central Government for laws on Union subjects and the state government for the rest, power to adapt and modify pre-existing laws by order, whether by repeal or amendment, for the purpose of facilitating their application in the successor states, and the power expires two years from the appointed day; the Madhya Pradesh Act states it in section 79 and the Bihar Act in section 85. After the window closed, only the competent legislature could change the inherited law. A separate presidential power to remove difficulties ran for three years, and a court's power to construe unadapted laws has no such limit.

### Q: Which questions from the 2000 reorganisations were still being litigated years later?

The individual and the financial questions, never the constitutional ones. Officers litigated their final allocation, their seniority in the split cadres, and which successor state could discipline them during the provisional period, as the Patna High Court's decisions on officers provisionally allotted between Bihar and Jharkhand show. The successor states and their boards litigated the measures of apportionment, above all the electricity balances. Private parties litigated the survival of benefits granted by the undivided state, and the Supreme Court held in the Swarn Rekha Cokes case that a sales tax exemption earned under Bihar's industrial policy survived in Jharkhand. No court was ever asked to undo a state.

### Q: How did the 2000 reorganisations differ from the Andhra Pradesh reorganisation of 2014?

At the points the 2000 template treats as load-bearing. In 2000 each new state's capital lay inside its own territory, each parent kept its capital, and each new state had its own High Court from the appointed day. The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014 instead made Hyderabad the common capital of both successor states for a period of years, kept a common High Court while its division was deferred, and recorded assurances on the new capital and special arrangements as commitments rather than self-executing provisions. The contested residue of the 2014 settlement is located at those departures, which is the strongest evidence for the soundness of the 2000 pattern within its conditions.

### Q: Why were the appointed days staggered over a fortnight instead of falling on a single date?

Because the Centre's machinery had to execute three separations, and a single date would have required every notification, allocation order, judicial transfer and treasury instruction for six states to take effect in one midnight. The stagger, 1, 9 and 15 November 2000, let the work be sequenced, and it cost nothing, because the three divisions were independent of one another: no institution was shared between any two of the three pairs. The dates also carried local meanings that a single date could not have carried, 1 November being the anniversary of Madhya Pradesh's own formation in 1956 and 15 November the birth anniversary of Birsa Munda.

### Q: Why does the Bihar Reorganisation Act carry a separate part on water resources?

Because the Bihar division divided a river system as well as a state. Part IX of the Bihar Reorganisation Act, 2000, sections 78 to 82, provides for the management and development of water resources and for a management board, reflecting the fact that the Sone river system waters the truncated Bihar's agricultural heartland from catchments and works lying in or shared with Jharkhand and the other riparian states. The Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh Acts carry no equivalent part, having no shared river question of the same order. The addition shows the template's method: the common text governs everything alike, and a part is added where a territory's own facts demand one.
