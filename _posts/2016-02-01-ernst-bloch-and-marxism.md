Ernst Bloch was a German Marxist philosopher of the twentieth century whose entire work turns on a single subject that his tradition had largely neglected: hope. Where the other great names of Western Marxism wrote about reification, hegemony, ideology, and the administered world, Bloch wrote about the forward pressure inside human wishing, and he tried to give that pressure the dignity of a philosophical system. His major work, The Principle of Hope, written in American exile during the nineteen forties and published in the decade after, argues that anticipations of a better life are not private decorations on an otherwise finished reality. They are, he claimed, an objective feature of a world that is itself unfinished, a tendency lodged in art, religion, myth, fairy tale, and the ordinary daydream, waiting to be read correctly. He is the principal counterweight, inside the Marxist tradition, to the pessimism associated with the Frankfurt School: where their critique of the present often ended in a guarded darkness, his ended in an instructed expectation.

This profile proceeds in the order that makes the thought intelligible. It begins with the life, briefly, because the life crosses the whole catastrophe of the German century and because the record contains one episode, his public defence of the Moscow trials in the nineteen thirties, that will be stated plainly in its place and neither elaborated nor excused. It then takes up the early books, the discovery of non-synchronism in the nineteen thirties, and the long construction of the philosophy of hope in exile. The middle of the article is given to the instruments a reader can actually use: the not-yet in its two forms, the distinction between concrete and abstract utopia, and the utopian surplus, set out in the table the brief for this profile requires. Later sections treat his turn toward religion, his quarrel and kinship with the Frankfurt thinkers, the East German years and the departure westward, and the strongest objection his method has to face, the charge that a surplus which can be found anywhere can never be shown to be absent anywhere. By the end, the reader should be able to say what would count, for Bloch, as a hope that deserves the name, and what would expose a hope as mere wishing.

## Who was Ernst Bloch?

He was born on 8 July 1885 in Ludwigshafen, an industrial town on the Rhine, the son of a Jewish railway official, and he died on 4 August 1977 in Tübingen, in his ninety third year. Between those dates lies nearly the whole arc of modern German history, and Bloch's biography touches most of its stations: the Wilhelmine university, the First World War spent partly in Swiss exile, the Weimar experiments, flight from the Nazis in 1933, a decade and a half of American exile, a professorial chair in communist East Germany, a public fall from favour in that state, and a final, honoured decade and a half in the West German university town where he died. Few thinkers of the tradition lived so many of its situations in sequence, and fewer still kept the same central question through all of them.

The education was broad in the manner of the period. He studied philosophy in Munich and Würzburg, with excursions into physics, music, and the experimental psychology of the day, and took his doctorate in 1908 with a dissertation on the neo Kantian Heinrich Rickert and the problems of modern epistemology. What matters more than the degree is the early evidence of the bent that never left him: a mind drawn simultaneously to rigorous system and to the expressive, image laden forms of thought that academic philosophy usually kept at the door. He moved in the Berlin circle around Georg Simmel and then in the Heidelberg circle around Max Weber, where the Sunday gatherings of that extraordinary milieu brought him into lasting contact with Georg Lukacs. The friendship formed there, between the two young men who would become the twin sources of so much later Marxist philosophy, is one of the organising relationships of this profile, and its later souring is one of its instructive episodes.

The First World War found him already a dissenter. He spent part of it in Switzerland, writing against the war for exile journals, and there composed the book that made his name in the avant garde: Geist der Utopie, The Spirit of Utopia, published in 1918 and substantially revised in 1923. In the same Swiss years he began the study of Thomas Münzer, the radical preacher of the German Peasants' War, which appeared in 1921 as Thomas Münzer als Theologe der Revolution, Thomas Münzer as Theologian of Revolution. These two early books already contain the whole later system in embryo: the conviction that music, myth, and religious revolt carry a truth about the future that conceptual prose cannot carry alone, and the conviction that the revolutionary tradition includes the heretics and millenarians whom orthodox historiography treated as embarrassing ancestors.

### Why is Bloch so much less read than the Frankfurt thinkers?

Partly the obstacle is material: his greatest book reached English readers only in the nineteen eighties, decades after Adorno and Marcuse had been absorbed into Anglophone debate, and its style, dense, expressionist, and deliberately literary, resists the summary habits of the academy. Partly it is temperamental: a philosophy of hope fitted badly with a century of left defeat.

The fuller answer adds a second material fact. Bloch spent the decisive postwar years in East Germany, inside a state whose official philosophy had no use for his heterodoxies and whose publishing apparatus exported them slowly, while the Frankfurt Institute rebuilt itself in the West with American connections and an English speaking audience. Reception follows infrastructure as much as merit. A thinker whose works appear late, in a difficult idiom, from the wrong side of a divided country, will be assigned a smaller place in syllabuses than his arguments warrant, and the assignment then justifies itself by pointing to the small place. Readers who approach him through the non-synchronism argument, which is short, concrete, and anchored in a famous historical puzzle, usually find the rest of the system less forbidding than its reputation, because the same habits of attention are at work everywhere in it.

His personal circumstances reinforced the pattern. Three marriages, repeated uprootings, and a refusal to trim his vocabulary to any party's catechism made him an awkward ancestor for every camp that might have promoted him. The orthodox East found his hope too religious and his materialism too speculative; the Western New Left, which did revive interest in him during the nineteen sixties, preferred the portable slogans of other writers to his thousand page inventories. What survived was a reputation rather than a readership: everyone in the field knew that Bloch was the philosopher of hope, and very few outside German scholarship had worked through the philosophy. This profile is written against that condition, on the premise that the instruments are more valuable than the reputation.

## The missing category in the tradition

Bloch's place is easiest to fix by noticing what the Marxist tradition around him lacked. The tradition possessed a powerful theory of how the existing order reproduces itself, a theory of ideology explaining why the dominated consent, a theory of crisis explaining why the order periodically convulses, and a theory of organisation explaining how a counter power might be assembled. What it did not possess, in any systematic form, was a theory of the future as a real dimension of the present: an account of where images of a radically better life come from, why they persist through every defeat, how they are to be distinguished from mere fantasy, and what work they do in history. Hope, in the writings of his contemporaries, appears as a mood to be encouraged or disciplined, never as an object of knowledge. The survey of the tradition given in [the western marxism complete guide](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/) shows the shape of the whole to which Bloch belongs, and his profile is best read as the account of the one member who set himself to supply the missing category.

Supplying it required a deliberate reversal of a piece of Marxist common sense. The common sense treated religion, myth, and utopian fantasy as superstructural decoration, interesting to the historian of ideology but carrying no cognitive weight of their own. Bloch's counter claim, announced in the Münzer book and never withdrawn, is that these forms carry a surplus: a content that points beyond the order that produced them and is not exhausted by the ideological service they perform for that order. A cathedral is an instrument of feudal and ecclesiastical power, and it is also a built anticipation of a condition in which human beings dwell otherwise; the second fact does not cancel the first, and an analysis that registers only the first has, by Bloch's standard, read the object with one eye shut. The rest of this profile is largely an unpacking of that single methodological decision, first in his account of fascism, then in the system of hope, then in the reading of cultural forms.

### What did Bloch share with Lukacs before the friendship cooled?

Both men came out of the same Heidelberg milieu, absorbed the same neo Kantian and Simmelian inheritance, greeted the Russian Revolution as a world event, and wrote, within a few years of each other, the two books that refounded Marxist philosophy in the West. Their divergence came later, over art, over party discipline, and over the permissible temperature of hope.

The divergence deserves emphasis because it structures so much of the later history. Lukacs moved toward a classical realism in aesthetics and a disciplined accommodation with the communist movement's line; Bloch defended the fractured forms of expressionism against the charge of decadence and kept a speculative, almost mystical register that Lukacs came to regard as a lapse from materialism. Their printed quarrel of the later nineteen thirties, the expressionism debate, is the public form of the estrangement, and readers who want the other side of that friendship, and of the reification concept against which Bloch's categories are often measured, should turn to [the lukacs reification guide](/2015/11/15/lukacs-reification-guide/). What the two never lost was the shared premise that philosophy, not economics alone, was where Marxism had to be rebuilt. They disagreed, profoundly and permanently, about what the rebuilt philosophy should sound like.

## The early books: spirit, music, and a revolutionary preacher

The Spirit of Utopia is an unlikely founding document. Written during a world war by a young philosopher in exile, it ranges across music, painting, architecture, and the philosophy of the self, and it reads, in stretches, like a prose poem that has swallowed a metaphysics. Its governing thought is that the most important human experiences are experiences of encounter with something not yet understood: the moment in which a person, hearing a melody or confronting a work of art, becomes aware of a self and a world that do not coincide with what exists. Bloch called the obscurity at the centre of such moments the darkness of the lived moment, and he held that philosophy begins in astonishment at that darkness rather than in the clear light of what can already be stated. Music receives the longest treatment, because music, on his account, is the art least chained to the representation of existing things and therefore the art in which the not yet can be heard most directly.

The Münzer book works the same intuition into history. Thomas Münzer, the theologian who preached revolution to the peasants of the German Reformation and was executed after their defeat at Frankenhausen in 1525, had been treated by the Marxist tradition, following Engels, as an interesting precursor whose theology was a costume worn by class interests. Bloch's reading reverses the valuation without denying the interests. The chiliastic expectation, the conviction that the Kingdom of God could be built on earth by the action of the poor, is for Bloch a genuine historical force, the form in which an oppressed class first learned to imagine that the order of the world could be otherwise. Theology, in this book, is not the disguise of the revolution but one of its native languages. The line from this early study to the late work on the Bible is straight and unbroken, and it explains why Bloch, alone among the major Marxist philosophers, treated religion as a treasury to be inherited rather than an error to be outgrown.

Between the early books and the exile writings stands a decade of Weimar journalism, teaching, and political engagement of an unaffiliated kind. Bloch wrote for the feuilletons, reviewed theatre and film, and composed the short, dense prose pieces he called traces, miniatures in which a street scene, a hotel corridor, or a fairground might be made to yield its philosophical content. The habit of reading small cultural facts as evidence about the state of hope in a society was formed in these years, and it is the habit that The Principle of Hope later raises to the scale of a system. Readers who find the great book forbidding can approach its method through these miniatures, where the movement from object to anticipation takes a page rather than a hundred.

## The expressionism quarrel and the defence of fractured form

The nineteen thirties forced every German Marxist intellectual to answer the same question in print: what should art look like under the shadow of fascism, and what does the broken surface of modern art signify? Lukacs answered that the fragmentations of expressionism and montage were symptoms of decay, a flight from the realistic portrayal of social wholes, and that the heritage to be defended was the classical realism of the nineteenth century novel. Bloch answered, in the debate conducted in the exile journal Das Wort in 1937 and 1938, that the fracture was in reality itself before it was in art, that a collapse era legitimately produces collapsed forms, and that the techniques of the avant garde preserved experiences of interruption and possibility that a seamless realism smoothed away. His defence of expressionism is the aesthetic counterpart of his philosophy of history: in both, the unfinished and the broken are not deficiencies but evidence, the visible sign that the existing order does not exhaust what is real.

The quarrel matters for this profile because it displays the difference of intellectual temperament that runs through everything else. Where Lukacs sought the totality already formed and judged particulars by their place in it, Bloch sought the totality in formation and judged forms by what they anticipated. The same difference separates their accounts of the past. For the later Lukacs, the heritage of bourgeois culture was a patrimony to be administered; for Bloch, heritage, Erbe, meant something stricter and stranger: only that part of the past which remains unfulfilled, whose promises were never cashed, legitimately descends to the present, and it descends as a task rather than as a possession. The concept of heritage in this sense quietly organises his whole reading of culture, and it will return in the discussion of the utopian surplus, where the surplus is precisely the unfulfilled portion of a cultural form, the part that its own age could not use.

### Why did the quarrel over expressionism outlast the decade that produced it?

Because it was never only about painting or the novel. The dispute encoded two rival answers to the question of how a Marxist should treat every broken, archaic, or apparently backward phenomenon, in art, in religion, and in politics. One answer files the phenomenon under decay; the other asks what unfulfilled wish the fracture is still carrying.

The political stakes became visible almost immediately. If fractured and archaic forms are merely decay, then the grievances expressed in archaic idioms, by peasants attached to their soil, artisans ruined by machinery, or believers attached to their churches, are merely decay too, and a politics that dismisses them leaves them available to whoever will speak to them in their own language. If such forms carry unfulfilled content, then the task is different: to inherit the content while refusing the reactionary form in which it has been conscripted. Bloch drew exactly this inference, and he drew it in the book that contains his most durable contribution to political analysis, the study of Germany published in Zurich in 1935 under the title Erbschaft dieser Zeit, Heritage of Our Times.

## Heritage of Our Times and the discovery of non-synchronism

Heritage of Our Times is a diagnosis of a civilisation coming apart, written in the first years of exile as the Nazi regime consolidated itself. Its chapters move through the debris of Weimar culture, its fairs and revues, its occult revivals and its youth movements, its philosophy and its streets, asking at each station the same question: what wishes are circulating here, and who is collecting them? The book's famous theoretical yield is the category of Ungleichzeitigkeit, non-synchronism, sometimes rendered as non-contemporaneity. The opening claim can be paraphrased plainly: not all people live in the same Now. They do so only in the calendar sense. In their material lives, their habits, their angers, and their hopes, different classes and regions inhabit different historical times simultaneously, and the contradictions that move them are not all contradictions of the most advanced present.

The point sounds modest until its target is identified. Orthodox Marxist analysis of the period recognised, in effect, one contradiction as fully real: the synchronous contradiction between capital and wage labour, native to the most developed sector of the economy. Everything else, the peasant's attachment to the land, the artisan's hatred of the factory, the small proprietor's resentment of both the trust and the union, the romantic anti capitalism of youth movements and völkisch sects, was classified as residue, a survival scheduled to disappear as modernisation completed its work. Bloch's objection is that the residues refused to disappear on schedule, and that in Germany they had just demonstrated their historical power by supplying a mass movement with its infantry. A category that files the grievances of millions as anachronisms, he argued, is not a materialist category at all. It is a wish disguised as a timetable.

## The non-synchronism explanation

The argument built on the category is the claim this profile is required to name, and it will be called here, throughout, the non-synchronism explanation: fascism drew strength from grievances belonging to an earlier historical time that a modernising left wrote off as backward, so the analytical failure was not that the left underestimated fascism's brutality but that it lacked a category for contradictions that were not contemporary with capitalism's most advanced sector. The explanation has two halves, and both must be kept. The first half concerns the fascist movement, which proved adept at addressing the non-synchronous strata in their own idioms: the peasant addressed as the eternal tiller of German soil, the artisan addressed as the betrayed estate of honest work, the ruined saver addressed as the victim of a finance conceived in racial terms. The second half concerns the left, whose propaganda spoke with great precision to the synchronous contradiction and with embarrassment or contempt to everything else, leaving the older grievances, in Bloch's phrase for the situation, unharvested by anyone but the enemy.

### What is non-synchronism and how does it explain fascism?

Bloch's argument that different classes and regions live in different historical times at once, so that pre-modern grievances persist as active forces. Fascism, he held, succeeded partly by addressing these non-contemporary discontents, which a modernising left dismissed as backward and left unanswered.

The evidence he marshalled was ethnographic as much as economic. He pointed to the survival of peasant economies inside an industrial state, to the strata of artisans and shopkeepers whose ruin was real but whose enemy image was archaic, to the irrationalist philosophies and nature cults and occult enthusiasms that a purely economic reading would wave aside. He distinguished, within the non-synchronous itself, between the merely subjective kind, resentment against the present that curdles into nostalgia, and the objective kind, the genuinely unfinished business of earlier formations: land hunger never satisfied, craft independence never replaced, promises of the bourgeois revolution itself, equality, fraternity, national unity, that the bourgeois order had issued and never honoured. The objective kind is the politically explosive one, because its grievances are just, and justice misdirected is more durable than injustice invented. A movement that can attach those unfulfilled promises to a racial or national myth acquires an energy that no amount of accurate statistics about wage rates will answer.

The explanation also reframes the famous puzzle of fascism's irrationalism. If the movement's mythology were simply a screen manufactured by monopoly capital, then exposing the screen should dissolve the loyalty beneath it, and the left repeatedly discovered that exposure did no such thing. On Bloch's account the mythology worked because it was addressed to real contents: real dispossession, real humiliation, real wishes for a world ordered by meaning rather than by the market. The Nazis, he wrote in the bitter summary of those years, spoke deceitfully but to people, whereas their opponents too often spoke the truth but only about things. An analysis that cannot tell the difference between a deceitful address and an empty one will keep winning arguments and losing countries.

### What makes a grievance non-synchronous rather than merely old?

Its object belongs to an earlier formation of social life, and it remains materially alive in the present: the peasant's land hunger, the artisan's destroyed independence, the small saver's vanished security. Mere age is not the criterion. An old grievance that no living stratum still carries is an antiquarian topic, not a political force awaiting an address.

The distinction matters because it separates Bloch's category from the lazy observation that the past lingers. What lingers, in his analysis, is not custom in general but contradiction in particular: relations of production and the expectations formed inside them, surviving into a period whose dominant relations are different. The peasant working a fragmented holding with horse and plough inside a state of chemical combines and autobahns is not a museum exhibit. He is a contemporary whose labour process belongs to another century, and his vote, his resentment, and his son's enlistment are contemporary facts. Non-synchronism names exactly this simultaneity of the non-simultaneous, and it gives the analyst a duty that the older scheme evaded: to take the inventory of all the times coexisting in a society before pronouncing on which of them will decide its future.

## Non-synchronism is not uneven development

Because the two ideas sound alike, and because the series treats the other one at length, the boundary should be drawn carefully and then respected. The structurally parallel concept is the uneven and combined development of whole societies under the pressure of the world market, the argument that backward countries, whipped by competition with advanced ones, combine archaic and ultramodern forms in a single social order and thereby generate distinctive revolutionary possibilities. That argument, its evidence, and its fate are the subject of [the study of uneven and combined development](/2014/10/01/uneven-and-combined-development/), and this profile will not retrace them. The difference can be stated in a sentence each. Combined development operates between societies, on the scale of states and world markets, and its unit is the formation as a whole in relation to other formations. Non-synchronism operates within a society, on the scale of strata, regions, and consciousness, and its unit is the historical time a group actually inhabits inside a shared calendar.

The two are complements rather than rivals, and each exposes the other's blind spot when used alone. An analysis of Germany between the wars that used only the international concept would describe a defeated power combining a reparations burden with an advanced industry, and would miss the internal archaic strata whose mobilisation decided the outcome. An analysis that used only Bloch's category would map the strata and miss the world market pressures that had ruined the savers and artisans in the first place. Bloch himself, it should be added, was concerned with the internal case because the internal case was where his side's propaganda failed; the category is a correction offered to a movement about its own country, not a general theory of world history, and it loses precision when inflated into one.

## American exile and the writing of the great book

Bloch fled Germany in 1933, by way of Switzerland, Austria, and Paris, and reached the United States in 1938, where he lived for eleven years in modest circumstances, without a university post, working on the manuscript that became The Principle of Hope. The exile was productive in the way that enforced leisure sometimes is: cut off from movements, parties, and audiences, he wrote the book as an inventory, amassing the evidence for hope from every province of culture he had ever studied. The three volumes appeared in East Berlin between 1954 and 1959, after his return to Europe, but their substance is a wartime work, composed while fascism ruled his country and while the outcome of the century remained undecided. That circumstance explains a feature of the book that readers sometimes miss. Its optimism of method is not the optimism of a victor surveying a settled world. It is a discipline adopted in the dark, by an exile who had watched his own earlier warnings about fascism's appeal go unheeded, and who set out to build the theoretical instrument whose absence had cost so much.

The book is organised as an ascent. Its first part inventories the small daydreams of ordinary life, the wishes that surface in shop windows, advertisements, fairy tales, and plans for Sunday. Its second part lays the philosophical foundations: the anticipatory consciousness, the not yet, and the categories of possibility. Its third part turns to the wishful images displayed in the mirror of culture, as the German subtitle has it: fashion, theatre, film, the fairground, the dance hall. Its fourth part collects the outlines of a better life drawn by medicine, by social utopias, by technology, architecture, geography, painting, music, and religion. Its fifth and final part gathers the wishful images of the fulfilled moment: morality, music again at the summit, the problem of death, and the religious hope of the kingdom. The architecture is itself an argument. Hope is not treated as one topic among others, to be handled in a chapter; it is treated as a dimension that runs through every region of human production, and the book's scale is the price of taking that claim seriously.

A word about the title is in order, since it is often misread as a piece of uplift. A principle, in the philosophical usage Bloch invokes, is not a slogan but a ground: that from which a domain of phenomena becomes intelligible. To call hope a principle is to claim that the phenomena of anticipation, wishing, expecting, planning, and imagining otherwise are not accidents of individual psychology but a structured feature of reality itself, with its own logic, its own degrees of maturity, and its own liability to error and correction. The book's famous discipline follows from this. If hope is a principle, it can be educated, tested, and graded; if it were only a mood, it could only be shared or deplored. The distinction between the book's reception as an inspiration and its intention as a system is the distinction between Bloch the reputation and Bloch the philosopher, and this profile follows the intention.

## The not-yet: a category of consciousness and of being

The master concept of the system is the not-yet, das Noch-Nicht, and it operates on two levels that must be held apart before they are joined. On the level of consciousness there is the not yet conscious, das Noch-Nicht-Bewusste: the content that presses forward in a mind without having become clear to it, the dawning awareness of a possibility that has no adequate language yet. Bloch insists on the difference between this and the Freudian unconscious. Freud's unconscious is the no longer conscious, the buried past of repression, recoverable by excavation. The not yet conscious faces the other way. It is not what has been forgotten but what has never yet been thought, the forward edge of awareness where new contents announce themselves first as feelings, images, and restless wishes. On the level of being there corresponds the not yet become, das Noch-Nicht-Gewordene: the objectively unfinished state of the world, in which real possibilities exist as tendencies and latencies before they ripen into facts.

The joining of the two levels is the system's wager. An anticipation in consciousness counts as knowledge, rather than as fantasy, when it corresponds to a tendency in the world: when the wish fastens on what is objectively ripening. Everything else in the philosophy is machinery for making that correspondence testable, or at least arguable, in particular cases. The darkness of the lived moment explains why the correspondence is hard to inspect directly: the moment a person lives in is the least visible to them, since it is the standpoint from which all their seeing is done, and so the newest content in consciousness arrives wrapped in obscurity even when it is genuine. Philosophy's task, as Bloch conceives it, is the patient clarification of that darkness, the conversion of dawning anticipation into educated hope. He gives the educated form a Latin name, docta spes, on the model of the learned ignorance of Nicholas of Cusa: hope that has been through the school of criticism and come out still hoping, but knowingly.

### Why does Bloch call the lived moment dark?

Because the moment a person is living cannot be stepped outside of: it is the place from which everything else gets seen, never an object in the view itself. What is becoming in experience therefore reaches awareness muffled and obscure, even when it is perfectly genuine, and clearing that obscurity is the first philosophical task.

The practical consequence for reading his work is that the not-yet is not a synonym for the future in general. The future contains much that is merely the present extended, the next instalment of the same. Bloch reserves the weight of the concept for the Novum, the genuinely new that a situation carries as its unrealised possibility, and behind the Novum he places the Ultimum, the final figure of fulfilment toward which the series of new things points without ever being exhausted by them. Between tendency, the direction a process is actually taking, and latency, the store of what a thing can become under changed conditions, his ontology of possibility is worked out in detail that this profile can only indicate. What a reader needs for the political and cultural arguments is the core pairing: a forward content in consciousness, a ripening tendency in the world, and the demand that the two be matched before the name of hope is conferred.

## Daydreams against night dreams

One of the system's most accessible distinctions, and one of its most consequential, is the rehabilitation of the daydream. Psychoanalysis had taught the century to read dreams as dispatches from the buried past, and the night dream, disguised, regressive, and decipherable only by an analyst, became the model of unconscious life. Bloch does not deny the night dream its territory. He denies it the monopoly. The daydream, the waking fantasy in which a person imagines a better apartment, a vindicated argument, an unrecognised talent finally recognised, a world arranged otherwise, differs from the night dream in every respect that matters: it is not censored into unintelligibility but remains lucid and steerable; it does not dissolve the ego but extends it; and its direction is forward, toward what is not yet, rather than backward toward what has been repressed. In the daydream, Bloch writes in effect, the wish keeps its eyes open.

The distinction does philosophical work beyond its charm. If the forward dream is a real mode of consciousness with its own dignity, then the contents it carries, the wishful images of a better life, are data for a theory of history rather than symptoms for a clinic. The entire inventory method of The Principle of Hope rests on this warrant. The advertisement, the fairy tale, the utopian novel, and the revolutionary programme are all, in different degrees of maturity, organised daydreams, and they can be studied as evidence about what an age was capable of wanting. There is a democratic consequence too. The night dream requires an expert interpreter; the daydream belongs to its dreamer, who can be brought, by criticism and by education, to interpret and correct it. Bloch's hope is meant to be a mass possession educated, not a mystery administered, and the daydream is where that education begins.

### Why does Bloch set the daydream above the night dream?

Because the daydream faces the direction his philosophy studies. The night dream, on the Freudian account he accepts for its own domain, reworks a buried past; the daydream elaborates a possible future, lucidly, with the ego intact and the wish still steerable, which makes it the primary document of anticipatory consciousness, the place where the not yet first becomes legible.

A qualification protects the distinction from a misunderstanding. Bloch is not claiming that daydreams are reliable. They are notoriously corruptible, and the culture industry material treated later in this profile shows the wishful image in its most manipulated form, manufactured to reconcile the dreamer with the order that exploits the dream. The claim is about direction, not about innocence. A compass can be deflected and still be the instrument that registers the pole; a daydream can be sold back to its dreamer in debased form and still testify, by the very energy spent debasing it, to the forward pressure it contains. The critical task is to separate the testimony from the debasement, which is the operation the utopian surplus names.

## Concrete utopia against abstract utopia

The system's most quoted distinction follows directly. Abstract utopia is wishing that has not done its homework: the image of a better world constructed in disregard of the tendencies actually at work in this one, compensatory fantasy that consoles its holder for the present instead of intervening in it. Concrete utopia is anticipation mediated by knowledge of the process, the wish that has allied itself with a real tendency and can therefore function as a lever inside history rather than as a decoration outside it. The distinction is not between modest and extravagant hopes. A concrete utopia may be far more radical than an abstract one; the difference lies in the mediation, in whether the image is fastened to the objectively possible, to what Bloch calls real possibility, the portion of the future whose conditions are already assembling in the present.

Marx's own procedure is Bloch's model for the distinction. The classical utopian socialists, on the familiar account, designed their phalansteries out of reason and benevolence and addressed them to any ruler enlightened enough to listen; Marx refused the design business and located the anticipation inside the movement of the existing society, in the organised class whose position made its interest coincide with the general emancipation. Bloch reads this refusal as the founding act of concrete utopia and his own system as its completion, since Marx, in his judgement, supplied the cold stream of analysis, the unsparing critique of what exists, while leaving the warm stream, the liberating intention and its images, undertheorised. His formula for the whole tradition follows: true Marxism unites the cold stream and the warm stream, sobriety about conditions with passion about the goal, and degenerates when either is practised alone. The cold stream alone produces the administrator and the cynic; the warm stream alone produces the dreamer and the demagogue's audience.

### How can a hope be tested against reality?

By its mediation. A hope earns the title concrete when its holder can name the tendency in existing conditions moving toward the hoped for state, the agents whose situation aligns them with it, and the obstacles that tendency must actually overcome. A hope naming none of these may be sincere, but it is abstract: it will console rather than act.

The test cuts in both directions, which is what makes it a test rather than a compliment. It condemns the revolutionary phrase that mistakes a wish for a tendency, the recurrent temptation of movements to read their own desires into the situation and call the reading analysis. And it condemns, with equal force, the realism that recognises no tendencies but the victorious ones, the counsel that treats every anticipation as fantasy until it has already happened. Between the two failures lies the narrow ground of docta spes, and Bloch's systems of categories, the not yet, the Novum, tendency and latency, are best understood as surveying instruments for that ground. Whether the instruments are precise enough is the question the strongest objection, treated in its own section below, presses hard.

## Docta spes: hope that has been educated

The phrase docta spes, educated or learned hope, names the mature form of the principle, and it is the point at which Bloch's philosophy most clearly parts company with the charge of naive optimism that follows him through the textbooks. Naive optimism expects the good outcome because expecting is pleasant; it is a temperament. Educated hope is a practice with three disciplines. The first is the discipline of the inventory: knowing what an age actually wishes, in all its debased and contradictory forms, rather than what its official doctrines say it wishes. The second is the discipline of mediation: testing each wish against the tendencies of the process, keeping the concrete and discarding the abstract, without discarding the energy stored in the abstract forms, which can often be inherited by better ones. The third is the discipline of heritage: recovering from past defeats and past cultures the unfulfilled contents that remain available, since a hope without ancestors is as rootless as a hope without prospects.

It is worth pausing on the word educated, because Bloch chose the Latin to make a claim about hope's rank among the virtues of the intellect. In the scholastic vocabulary he is raiding, spes is a theological virtue; docta is what learning does to a raw capacity. The compound asserts that hope can be a form of knowledge, fallible, corrigible, and improvable by study, and that its opposite is not despair but ignorance: the failure to have learned what the world is pregnant with. Despair, on this scheme, is often only a premature conclusion, the mistaking of a blocked tendency for an absent one. Equally, his scheme has no indulgence for the professional encourager. Hope that refuses the inventory and the mediation has declined the education, and Bloch's pages on the swindles practised with hope, by advertisers and by regimes that administer optimism as a sedative, are as cold as anything in the Frankfurt literature to which he is conventionally opposed.

## The utopian function and the surplus within it

With the instruments in place, the reading of culture can begin. Bloch assigns to the human imagination a utopian function: the capacity, present in every cultural product, to exceed the given, to show the world in the light of what it might become. The function is not confined to works that announce themselves as utopias. It operates in the cathedral and the symphony, certainly, but also in the cheap novel, the travel poster, the illuminated shop window, and the daydream of the clerk on the tram. Wherever a form promises more than the conditions of its production can deliver, the utopian function is at work, and the excess of the promise over the deliverable is what Bloch calls the utopian surplus, the Überschuss of the form.

The surplus is a precise category, not a compliment, and it is defined against the ideological function that the same form simultaneously performs. A cultural form under capitalism or any class order does ideological work: it reconciles, distracts, legitimates, decorates the arrangements that produced it. Bloch never denies this work, and his readings are often as severe about it as any in the Frankfurt literature. His claim is that the ideological function does not exhaust the form, because the form must borrow its power from genuine human wishes in order to do its reconciling work at all. The advertisement must promise a real good, freedom, beauty, youth, abundance, in order to attach that good to a product; the promise is the surplus, separable in analysis from the attachment, and it survives the product's obsolescence as a standing indictment of a world in which the good is available only as an image. Ideology, in short, must tell a truth about human wanting in order to lie effectively about its satisfaction, and the truth it tells is inheritable.

## The utopian surplus table

The utopian surplus table sets the operation out in a form a reader can apply. For each cultural form it distinguishes the ideological function the form serves, the anticipatory content Bloch identifies within it, and the way the two are distinguished in practice. The distinguishing test is uniform throughout: the anticipatory content is the element that points beyond the order which produced the form, that would remain unintelligible as mere legitimation, and that can be detached and inherited by a different order without embarrassment. The ideological function, by contrast, is dated with its order; when the order falls, nobody needs to salvage its excuses.

| Cultural form | Ideological function it serves | Anticipatory content Bloch identifies | How the two are distinguished |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fairy tales | Consolation of the powerless, patience taught as virtue | The small and despised outwit the mighty; the world is arranged so that cunning and kindness are finally rewarded | The reward structure imagines a justice the feudal order never practised, so the tale indicts its own world while consoling its hearers |
| Religious imagery | Legitimation of hierarchy, the priestly monopoly on the sacred | A kingdom in which the last are first, suffering is answered, and no tear is final | The imagery promises the reversal of the very hierarchy that administers it, an excess no legitimation requires |
| Popular fiction | Distraction of the tired reader, dreams sold by the instalment | Adventure, vindication, and abundance imagined for lives the market prices cheaply | The wish survives the formula that exploits it; readers keep the longing and discard the plot |
| Architecture | Display of the patron's power, the ordering of subjects in space | The built anticipation of a dwelling worthy of human beings, most visible in the cathedral and the utopian city plan | Stone outlasts the patron; the form can be reinhabited by a society that has forgotten what it was built to glorify |
| Music | Accompaniment of ceremony, the decoration of power and worship | The most direct registration of the not yet, an order of feeling in which reconciliation is experienced before it exists | Its content cannot be paraphrased into any doctrine it serves, which is the mark of the surplus over the function |
| Advertising | Attachment of wishes to products, the manufacture of needs | A displayed world of ease, beauty, and recognition, promised universally and delivered selectively | The promise addresses everyone while the product addresses a buyer, and the gap between the two addresses is the surplus made visible |

### How does Bloch grade a cultural form for its surplus?

He asks what the form promises beyond what its order can deliver, and whether the excess survives separation from the use its order makes of it. A form whose promise dies with its patron performed ideology only. A form whose promise survives translation into another arrangement, and still speaks there, carries a surplus, graded by its depth of anticipation.

Working the test on the table's rows clarifies its edge. The fairy tale's patience teaching perishes with the order that needed patient subjects; its conviction that the third son, the miller's daughter, the despised youngest will see the mighty confounded does not perish, because it was never a description of the feudal world but a standing reproach to it. Religious imagery offers the hardest case, since its custodians have the strongest interest in denying that any separation is possible, and Bloch's treatment of it occupies a later section of this profile. Advertising offers the easiest case, since the separation is performed daily by its audience, who absorb the promise of the displayed world while forgetting, by design and without regret, which commodity the promise was hired to move. The grading is therefore not a measurement but a judgement, trained by criteria and answerable to counter examples, and the objection that such judgement cannot be made rigorous is the serious objection to the whole method, reserved for treatment at full strength below.

## Small forms: fairy tales, colportage, and the shop window

The system's evidentiary base is deliberately unrespectable. Bloch's favourite documents are not the canonical masterpieces but the small change of culture: the fairy tale told to children, the colportage novel hawked at fairs, the dance hall, the revue, the illuminated window of the department store. The preference follows from the theory. If anticipation is a mass phenomenon, its purest records will be where it is least filtered by educated taste, and the wishes of the many survive in the forms the many actually consumed. The fairy tale, on his reading, is the form in which the oppressed imagination kept its longest memory: a world in which the weak are armed with wit, in which helpers arrive, in which the ogre's strength is a puzzle to be solved rather than a fate to be endured. He notes that the fairy tale's hero is characteristically small, young, female, or despised, and that the tale's justice is distributive in a way its society was not. The colportage novel, the cheap adventure in instalments, contributes the complementary wish: for vastness, for the world beyond the parish, for a life in which events happen.

The shop window receives the most modern of these readings. In the illuminated display, the commodity is staged as the inhabitant of a better world: lit, arranged, and promised to everyone who passes, it performs a universal address that the transaction then betrays. Bloch, who had watched Berlin's windows in the nineteen twenties, treats the passer by not as a dupe but as a reader who takes from the display what the display genuinely contains, the image of a life of ease and beauty, while declining, most of the time, the particular purchase. The wishful image is thus a kind of involuntary public exhibition of the age's desires, maintained at commercial expense. It is a characteristic Blochian reversal: the most compromised form yields, under the right questions, the least compromised evidence, because the advertiser, to sell at all, must show the customer a world the customer already wants, and the wanting is the document.

## Religion: the largest storehouse of anticipation

No part of the system has caused more misunderstanding than its treatment of religion, and the misunderstanding usually begins by treating the topic as an eccentricity, a private consolation of the philosopher's old age. It was neither late nor private. The Münzer book of 1921 already reads a religious revolt as a treasury of revolutionary energy, and the interest runs unbroken to the book on atheism and Christianity published when he was eighty three. For Bloch, religion is the largest historical storehouse of the utopian function, the institution in which humanity stored, over millennia, its most radical wishes: that death not be final, that the suffering of the innocent be answered, that the last be first, that a kingdom of justice arrive. A materialism that simply discards the storehouse, on the ground that its custodians were priests, abandons the richest archive of anticipation to the enemy and leaves the wishes it contains in the custody of the institutions that administered them. His counter proposal is inheritance: to take the wishes back, translated out of their theological form, while regarding the God hypothesis itself as the hypostasis, the personified projection, of the human ideal.

This is the sense, and the only sense, in which his much quoted sentence about atheism and Christianity should be read. In Atheismus im Christentum, Atheism in Christianity, of 1968, he argues that the Bible contains two traditions at war: the religion of the creator God who demands obedience from above, and an underground tradition of Exodus, prophecy, and apocalypse in which God himself is imagined as the one who goes before a people toward a promised future, and in which the serpent's promise in Eden, that human beings shall be as gods, is treated as the book's own buried truth. Hence his formulation that only an atheist can be a good Christian: only the reader who has surrendered the heavenly guarantor can recover what the underground tradition actually hoped, and only the Christian formed by that tradition can be a good atheist, one whose unbelief keeps the hope the churches administered. The aphorism is a programme of inheritance compressed into a paradox, not a flirtation with piety.

### Why does a Marxist read the Bible as an ally rather than an obstacle?

Because the Bible, for Bloch, is a divided book. Alongside the God of command and punishment runs an Exodus tradition in which the divine is the name for the not yet, for a future that summons a people out of bondage. That tradition is the oldest record of the hope his philosophy studies, and discarding it would discard the evidence.

The Exodus material deserves its sentence of emphasis, because it supplies Bloch's model for how a religious form carries a surplus. The God who speaks from the burning bush names himself, in the Hebrew that Bloch, following a long line of readers, stresses, not as the one who is but as the one who will be: a God defined by futurity, whose first historical act is the liberation of slaves. Bloch builds on this the notion of the religion of Exodus and the Kingdom, a faith whose content is departure from every settled order, his own included, and he finds the same content surviving, against ecclesiastical management, in the prophets' denunciations, in the apocalyptic literature, and supremely in the figure of Jesus read as the Son of Man who announces the kingdom to the poor. The reader need not share any of this theology to follow the analytical point, which is the one the table assigns to religious imagery: the imagery promises a reversal that its administering hierarchy has every reason to fear, and the promise is therefore not the hierarchy's creature.

The reception of this side of his work is the strongest evidence that it is not an eccentricity. Jürgen Moltmann's Theology of Hope, published in 1964, took The Principle of Hope as its explicit philosophical provocation and rebuilt Protestant eschatology around the category of promise, acknowledging the debt in its opening pages. From that encounter, and from parallel readings in Latin America, the influence passed into the liberation theology of the following decades, whose practitioners found in Bloch a Marxist who treated their own scriptures as an archive of the oppressed rather than as an opiate. That afterlife, its contexts and its controversies, belongs to [the account of liberation theology and marxism](/2018/12/15/liberation-theology-and-marxism/); here it is enough to register that the religious turn in Bloch was received, by working theologians, as the most usable part of his system.

## Hope against the Frankfurt darkness

Bloch's relation to the Frankfurt School is a study in kinship and refusal. The kinship is real: shared exile, shared teachers, a shared conviction that Marxist theory had to be rebuilt in philosophy and that fascism was the event the rebuilding had to explain. He knew the Institute's principals, corresponded and conversed with Adorno across decades, and the famous recorded dialogue between them on utopia, conducted for radio in 1964, remains the most efficient introduction to both men, precisely because the disagreement is conducted between friends who grant each other's premises. The refusal is equally real. The Frankfurt trajectory, above all in Adorno, moves toward a negative dialectics in which the utopian content may be honoured only by being withheld from portrayal, guarded by the prohibition on images that Judaism lays on the name of God. Bloch's trajectory moves the opposite way: the content must be portrayed, inventoried, educated, because a hope that may not be pictured cannot be tested, inherited, or organised around.

The contrast in temperament has a theoretical root that the staging of [the comparison in critical theory vs classical marxism](/2015/09/01/critical-theory-vs-classical-marxism/) brings out from the other side. The Frankfurt analysis of the administered world tends to totality of domination: the culture industry integrates every wish in advance, and the space left for anticipation shrinks to the negations preserved in difficult art. Bloch's analysis of the same culture grants the integration and denies the shrinkage, because the integration works by borrowing the wish, and the borrowed wish remains in circulation as the surplus. Where Adorno hears in the hit song only the administered consolation, Bloch hears also the genuine longing the consolation had to imitate, and he regards the longing as historically the more important fact. Neither man is refuted by the other's hearing; the difference is a wager about where history keeps its reserves. Bloch's wager is the riskier and the more exposed, since it commits him to finding the reserve in compromised places, and the objection that his findings are unfalsifiable takes its force from exactly this exposure.

### Where exactly do Bloch and Adorno part company?

On the permissibility of the image. Adorno holds that a portrayed utopia is already betrayed, reconciled with the order it negates, and that hope survives only in the refusal to picture it. Bloch holds that an unpictured utopia cannot be criticised, taught, or inherited, and that the prohibition, however noble its motive, leaves the field of images to the advertisers.

There is a biographical undertow to the disagreement that both men acknowledged. They had shared the catastrophe, but they had not shared its sequel: Adorno returned to a rebuilt Frankfurt Institute inside a West Germany aligned with the Atlantic alliance, while Bloch took a chair inside the state that claimed to be building the hoped for order and then had to reckon, publicly, with what that state did to his philosophy. The reckoning belongs to the next section. What the dialogue of 1964 preserves is the moment before the undertow became the story: two exiles agreeing that something is missing from the world as arranged, disagreeing about whether the missing thing may be described. Listeners ever since have tended to report that Bloch won the exchange on points of warmth and Adorno on points of rigour, which is perhaps the correct verdict on the whole relationship.

## The political record: the German Democratic Republic and the departure

The record after 1945 is quickly told and should be told without embroidery. Bloch returned from American exile to a Germany in ruins and accepted, in 1949, the chair of philosophy at Leipzig, in the Soviet zone that became the German Democratic Republic. He was honoured there: a national prize, an academy membership, a journal to edit, students of real talent. He was also, from the middle nineteen fifties, in growing trouble with the state he had chosen. His philosophy, with its religious vocabulary and its refusal of catechism, was denounced as revisionist; after the Hungarian rising of 1956 and his refusal to join the ritual condemnations in the required form, the pressure hardened into removal. He was forced into emeritus status in 1957, against his will, while his books continued to appear and his name continued to draw students the regime regarded as a problem.

One earlier item of the record belongs in this section because it belongs to the same ledger of political judgement, and this profile's brief requires it to be stated plainly. In the later nineteen thirties, writing in the exile press, Bloch publicly defended the Moscow trials. The statement stands here as part of the documented record of his political life. It is not elaborated, and it is not excused, and it does not govern the reading of the philosophy of hope in what follows, any more than the philosophy is permitted to launder it.

The end of the eastern chapter came by accident of timing. In August 1961 Bloch was in the West, at Bayreuth for the Wagner festival and then in Tübingen, when the Berlin Wall was built on 13 August and the border closed behind him. He chose not to return. The decision was presented, by him, without theatricality: the state that had pensioned him against his will and policed his students had made the choice before he did. Tübingen offered a guest professorship that became a second career; students of a new generation, the generation of 1968, found in the old philosopher of hope a teacher whose Marxism had been tested against both fascism and the actually existing alternative and had come out heterodox from both ordeals. He worked there until his death in 1977, publishing to the end, the last major statement being the metaphysical Experimentum Mundi of 1975.

### What happened to Bloch in East Germany after the rising in Hungary?

Honours turned to discipline. A philosophy that spoke of God, hope, and the soul's darkness was intolerable as an official doctrine, and after 1956 the apparatus moved: denunciation as a revisionist, the harassment of his circle, removal from his chair into forced retirement, and the policing of his students. The state kept his name and withdrew its trust.

Judged whole, the political biography shows a consistent disposition rather than a party loyalty: an attachment, formed in 1917 and never entirely withdrawn, to the hope that the Russian Revolution had embodied for his generation, combined with an increasing inability, across the Leipzig years, to mistake the administrative state for the hope's fulfilment. Readers will weigh the items of the record differently, and the Moscow item in particular will weigh heavily with many. What the record does not show is a thinker who trimmed the philosophy to the politics. The heterodoxy was the constant; the affiliations were its casualties, in sequence.

## Heritage, matter, and the unfinished world

Two late pieces of the system deserve a paragraph each, because they show its reach. The first is the concept of heritage already glimpsed in the expressionism debate. In its mature form it is a rule for reading the whole of past culture: a thing is inherited legitimately only in the portion of it that its own time left unfulfilled. The Gothic cathedral, the peasant war, the bourgeoisie's own unredeemed promises of liberty and equality, all descend to the present not as assets but as unpaid bills. The rule disciplines the revolutionary's relation to tradition, replacing both the antiquarian's reverence and the iconoclast's bonfire with an audit: keep what is still owed, and let the rest be history. It also completes the answer to the charge that his cultural readings collect everything and discriminate nothing. The audit has a criterion, and the criterion is the unpaid bill.

The second is the speculative doctrine of matter. Bloch's materialism is not the mechanical materialism of billiard atoms, which he regarded as a bourgeois residue inside Marxist orthodoxy. Drawing on what he called the left wing of Aristotelianism, the line from Avicenna and Averroes through Giordano Bruno, for whom matter was not dead extension but a bearing, productive potency, he treats matter itself as unfinished, as the That in process whose possibilities the not yet become names. Critics have found this the least defensible stratum of the system, a metaphysical romance dressed as physics. Its function inside the system is nevertheless clear: it supplies the ontology that the politics requires, a world whose grain runs toward the Novum rather than merely permitting it. A reader may decline the metaphysics and keep the method, as many of his best readers have done, but the architecture should be seen whole at least once: hope is warranted, for Bloch, not because human beings are stubborn, but because being itself is still in labour.

### Can the utopian surplus be tested, or only asserted?

It can be argued, case by case, against criteria: whether an anticipation corresponds to a real tendency in the conditions, whether the form's promise survives the order that commissioned the form, and whether independent readers, applying them to the same form, converge. What it cannot have is a balance sheet's certainty, and his honesty lies in not pretending otherwise.

## The strongest objection: a method that cannot lose

The objection must now be stated at the strength its best proponents would want. A method that instructs its user to find, in virtually any cultural form, an excess pointing toward a better world is a method that cannot be refuted by any exhibit. Show the interpreter a piece of pure propaganda, and the surplus is found in the longing the propaganda had to borrow. Show him a commercial artefact of perfect cynicism, and the surplus is found in the promise the cynicism had to imitate. Show him a form in which he finds nothing, and the failure is assigned to the interpreter's insufficient attention rather than to the method. A finding that no evidence can disconfirm is not a finding but a licence, and the utopian surplus, on this charge, is a licence to read one's own hopes into the whole of culture and call the reading scholarship. The objection is the standard one raised against every hermeneutics of sympathy, and Bloch's version is an unusually exposed target because his inventory is unusually comprehensive.

His answer, assembled from across the work, has three parts, and the profile gives it in his terms before judging it. First, the surplus is not a free projection but a correspondence claim: an anticipation counts only where it fastens on a real tendency, on conditions whose maturation can be described in the cold vocabulary of economics and politics, and a reading that cannot name the tendency fails by the method's own rule. Second, the method has negative cases and uses them: abstract utopia is precisely the category of anticipation judged and found wanting, and whole classes of form, the administered optimism of regimes, the consolation that reconciles without remainder, are read by Bloch himself with a severity equal to any Frankfurt page. Third, the heritage audit discards more than it keeps: only the unfulfilled portion descends, and the fulfilled promises of dead orders, their pride and their self satisfaction, are assigned to history without appeal.

The concession required by this profile's brief, and required by honesty in any case, comes after the answer and is not withdrawn by it. Applying the criterion of real tendency demands exactly the judgement whose reliability the objection questions. Two competent readers, applying Bloch's tests to the same artefact, can divide, and the method supplies no tribunal above their division except the subsequent course of history, which delivers its verdicts late and ambiguously. The utopian surplus is therefore best regarded as a research programme with a disciplined heuristic, not as a detector with a dial: it tells the investigator where to look and what questions to bring, and it earns its keep, in the non-synchronism case above all, by the explanations it has actually produced. A programme that produced that explanation has a claim on attention that a merely self sealing doctrine would not have, and the claim survives the objection, diminished but standing.

## Misreadings to correct

Five errors recur in the short accounts of Bloch that circulate in textbooks and reference pages, and each can be retired briefly. First, he was not a naive optimist. Concrete utopia is defined against wishful thinking, docta spes is hope that has survived an education in disappointment, and the theorist of hope was a refugee who had predicted, more accurately than most, the disaster that overtook his country. Optimism is a forecast; hope, in his vocabulary, is a discipline, and the two have opposite relations to evidence. Second, his interest in religion was not a departure from his Marxism but one of its founding applications: the Münzer study precedes his mature Marxism and supplies its model of inheritance, and the late book on atheism and Christianity is continuous with it in method and in aim. Third, non-synchronism is not uneven development under another name; the one maps times within a society, the other stages between societies, and confusing them loses the specific explanation of fascism that the first was built to carry. Fourth, the utopian surplus is not the claim that popular culture is secretly progressive. The surplus is a separable excess, quite compatible with the form that carries it doing reactionary work in its own moment; the fairy tale's justice is a reproach, not a programme, and it becomes politically active only when an educated hope takes it up. Fifth, he was not a peripheral figure. He held one of the prominent chairs of his country, formed a school, drew the attention of theologians of the stature of Moltmann, and left a system whose English reception, though delayed, has been steadily widening since the translations arrived. Peripherality describes his place in the Anglophone syllabus of a particular generation, not his place in the tradition.

## Style as method, and the delayed arrival in English

A last section of the profile belongs to the prose itself, because with Bloch the manner is an instrument. The Spirit of Utopia opens not with definitions but with a meditation on a jug, on the self, on the darkness at the centre of experience, and the great book sustains the method at scale: concepts are earned by passage through images, anecdotes, quotations, and exhibits, so that the reader is made to perform the anticipatory attention the theory describes. Admirers call this the only honest style for a philosophy of the not yet, since a treatise that merely reported hope in the idiom of the registrar would refute its content by its form. Detractors call it a shelter, a prose in which imprecision can always plead poetry. Both judgements identify the same fact: Bloch asks his style to do argumentative work, and the reader must decide, page by page, whether the work has been done. The question is left open here deliberately, because it is the live question in the scholarship, and because a profile that settled it would misrepresent the state of the field.

The English arrival came in a rush once it came. The Principle of Hope appeared in 1986 in the translation of Neville Plaice, Stephen Plaice, and Paul Knight; Heritage of Our Times followed in 1991; The Spirit of Utopia in 2000. The effect of the translations was to reveal how much Anglophone debate, on utopia, on fascism's appeal, on religion and politics, had been conducted in ignorance of its most systematic participant, and the secondary literature has been repairing the omission since. For the reader of this series, the route into the system that costs least and yields most runs from the non-synchronism chapters of the Heritage, through the second part of the great book, where the categories are laid down, to the cultural readings of the later parts, taken as exercises in the method rather than as verdicts to be memorised. The companion treatment in this series of the tradition's reading habits, the guide to its cultural theory, is at [the marxist cultural theory guide](/2020/06/01/marxist-cultural-theory-guide/), where Bloch's readings are set among the rival schools of interpretation they have to compete with.

## The works in depth: the self that does not have itself

The Spirit of Utopia begins with a sentence that has been quoted ever since by those who want the whole philosophy in miniature. The philosopher writes, in paraphrase, that he is, but that he does not have himself, and that we are therefore in the process of becoming. The thought is stranger than its brevity suggests. The self, on this account, is not a possession secured at birth but a task set by an obscurity at the centre of experience: the lived moment is dark to itself, because the one who lives it cannot step outside it and inspect it, and the whole of human self seeking, in art, in religion, in philosophy, is the attempt to catch up with a self that is always slightly ahead of its owner. Bloch calls this condition by a phrase that runs through the work, the darkness of the just lived moment, and he treats the darkness not as a defect of introspection but as the signature of the not yet inside the person. What has not yet become cannot be seen in full light, and the self is the nearest specimen of what has not yet become.

The early book builds its philosophy of art on this foundation. A work of art matters, in its pages, when the maker succeeds in objectifying a piece of the darkness, in giving the unspoken self encounter a form in which others can meet their own. The famous discussions of the ornament, of the carpet pattern, of pottery and of Gothic building, are applications of this single test. Some forms merely decorate the given world, reconciling the eye to what exists. Others press beyond their material toward a condition that does not exist, and the pressing can be felt, Bloch insists, by any attentive beholder, in the surplus of expression over utility and over representation. The aesthetic theory of the mature work, with its doctrine of anticipation, is this youthful test grown systematic, and readers who begin with the early book often find the great one's inventories less bewildering, because they have already watched the test applied at the scale of a jug and a church window.

## Vor-Schein: art as anticipatory illumination

The mature name for what art does, in Bloch's vocabulary, is Vor-Schein, a term that English renderings give as pre appearance or anticipatory illumination. The concept is needed because of a problem the utopian surplus raises in its sharpest form for the arts. A painting or a symphony that merely copied the existing world would be, by his standard, a failure of the utopian function even if its craftsmanship were perfect, since it would show the reader nothing that the world had not already shown. A work that simply invented a fantasy world would fail in the opposite direction, as abstract utopia in pigment or sound. Vor-Schein names the third possibility: a semblance that is more than semblance, an appearance in which a real possibility of the world genuinely, if partially, appears in advance. The work does not depict the fulfilled condition. It lets a fragment of it be experienced, in the mode of art, ahead of its historical existence, and the experience is a form of knowledge because the possibility is real.

The doctrine explains several of Bloch's otherwise puzzling judgements. It explains why he ranks music highest among the arts: music, having almost no representational duty, is the purest medium of anticipation, sounding the not yet without having to describe anything. It explains his defence of the fragment and the ruin in modern art, since a broken form may carry its anticipation more honestly than a completed one in an age whose own completion would be a lie. And it explains his severity toward art that settles into accomplished beauty on the world's behalf, the art of the achieved facade, which he treats as the aesthetic analogue of ideology in its strict sense. Vor-Schein is therefore the technical hinge between the cultural readings and the political philosophy: the same correspondence between image and real tendency that grades a hope grades a work of art, and fails both when the correspondence cannot be shown.

## Bloch and Freud: the inheritance refused and accepted

The relationship to psychoanalysis is the clearest case of his method of inheritance applied to a modern doctrine. Bloch accepts from Freud the discovery that consciousness is not master in its own house, that wishes work in disguise, and that the dream is a document in which the wishes can be read. He then contests the tense of the whole apparatus. The Freudian unconscious, he argues, is a museum of the past: its contents are what consciousness once contained and repressed, and its analysis is an archaeology. Against this he sets the not yet conscious, whose contents were never present to be repressed, because they belong to a future that has not yet furnished them with objects. The analyst digs; the philosopher of hope, on Bloch's picture, listens forward, attending to the stammering in which the genuinely new announces itself in daydreams, in artistic experiments, and in the restlessness of an age before it knows what it wants.

The polemic has a further edge that readers sometimes miss. Bloch charged psychoanalysis, as practised and as theorised, with mistaking the historically specific contents of the bourgeois soul for the structure of the soul as such: the family romance of the Viennese consulting room universalised into human nature. A forward looking doctrine of the wish, by contrast, must be historical through and through, since what an age can wish depends on what its conditions have made imaginable and ripening. The disagreement is therefore not a rivalry of clinics but a dispute about whether the inner life has a history that runs forward as well as back. Bloch's own writings on the daydream, on the fairy tale, and on the wishful image are his evidence: exhibits of an inner life whose most characteristic products point, demonstrably, at objects that do not yet exist.

## The small daydreams: an inventory of ordinary wishing

The opening part of the great book is the least philosophical and, for many readers, the most winning, because it performs the inventory method on the wishes everyone recognises. Bloch walks through the small daydreams of the ordinary day: the wish for the admired body seen in the mirror's despite, the fantasies that gather around illness and the doctor, the daydreams of youth about fame and of age about youth, the Sunday wishes, the travel wishes, the wishes invested in clothes, in rooms, in being recognised at last for what one is. Each wish is read twice, in the manner this profile has described. Read once, it is what the market and the magazines have made of it, a hook for products and a sedative for discontent. Read again, it is a genuine register of unmet human claims: for health that does not depend on wealth, for recognition that does not depend on rank, for a life in which the body's decay and the day's labour are not the whole story.

The exercise matters because it relocates the utopian from the library to the street. The philosophers of utopia, from Plato to the socialist designers, had composed their better worlds at desks; Bloch's inventory insists that the better world is already being composed, fragmentarily and corruptibly, in the wishes of people who have never read a line of philosophy. The theorist's task is editorial rather than authorial: to collect the fragments, purge them of their commercial and consolatory distortions, and return them to their owners in educated form. There is a politics implied in the editorial metaphor that Bloch does not hide. A movement that addresses people only at the level of their interests, wages, hours, security, speaks to the night side of their existence; the daydreams are where their demands on life itself are stored, and a politics that cannot read them will always be surprised, as his generation was surprised, by the movements that can.

## Wishful images in the mirror: fashion, dance, and the screen

The third part of the great book extends the inventory to the public mirrors in which an age displays its wishes to itself. Fashion is read as the most ephemeral and therefore the most honest of these mirrors: its rapid turnover confesses that no existing arrangement of the body and its coverings satisfies, and each season's novelty is a small, commercialised Novum, a proof that the wish for a changed appearance of life renews itself faster than any industry can exhaust it. The dance hall receives a reading of similar structure. In the collective rhythm of popular dancing Bloch finds an acted anticipation of unalienated sociality, bodies moving together for pleasure rather than for production or drill, and he is severe only about the industry's fencing of the pleasure, not about the pleasure itself, which he treats as a fragment of the good life rehearsed under bad conditions.

Film, the youngest of his exhibits, draws his most ambivalent pages. The cinema inherits the fairy tale's audience and the daydream's structure, and it can do what no earlier form could: show the wishful image in motion, to millions, in the dark, simultaneously. The same capacities make it the most efficient instrument for administering the wish, and Bloch's judgements on particular products swing between gratitude and contempt in a way that has puzzled readers who expect a verdict on the medium. His consistency lies one level down, in the test rather than the verdict: whatever the product, ask what wish it had to borrow, whether the borrowing honours or betrays the wish, and what of the wish survives the sitting. The method transfers without alteration to media he never saw, which is why this part of the book continues to be raided by critics of forms that postdate him by generations.

## The social utopias examined and graded

The fourth part of the book conducts the most sustained examination ever attempted from inside the Marxist tradition of the utopian literature itself, and it is here that the grading of hopes becomes a literary history. Bloch walks the long shelf: the island commonwealths of the Renaissance, the city of the sun, the phalanstery with its calculus of the passions, the news from nowhere, the technological paradises and the theocratic ones, the medical utopias of the deathless body and the geographical utopias of the emptied continent. His judgements are never merely dismissive, even where the construction is hopeless as politics, because each design is cross examined for its surplus: the human claim it registers for the first time, the misery of its own age that it answers, the element that later, better mediated hopes can still inherit. Fourier, whimsical and grandiose, is praised for the seriousness with which he treated the organisation of desire and labour's attractiveness. The stern authoritarian designs are made to confess the fear of freedom that built them. The technological utopias are asked what their machines are for, and found eloquent about production and silent about the purposes production might serve.

The cumulative effect of the survey is a redefinition of the tradition's own ancestry. Marxist orthodoxy had treated the utopian socialists as honoured forerunners whose scientific deficiency was their defining trait. Bloch's shelf reverses the emphasis: the deficiency is real, and it is the abstractness his own distinction names, but the defining trait is the surplus, the fact that these writers kept the image of the good life in circulation through centuries when no movement could carry it. The scientific socialism that succeeded them inherited their images along with the critique that exposed their weakness, and it impoverishes itself when it forgets the debt. This is the warm stream doctrine applied to literary history, and it explains why The Principle of Hope, alone among the classics of the tradition, reads at times like a history of everything human beings have ever wanted.

## Medicine, the body, and the hardest anti utopia

Among the outlines of a better life collected in the fourth part, the medical chapters strike readers as the most unexpected and, on reflection, the most revealing. Bloch treats the wish that animates medicine, the wish for the body that does not decay on schedule, as one of the oldest organised utopias, older than any political design, and he reads the history of healing as the history of that wish acquiring technique. The doctor in his pages is a figure of anticipation in a white coat, his art suspended between the given limits of the organism and a horizon in which those limits are treated as provisional. The wish is easily mocked as the wish for immortality in disguise, and Bloch concedes the disguise while refusing the mockery. Underneath the fantasy of endless life stands a soberer claim, that suffering and premature death are not natural facts to be contemplated but conditions to be abolished, and that medicine's whole dignity derives from having organised itself, however imperfectly, around that abolition.

The theme darkens in the final part, where death is named the hardest anti utopia, the strongest counter instance to everything the system affirms. Bloch does not dissolve the problem with the consolations he has spent a book confiscating from the churches. His treatment works instead at two points where he thinks the anti utopia can be honestly answered. The first is the distinction between the death of the individual and the fate of what the individual has joined: the work done toward the kingdom, the content a life has added to the world's store of the not yet, is not annulled by the worker's extinction, and the tradition that carries it is a real, not a rhetorical, survival. The second is the character of the fulfilled moment itself, which the book's closing pages describe in terms of an identity between the subject and its world so complete that the question of duration loses its sting. Readers divide sharply over whether these answers console, and the division is recorded here without a verdict, because Bloch's own text holds the two registers, the argumentative and the invocatory, in a suspension he clearly intended. What the death chapters accomplish beyond dispute is to show the system at its limit, testing its categories against the one phenomenon that no tendency in the present seems likely to repeal, and declining, at the limit, to cheat.

## Built anticipation: architecture and the geography of wishes

The treatment of architecture extends the argument of The Spirit of Utopia, where building had already served as a chief exhibit. A building, for Bloch, is the wish of an age rendered in stone and therefore legible long after the age's texts have gone silent. The Egyptian pyramid and the Greek temple embody orders in which death and measure rule; the Gothic cathedral embodies a different longing, vertical, restless, dissolving stone into light and aspiration, and his early book famously reads the Gothic interior as the self encounter of a humanity reaching beyond its assigned place. The reading is not art history in the professional sense and does not compete with it. It is the surplus test applied to masonry: what did this form promise its builders beyond shelter and glorification, and does the promise still address a passer by who shares nothing with the builders' creed? The cathedral, on his account, passes the test that the palace fails, because the palace's promise was exhausted by the power it displayed, while the cathedral's vertical longing survives the faith that commissioned it.

Geography receives the same treatment at the scale of the earth. The fourth part surveys the wishful landscapes of the tradition: the islands of the blessed, the southern seas of the explorers' reports, the emptied continents onto which Europe projected its designs for a fresh start, the painted landscapes in which an unspoiled nature waits like a held breath. Bloch is alert to the colonial violence inside the projections, to the fact that the empty land of the European imagination was inhabited, and his readings of the discovery literature are among the earliest in the tradition to treat the geographical utopia as a document of both hope and appropriation. What he salvages from the wreckage is the underlying wish itself, for a homeland reconciled with human dwelling, which he renames with the word that closes the whole system. The wish survives its criminal vehicles, as the surplus doctrine predicts, and it descends, as a task, to any politics that proposes to settle human beings on the earth without repeating the appropriation.

## Music at the summit

The fifth and final part of the work, the part of the fulfilled moment, gives its longest and most intense chapters to music, and the placement is a thesis in itself. Morality is treated, religion is treated, death is confronted, but the summit of the anticipatory arts is occupied by the art that Bloch had placed at the centre of his first book sixty years before. The reasons are systematic. Music is the art least bound to the representation of what exists; its material is time organised by feeling, and the not yet, which is itself a structure of time and feeling, finds in music its least distorted register. A melody's phrase is literally an anticipation that the following phrase confirms, corrects, or disappoints, and Bloch builds an entire phenomenology on this observation: in music, the listener experiences, in purified form, the forward structure that in ordinary life is buried under habit and merchandise. The great composers are read as the cartographers of this experience, with Beethoven's Fidelio, the opera of liberation from the dungeon, standing as the type of the work in which the anticipation becomes an event inside the artwork that prefigures the event outside it.

There is a consequence for the system's architecture that should be made explicit. A philosophy that ends in music has declined, at its summit, the resources of prose argument, and Bloch knows it. The closing movements of the book are written in the full knowledge that the Ultimum, the final figure of fulfilment, cannot be described in the indicative mood without falsification; it can be approached only through the forms, musical, religious, and poetic, in which humanity has practised approaching it. Critics who charge the ending with abandoning philosophy for invocation are registering a real feature. The defence available to him, and it is the defence his whole aesthetic implies, is that the invocation is itself evidence: the fact that the imagination, at its most disciplined, produces these figures and cannot produce their discursive equivalents is a datum about the structure of the not yet, not a failure of the author's nerve.

## Natural law, dignity, and the upright gait

The Tübingen years produced, alongside the metaphysical and theological works, a book on law that Bloch's political readers regard as the system's most direct bequest: Naturrecht und menschliche Würde, Natural Law and Human Dignity, published in 1961. Its argument recovers a tradition that Marxist jurisprudence had surrendered to its opponents. Orthodoxy had treated natural law, the doctrine that human beings carry claims by nature that no positive law may cancel, as a bourgeois illusion, and had filed the rights of man under ideology. Bloch replies that the tradition divides, like every other inheritance, into an ideological function and a surplus. The function is real: rights talk has decorated orders that violated every right it praised. The surplus is also real: the doctrine of human dignity, of the upright gait, as he calls the posture of the human being who no longer bows, is the one element of the bourgeois inheritance that points beyond every order that has administered it, including the socialist orders of his own century.

The phrase upright gait is worth dwelling on, because it condenses his political anthropology into an image. The history of domination is written, in his reading, in postures: the bowed back of the peasant, the kneeling of the subject, the averted eyes of the servant, and against them the figures of refusal, Münzer's peasants with their articles, the citizens who would no longer uncover, the workers whose organisations taught them to march upright in ranks. A politics of hope, on this showing, has a somatic test available to it at every moment: whether the human beings it addresses are being taught to stand straighter or trained to bow lower. The test is crude and Bloch knew its crudeness; he valued it because it could not be met by statistics, and because the states that claimed his allegiance failed it in ways their growth figures could not answer. The natural law book is therefore the bridge between the philosophy of hope and the criticism of actually existing socialism that the Leipzig years had made necessary, and it is not an accident that it appeared in the year of his departure from the East.

## Alliance technology and the question of nature

A further late theme, developed across the geographical and technical chapters of the great book and sharpened in the Tübingen writings, concerns the human relation to nature. Bloch rejects the picture of technology as the domination of a passive nature by an active humanity, the picture he finds in the Baconian programme and in the practice of both capitalist and socialist industrialism, which in this respect struck him as twins. Against it he sets the idea of an alliance technology, a Technik that would work with the productive tendencies latent in nature itself, treating natural process as a co producer rather than a warehouse. The idea rests on the speculative doctrine of matter encountered earlier in this profile: if nature is not dead extension but unfinished potency, then a technology that merely extracts and discards is not only destructive but ignorant, a failure to read the material it handles.

The theme has made Bloch a reference point for ecological readers who arrived long after his death, and the appropriation needs the usual caution. He was no prophet of limits and no enemy of production; his quarrel was with a metaphysics of dead matter and with the waste it licensed, not with the human transformation of nature, which his system celebrates as the medium of the not yet. What the ecological readers legitimately inherit is the question his technology chapters teach one to ask of any technical programme: whether it treats its material as an adversary to be subdued, a stock to be spent, or a tendency to be allied with, and whether its products answer the wishes inventoried in the daydreams or merely the wishes administered by the market. The question is Blochian in form even where the answers would have surprised him, which is the mark of a method that has detached itself from its author's positions and entered general circulation.

## The categories completed: Front, Novum, Ultimum, and real possibility

The technical vocabulary of the second part of The Principle of Hope repays a compact exposition, because these are the instruments with which the rest of the system is operated. Bloch thinks of reality as layered by degrees of possibility. There is the formally possible, whatever does not contradict itself, the cheapest currency of speculation. There is the factually grounded possible, what the known conditions permit one to expect. There is the objectively real possible, the decisive category: that whose conditions are not merely compatible with the present but are partially assembled within it, ripening in the womb of the process, so that its realisation is a tendency and not a lottery. Concrete utopia, in the vocabulary of this scheme, is the imagination's alliance with the third layer, and abstract utopia is its dalliance with the first two. The scheme gives the criterion of real tendency, on which the answer to the unfalsifiability objection depends, its precise sense: a tendency is not a hope with footnotes but a demonstrable partial assembly of conditions, and an anticipation that claims the third layer while exhibiting only the first is convicted, by the system's own court, of abstraction.

Three further terms govern the dynamics. The Front is Bloch's name for the foremost segment of time, the narrow band where the new is actually deciding itself, and he insists that philosophy must station itself at the Front rather than in the archive or the observatory, since the categories of the not yet can be filled only by attention to what is emerging. The Novum is the content that emerges there when it is genuinely new, not the rearrangement of the old but the irruption of what no inventory of the present could have listed in advance, and its possibility is what separates his ontology from every closed dialectic in which the future is the past unfolded. The Ultimum is the final figure, the state of identity between the human project and the world toward which the series of Nova points as toward a horizon: never possessed, never dispensable, the pole that gives the Front its direction and hope its seriousness. A reader who holds these five terms, real possibility, tendency, Front, Novum, Ultimum, holds the skeleton of the system, and the flesh of the inventories can then be read as the evidence locker in which the skeleton is tested.

## Bloch as a reader of Hegel

The system's debts are usually traced to Marx, Schelling, and the mystical tradition, and all three are real, but the Hegel debt is the one Bloch himself was most anxious to state correctly, and his book Subjekt Objekt, published in 1949 and expanded in 1951, is the record of the attempt. He reads Hegel as the philosopher in whom the world first became a process to itself, in whom the subject and the object of history were shown to be one movement apprehending itself, and he accepts this inheritance entire. What he refuses is the closure: the point at which Hegel's system, having generated the whole movement out of the Idea, declares the movement complete in the philosopher's comprehension of it. For Bloch the comprehension arrives too early, since the world it comprehends is unfinished; a philosophy of the whole that closes the account while the account is still being written has mistaken its own summary for the world's end. His formula for the correction became one of his signatures: the truth is not a minted coin but a process, and the subject of the process is not yet identical with its object, which is why there remains something for hope to do.

The correction locates Bloch precisely within the family of left Hegelianisms that the series treats across this cluster. Where the Frankfurt thinkers preserve Hegel's negativity by surrendering his affirmations, and where the orthodox preserved the affirmations by surrendering the negativity to a catechism, Bloch attempts the expensive option of keeping both, the negativity as the engine and the affirmation as the horizon, at the price of the speculative doctrine of matter that his critics find indigestible. The price may be refused. What the Subjekt Objekt reading establishes, even for those who refuse it, is that the philosophy of hope is not an abandonment of the Hegelian Marxist inheritance but one of its most learned continuations, written by a man who had read the whole of Hegel with a pencil in his hand and intended the correction as an act of loyalty.

## Bloch and Benjamin: two readers of the debris

The comparison with Walter Benjamin imposes itself on every reader of both men, and a profile of Bloch is the poorer for omitting it, since they are the two great collectors of the tradition, the two who believed that the truth of an age hides in its discarded objects. The affinities are extensive. Both read the nineteenth century through its arcades and its rubbish; both treated allegory and the fairy tale as philosophical documents; both refused the doctrine of progress in its social democratic form, Benjamin in the theses that picture the angel of history blown backward into the future, Bloch in the category of non-synchronism, which denies that the present is one time at all. Both, finally, gave theological motifs a working place inside a materialism, Benjamin in the figure of a weak messianic power, Bloch in the Exodus God and the kingdom. German exile scattered them to different fates, Benjamin to his death at the Spanish border in 1940 and Bloch to the long American productivity, and the divergence of fates should not be allowed to hide the divergence of doctrine inside the affinity.

The divergence is the familiar one of this profile, stated now in its most refined form. Benjamin's anticipation is structured by interruption: the past flashes up in a moment of danger, and redemption, if the word is allowed, is the arrest of a continuum rather than its fulfilment. Bloch's anticipation is structured by ripening: the tendency matures, the Front advances, and fulfilment, however distant, is the direction of the process rather than its arrest. Critics have treated the two as rival heirs competing for the same estate, the Marxist philosophy of history in the age of catastrophe. The more useful judgement, and the one this series' separate treatments support, is that they are instruments calibrated to different emergencies: Benjamin's to the emergency of a tradition about to be erased, which must be seized in the flash, and Bloch's to the emergency of a hope about to be administered to death, which must be educated for the long haul. A complete equipment probably requires both instruments, and the reader is not asked to choose a loyalty, only to know which emergency each was built for.

## Heimat: the last word of the system

The Principle of Hope ends with a passage so often detached from its context that it has become a motto on calendars, and the profile restores the context in closing the exposition. The final pages argue that all the wishes inventoried in the book converge on a single figure, which Bloch names with the German word Heimat, home. The word is loaded, in German, with the kitsch of the homeland film and the worse load of the nationalism that made home a border with guards. Bloch's use of it is a deliberate confiscation, of the same kind as his confiscation of religious hope. Heimat, redefined by the argument of a thousand pages, names the condition in which human beings would be identical with their world as the child is identical with the world of childhood, before the estrangement begins, except that this identity would be achieved at the level of the whole species and its remade conditions, and would therefore be a homecoming to a place where no one has yet been. The last clause is the signature of the system in a single turn: the deepest wish is for something that has never existed, so that nostalgia, the ache for the lost home, is unmasked as anticipation in disguise, the forward drive wearing the costume of the backward look.

The figure also completes the answer to the question of what the Ultimum could possibly mean without theology. A home where no one has yet been is not a place elsewhere, not a heaven and not a lost paradise; it is the world's own content in the state of being finally adequate to the beings who produced it, and the definition is deliberately left at the level of a figure, since the system's whole discipline forbids furnishing the figure with furniture. Readers who find the ending a swindle, the substitution of a beautiful word for an argument, are referred to the architecture they have traversed: the figure is earned, or not, by the inventories and the categories that precede it, and it stands in the text as their sum rather than as their replacement. The philosopher who began by writing that he was but did not have himself ends by naming the condition in which the self and its world would have each other. Between those two sentences the system lives, and a profile can do no more, in the end, than show that the distance between them was traversed with instruments and not merely with longing.

## Learning hope: the pedagogy hidden in the system

The opening pages of The Principle of Hope pose the questions with which the book begins, in paraphrase: who are we, where do we come from, where are we going, what do we expect, what is waiting for us. The questions are a child's and a prisoner's at once, and Bloch's insistence on beginning there, rather than with a definition, states the pedagogical thesis of the whole work: hope is not a gift of temperament that some possess and others lack but a capacity that can be learned, and unlearned, and taught badly by societies that profit from its absence or its corruption. The first affect to be schooled is the negative one. Fear, anxiety, and despair are not refuted by encouragement; they are outbid, when the inventory shows the despairing person that the world contains more ripening possibility than the fear had registered, and that the registration, not the world, was the prison.

The pedagogy explains the otherwise curious form of the book, its refusal to behave like a treatise. A treatise informs; an inventory trains. By marching the reader through hundreds of exhibits, wishes in windows and wishes in cathedrals, wishes in fairy tales and wishes in systems, the book exercises the anticipatory attention itself, so that finishing it leaves the reader with a strengthened faculty rather than with a memorised doctrine. Teachers who have used the book report the same experience in their students as readers report privately: the difficulty is not assent but stamina, and the reward is not a conclusion but a changed way of noticing. It is fitting that the series' own classroom guidance, given in the persona sections below, should end where the system ends, with the proposal that hope, like reading, is a skill with a technique, and that the technique can be practised on any cultural material whatsoever once the surplus test has been learned.

## The Bible read from below: Exodus, Job, and the serpent

The book on atheism and Christianity is the fruit of the religious reading practised since Münzer, and its method deserves a fuller account than the aphorism it is usually reduced to. Bloch reads the Bible as a document with a fault line running through it. On one side stands the religion of the creator God, the Lord of heaven who made the world as a potter makes a vessel and demands obedience as the rent for existence; this is the Bible of the priesthoods and the catechisms, the book as an instrument of patience. On the other side, never extinguished, runs what he calls the Bible's underground: the Exodus, in which the divine name itself is a promise of departure and the God of slaves defines himself by a future tense; the prophets, who turn the cult against the kings; Job, who accuses heaven and is answered, in Bloch's daring reading, by a voice that concedes more than the comforters allowed; the serpent in Eden, whose promise that the eaters would become as gods Bloch reads not as the book's lie but as its buried truth; and finally Jesus, read as the Son of Man, a title Bloch stresses against Son of God, whose kingdom is announced to the poor and whose cross is the empire's answer to the announcement.

The reading is not offered as church history, and scholars of the Bible have contested many of its philological moves, the rendering of the Exodus name above all. Its interest for this profile is the method it completes. The Bible is treated exactly as the table treats the cathedral and the fairy tale: as a form whose ideological function, the sanctification of obedience, is undeniable, and whose surplus, an archive of departure, accusation, and kingdom, exceeds the function so massively that the custodians have spent two millennia trying to contain it. Transcending without transcendence is Bloch's formula for the inheritance he proposes: the movement of going beyond every given condition is kept, the heavenly object that was supposed to receive the movement is surrendered, and the kingdom is relocated to the only place his philosophy ever locates fulfilment, the future of this world. Whether the formula describes a possible religion or the elegant dissolution of religion into hope is a question his theological readers have argued ever since, and Moltmann's school, while taking the hope, declined the dissolution, which is one reason the encounter between the Marxist and the theologians remained an encounter and not a merger.

## Low forms and their dignity: the detective novel, Karl May, and kitsch

The defence of unrespectable forms is not confined to the great book's inventories; it runs through the essays of a lifetime and constitutes one of his most imitated habits. Consider the detective novel, to which he devoted a philosophical essay. The genre's machinery, the corpse, the clues, the detective's reconstruction, is read as a training in a specific cognitive posture: the world of the story is one in which nothing is accidental, in which the overlooked detail is the significant one, and in which a hidden order of guilt can be brought to light by attention. That posture, Bloch argues, is the posture of the historical materialist confronting a society whose surface conceals its workings, and the mass appetite for the genre is evidence of a mass appetite for a world in which appearances do not have the last word. The essay neither canonises the genre nor excuses its formulae; it performs the surplus test on a form that cultivated taste had dismissed unread, and finds, as the test predicts, a genuine cognitive wish inside the commercial husk.

Karl May, the fabulist of the American West and the Orient whom generations of German boys read under the blanket, receives the same treatment, and Bloch, who had praised him in print as a young man when praise cost reputation, never retracted the judgement. May's geographies were invented, his ethnology was fantasy, and his plots were machines; what the boy readers took from him, on Bloch's account, was the figure of the just companion and the wide world in which justice could ride, a wishful image of solidarity across the frontier that the official literature of the empire did not supply. Kitsch, the category under which such forms are usually buried, is thereby split in two: there is the kitsch that reconciles, the sweetened image that sends its consumer back to work consoled, and there is the kitsch that smuggles, in debased form, a content the high culture of the age was too timid to carry. The distinction is the surplus test again, in its most provocative application, and it is the point at which Bloch's aesthetics parts company most visibly from the Frankfurt severity, for which the debased form was the message and no smuggled content survived the debasement.

## Against Heidegger: the ontology of the already become

The confrontation with Heidegger is the least advertised and in some ways the most instructive of Bloch's polemics, because the two men were the century's rival ontologists of the human relation to time. Heidegger's analysis had made being toward death the horizon that gives existence its seriousness, and anxiety the fundamental mood in which the truth of existence is disclosed. Bloch's counter analysis, developed across the book's foundational part and the Tübingen lectures, accepts the challenge on the chosen ground and disputes both terms. Death, he argues, is indeed the hardest anti utopia, and he says so in his own pages without flinching; but an ontology that takes the end as the horizon mistakes the boundary of life for its content, and builds the nothing into the foundation where the not yet belongs. Anxiety, correspondingly, is demoted from fundamental mood to what it is in experience: one affect among others, the affect of a threatened existence, whose opposite number, hope, has the better claim to fundamentality because it alone is directed at the dimension, the future, in which everything undecided about existence actually lies.

The deeper charge is methodological, and it generalises beyond the one opponent. Heidegger's ontology, for all its talk of historicity, is in Bloch's reading an ontology of the already become: its categories are drawn from what existence has been, its temporality is dominated by a past that throws the self forward only to gather it back into finitude, and the genuinely new, the Novum, has no category and therefore no existence in it. A philosophy that cannot think the new will always, whatever its politics, serve as the metaphysics of resignation, since it teaches that the utmost available to human beings is a lucid relationship to what they already are. Bloch's whole system can be read as the construction of the missing categories against this resignation, which is why the polemic with Heidegger, conducted mostly by implication and in footnotes, is worth a section in a profile: it shows the not yet doing its most purely philosophical work, contesting the description of time itself with the century's most formidable describer.

## The unkept promises of the bourgeoisie

One extension of the non-synchronism argument deserves separate notice, because it turns the category against the class that considered itself the very type of the synchronous. The bourgeoisie, Bloch observes, is itself the bearer of an enormous non-synchronism, not in its material base but in its promises. Liberty, equality, fraternity; the rights of man; the career open to talents; the rule of law against the rule of birth: these were issued by the bourgeois revolution as the charter of the new order, and the new order proceeded to honour them in a currency so debased that the charter became the standing indictment of its issuer. The promises are not residues of an earlier formation. They are the unfulfilled content of the ruling formation itself, its own not yet, and they therefore possess a legitimacy inside the order's own terms that no imported doctrine can match. Every movement that has marched under the bourgeoisie's own slogans against the bourgeoisie's own practice, from the Chartists citing the rights of man to the civil rights marchers citing the constitution of their republic, has been drawing on this account, and Bloch's theory is, among other things, the theory of why the account never empties.

The point completes the political teaching of the Heritage analysis. Fascism's mobilisation of the archaic strata was one raid on the storehouse of unfulfilled wishes; the bourgeoisie's unkept charter is another storehouse, nearer the centre of the modern order, and the left that Bloch addressed was nearly as negligent of the second as of the first, preferring to denounce the charter as a fraud entire rather than to present itself as the charter's executor. His counsel, constant from the nineteen thirties to the Tübingen years, is that a hope which cannot inherit, from peasants, from artisans, from believers, and from the bourgeoisie's own betrayed promises, will find every storehouse in the enemy's hands, stocked with real goods and guarded by real grievances. The philosophy of hope is sometimes read as a consolation. In its political chapters it is closer to a logistics: an account of where the energies of transformation are stored, and of the cost of leaving them uncollected.

## Bloch's Marx: the cold stream and the warm

The formula of the two streams, cold and warm, was introduced earlier in this profile as Bloch's characterisation of Marxism as a whole. Its textual basis in Marx deserves a section, because Bloch was a close and idiosyncratic reader of the founding texts and his Marx is not the Marx of the catechisms. He fastens, first, on the young Marx of the 1843 letter to Ruge, in which the reformer of consciousness is described as making the world aware of a dream it has long possessed, and the task is stated as the realisation of thoughts the age already carries. For Bloch this letter is the charter of concrete utopia inside Marx's own work: the new world is not imported by the critic but midwifed from the old world's own dreaming, and the critic's ruthless criticism of everything existing is ruthless precisely because it serves the dream's clarification rather than its dismissal. He fastens, second, on the eleventh thesis on Feuerbach, the demand that philosophy interpret the world in a changed key, as the charter of the Front: theory stationed at the point where interpretation passes into the making of the Novum.

The reading has been contested, predictably, from the side of those who find in Marx a scientist embarrassed by his youthful rhetoric, and Bloch's answer is the wager of his whole interpretation: that the warmth is not the youth's residue but the system's engine, and that a Marx cooled to pure analysis reproduces, inside the movement, the contemplative posture the theses were written against. The publication of the early manuscripts in 1932, which disclosed the young Marx's philosophy of alienation to a startled movement, was greeted by Bloch as a vindication, and his reviews of those years are among the documents of the humanist reading of Marx that the middle of the century debated. The debate itself belongs to other profiles in this cluster, but its Blochian premise should be registered: that the author of Capital and the author of the manuscripts are one writer with one project, and that the project's name, in the vocabulary of this profile, is the education of a hope that capital itself had schooled in wishes it could not satisfy.

## Experimentum Mundi and the last system

The final metaphysical statement, Experimentum Mundi, appeared in 1975, when its author was ninety, and it gathers the system's categories into their most abstract form. The title is the thesis: the world is an experiment, a process whose outcome is not guaranteed by any logic, in which matter, life, and consciousness are successive trials of a That which has not yet found its What. The book's categories, question, trial, the open system, the identity still outstanding, are the not yet translated into the idiom of a philosophy of nature and knowledge, and its difficulty is notorious even among his readers. It is best approached, in the judgement of this profile, as a coda rather than a door: the place where the system's author, at the end of his life, states in the most compressed form what the inventories had exhibited, that reality is to be understood as a workshop and not as a museum. The political writings of the same years show the categories still at work in the world: the old man protested the war in Vietnam in terms his earliest anti war journalism would have recognised, addressed the students of 1968 as the Front of their moment while warning them against the abstract utopia of pure refusal, and made no secret of his condemnation when the armies of the Warsaw Pact entered Czechoslovakia. The record of the last decade is thus consistent with the record of the first: the affiliations kept breaking on the philosophy, and the philosophy did not break.

## The quarrel with the catechism, and the verdict of the tradition's historians

Bloch's standing inside official Marxism was, across five decades, that of a licensed and then unlicensed deviation, and the terms of the quarrel are instructive about both parties. The catechism required a materialism of dead matter and dialectical laws, a reflection theory of knowledge, and an aesthetics of administered realism. Bloch offered a matter in labour, a knowledge that anticipates, and an aesthetics of the fragment and the surplus. The charges brought against him, irrationalism, mysticism, revisionism, were the catechism's names for these offers, and his answers never conceded the premise that the catechism's version was the doctrine and his the decoration. He held, on the contrary, that the catechism had frozen a living method into a world view for functionaries, and that the frozen version would fail exactly where his categories were built to work: before the archaic strata, before the religious wish, before the daydream of the clerk, before every phenomenon that the doctrine could classify but could not address.

The historians of the tradition have rendered a divided verdict that mirrors the division in the work. Leszek Kolakowski, the most formidable of the school chroniclers and no friend to its consolations, treated Bloch as the most considerable metaphysician the Marxist philosophy produced and as the proof, by his fate in the East, of what that philosophy cost its honest practitioners; the system, in that reading, is a magnificent survival of religious thinking inside a doctrine that could neither digest it nor do without the energies it organised. Friendlier readers have stressed the methodological yield, the surplus test, the non-synchronism explanation, the two streams, as instruments that retain their edge after the metaphysics is bracketed. The division is unlikely to close, because it reproduces the system's own division between its warrant and its instruments. A profile can only register that both verdicts cite real features, and that the instruments, at least, have passed into the hands of readers who owe the metaphysics nothing, which is the fate Bloch's own doctrine of heritage would have predicted for the unfulfilled portion of a body of work: it descends, and the rest becomes history.

## The generation of the students and the afterlife of the slogans

The last reception to be recorded is the one that returned Bloch to the centre of German intellectual life in his own lifetime. The student movement of the nineteen sixties, shopping for a Marxism that had not been administered by either of the German states, found in the Tübingen professor a living anachronism of the best kind: a man who had known Lukacs and argued with the century, who blessed their impatience while instructing it, and whose phrases, above all the upright gait, passed into the movement's rhetoric as descriptions of what it felt itself to be doing. The scene has its ironies, which he savoured: the philosopher of the not yet become, at eighty, the teacher of a generation for whom the Front was a street in Frankfurt or Berlin. His counsel to them, preserved in the interviews of those years, was characteristically double. He refused the consolation that history was on their side, since history, in his system, is on no one's side until it is made; and he refused the despair their defeats prepared for them, since a defeat, in the same system, is an inventory of unfulfilled contents awaiting a better mediation. Political theology took the same lessons into the churches in those years, in the political theology of Johann Baptist Metz as in the theology of hope, and the rhetorical figure of hope as a discipline rather than a mood passed, through these channels, into the common stock of European moral vocabulary, usually without its author's name attached. The doctrine of heritage permits a dry satisfaction at this outcome: the unfulfilled portion of the work descends, anonymously if necessary, and the name on the title page is part of what becomes history.

## Ideology, utopia, and the argument with Mannheim

Bloch's theory of ideology is the hinge on which the whole cultural method turns, and it was hammered out in a specific argument that intellectual historians can date. Karl Mannheim's Ideology and Utopia, published in 1929, had given the sociology of knowledge its classic distinction: ideologies are the thought forms that preserve an existing order by disguising it, utopias are the thought forms that shatter an order by reaching beyond it, and the sociologist stands above both, mapping their functions with a free floating detachment. Bloch, who reviewed the book and returned to it for years, accepted the distinction and rejected the detachment. A sociology that files every hope under its social function, he argued, performs on thought exactly the operation his non-synchronism refuses for strata: it treats the future directed contents of consciousness as residues of their conditions, exhaustively explained when their origins are named. Against the free floating intellectual he sets the participant analyst, whose inventory of an age's wishes is itself an act inside the age's struggle over which wishes will be inherited.

His own doctrine of ideology follows, and it is more double edged than the textbook summaries suggest. Ideology, for Bloch, is never a simple lie. It is the necessary appearance form of a society's self understanding, and as such it always contains, alongside its apologetic function, a truthful registration of what that society's members actually want and fear. The registration is the surplus in its ideological form. This is why his analyses of enemy material, of völkisch mythology in the Heritage, of the administered optimism of later regimes, are never content with exposure. Exposure identifies the function; the surplus test then asks the harder question of what genuine wish the function had to capture in order to operate, and that wish, once identified, becomes available for inheritance by the function's opponents. The doctrine thus completes the political logistics sketched earlier in this profile. Ideology critique, practised in the Blochian manner, is not the demolition of the enemy's storehouse but its inventory, conducted with a view to a transfer of ownership.

## Wonder, the question, and the darkness at the origin

The philosophy begins, as the early book announced, in astonishment, and the theme is worth isolating because it distinguishes Bloch's starting point from both of the usual ones in his tradition. Dialectical orthodoxy begins with contradiction, already a sophisticated instrument; the Frankfurt critique begins with domination, already a political diagnosis. Bloch begins with the child's question and the astonishment of the adult who has not unlearned it: the moment in which the familiar world becomes strange to itself and the question of what it might be, beyond what it is, forces itself into consciousness. He collects these moments with the same care he gives to fairy tales: the astonishment before a melody, before the night sky of childhood cosmology, before the strangeness of one's own name, before death's first announcement. Each is a small experience of the not yet, the point at which the given world fails to coincide with itself and the surplus of possibility becomes, for an instant, palpable.

This darkness at the heart of the lived moment, introduced at the start of this profile, is the theoretical form of the same starting point. The moment we live in cannot be illuminated from within, because it is the standpoint of all our illuminating; hence the most important thing about every present, what is deciding itself in it, is the thing least visible to it. Bloch draws a methodological moral that governs his whole practice of reading. Since the present is dark to itself, its truth must be approached indirectly, through its products and its wishes, through the forms in which it has objectified its anticipations without understanding them. The interpreter of culture is therefore not a judge standing over the material but a midwife attending a birth the material does not know it is undergoing. The image is his, in tendency if not in letter, and it marks the temperamental gulf between his hermeneutics and the hermeneutics of suspicion with which his tradition is usually identified. Suspicion asks what the form is hiding. Bloch asks that, and then asks what the form is carrying, and the second question is the one his century of suspicion forgot to ask.

## A genealogy of the oppressed: Münzer's descendants

The Münzer book founded a historiographical practice that Bloch maintained for life: the construction of a counter genealogy, a lineage of the defeated through whom the unfulfilled content of history is transmitted. Its stations, as they recur across his work, form a recognisable company: the heretical sects of the middle ages, for whom the kingdom was a social arrangement before it was a doctrine; Münzer and the peasants of the German war; the Anabaptist kingdom at Münster, read with a sympathy for its communism of consumption that its horrors do not cancel; the Diggers on Saint George's Hill, planting the common treasury; the Levellers with their agreement of the people; Babeuf and his conspiracy of equals; the utopian socialists; and so to the movements of his own century. The genealogy is the opposite of a triumphal procession, since nearly every station is a defeat, and that is its point. In the official historiography of progress, defeat deletes; in Bloch's, defeat deposits. The content that a defeated movement carried does not vanish but passes into the stock of the not yet, available to heirs who may not know its name.

The practice has an exact methodological warrant in the doctrine of heritage, and it explains the otherwise puzzling warmth with which a Marxist philosopher writes about millenarian preachers. The question he puts to each station is the surplus question in historical dress: what did these people want that their world could not give and that a later world still owes? The answers accumulate into his distinctive version of the philosophy of history, in which the past is not a corridor of stages leading to the present but a storehouse of uncompleted beginnings, any of which may be resumed. Against this background the political moral of the Heritage analysis sharpens further. Fascism, too, constructed a genealogy, of blood and soil and fallen empires, and collected the archaic contents the left had scorned. The struggle over the past, on Bloch's showing, is a struggle over the future's raw material, and it is lost by whichever side believes the past is finished.

## Progress, rescued and made difficult

No concept in the tradition was more damaged, by the nineteen thirties, than progress, and Bloch's handling of it shows his characteristic refusal to surrender damaged goods. The social democratic faith in automatic advance had been refuted by events; the fascist regimes advertised their own dynamism as the true progress of the age; and within the Marxist opposition, the temptation grew to abandon the concept altogether, as Benjamin's theses, with their storm blowing from paradise, memorably did. Bloch's position, worked out in the Heritage and restated in the Tübingen writings on the concept, is that the word should be neither retained in its innocence nor surrendered, but rebuilt. Genuine progress, he argues, cannot be measured on a single line, since a society advances in one stratum while regressing in another, and the technical advance of the productive forces, which the old doctrine took as its index, is compatible with every barbarism the century exhibited. Progress must be pluralised and re indexed to its content: progress is the increase, in a given domain, of the objectively possible toward the figure of the Ultimum, the growth of the realm in which human beings stand upright, and it can therefore coexist with catastrophe in the domains not being measured.

The reconstruction is less often cited than the non-synchronism argument, which is its sibling, but the two belong together as the positive and negative faces of one insight. Non-synchronism says the present is not one time; the rebuilt concept of progress says the future is not one road. A movement equipped with both insights is cured of the two diseases that Bloch diagnosed in the left of his youth: the evolutionism that waited for the advanced contradiction to ripen while the archaic strata were harvested by the enemy, and the catastrophism that, having lost faith in the road, lost interest in the destination. Between the diseases stands the Front, where tendencies are read without guarantees, and the rebuilt progress is simply the name for what a successful reading accumulates. That the concept survives in this chastened form, as a measure rather than a promise, is a fair emblem of the whole system, which everywhere trades the comforts of doctrine for the disciplines of a method.

## Bloch and Brecht: estrangement as a shared instrument

The friendship and the quarrel with Bertolt Brecht belong to the expressionism years, and they illuminate a side of Bloch's aesthetics that the Frankfurt comparison leaves in shadow. Brecht's epic theatre renounced the audience's absorption in illusion and substituted the estrangement effect, the device by which the familiar action is made strange so that its changeability becomes visible: the mother shown as a businesswoman, the hero shown hesitating, the placard announcing what the scene is about to prove. Bloch recognised in the device a theatrical form of his own philosophical operation. Estrangement, like the surplus test, refuses to let a represented world pass as nature; it arrests the spectator's consent and converts the given into the questionable. His defences of Brecht against the Lukacsian charge of formalism make the point explicitly: a theatre that shows the world as alterable performs anticipatory work, whatever its surface fractures, while a theatre that reproduces the world in seamless verisimilitude confirms the audience in the unalterability it came in with.

The agreement had limits that both men acknowledged with good humour in their recorded arguments. Brecht's didacticism trusted the lesson; Bloch's hermeneutics trusted the wish, and the difference shows in their judgements of the audience's daydreams, which Brecht tended to treat as the opiate to be interrupted and Bloch as the raw material to be educated. Posterity has mostly used the two together, the estrangement to break the spell and the inventory to collect what the spell was made of, and the combination is a fair example of how the instruments of this cluster compose when their owners' polemics are set aside. The reader of the series will find the same pattern repeated across its profiles: the tradition's thinkers are most valuable not as parties to be joined but as a toolkit whose pieces correct one another, and Bloch's piece, the surplus test, is the one that asks after every estrangement what the estranged material is still good for.

## The sciences in the open system

A stereotype of Bloch casts him as the poet among the Marxists, licensed to ignore the sciences, and the cast does not survive his pages on the subject. His complaint against the science of his day was never its rigour but its metaphysics, the positivist residue that mistook the measurable for the real and the finished law for the finished world. Against that residue he sets the sciences' own best self understanding, in which laws are the habits of a process and every well founded result reopens the question of the system that contains it. The atom of the old materialism, indivisible and dead, had already been dissolved by physics into a structured latency of energies, and Bloch reads the dissolution as nature's own commentary on his ontology: at the most exact level of inquiry, matter itself refuses to behave as a finished thing. His use of such material is analogical and he marks it as such; the point is not that physics proves the not yet but that the image of a closed, calculable nature, which the catechism borrowed from the nineteenth century, is no longer available to be borrowed.

The positive proposal matches the alliance technology described earlier. A science adequate to an unfinished world would understand its own interventions as experiments within the process it studies, which is the burden of the last book's title, and would hold its categories open at the front where the Novum enters. The proposal has had few takers among working scientists, and Bloch did not expect otherwise; its function in the system is disciplinary rather than programmatic, a reminder to his own side that the cold stream of analysis must be as hospitable to the new in nature as the warm stream is to the new in society. The stereotype of the poet survives because the inventories are more readable than the epistemology. A profile concerned with the system's actual shape should record that the epistemology exists, is argued, and is the part of the work its philosophical critics have taken most seriously.

## Traces: the miniature as a philosophical genre

Between the early books and the great system stands a small book that many of his admirers love best, the Spuren, Traces, of 1930, assembled from the feuilleton pieces of the Weimar years. Its form is the anecdote raised to a method. A man tells a story at a hotel bar; a traveller misreads a street; a Hasidic tale is retold; a childhood fright is reconstructed; and each miniature is made to disclose, in a page or two, the structure that the system will later exhibit at scale: the moment when the ordinary arrangement of things cracks and the not yet looks through. Bloch called the pieces traces because the quarry of the philosophy, the content of anticipation, leaves no other spoor in ordinary life; it cannot be photographed in the wild, only tracked by the marks it leaves on stories, objects, and turns of phrase. The storyteller's art is therefore not the decoration of the method but its elementary form, and the lineage he claims, from the Hasidic tale as the collections of his day transmitted it to the Berlin feuilleton, is a lineage of forms in which compressed narrative carries metaphysical freight.

The book also offers the gentlest introduction to the doctrine of the lived moment's darkness, since its miniatures repeatedly stage the experience of understanding an event only after it has closed, of recognising, in the retelling, the significance that the living of it concealed. Readers who intend to attempt the great book are often advised to begin here, and the advice is sound for a reason beyond accessibility. The Principle of Hope is the Traces method industrialised, an inventory of ten thousand miniatures organised by a system, and the reader who has learned the movement of the small form, from anecdote to disclosure, possesses the key to the large one's chapters, which perform the same movement on cathedrals and symphonies. Genre, in Bloch's hands, is never innocent; the miniature is the daydream of literary forms, lucid, brief, and pointed at a world its brevity cannot contain.

## The glass dreams: building and the avant garde

The Weimar avant garde gave the philosophy of anticipation a built environment to read, and Bloch, who moved in its circles and wrote for its journals, read it closely. The expressionist architects dreamed in glass: Bruno Taut's glass house at the Werkbund exhibition, the crystalline cathedrals of the glass chain correspondence, the alpine architecture that was to crown the mountains with transparent temples of a new communal life. The drawings were unbuildable and were known to be; their interest, for Bloch, lay exactly there, in the frankness with which a generation of builders confessed that the point of their designs was not shelter but anticipation, the pre appearance, in glass and light, of a sociality that did not exist. His writings on the new building extend the reading to the sober functionalism that succeeded the glass dreams, finding in the flat roof, the ribbon window, and the abolished ornament the same anticipatory content in a colder idiom: the refusal of the bourgeois interior, with its plush and its ancestral gloom, in favour of rooms arranged for lives that had not yet been lived.

The episode matters to the profile because it dates the method's formation. Before the Heritage gave the method its political concept, and before the exile gave it its great inventory, the avant garde gave it its confidence that the newest forms of an age are its most honest documents, since in them the wish has not yet learned to disguise itself as a necessity. The Bauhaus appears in his pages neither as a style nor as a school but as a workshop of the not yet, and the judgement has worn well enough to be quoted by the historians of design. It also marks a contrast with the Frankfurt treatment of the same material, where the new building's anticipations are read chiefly as the advance guard of the administered world. Both readings catch something. Bloch's catches the wish, and this profile has been written in the conviction that the wish is the part the other readings leave out.

## The festival and the joke: two minor forms with major evidence

The inventory method is at its most persuasive on forms too slight for the dignity of a theory, and two of them deserve their paragraphs before the exposition closes. The festival, the feast day that interrupts the calendar, is read by Bloch as the socially licensed rehearsal of the fulfilled moment: for its duration the ordinary order of labour, rank, and scarcity is suspended, abundance is displayed rather than sold, and the community experiences, in costume and in earnest, the proposition that life could be arranged as celebration rather than as toll. Regimes have always understood the form's charge, which is why they administer it so carefully, and the history of the revolutionary festival, from the festivals of the French Revolution with their altars to the fatherland to the choreographed anniversaries of later states, is the history of attempts to keep the rehearsal from becoming a demand. The surplus in the festival is the easiest of all to isolate, since it consists simply in the memory, carried by everyone who has danced at one, that the suspension felt more honest than the order it suspended.

The joke receives a parallel reading. Laughter, in his scattered pages on the comic, is the moment at which the mighty are cut down to the size of the room: the pompous deflated, the uniform made ridiculous, the official phrase repeated until its hollowness sounds. A joke cannot be administered as reliably as a festival, because its timing is anarchic and its target is chosen by the teller, which is why every police order fears the joke out of proportion to its force. For the inventory, the comic is the record of a judgement the powerless pass on the powerful in the only court always in session, and the wit that survives in a people's store of jokes is an index, as sensitive as any in the system, of what that people has refused to believe about its rulers. The two forms belong together in this profile because they exhibit the method at its most democratic: the evidence for the not yet is not confined to the works that libraries preserve, but circulates in the calendar and in the corridor, and a philosophy of hope that could not read a feast day or a joke would have failed its own inventory before opening a book.

## The God who may not be pictured: Bloch and the Jewish inheritance

Bloch's descent was Jewish, his formation was German and philosophical, his observance was nil, and his relation to the Jewish tradition in his work is nevertheless structural rather than biographical, because the tradition supplied his system with one of its load bearing distinctions. The prohibition on images, the second commandment's ban on the graven image of God, had served the Frankfurt thinkers, Adorno above all, as the emblem of a hope that must not be betrayed by portrayal. Bloch's reading of the same prohibition, developed in Atheismus im Christentum, turns it in his own direction. The Exodus God may not be pictured, he argues, not because he is too holy for depiction but because he is not yet: the God who names himself by a future tense has no present form to depict, and the ban protects the futurity of the divine from the idolatry of the present. The image prohibition, on this reading, is not the enemy of his inventory of images but its oldest charter, since it forbids precisely the confusion the surplus test exists to prevent, the confusion of the anticipated with the achieved.

The Moses of his reading is correspondingly the figure of departure itself: the leader who sees the promised land and does not enter it, whose biography is the biography of the not yet in a single life, and whose law is given on the road rather than in the settlement. Bloch's attachment to this figure across sixty years, from the Münzer period to the late theology, marks the continuity between the Jewish and the Christian materials in his work, both read as archives of the Exodus pattern: a people, or a preacher, or a class, summoned out of the house of bondage toward a condition that the summons itself has not yet described. His treatment has been claimed by Jewish readers as a recovery and criticised by others as an appropriation, and the profile records the dispute without settling it, as it has recorded the other disputes that gather where his method touches living traditions. What is not disputed is the structural fact: the philosopher of hope found the grammar of his system in the oldest archive of hope his culture possessed, and said so, in print, when saying so cost more than it paid.

## The state of the text: editions, archive, and the untranslated remainder

The material basis of Bloch study is better than his reputation suggests and worse than his importance warrants. The collected edition published by Suhrkamp runs to sixteen volumes and gathers the books, the essays, and a selection of the occasional writings; the literary essays, the traces, the natural law book, the atheism and Christianity volume, and the great trilogy itself have all appeared in English, most of them since the nineteen eighties, in translations of high quality. The Ernst Bloch archive in his birthplace, Ludwigshafen, preserves the manuscripts and correspondence, and a scholarly society and its yearbook keep the secondary literature in order. What remains untranslated or half translated is the connective tissue: much of the journalism, the lectures in their spoken fullness, the political interventions of six decades, and the correspondence in bulk, so that the Anglophone reader meets a system whose documentary base is still partly in shadow. The shadow matters because Bloch, more than most system builders, practised his method in the occasional forms, the review, the miniature, the radio talk, and the definitive account of several of his concepts is scattered across pieces that no English reader can conveniently assemble.

The open questions for research follow from the state of the text. The genesis of Heritage of Our Times, written at speed in the first exile and revised across decades, still awaits its full documentary history, and with it a precise answer to the question of when the non-synchronism category reached its final form. The relation between the Traces miniatures and the inventories of the great book, asserted in this profile as a relation of method, needs the edition based study that would prove or qualify it. The political writings, whose ledger this profile has summarised in a page, deserve the monograph length audit that would let the record be judged whole rather than in exhibits, by friend and critic alike. And the correspondence with Adorno, published in part, promises the sharpest available record of how the two rival instruments of this cluster were calibrated in private. None of these gaps is an accident of neglect alone. They are the predictable consequence of a reception that arrived fifty years late to its subject, and they define the work that the next generation of his readers, in whatever language, has in front of it.

## The philosopher assessed: what stands, what is exposed

A profile that has followed its subject through the works, the record, and the objections owes the reader a summation, and the summation can be as brief as the evidence allows. What stands, after the objections have done their work, is a set of instruments that no subsequent practitioner has been able to discard. The non-synchronism explanation remains the indispensable correction to every analysis of reaction that contents itself with the advanced contradiction, and its logic reappears, under other names, wherever a politics is surprised by the archaic. The surplus test remains the only instrument in the tradition that grades cultural forms by a criterion other than their function, and it disciplines, even in the hands of readers who reject its metaphysics, the lazy habit of reading a form's use as its meaning. The two streams remain the most economical statement of what a complete Marxism would have to hold together, and the doctrine of heritage, the audit of the past by its unpaid bills, remains the tradition's most usable rule for the treatment of its own defeats. These four bequests are detachable from the system, and their detachment is not a betrayal of it but its predicted fate.

What is exposed is equally definite. The speculative doctrine of matter asks for an assent that the evidence, organised by his own cold stream, does not compel, and most of his inheritors have quietly declined it while keeping the instruments it was built to warrant. The judgement demanded by the surplus test remains, at the limit, judgement, and the objection that it cannot be made compulsory has been answered with a research programme rather than with a refutation. And the political record contains the item that this profile has stated plainly and will not summarise away: a defence, in print, of the Moscow trials, standing in the biography of the philosopher of hope as the heaviest single entry in the ledger of his political judgement. The summation that the evidence supports is therefore a divided one, and the division is not a defect of the summation but the shape of the subject. Bloch built the tradition's most complete philosophy of the future and its most searching account of why the future's energies are misdirected; he read fascism's appeal more accurately, earlier, than the movement that might have profited from the reading; and he lived long enough to be failed by both of the states that claimed the future in his century, and to leave, from the second failure, the beginnings of the philosophy by which the failure could be named. For a series that profiles the carriers of a tradition, that record, instruments, metaphysics, and ledger together, is the whole of the man it can honestly present.

## The city as an inventory: streets, hotels, and the metropolitan daydream

The Weimar feuilletons that fed the Traces were written in and about Berlin, and the city remained Bloch's favourite laboratory for the inventory method, because the metropolis concentrates the wishes of an age in displayable form. His urban pieces read the street as a text in which every stratum's time is simultaneously legible: the horse drawn cart beside the motor omnibus, the pawnshop beside the bank, the fairground beside the cathedral of commerce. The department store, whose windows were treated earlier in this profile, is in these pieces only the most systematic of the city's wish machines. The hotel lobby, the dance palace, the cinema palace, the illuminated advertisement climbing a facade, each is interrogated for the life it promises its patrons and for the portion of the promise that survives the patron's return to the street. The city, for the young Bloch, is the place where the not yet has gone into business, and his ambivalence toward it is the system's ambivalence in miniature: no other environment manufactures the wish so efficiently, and none betrays it so efficiently, and the two efficiencies are one.

There is a methodological legacy in these slight pieces that the later system retains. The urban inventory taught him to read synchronically what the Heritage would teach him to read temporally: a cross section of the present, taken at any street corner, exhibits all the times of the society at once, if the reader knows how to look. The flâneur of the Benjamin circle strolled to collect the shocks of the modern; Bloch's walker collects something else, the offers. Every facade is an offer of a life, every window an offer of a world, and the walker's education consists in learning to distinguish, among the offers, the ones whose fulfilment would change the walker from the ones whose fulfilment would merely change his purchases. It is the surplus test at walking pace, and it explains why the great book, for all its metaphysics, never loses the feuilleton's habit of beginning from a thing seen in the street.

## Youth and age: the generations as non synchronous strata

The Heritage analysis gives one of its sharpest chapters to a stratum defined not by production but by birth: youth. The German youth movement, the Wandervogel with its hiking, its folk songs, its contempt for the furnished bourgeois interior, appears in Bloch's pages as a non synchronous formation in the precise sense, a stratum whose members inhabited the calendar present while their wishes were furnished from an imagined earlier world of wandering, craft, and unalienated fellowship. The movement's tragedy, on his reading, prefigures the general one: its wishes were genuine, its anti capitalism was sincere, and its idiom was archaic, so that when the harvest came it was gathered, in large part, by the movement that promised the young a national community with the same songs and harder marching. The analysis extends the surplus doctrine to biography. A generation, like a class or a church, is a storehouse of wishes, and the question of who educates those wishes, and toward which mediation, decides the politics of the succeeding decade.

Age receives the complementary treatment in the inventories of the great book, where the daydreams of the old, the wish for the lost years returned with their strength intact, the wish for the work to outlast the worker, are catalogued with the same respect as the daydreams of the young. Bloch is alert to the specific cruelty with which his century administered the wishes of age, pensioning the experience it had no use for and selling back, as nostalgia, the images of the world the pensioners had built. The nostalgia industry is, in his analysis, the advertising of the past tense, and its product is the debased form of the heritage relation: the past consumed as comfort rather than inherited as task. Between the youth whose archaic wishes await their educator and the age whose wishes are sold back as sentiment, the system's political teaching stands complete in biographical form: the wishes are always there, in every stratum and every season of life, and history is made by whoever takes them seriously enough to grade them.

## The peasant: the oldest figure of the not yet

One figure walks through the whole work from the first book to the last, and a profile that has used him as an exhibit owes him a section as a subject. The peasant, in Bloch's writing, is not the picturesque survivor of the textbooks but the bearer of the oldest continuous wish in the European record: the wish for the land and for the order in which the tiller's labour and the tiller's table are one household. It is this wish that Münzer preached into revolution, that the Anabaptists armed, that the Diggers planted, and that every subsequent agrarian movement has carried into the industrial age as the countryside's specific contribution to the not yet. Bloch's treatment of it is distinguished by its refusal of both available condescensions. Against the industrial left, he insists that the land wish is not a superstition scheduled for dissolution by the tractor; it is a claim about the human relation to work and sustenance that industrial society has answered no better than feudal society did, and it will persist until it is answered. Against the romantics of blood and soil, he insists that the wish's content is universal, the free tiller on free land, and that its conscription into the mythology of race is the theft the non-synchronism explanation exists to expose.

The figure also completes his account of the Russian case that shadows the whole tradition. The revolution that the orthodox theory expected in the advanced West occurred, as his contemporary Lenin saw and as the category of overdetermination in this series' glossary formalises, where the peasant wish and the workers' movement could be allied, in the slogan of land and peace. Bloch's version of the same insight runs through the Heritage: the peasantry is the stratum in which the non synchronous survives in its most massive and most justified form, and therefore the stratum on whose education the fate of revolutions disproportionately depends. The German catastrophe is his negative proof, the Russian alliance his positive one, and the figure of the peasant, despised by the planners of both his Germanys, stands in the work as the standing rebuke to every politics that mistakes the map of production for the map of hope.

## The doctrine of signatures: reading the ciphers of nature

A last piece of the speculative machinery deserves exposition, because it explains the texture of Bloch's nature writing and his attraction to the heterodox tradition in the philosophy of nature. The doctrine of signatures, descending from Paracelsus and Jakob Böhme, held that things bear outward marks of their inward virtues, that the world is a book of ciphers in which the healing plant announces its use and the mineral its tendency to the reader trained in the script. Bloch takes up the doctrine in the same spirit in which he takes up the fairy tale: not as a science to be revived but as a document of a wish, the wish that nature be legible as a system of tendencies rather than mute as a warehouse of facts. His speculative materialism translates the wish into his own idiom. If matter is unfinished potency, then the forms of nature are its experiments, and the attentive observer reads them as the physician reads symptoms, for what they are tending toward. The cipher reading is therefore the natural philosophy's version of the surplus test: in the cultural case, the form's excess over its function; in the natural case, the form's excess over its present state, the direction inscribed in it.

The doctrine's presence in the system explains the otherwise strange company the work keeps, its hospitality to alchemists, to Böhme the shoemaker theologian, to the nature mystics of the German tradition, alongside its Marx and its Hegel. These figures are in the inventory as the Münzers of natural knowledge, the defeated carriers of a content, the alliance with nature's tendencies, that the victorious mechanical philosophy discarded and that the ecological emergencies of a later century would make legible again. A reader need not follow the metaphysics to profit from the habit it trains, which is the habit this profile has described in a dozen forms: to read every given thing, a stone, a story, a stratum, a song, as the trace of a tendency, and to ask of the trace the two questions that constitute the whole method, what made it, and what it is still on its way to becoming.

## The American decade: exile in the land of the manufactured wish

The eleven American years are the biography's paradox: the philosopher of hope wrote his summa in the country where hope had been most thoroughly industrialised, without a chair, without an audience to speak of, in a language he never made his literary own. The exile's judgement on his refuge, gathered from the letters and essays of the period and from the use he made of American material in the great book, was double in the established pattern. What he honoured was the energy of the uncompleted: a society that had abolished the furnished interior of the old world and stood, in its own estimation, at the beginning of its history, and whose popular culture, its films, its jazz, its comic strips, carried the wishes of its millions in forms of a directness Europe's educated filters had refined away. What he indicted was the machinery of administration that stood ready at the wish's birth: no society had ever organised so completely the capture of anticipation by the commodity, the conversion of every daydream into a purchase plan, and the exile who had theorised the shop window in Berlin now watched the window become a continent.

The American material in The Principle of Hope is correspondingly extensive, and its presence corrects a lazy assumption about the book's geography. The fairy tales are Grimms', but the colportage has become the Western and the detective story in their American mass forms; the dance hall is Berlin's and Harlem's at once; the displayed world of the advertisement is read in its most advanced laboratory. The exile's distance had a methodological yield that Bloch acknowledged: removed from the German catastrophe's daily urgencies, he could treat his material at the scale of the species, and the book's universalism, its confidence that the inventory of German daydreams could stand for the wishing of humanity, is partly the product of an American desk. He left in 1949 for the ruins of Leipzig with the manuscript substantially complete, choosing the harder audience and the state that claimed his hopes, in the last act of political judgement whose sequel this profile has recorded.

## The teacher in the lecture hall

A word on the teaching, since the system holds that hope is learned and its author's classrooms were the laboratory of the doctrine. Bloch lectured as he wrote, by inventory and ascent: a concept was never announced until its exhibits had been paraded, and the exhibits ranged, in a single hour, from a peasant proverb to a page of Hegel, from a fairground song to the structure of the atom, each made to yield its anticipatory content before the category that organised them was permitted on the board. Students' memoirs of the Leipzig seminars describe the effect as an intoxication that the examinations then had to sober, and the regime's policing of his circle testifies, from the other side, to the teaching's power to form attachments that no syllabus had sanctioned. In Tübingen the performance was repeated for a generation that came armed with suspicion of all masters and stayed, in surprising numbers, for the old man's refusal to be one: he taught, the survivors report, by interrogating the exhibits and letting the categories arrive as verdicts the evidence had forced.

The pedagogy is the system's self application, and it deserves the student's attention for that reason. A doctrine whose central claim is that anticipation can be educated cannot be transmitted as information; it must be practised on material in the student's presence until the practice becomes a habit of the student's own attention. The lecture hall inventories, the seminar's grading of wishes, the famous seminars on the fairy tale and on the detective novel, are therefore not illustrations of the philosophy but instances of it, the education of hope conducted as an academic exercise. Teachers who adopt the exercises proposed in the persona section of this profile are, knowingly or not, reproducing the pedagogy of the Leipzig and Tübingen rooms, where the surplus test was first a classroom game before it was a chapter of a system.

## Quixote and Faust: the two tutelary figures

The literary imagination of the system is governed by two figures who recur across the work like patron saints, and a profile may close its exposition by attending to them, since together they state the anthropology more compactly than the categories do. Don Quixote is the first. The knight who mistakes inns for castles and flocks for armies is, in the standard reading that Bloch inherits only to reverse, the emblem of abstract utopia, the dreamer whose image has no mediation with the world and whose beatings are the world's tuition. Bloch's counter reading finds in the knight the fidelity that the sane world lacks: Quixote's error is in his inventory, not in his allegiance, for the world he serves, of giants to be fought and wrongs to be righted and ladies to be honoured, is the world as it ought to be opposing itself to the world as the innkeepers keep it, and his madness differs from the innkeepers' sanity as the Front differs from the archive. The novel's greatness, on this reading, is that it keeps both verdicts in suspension to the end, letting the reader feel the beatings and the fidelity in the same paragraph, which is exactly the suspension in which educated hope lives.

Faust is the second figure, and the greater, since Goethe's drama poses the system's final question in its wager. Faust's bargain stakes his soul on the moment: if ever he says to the passing moment, stay, you are so beautiful, then the devil may take him, for a man who blesses the moment has surrendered the not yet that makes him human. Bloch reads the wager as the precise inversion of his own closing figure. The fulfilled moment, the Heimat in which no one has yet been, cannot be a moment at all in the series of moments, since every moment in the series is unfulfilled by definition; it must be the moment transfigured, the identity of the self with its world that ends the series by completing it. Faust's error, in the reading, is to hunt the fulfilment along the series, in experience after experience, as the tourist hunts the view; the drama's own ending, in which the striving figure is received by the eternal feminine that draws humanity onward and upward, is treated by Bloch with an admiration he grants few theological constructions, as the poem's confession that its hero's element was never possession but direction. Between the knight who serves a world that is not there and the scholar who refuses every world that is, the anthropology stands stated: the human being as the creature whose home is ahead of him, and whose dignity is the refusal to call any lodging on the road by the home's name.

## Against recollection: knowledge that faces forward

The philosophical tradition Bloch inherited was built, at its origin, on a theory of knowledge that faces backward. Plato's doctrine of recollection taught that learning is remembering: the soul, having seen the forms before its imprisonment in the body, recognises them again in their shadows here, and all genuine knowledge is therefore the recovery of a possession. The doctrine's long posterity, through every philosophy for which truth is the adequate reflection of what already is, receives in the book's second part its most systematic refusal. Against recollection Bloch sets anticipation. The most important contents of knowledge, he argues, are not recovered from a past, whether the soul's or the species', but constructed toward a future: the categories in which a genuinely new content will be understood do not exist before the content, and the knower's relation to them is not memory but the not yet conscious disciplined into docta spes. Even the knowledge of the past is reorganised by the reversal, since what the historian can know of a dead age is selected and shaped by the questions the Front puts to it, so that historiography itself, honestly practised, is a branch of the forward facing knowledge.

The refusal has a polemical address inside his own century as well. The reflection theory of knowledge, canonised in the catechism as the doctrine that consciousness mirrors a finished being, is Platonism in materialist dress, recollection with the soul replaced by the object, and it fails at exactly the point where the system begins: it cannot think the new, since a mirror registers only what stands before it, and it cannot think the knower's own darkness, since the lived moment does not stand before itself. Bloch's epistemology, in which the subject is inside the process it knows and knows it by participating in its tendencies, is the consistent alternative, and its difficulties, the standing invitation to mistake participation for licence, are the same difficulties the objection section of this profile weighed under another name. The answer given there holds here: the correspondence with real tendency is the discipline, and where the tendency cannot be shown, the anticipation, however luminous, is demoted by the system's own court to the rank of a wish. A philosophy that builds demotion procedures for its own central category has earned the right to be judged by them rather than by its slogans.

## Hope must be able to be disappointed

The last refinement of the doctrine, and the one that most completely separates it from the optimism of the calendar motto, is Bloch's insistence that genuine hope is disappointable. The argument runs against both of hope's counterfeits. Confidence, the expectation that things will go well because they have gone well or because the speaker's party holds the future's lease, is not hope but a mortgage on the future, and its collapse in every catastrophe of his century is the record of what mortgages are worth. Despair, its mirror image, is confidence inverted, the same certainty with the sign reversed, and equally closed to the Front, where nothing is certain in either direction. Hope, as the system defines it, is the affect appropriate to an objectively open process: it expects the Novum without guaranteeing it, it stakes itself on tendencies it has examined, and it retains, as a constituent of its honesty, the knowledge that the stake can be lost. A hope that could not be disappointed would be a knowledge, and would belong to a finished world; in the unfinished one, disappointability is not hope's weakness but its certificate of reality.

The doctrine completes the answer to the charge of naivety with which this profile's misreadings section began, and it can be put to the reader as the single test of whether Bloch has been understood. The philosopher who wrote that hope must be able to be disappointed was not selling encouragement. He was describing the emotional discipline of beings stationed at the Front of a process whose outcome their own action helps to decide, and for whom the alternative disciplines, the mortgage and the mirror, had both been tried in his lifetime and had both ended in the catastrophe he spent his work explaining. The education of hope, the docta spes with which the exposition of the system began, is therefore not an education in expecting the best. It is an education in reading tendencies without flinching, in inheriting wishes without illusions, and in acting on an examined anticipation in the full knowledge that the examination confers no guarantee. That is the principle of the book's title, stated at last without its images, and it is severe enough to be worth the thousand pages that were required to earn it.

## The Romantic inheritance: the blue flower and the protest against disenchantment

Bloch's relation to German Romanticism is a late appearing piece of the inheritance pattern, and it resolves a puzzle in his taste. The Romantics were, by the standards of the Marxist historiography he inherited, the ideologists of reaction, the dreamers who fled the bourgeois present into a gilded feudal past. His reading grants the flight and confiscates the dream. The Romantic protest against the disenchanted, calculated, prosaic world of early capitalism registered, in the idiom of longing, a genuine inventory of what that world was destroying: the festival, the craft, the ensouled landscape, the night side of the soul that the Enlightenment's noon had declared empty. Novalis's blue flower, the emblem of a longing that no attainable object satisfies, is read in his pages without irony, as the most exact image the period produced of the not yet conscious itself, a wish whose objectlessness is its truth, since the object it seeks has not yet become. The medievalism is then separable, by the usual test, into its function, the consolation of thrones and altars, and its surplus, the protest of the imagination against a world arranged for account books, which descends to any later movement willing to inherit it in a better mediation.

The reading matters beyond the literary history, because it generalises the non-synchronism argument to the history of sensibility. Every age, on this showing, deposits in its Romanticism the wishes its dominant order cannot house, and the deposits outlast the order, so that the Romantic stratum of any period becomes a legible archive for the historian of hope. Bloch's own prose, with its expressionist inheritance and its unembarrassed lyricism, is a self conscious deposit of the same kind, a fact his detractors have always held against him and his defenders have always conceded with the addition that the concession was the point. A philosophy that teaches its readers to inherit the Romantics cannot consistently be ashamed of being caught, by its enemies, in the act of resembling them.

## The word itself: ciphers, metaphors, and the language of the Front

The system's last instrument is linguistic, and a profile of its author should register it before closing the exposition. Bloch held that the concepts in which an age thinks its future arrive first as metaphors, because the content they carry has not yet acquired the objects from which exact terms are coined. His own vocabulary is built on the admission: the Front, the Novum, the darkness of the moment, the upright gait, the warm and cold streams, are metaphors disciplined into concepts by use, and he defends the procedure against the two linguistic orthodoxies of his century, the catechism's frozen terminology and the analytic school's demand that every term present its credentials in advance. A thought stationed at the Front, he argues, cannot wait for its credentials; it must speak in ciphers, in images whose correspondence to the ripening content is argued case by case, exactly as the surplus test argues it for cultural forms. The metaphor is thus not the enemy of the concept in his practice but its first form, and his prose's notorious density is the visible cost of a terminology perpetually in the act of being coined.

The doctrine has a corollary for the reader that this profile can endorse from experience. Bloch's texts cannot be skimmed for their theses, because the theses are carried by the ciphers and the ciphers by the exhibits, so that a summary, this one included, is a map of a country that must still be travelled on foot. The reviews that complain of the difficulty and the readers who testify to the exhilaration are describing the same fact from opposite sides of the labour. What the labour purchases is a vocabulary in which a set of phenomena, the dawning content, the ripening tendency, the wish inside the advertisement and the grievance inside the myth, become sayable at all, and a tradition is fortunate when one of its members pays the price of the coinage. The words have entered the language, as coinages do, largely without their minter's name, and the inventory of their circulation would be the pleasantest chapter of the reception history that the scholarship has yet to write.

## Hunger: the first evidence that the given is not enough

The anthropology on which the work's foundational part builds begins lower than consciousness, in the body and its most elementary refusal. Bloch takes hunger as the fundamental drive, the self preservation drive in its purest form, and he chooses it for a reason that states the whole doctrine in miniature. Hunger is the one drive that cannot be finally satisfied: it renews itself daily, it teaches the organism, before any thought, that the given state of things is insufficient and that the insufficient thing can be changed by reaching beyond it. Every satisfaction is temporary and every renewal is a fresh lesson, so that the hungry body is the first school of the not yet, the place where the forward structure of life is not argued but suffered. The other drives, the sexual drive that Freud enthroned, the will to power that other schools proposed, are read by Bloch as specifications or displacements of this primary unrest, and the affects that organise the inner life, fear and hope above all, are keyed to its prospects: fear is hunger's expectation of continued deprivation, hope is hunger's expectation, instructed by tendencies, of the meal that history has not yet served.

The primacy of hunger also disciplines the system's politics in a way its critics in the seminar room sometimes miss. A philosophy of hope founded on hunger cannot float into the stratosphere of pure spirit, because its founding phenomenon is the emptiest stomach in the room, and its inventories of fairy gold and cathedral light are answerable, at every point, to the question of who eats. Bloch's pages return to the bread question with a regularity that the aesthetic reputation of the work obscures: the wish for the miraculous loaf runs from the manna of Exodus through the fairy tale's wishing table to the bread riots of the modern city, one continuous document of the drive and its dreams. The warm stream, at its source, is not a metaphor for enthusiasm. It is the temperature of the body that has not yet been fed, and the cold stream of analysis earns its place in the system precisely by showing the body where its bread is realistically to be found.

## The formalism quarrel in the East: the Leipzig position defended

The Leipzig years contained a second front, beside the political one, on which Bloch's position was fought out in the open, and it belongs in a complete profile because it shows the philosophy refusing, in practice, the catechism it refused in theory. The cultural doctrine of the socialist states prescribed a realism administered from above, in which modernist experiment figured as formalist decadence and the heritage of the avant garde was treated as a bourgeois infection. Bloch, who had defended expressionism against the same charge from Lukacs in the exile journals, now defended it again, in his own university and in the republic's cultural journals, against the officials of a state that claimed to embody his hopes. His argument was the system's: the fractured forms of the modern arts are the register of a fractured reality, the demand for seamless wholes in art is the demand for a lie about the world, and a culture that punishes its artists for registering the fracture will soon be unable to register anything else, including the approach of its own crises.

The episode clarifies the relation between the philosophy and the political record better than any summary can. The defence of the Moscow trials and the defence of expressionism issue from the same disposition, the loyalty to the hope's historical carrier combined with the refusal to let the carrier dictate the hope's content, and the two defences stand in the record with their opposite signs for every reader to weigh. What the record will not support is the picture of a court philosopher. Bloch took the chair as an act of allegiance, spent the allegiance in defence of everything the chair's owners found indigestible, his God talk, his mystics, his modernists, his unrepentant categories, and was removed when the bill came due. The upright gait, which his natural law book was preparing in the same years, had its biographical demonstration in the conduct of its author, who bowed, in the Leipzig decade, to no syllabus but his own.

## The founding island: More and the birth of the genre

One exhibit from the survey of the social utopias deserves its own paragraph of emphasis, because the genre begins with it and Bloch's reading of it states his whole relation to the tradition in miniature. Thomas More's island, published in 1516, carries its programme in its name, a coinage that puns between no place and the good place, and Bloch fastens on the pun as the genre's birth certificate: from the beginning, the utopian construction knows that its country does not exist, and the knowledge is not its shame but its method, since the island's business is not to be found but to be used, as a measuring rod laid against the reader's own country. More's arrangements, the common ownership, the six hour day, the cities built to a human scale, the contempt for gold that makes chamber pots of it, are cross examined by Bloch in his usual double manner. As a design for adoption they are abstract, addressed to no tendency and no agent, a humanist's wish in a councillor's Latin. As a surplus they are inexhaustible: the first modern demonstration that property, labour time, and the honours of wealth are arrangements rather than natures, performed with an irony that has kept the demonstration alive for five centuries. The genre that descends from the island, through the city of the sun and the phalanstery to the news from nowhere, is on his showing the secular scripture of the not yet, and his own three volumes are its longest commentary, written from inside the faith that the measuring rod, laid patiently against enough centuries, becomes an instrument of the building trade.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This material teaches best as a pair of exercises. The first takes the non-synchronism argument into the classroom's own subject, the appeal of reactionary politics, and it has the rare virtue of explaining that appeal without condescension toward the appealed to: students reconstruct the strata of a chosen society, assign each its historical time, and identify which grievances the respectable parties are leaving unharvested. The second exercise works the surplus table against a form the group knows from the inside, an advertisement, a song, a festival, and requires the three columns to be filled against resistance from the group's own sceptics, who should be assigned the unfalsifiability objection to prosecute at full strength before the criteria are produced in answer. The aim of both exercises is the skill the system exists to teach: an attention that can register what a form is for, what it carries, and what its audience is still owed.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Treat Bloch as a source of hypotheses about evidence rather than as a commentary on it. In the archive his categories instruct you to widen the inventory of times before writing the narrative of an event: to read the records of the archaic strata, the parish, the small town savings bank, the veterans' league, with the same seriousness as the records of the ministries and the great firms, because the non-synchronism hypothesis predicts that the deciding grievances will be filed in the subordinate paper. In comparison his work instructs you to hold the distinction drawn in this profile between the times within a society and the stages between societies, for which the necessary companion treatment is [the companion study of the parallel concept](/2014/10/01/uneven-and-combined-development/), which lets each concept police the other's excesses. In the religious material his reading instructs you to treat the archive of hope as evidence about the hopers rather than as a puzzle about their doctrines, a discipline whose fruits are gathered in [the series' treatment of that theological afterlife](/2018/12/15/liberation-theology-and-marxism/). Journalists borrowing the vocabulary should complete its grammar, non-synchronous with what, surplus over which function, or leave the words unused; the terms are instruments, and they cut the hand that waves them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Ernst Bloch?

A German Marxist philosopher born in Ludwigshafen in 1885 and deceased in Tübingen in 1977, Ernst Bloch built the most complete philosophy of hope in his tradition. Educated in the neo Kantian universities, formed in the circles of Simmel and Weber, he fled the Nazis, wrote his major work in American exile, held a chair in East Germany, fell from favour there, and spent his last years in the West. His claim on attention rests on a single wager: that hope is not a mood but a principle, a structured feature of an unfinished world, which can be studied, educated, and graded like any other object of knowledge.

### Q: What is The Principle of Hope about?

It is an inventory of human wishing raised to the scale of a system. Across three volumes Bloch collects the daydreams of ordinary life, the wishful images of art, fashion, film, and the fairy tale, the sketches of a better existence contributed by medicine, architecture, and the utopian writers, and the hopes of religion, and he reads every exhibit twice: once for the ideological service it performs, and once for the anticipation it carries beyond that service. On the foundation of the inventory he builds his categories, the not yet conscious and the not yet become, educated hope, and the distinction between abstract wishing and concrete utopia, anticipation fastened to a tendency genuinely ripening in the world.

### Q: What is concrete utopia?

Concrete utopia is anticipation that has done its homework. It names a hoped for condition whose approach can be traced in tendencies already at work in the present: conditions partially assembled, agents whose situation aligns them with the outcome, obstacles identified rather than wished away. Its opposite, abstract utopia, is the wish that skips the homework and consoles its holder with an image unconnected to any ripening process. The distinction is not between bold and timid hopes, since a concrete utopia may aim at a transformation far more radical than any fantasy. It is between hopes that can function as levers inside history and hopes that function as decorations outside it.

### Q: What is the not-yet?

The not-yet is Bloch's master category, and it operates on two levels. In consciousness it is the not yet conscious: content pressing toward articulation from the future side of experience, the dawning edge of awareness at which possibilities announce themselves as images, feelings, and restless wishes before they acquire concepts. In the world it is the not yet become: the objective unfinishedness of things, the store of real possibilities held as tendencies and latencies in existing conditions. The whole philosophy is an attempt to bring the two levels into disciplined correspondence, so that an anticipation counts as knowledge when it fastens on a tendency, and as fantasy when it cannot.

### Q: What is non-synchronism and how does it explain fascism?

Non-synchronism is the condition in which the inhabitants of one society live in several historical times at once: the peasant in his labour process belongs to an earlier century than the engineer in the same town, and the grievances formed in the earlier time, land hunger, ruined craft independence, betrayed promises, remain active political forces rather than fading residues. Fascism, on Bloch's explanation, harvested these non contemporary discontents by addressing each stratum in its own idiom, while the workers' parties, recognising only the modern contradiction between capital and labour, dismissed the older grievances as backward and left them unanswered. The failure he names is therefore not a failure to foresee brutality but a failure of address, a missing category for contradictions out of step with the most advanced sector.

### Q: Why does Bloch write about religion?

Because religion is, on his account, the largest archive of human hope ever assembled, and a materialism that discards the archive abandons its contents to the institutions that administered them. He reads the Bible as a divided book: beside the God who commands obedience stands the Exodus tradition, in which the divine name is a future tense and the first act is the liberation of slaves, and beside the priests stand the prophets, Job's accusation, and a kingdom announced to the poor. His proposal is inheritance rather than reverence or dismissal: to recover the wishes stored in the religious forms, the reversal of fortune, the answer to innocent suffering, the kingdom of justice, and to translate them out of their theological custody into the hope of this world.

### Q: Did Bloch defend the Moscow trials?

Yes. In the exile journals of the later nineteen thirties he defended them publicly and in print. The fact belongs to the documented record of his political life, and this profile states it plainly, as the brief for it requires, without elaboration and without excuse. It stands in the ledger alongside the rest of the record: the early warnings about fascism's appeal that went unheeded, the chair accepted in East Germany in 1949, the refusal of the ritual condemnations after 1956, the forced retirement that followed, and the decision in 1961 not to return across the new border. Readers will weigh the items for themselves; the biography neither hides the entry nor permits it to be used as a solvent for the philosophy, which must be judged, as he would have insisted, by its arguments.

### Q: How does Bloch differ from the Frankfurt School?

The kinship is genuine: both men were exiles, both sat under the same teachers, both held that fascism was the event Marxist theory had to explain. What divides them is a wager over where history keeps its reserves. The Frankfurt analysis, in its Adornian form, finds the administered world closing over every wish in advance, so that hope survives chiefly in negation, in the difficult artwork that refuses to picture the reconciliation it demands. Bloch grants the administration and denies the closure, because the administration works by borrowing genuine wishes, and the borrowed wishes remain in circulation as a surplus that can be inherited. Where Adorno forbids the image of utopia, Bloch inventories a thousand of them and grades them. The two positions are best read as instruments calibrated to different emergencies, the one to a tradition about to be erased, the other to a hope in danger of being managed out of existence.

### Q: What is the utopian surplus?

The utopian surplus is the excess of a cultural form's promise over the use its own order makes of it. A cathedral legitimates a hierarchy, and it also rears an anticipation, a dwelling made worthy of human beings, that no legitimation required; an advertisement attaches wishes to a product, and it also displays, to everyone who passes, a world of ease and recognition that the product cannot deliver. The surplus is identified by a test: it is whatever in the form reaches past the order that produced it, and survives translation into a freer arrangement without embarrassment. Nor does it assert that popular culture is secretly progressive. It is the claim that ideology, to work at all, must borrow real wishes, and that the borrowed wishes can be recovered from the borrowing.

### Q: Is Bloch worth reading today?

He is worth reading wherever the questions his categories were built for are still the questions being asked. Why do movements of reaction recruit among strata whose grievances the respectable analysis files as anachronisms? What is stored in the cultural forms an age consumes, beyond the uses those forms are put to? How can a hope be told from a wish, and a tendency from a desire? A reader who brings those questions to the Heritage chapters on non-synchronism, or to the grading of the utopian writers, will find instruments that work, in a prose that makes no concessions. He is not an easy read, and the metaphysics that crowns the system can be declined. The method survives the declining, which is the strongest thing this profile knows how to say for any philosopher.

### Q: How does the non-synchronism explanation handle a countryside inside an industrial state?

It treats the countryside not as the state's past lagging behind its present but as a second present, with its own time, its own contradictions, and its own political availability. The countryman on a fragmented holding, working it with horse drawn equipment inside a republic of chemical combines, is a contemporary, and his land hunger, his resentment of the city, and his memory of betrayed promises are contemporary facts with contemporary effects, visible in his vote and in his son's enlistment. The explanation therefore instructs the analyst to stop asking when the countryside will catch up, a question that assumes one clock, and to ask instead what the countryside's own clock is striking, and who is listening to it.

### Q: What should a student separate between hope as doctrine and hope as mood?

The student should separate the claim from the feeling. Hope as a mood is a private weather, pleasant to inhabit, worthless as evidence, and compatible with doing nothing. Hope as Bloch's doctrine is a set of testable assertions: that the world is unfinished, that real possibilities ripen in it as tendencies, that anticipations in consciousness can correspond to those tendencies, and that the correspondence can be examined case by case. An essay that treats the doctrine as a mood will praise or deplore the author's optimism and miss every one of the assertions. An essay that treats it as a doctrine can dispute the assertions, produce counter cases, and engage the philosophy on the ground it chose.

### Q: Why did the defence of the Moscow trials sit inside a philosophy of hope?

It sat there because the hope of his generation had a historical address, and he had given his loyalty to the address. Bloch had greeted the Russian Revolution as the event in which the not yet had first acquired a state, and in the nineteen thirties, as fascism advanced through Europe, he treated the Soviet Union as the material carrier of the anticipation his philosophy described, the one existing power whose survival seemed to him identical with the survival of the hope itself. Defending its trials in print was the form that loyalty took in the exile press of those years. The philosophy itself supplied the categories in which the loyalty can be judged: an anticipation that forbids criticism of its carrier has ceased to be educated hope, and the later record, the Leipzig collision and the refusal of the required condemnations, shows the same man applying the prohibition in the other direction. The tension is left standing, because it is the biography's central tension and not a puzzle with a tidy solution.

### Q: How does the utopian surplus table grade a cultural form?

The table grades by separation. For a given form it first names the ideological function, the service the form performs for the order that produced it; it then names the anticipatory content, what the form promises beyond that order's capacity to deliver; and it grades the form by the result of the test that distinguishes the two, whether the promise survives the order's fall and still speaks in a freer arrangement. A form whose promise perishes with its patron scores as ideology only. A form whose promise translates, as the fairy tale's justice and the cathedral's vertical longing translate, carries a surplus, and it is graded by the depth and specificity of what it anticipates, not by the refinement of its execution.

### Q: What separates the not yet conscious from simple ignorance?

Ignorance is a gap where a content exists and could be supplied: the fact not yet learned, the language not yet acquired, the skill not yet practised. Remedy and object are both known in advance, which is why ignorance can be cured by teaching. The not yet conscious is a different condition entirely. Its content exists nowhere in finished form, neither in the learner nor in any book, because it belongs to a possibility that is still ripening; it announces itself in advance of its object, as restlessness, as image, as the feeling that the available words are wrong for what is pressing to be said. The student who lacks a fact needs instruction. The age that lacks a category needs the slower work Bloch calls education of hope, in which the dawning content is clarified until it can be tested against the tendencies of the world.
