For roughly a century, the throne of Lower Egypt was occupied by kings whose fathers had names no scribe of the Twelfth Dynasty would have recognized. The Second Intermediate Period, running circa 1650 to 1550 BCE, is the stretch of Egyptian history in which a dynasty of West Asian origin, remembered by the Egyptian phrase that gives us the word Hyksos, ruled the eastern Delta from a city called Avaris while a rival Egyptian line held the south from Thebes and an African kingdom centered on Kerma held Nubia. Egyptian tradition treated the episode as a wound. Modern scholarship treats it as the most consequential hundred years in the country's long middle, because the state that emerged from it was not the inward-facing kingdom that went into it. It was an empire.

That distinction is the whole argument of this guide. A reader who leaves knowing only that foreigners once ruled the Delta and were eventually thrown out has learned the least interesting part. The interesting part is causal: a kingdom that had spent seven centuries defining itself as the world entire, bounded by deserts and defended by fortresses it built to keep the outside out, spent one century sharing the Nile with two rivals and discovered that the outside could not be kept out. The generation that ended the division did not restore the old arrangement. It went north and east and south with chariots and a standing army and built the first Egyptian empire in the sense the word properly carries. The Second Intermediate Period is where the pivot happens, and understanding the pivot means understanding what the era actually contained rather than what the propaganda of the victors said it contained.

![The Hyksos and the Second Intermediate Period of Egypt, explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-113.webp)

The obstacle to understanding is that almost every ancient account of this period was written by people with an interest in making it look like a catastrophe. The Eighteenth Dynasty kings who expelled the Hyksos owed their legitimacy to the expulsion, so their inscriptions describe a land defiled and a restoration achieved. Manetho, the Ptolemaic-era priest whose dynastic scheme still organizes the way Egyptologists count Egyptian dynasties, wrote more than a thousand years after the events and preserved a version, transmitted to us at second hand through the Jewish historian Josephus, in which a savage race from the east descended on a defenseless country, burned the cities, and razed the temples of the gods. That story has enormous narrative appeal and very little archaeological support. The spade at Avaris has produced a trading city, not a burned one, and a dynasty that wrote its names in hieroglyphs, took Egyptian royal titles, patronized an Egyptian god, and copied Egyptian mathematical textbooks.

What follows treats the era as a hub. It sets the chronology, explains who the Hyksos were and where the name comes from, maps the three simultaneous powers that divided the Nile, describes the character of Hyksos rule and the shape of the Theban and Kushite states around it, explains how the era began and how it ended at the level of the whole period, and then sorts through the historiography that has swung from barbarian-invasion melodrama to something considerably more careful. The specialist questions belong to the specialist articles in this cluster, and this guide routes to them rather than duplicating them: the mechanics of how a Delta minority became a ruling dynasty, the war that ended the division, the origins debate, the technology transfer, the trade networks, and the question of what foreign rule did to Egyptian self-understanding each have their own treatment. The pillar's job is the map and the argument.

## What the Second Intermediate Period Was and When It Ran

The Second Intermediate Period is a modern label, not an Egyptian one. No scribe at Avaris or Thebes woke up thinking of himself as living in an intermediate age, any more than a person living through a war thinks of it as a chapter heading. Egyptologists divide pharaonic history into three long stretches of unified, centrally governed statehood, called the Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms, separated by stretches in which central authority fragmented and rival centers claimed the crown. The gaps got the name intermediate because they sit between the kingdoms, and the numbering follows their order. The First Intermediate Period sits between the Old and Middle Kingdoms. The Second Intermediate Period sits between the Middle and New. The Third sits after the New Kingdom.

Conventionally the period runs from circa 1650 BCE to circa 1550 BCE, and both of those dates deserve the word circa in bold type. Egyptian chronology before the first millennium BCE is a reconstruction, not a record. It is built by counting reign lengths forward from later fixed points, cross-checking against king lists that survive in fragments, and anchoring the whole structure to a small number of astronomical observations whose interpretation is itself contested. Different scholars using different assumptions produce dates that can differ by decades, and the Second Intermediate Period is one of the worst offenders because its sources are the thinnest in the pharaonic sequence. When this guide gives a date, it gives the conventional consensus figure and expects the reader to hold it loosely.

The period covers what Manetho's scheme calls Dynasties Thirteen through Seventeen, though the word dynasty does more damage here than help. In a unified era, a dynasty is a sequence: one royal family rules, then another. In the Second Intermediate Period, several of the numbered dynasties are contemporaries, ruling different parts of Egypt at the same time. The Fifteenth Dynasty, the Hyksos great kings at Avaris, overlaps with the Sixteenth and Seventeenth, which are Theban. Reading the dynasty numbers as a straight line produces a picture of Egyptian history in which the same century happens three times in a row. Reading them as parallel columns produces the picture that actually happened.

### What makes a period intermediate rather than a kingdom?

The name is a modern convenience. Egyptologists reserve the word kingdom for eras when one king ruled the whole Nile Valley from a single administration, and they call the gaps between those eras intermediate periods. The label describes political fragmentation, not decline, and the Egyptians themselves never used it.

The chronological spine, stated in the plainest available terms, runs like this. The Twelfth Dynasty, the classic Middle Kingdom, ends around 1802 BCE with the reign of Sobekneferu. The Thirteenth Dynasty follows, ruling from the Middle Kingdom capital of Itjtawy near the Faiyum, and although it lasts perhaps a century and a half it produces something like fifty kings, which is a statistic that tells its own story about the stability of the throne. Somewhere in that stretch, a separate line of rulers with West Semitic names establishes itself in the eastern Delta, and Manetho's scheme calls it the Fourteenth Dynasty. Around 1650 BCE the Fifteenth Dynasty, the Hyksos proper, takes Memphis and the Thirteenth Dynasty state collapses or retreats. The Theban Sixteenth and then Seventeenth Dynasties hold Upper Egypt. Around 1550 BCE Ahmose, a Theban, takes Avaris and reunifies the country, and by convention his accession or his victory marks the start of the Eighteenth Dynasty and the New Kingdom.

That is the skeleton. Nearly every joint in it is debated. Whether the Fourteenth Dynasty preceded the Fifteenth or overlapped it, whether the Sixteenth Dynasty was Theban or a set of minor Delta rulers, and where exactly the boundary between the Thirteenth Dynasty's collapse and the Hyksos ascendancy falls are all live questions, and the reconstruction that most working Egyptologists now use rests heavily on Kim Ryholt's detailed reworking of the period's king lists and seal evidence, published in the 1990s and argued over ever since. Where this guide follows one reconstruction over another, it says so.

### Which sources give us the dates at all?

The Second Intermediate Period is reconstructed from three kinds of evidence, none of them generous. First, king lists compiled long afterward, chiefly the Turin King List, a Ramesside papyrus that once recorded the whole sequence of Egyptian rulers with reign lengths and now survives as a heap of fragments that scholars have spent two centuries trying to reassemble. Its treatment of this era is precious because it is the only Egyptian document that appears to count the Hyksos kings as kings, giving the Fifteenth Dynasty six rulers of foreign lands and a total of a hundred and eight years, and because that figure is one of the few hard numbers anywhere in the period. What the papyrus can and cannot support is worked through in [the guide to what the Turin King List tells us](/2003/02/01/turin-king-list-explained/), which owns the source's methods and limits.

Second, contemporary objects: scarab seals bearing royal names, stelae, statues reused and reinscribed, and the occasional inscribed monument. Seals are the workhorse. A Hyksos king's name on a scarab found at a given site is evidence that his administration reached that site, and the distribution of those scarabs is how the geography of Hyksos authority has been mapped. The method has limits, since seals travel in pockets and get reused for centuries, but it is better than nothing and it is often all there is.

Third, the archaeology of the sites themselves, above all Tell el-Dab'a in the eastern Delta, identified as Avaris and excavated over decades by the Austrian team led by Manfred Bietak. That excavation is the single most important body of evidence for the period, because it turned a textual argument about who the Hyksos were into a stratified physical record of what their city contained: house plans, burial customs, pottery, imports, temples, and the layered story of a settlement that grew from a modest Middle Kingdom foundation into the largest port in the eastern Mediterranean world of its day.

## How the Era Began: The Middle Kingdom's Long Unraveling

Nothing about the Second Intermediate Period makes sense without the state that preceded it, which is why this guide starts there and then hands off. The Middle Kingdom, treated at full length in [the complete guide to the Middle Kingdom of Egypt](/2001/09/01/middle-kingdom-of-egypt-guide/), was in many ways the most administratively sophisticated version of the Egyptian state that had yet existed. Its Twelfth Dynasty kings had broken the power of the provincial governors who had torn the Old Kingdom apart, built a professional bureaucracy that ran on written records, pushed the southern frontier deep into Nubia and held it with a chain of mud-brick fortresses that would not look out of place in a medieval European siege manual, and presided over a literary flowering whose texts were still being copied as school exercises a thousand years later.

It did not fall to an invasion. It fell to a slow structural failure that took most of a century, and the failure was in the succession.

### Why did the Middle Kingdom lose the Delta?

Central authority did not collapse in a single blow. The Thirteenth Dynasty cycled through kings so quickly that no ruler could build a lasting power base, the crown's grip on distant provinces loosened, and the eastern Delta, already home to a large West Asian population, slipped into the hands of local rulers who simply stopped remitting authority upward.

The Thirteenth Dynasty is one of the strangest constructions in Egyptian history. It kept the Middle Kingdom's institutions running, kept the capital at Itjtawy, kept the vizierate and the treasury and the scribal apparatus staffed, kept the same art style, and kept producing kings at a rate that suggests the throne had become an office rather than an inheritance. Reign lengths measured in months are common. Some kings are known from a single seal. The vizier's office, by contrast, shows signs of real continuity, and one plausible reading of the evidence is that the bureaucracy was governing while the kingship rotated, which is a workable arrangement for a while and a fatal one when a real challenge arrives.

Meanwhile the eastern Delta had been changing for two centuries. The Twelfth Dynasty's own policies had brought Asiatics, the Egyptian term for people from the Levant and Sinai, into Egypt in large numbers as laborers, servants, soldiers, sailors, craftsmen, and traders. Egyptian sources from the Middle Kingdom show them in households, on work gangs, and in the record of administrative documents; a well-known papyrus from the Thirteenth Dynasty lists a household staff with dozens of Asiatic names. This was not infiltration. This was the ordinary demographic consequence of a state that traded with the Levant, campaigned in the Levant, and imported labor from the Levant. The eastern Delta, closest to the land route out of Egypt and the natural place for that population to settle, became a region where a substantial share of the inhabitants had Levantine names, buried their dead with Levantine customs, and worshipped gods with Levantine attributes, while living in Egyptian houses under Egyptian administration.

At some point in the Thirteenth Dynasty, a line of rulers with West Semitic names emerges in that Delta, based on the evidence of their scarab seals and a small number of monuments, and Manetho's scheme numbers them the Fourteenth Dynasty. The best-attested of them is a king named Nehesy, whose name means the Nubian and whose monuments cluster around Avaris. Whether these rulers were independent kings, semi-autonomous governors who inflated their titles, or something in between is one of the many things the evidence does not settle. What matters for the pillar's argument is the sequence: a large Levantine population in the Delta long predates the Hyksos, and local rule in the Delta predates the Hyksos, and by the time the Fifteenth Dynasty arrives it is stepping into a political vacancy that has been widening for generations rather than kicking down a door.

The mechanics of that step, which is the moment a Delta power became the ruling power of Lower Egypt, belong to [the account of how the Hyksos took over Egypt](/2002/08/01/how-hyksos-took-over-egypt/). This guide does not re-run it. The pillar's point is narrower and structural: the Second Intermediate Period begins because the Middle Kingdom state stopped being able to hold the north, not because a foreign army arrived and made it stop.

## Who the Hyksos Were: The Name, the People, and the Evidence

The word Hyksos is a Greek mangling of an Egyptian phrase, and the mangling has done more historical damage than almost any other mistranslation in the field. The Egyptian is heqau khasut, commonly rendered rulers of foreign lands. It is a title, not an ethnicity. Egyptian scribes had used it for centuries before the Fifteenth Dynasty existed, applying it to chieftains and princes outside Egypt's borders in Nubia, Libya, and the Levant, in exactly the way a modern civil service might write foreign head of state. It describes an office and a geography. It does not describe a nation, a tribe, a language, or a race.

Greek writers, working from Egyptian sources through many intermediaries, heard the phrase as a name and turned the rulers of foreign lands into a people called the Hyksos. Josephus, quoting Manetho, went further and offered an etymology in which the first syllable meant king and the second meant shepherd, producing shepherd kings, which is how the Hyksos entered European scholarship and why nineteenth-century books are full of nomadic shepherd hordes sweeping out of the desert. The etymology is wrong. The image it generated survived the correction by about a century, because a good story outlives a good footnote.

### What did the Hyksos borrow from Egyptian kingship?

Nearly the whole apparatus. Fifteenth Dynasty rulers wrote their names in hieroglyphs inside royal cartouches, took throne names compounded with the sun god Ra in orthodox Egyptian fashion, used the traditional titles of Egyptian kingship including son of Ra, and had their scribes produce documents in Egyptian on Egyptian materials in Egyptian formats.

So who were they, in the terms the evidence can actually support? The consensus, and it is a genuine consensus rather than a polite compromise, is that the Hyksos rulers came out of the West Semitic-speaking population of the Levant and the eastern Delta. The evidence is convergent and unglamorous. Their personal names, where they are not Egyptian throne names, are West Semitic in form. The god they promoted above others at Avaris was the Egyptian Set, but a Set identified with the Levantine storm god Baal or Hadad and shown with Levantine iconography, which is what happens when a foreign community finds the closest local equivalent to its own god and merges the two. The material culture of Avaris in the relevant strata is full of Levantine forms: Middle Bronze Age pottery from Canaan, Levantine weapon types in burials, donkey burials in front of tombs in a custom well attested in the Levant and unknown in Egyptian practice, and house plans that follow Syro-Palestinian rather than Egyptian conventions. Scientific work has added a further layer: strontium isotope analysis of teeth from the Tell el-Dab'a cemeteries, published by a team including Chris Stantis and working with the excavation's own material, found that a substantial share of the sampled individuals had grown up locally rather than abroad, and that non-local individuals were disproportionately female, a pattern consistent with a resident community that had been in the Delta for generations and was drawing in marriage partners from outside.

That last finding is a good illustration of why the origins question is more interesting than the invasion question, and it is also why the origins question has its own article. Whether the Hyksos are best understood as invaders who conquered from outside or as an immigrant population that rose from within is a real scholarly debate with real evidence on both sides, and it is worked through and decided in [the treatment of whether the Hyksos were invaders or immigrants](/2003/03/01/were-hyksos-invaders-or-immigrants/). The pillar records the outcome of the argument as it bears on the era's character: the Fifteenth Dynasty was West Semitic in origin, Egyptianized in its public self-presentation, and deeply rooted in the Delta by the time it ruled it.

The kings themselves are a short and frustrating list. The Turin King List gives the Fifteenth Dynasty six rulers and a hundred and eight years, which averages to eighteen years apiece and implies a stability the Thirteenth Dynasty would have envied. Manetho's version, as transmitted, opens with a king he calls Salitis, a name that does not appear on any contemporary Egyptian object, which is the sort of discrepancy that keeps the period's specialists employed. The two Hyksos kings who are unmistakably real and well attested are Khyan, who used the throne name Seuserenre, and Apepi, often written Apophis, who used several throne names across a long reign and who is the king the Theban war was fought against. A last king, Khamudi, appears at the end of the sequence and is essentially a name.

Khyan is the more revealing of the two, because of where his name turns up. Objects bearing it have been recovered far outside Egypt: a lid at Knossos on Crete, a fragment at Hattusa in Anatolia, an object recorded from Mesopotamia. Individually each find is arguable, since small inscribed objects travel and get traded and get deposited centuries later. Collectively the pattern says something a purely Egyptian record could never say, which is that a king ruling from the eastern Delta had a name worth carrying into the palaces of the Aegean and Anatolia. That is not the profile of a desert raider. It is the profile of a commercial power.

## The Three-Power Map: Avaris, Thebes, and Kerma

Here is the single most useful thing to fix in the mind before anything else about this era: for roughly a century, there was no such place as Egypt in the political sense. There were three kingdoms sharing a river system, and each of the three was a real state with a capital, an army, a treasury, and a foreign policy. Most thin accounts of the period mention the Hyksos and Thebes and forget the third power entirely, which is like describing a three-body problem by watching two of the bodies.

### How did three rival powers divide Egypt at once?

The Hyksos Fifteenth Dynasty held the eastern Delta and the north from Avaris, extracting recognition from the middle of the country. A Theban dynasty held Upper Egypt from roughly Abydos south to Elephantine. The Kushite kingdom of Kerma held Nubia above the first cataract, including the fortresses Egypt had built to contain it.

| Power | Capital | Territory | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Hyksos Fifteenth Dynasty | Avaris (Tell el-Dab'a), eastern Delta | The Delta and Lower Egypt, with authority reaching Memphis and influence claimed as far as Cusae in Middle Egypt | West Semitic in origin, Egyptianized in royal style, commercially oriented, plugged into Levantine and Aegean trade, patron of Set identified with Baal |
| The Theban Sixteenth and Seventeenth Dynasties | Thebes (modern Luxor) | Upper Egypt, roughly from the Abydos region south to Elephantine at the first cataract | Culturally conservative and self-consciously Egyptian, poorer and smaller, custodian of pharaonic tradition, ultimately the aggressor |
| The Kingdom of Kush | Kerma, above the third cataract in Upper Nubia | Nubia from the first cataract southward, including the Middle Kingdom fortress chain | An indigenous African state with monumental architecture, mass royal burials, and a strong metal industry; a Hyksos diplomatic partner and Thebes's southern threat |

That table is the era in a single frame, and it is worth reading it as a strategic problem rather than a list. Thebes sat between two powers that had every incentive to cooperate against it. The Hyksos in the north controlled the Mediterranean trade and the land route to Asia. Kerma in the south controlled the gold of Nubia, the trade routes to inner Africa, and the fortresses that Egypt had built to keep Kush out and that Kush now held. Whatever Thebes wanted to do, it had to do while watching both directions, and the surviving Theban war documents make it clear the Thebans knew it: the Kamose material describes an intercepted message between the northern king and the ruler of Kush, and whatever the exact circumstances, the fact that the Theban court thought such a message plausible tells us how the strategic situation looked from the middle.

### Where the boundaries actually ran

Frontier lines in this period are soft, and the honest answer to where the Hyksos realm ended is that it depends on the decade and on what you count as control. The Egyptian evidence and the Theban war texts point to a boundary in Middle Egypt around Cusae, north of Asyut, as the practical limit of direct Theban authority and the notional beginning of the northern king's sphere. Between Cusae and Memphis, the arrangement seems to have been something looser than provincial administration: local rulers who acknowledged Avaris, paid something, and were left alone. The distribution of Hyksos royal seals fits that reading, thinning out as it moves south and appearing at sites in a pattern that looks more like commercial and diplomatic reach than like a governed province.

South of Elephantine, the Theban writ ended and Kush began. The Middle Kingdom's great fortress chain in Lower Nubia, built by Senusret III and his predecessors to police the Nubian frontier and described in [the account of how Egypt conquered and held Nubia](/2002/05/01/egypt-conquest-of-nubia/), had passed into Kushite hands. It did not fall to a siege. The garrisons appear to have stayed on and transferred their loyalty, and the stela of an official at Buhen records service under the ruler of Kush in language that treats the arrangement as unremarkable. A fortress built to keep a kingdom out, garrisoned a century later by the descendants of its builders working for that kingdom, is the Second Intermediate Period in miniature.

### Was Egypt ever a single country during this era?

Not in any period between the Thirteenth Dynasty's collapse and Ahmose's victory. What did survive was the idea of a single country. Both the Theban and the Hyksos kings used the full titulary of a king of Upper and Lower Egypt, which meant that both were claiming the whole of a land neither controlled. That is not a scribal error. It is a statement of intent, and in the Theban case it eventually became a war aim. The Egyptian conception of kingship had no vocabulary for a legitimate partition. A king was the king of the Two Lands or he was a pretender, and since neither side would concede the title, the logic of the ideology pointed at a fight from the beginning, even during the long decades when no fighting happened.

## Avaris: The City That Rewrote the Period

Avaris is the reason the modern picture of the Hyksos differs so sharply from the ancient one. Everything else about the era is textual and thin. Avaris is physical and thick, and it has been dug for decades under the direction of Manfred Bietak and the Austrian Archaeological Institute at the site of Tell el-Dab'a, in the northeastern Delta near the modern town of Qantir. The identification of the tell with the Hyksos capital rests on the stratigraphy, the material culture, the location matching what the texts say about the city's position, and the continuous occupation sequence that runs from the Middle Kingdom into the New. It is as secure as identifications of this kind get.

What the excavation found was not a fortress of conquest. It was a port.

The story of the site begins before the Hyksos. A planned settlement was founded there in the Twelfth Dynasty, an orderly Egyptian foundation with a grid of small houses, apparently a royal project connected to the eastern frontier and the trade route out to Asia. Within a few generations, the character of the place changes. The pottery assemblage shifts, Levantine forms appearing first alongside and then in quantity beside Egyptian ones. The house plans change to a Syro-Palestinian type with a central room and a broad-room plan unlike anything in Egyptian domestic architecture. The burial practices change: tombs are dug within the settlement rather than in a separate desert cemetery, which is not Egyptian custom, and some of them have donkeys buried in pairs at the entrance, a practice with clear Levantine parallels and no Egyptian ones. Weapons appear in male burials, another Levantine habit. Temples are built on Levantine plans, including a broad-room temple type known from Syria.

None of this is subtle, and none of it required a conquest to produce. It is the signature of a community that had come from the Levant, kept its own customs, and made a city out of them on Egyptian soil, with Egyptian administrators next door and Egyptian pottery on the same floors. By the time the Fifteenth Dynasty is ruling from the place, Avaris has become enormous by the standards of its world, spreading over a large area with a harbor complex linked to a branch of the Nile, and the imported material in its levels reaches from Cyprus and Canaan to the Aegean.

### Why a port and not a fortress?

Geography explains most of it. Avaris sat on the Pelusiac branch of the Nile, close enough to the coast to ship and close enough to the land route across northern Sinai to caravan. Anything moving between Egypt and the Levant, whether by sea or by donkey, passed within reach of it. A ruler who held Avaris held the door, and holding the door was worth more than holding territory, because the wealth of the eastern Mediterranean in the Middle Bronze Age moved through doors. The Fifteenth Dynasty did not need to conquer Upper Egypt to be rich. It needed to control the interface between Egypt and everywhere else, and it did.

That commercial character is the deep explanation for a great deal of the era's strangeness, including the long stretches in which the Hyksos and Thebes seem to have simply coexisted, trading grain south and gold north and leaving each other alone. A regime whose revenue comes from tariffs and exchange has a structural preference for quiet borders. A regime whose legitimacy comes from being the rightful king of a land it does not hold has a structural preference for war. The Second Intermediate Period ends the way it does partly because those two structures were never going to reach the same conclusion. The trade system itself, its routes, its goods, and its accounting, is the subject of [the guide to the trade networks of Hyksos Egypt](/2003/01/01/trade-networks-of-hyksos-egypt/), which owns those questions in this cluster.

### The Minoan problem

One of the most discussed finds at Tell el-Dab'a is a body of wall painting fragments in an unmistakably Aegean style, bull-leaping scenes and labyrinth patterns of the kind familiar from Knossos, found in a palace complex at the site. For a while these were taken as proof of a Hyksos-Minoan alliance, which made for excellent headlines. Continued work on the stratigraphy has pushed the accepted date of the frescoes later, into the early Eighteenth Dynasty, meaning they most likely belong to an Egyptian palace built after the Hyksos were expelled, not to a Hyksos one. The dating has been argued over at length and the argument is not fully closed, but the direction of travel in the scholarship has been consistent. It is a useful case for a reader learning how this field works: a spectacular find, an irresistible early interpretation, and a slow correction driven by stratigraphy that never quite catches up with the popular version.

## The Shape of Hyksos Rule

What did a Hyksos king actually do all day? The honest answer is that we have very little direct evidence of administration, because the Delta's wet soil destroys papyrus and because the Eighteenth Dynasty had every reason not to preserve the records of the regime it replaced. What survives is inference from the shape of the thing.

The Fifteenth Dynasty presented itself as Egyptian kingship. Its rulers took prenomen throne names built on the sun god's name in the standard Egyptian pattern, wrote those names in cartouches, used the titles of the traditional royal titulary, dated documents by their regnal years exactly as an Egyptian king would, and had themselves represented in Egyptian form on Egyptian monument types. In several documented cases they took over existing Egyptian statuary and monuments and had their own names carved onto them, a practice called usurpation that Egyptian kings themselves used constantly and that signals continuity rather than contempt. The point of the exercise was legitimacy. A ruler in Egypt who wanted to be obeyed by Egyptian officials, taxed by Egyptian scribes, and prayed for by Egyptian priests had to be an Egyptian king in form, whatever his grandfather's name had been.

Underneath the royal surface, the machinery seems to have been inherited rather than invented. Egyptian officials with Egyptian titles appear serving Hyksos kings. The scribal tradition continued in Egyptian, in hieratic, on Egyptian papyrus, in Egyptian formats. The most famous surviving document from Hyksos Egypt is the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, a compendium of arithmetic and geometry problems copied by a scribe named Ahmose, whose note records that he was copying an older Middle Kingdom original and that he did so in a regnal year of a king whose throne name belongs to Apepi. That single document carries an enormous amount of weight in the modern reassessment of the period, because a regime under which a scribe sits down to copy a centuries-old Egyptian mathematical textbook in year thirty-three of a reign is not a regime that has burned the libraries.

### Was there a Hyksos empire?

Not in the sense the word usually carries. The Fifteenth Dynasty's power in Egypt looks like a core of direct rule in the Delta, a zone of firmer control around Memphis and the entrance to the Faiyum, and a wide outer zone of vassalage and commercial influence in which local rulers governed themselves and acknowledged Avaris. Whether that acknowledgment involved tribute, and how much, is not documented in any way that would satisfy an accountant. The Theban war texts assert that the northern king's authority reached Cusae and that Egyptian territory was being held by a foreigner, but those are polemical documents written to justify a war, and the specific claims in them have to be handled as claims.

Outside Egypt, the picture is even softer. The distribution of Khyan's name and the volume of Levantine material at Avaris show reach, but reach through trade and diplomacy is not the same as rule. There is no evidence for a Hyksos provincial administration in Canaan, no garrison inscriptions, no governor's residences that anyone has convincingly identified as such. The Hyksos state looks like a commercial thalassocracy with an Egyptian face, not a territorial empire. That distinction matters for the era's argument, because the empire that followed was territorial in exactly the ways this one was not.

## Religion and Belief in a Divided Egypt

Egyptian religion did not stop at the Hyksos frontier and it did not stop under Hyksos rule. What changed was the emphasis at the top, and the change is one of the clearest windows into how the Fifteenth Dynasty understood itself.

The god the Hyksos kings promoted at Avaris was Set. That choice looks strange to a reader who knows Set only as the villain of the Osiris myth, the murdering brother, the embodiment of disorder. It is much less strange in context. Set in the Middle Bronze Age was a legitimate and widely worshipped Egyptian deity with a long pedigree, the god of the desert, of storms, of foreign lands, of the violent edge of the world, and he had been a royal god in his own right; the Second Dynasty produced a king who wrote his name under Set's animal rather than Horus's falcon, and the Nineteenth Dynasty would name kings after him. His demonization is a later and gradual development, sharpened long after this period.

For a dynasty of Levantine origin ruling from the eastern edge of Egypt, Set was the obvious choice, because Set was already Egypt's slot for the god they had brought with them. The Levantine storm god, Baal or Hadad, was a deity of weather, violence, and power, and Egyptian religion had a long-established habit of identifying foreign gods with domestic ones that shared attributes. At Avaris the identification is visible in the iconography, where the god appears with Levantine dress and attributes on Egyptian-format objects, and in the personal names. This is not a foreign cult imposed on Egypt. It is a foreign community using the Egyptian pantheon's own logic to find a home for its god, and then a dynasty elevating that god to the position of royal patron because it was theirs.

### Did the Hyksos suppress Egyptian religion?

The Eighteenth Dynasty said so. Hatshepsut's inscription at Speos Artemidos, carved a century after the expulsion, contains the most famous statement of the charge, describing a time when Asiatics ruled in the north and the temples lay neglected until she set things right. Ramesside sources continue the tradition. The archaeological record does not support it. Egyptian temples in the Delta continue to function through the period; Egyptian gods continue to receive dedications; the scribal and priestly apparatus continues; and no destruction layer of the kind a religious persecution would leave has been identified at the relevant sites. The charge is best read as what it plainly is, a piece of retrospective legitimation by a dynasty whose whole claim to rule rested on having been the ones who cleaned up.

Goddesses and gods of the Delta's Levantine population also entered the Egyptian scene in this era in ways that stuck. Anat and Astarte, Levantine goddesses of war and love, appear in Egyptian religion afterward and are worshipped in the New Kingdom with royal patronage, which is a curious afterlife for the religion of the supposed defilers. Reshep, another Levantine deity, follows the same path. The Second Intermediate Period is one of the main channels through which Levantine religion entered the Egyptian pantheon, and the entry outlasted the regime by many centuries.

In the south, meanwhile, the Theban dynasty leaned hard into orthodoxy. The Theban kings promoted Amun, the local god who would become the imperial deity of the New Kingdom, and their monuments and burials are conspicuously traditional. Some of that is conviction and some of it is politics: a poorer, smaller kingdom claiming to be the legitimate Egypt had every reason to be more Egyptian than the Egyptians. The religious dimension of the era's politics, and the way each side's gods served its claim, runs alongside the military story and helps explain why the war, when it came, was framed as a purification rather than a conquest.

## The Economy of a Divided Nile

A country cut into three pieces does not simply lose two thirds of its economy. It develops three economies with different foundations, and the differences explain a great deal about who could afford what.

The Hyksos north ran on exchange. Its foundation was the agricultural wealth of the Delta, which is the richest farmland in Egypt, layered over with the revenue of a trade system reaching Canaan, Cyprus, Syria, and the Aegean. The imports in the Avaris strata are the record: Canaanite storage jars in quantity, Cypriot wares, Syrian goods, materials that had to come by sea. A Delta ruler with a harbor sat at the junction of the maritime route along the Levantine coast and the river route into Africa, and every transaction that crossed that junction was taxable. This is the economic base that let a dynasty ruling perhaps a third of Egypt behave like the strongest power in the country for a century.

The Theban south ran on the older Egyptian model, which is to say on grain, land, and the Nile flood, supplemented by whatever could be extracted from the eastern desert quarries and from trade with the south. It was poorer in absolute terms and it was cut off from the Mediterranean, which meant cut off from the sources of copper, tin, silver, timber, and the imported luxuries that Egyptian elite culture had assumed were available. Theban burials of the period are visibly thinner than their Middle Kingdom predecessors. The Seventeenth Dynasty's royal tombs are small. The kingdom was not destitute, but it was living within reduced means and it knew it.

Kush ran on gold and on the routes into inner Africa. Nubia was where Egypt had always gotten its gold, and with the fortress chain in Kushite hands the flow now ended at Kerma rather than at Thebes. Kerma also stood at the top of the trade in ivory, ebony, incense, animal skins, and, on the evidence of Egyptian sources across many periods, people. The Kushite state that Egypt met in this era was wealthy, and its wealth is visible in the scale of what it built.

### The grain and gold squeeze

Set those three side by side and the Theban strategic problem writes itself. Thebes had food and not much else. The north had the sea and the manufactures and the metals. The south had the gold. Thebes sat between them, needing both, controlling neither, and paying for what it wanted with grain. The Kamose material's complaint about sharing the land with an Asiatic and a Nubian is usually read as an expression of wounded pride, and it is, but it is also an accurate description of a commercial position: Thebes was the customer in an arrangement where the other two were the suppliers, and the customer with an army eventually reconsiders the arrangement.

That reconsideration is the thread that connects the Second Intermediate Period's economics to the New Kingdom's imperialism. When the Eighteenth Dynasty went out and conquered, it conquered exactly the two things the Theban kingdom had spent a century going without: the Levantine corridor with its ports and metals, and Nubia with its gold. The empire's map is a map of the shortages.

## Culture, Art, and Writing Under Division

If the Second Intermediate Period were the cultural blank of the old textbooks, its artifacts would show it. They do not, quite. What they show is something more interesting: divergence.

In the north, Egyptian craft traditions continued and absorbed. Pottery production kept Egyptian forms while adopting Levantine ones; metalworking took up Levantine weapon types, including the socketed axe and the sickle-shaped sword called the khopesh in its Egyptian form, which arrives in Egypt in this era and becomes standard equipment for the next thousand years. Scarab seal production, which is one of the period's few abundant classes of evidence, actually flourished, and the styles developed at Avaris and in the Delta spread widely. The overall impression of Hyksos-era material culture in the north is of hybridity rather than replacement: Egyptian forms with Levantine additions, made by workshops that had both traditions available.

In the south, the Theban material is conservative and constrained. Art styles hold to Middle Kingdom conventions with declining technical quality, which is what happens when the patron base shrinks and the supply of good stone and imported materials tightens. Royal tombs at Thebes in this era are modest pit-and-chamber affairs, and the distinctive rishi coffins of the period, painted with a feathered pattern enveloping the mummy, are a genuinely Theban innovation and a rather beautiful one, but they are made of local materials by workshops working at a smaller scale than their predecessors.

### What survived intact

The scribal tradition is the answer, and it is the answer that most damages the dark-age reading. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, copied under Apepi from a Twelfth Dynasty original, proves that the mathematical curriculum of the Middle Kingdom was being transmitted in Hyksos Egypt by scribes who could read and copy a text centuries old. The Westcar Papyrus, a collection of tales about the Old Kingdom court, is generally dated to this era on paleographic grounds. Copies and versions of Middle Kingdom literary classics continue. The Egyptian language, the hieratic script, the mathematical system, the medical tradition, the administrative formats, and the literary canon all cross the Second Intermediate Period intact and emerge on the other side. Whatever the era was, it was not a break in the transmission of Egyptian knowledge, and the wider Middle Kingdom literary inheritance that survived the crossing is treated in [the guide to the golden age of Egyptian literature](/2002/01/01/golden-age-egyptian-literature/).

Where transmission did suffer is in monumental building, which requires surplus, stability, and a state large enough to organize labor at scale. Neither Avaris nor Thebes built at Middle Kingdom scale, and nothing in the era approaches the pyramid complexes of Senusret III or Amenemhat III. That is a real observation and it is the strongest single point in favor of calling the period diminished. It is not a point in favor of calling it dark, because the absence of monuments is a statement about political scale, not about civilization.

## Technology: What Came In and What It Did

The Second Intermediate Period is the moment when a package of Middle Bronze Age technologies, developed in the wider Near East over the preceding centuries, arrived in Egypt and stayed. Egypt had been the most technologically self-satisfied society in the region for a millennium, and self-satisfaction has costs. The Middle Kingdom army that garrisoned the Nubian forts fought on foot with copper-alloy weapons and simple bows, in a style that would have been recognizable to the soldiers of the Old Kingdom. The armies of the Levant and Mesopotamia had moved on.

The most consequential arrival is the horse-drawn chariot: a light two-wheeled vehicle with spoked wheels, drawn by a pair of horses, carrying a driver and an archer. It is a system rather than a single object, since it requires horse breeding, horse training, harness technology, a carpentry tradition capable of building a spoked wheel that will survive a battlefield, and a social organization capable of maintaining all of it. Egypt had none of those things in 1700 BCE and had all of them by 1500 BCE. The composite bow arrived in the same window, a laminated construction of wood, horn, and sinew that stores far more energy than a self bow of the same length and can be shot from a moving vehicle. So did improved bronze metallurgy, the khopesh, better body armor, and the fortification techniques that produce the massive earthen ramparts visible in Middle Bronze Age Levantine sites.

None of these were Hyksos inventions. This point is worth stating flatly because the popular version routinely gets it wrong: the Hyksos did not invent the chariot or the composite bow. They came from a world that already had them, and Egypt's contact with that world through Delta settlement, trade, and eventually foreign rule is the channel through which the package crossed the frontier. The technical detail of how the chariot worked and what its arrival meant for Bronze Age Egypt belongs to [the guide to the chariot and Bronze Age Egypt](/2002/09/01/chariot-and-bronze-age-egypt/), and the transformation of Egyptian military practice that followed is the subject of [the account of how the Hyksos changed Egyptian warfare](/2003/04/01/how-hyksos-changed-egyptian-warfare/). The pillar's interest is in the consequence.

### Why the transfer mattered more than the transfer

The technology is the part everyone remembers, and it is the smaller half of the story. Technology transfers all the time without changing anything, because a society has to want the technology, be able to pay for it, and reorganize itself to use it. Egypt did all three, and it did them because the Second Intermediate Period made the case unanswerable.

A chariot force is expensive in a way that a levy of farmers with spears is not. It requires a permanent professional establishment: breeders, trainers, wheelwrights, a standing corps of men who do nothing else. A kingdom that raises troops at harvest's end and sends them home cannot field one. The Egyptian state that emerged from this era built exactly that permanent establishment, and having built it, it had a standing army to feed, which is one of the classic engines of imperialism. The lesson Egypt learned from the Hyksos was not how to build a chariot. It was that a state which cannot project force beyond its borders will eventually find someone else's state inside them, and that the correct response is a permanent military instrument pointed outward.

The Delta also received a set of civilian technologies in the same window whose long-run effects are less dramatic and more pervasive: improved vertical looms for weaving, new musical instruments including the lyre and the oboe, olive and pomegranate cultivation, and humped zebu cattle. Egyptian daily life afterward contains a good deal of the Second Intermediate Period in it, absorbed so thoroughly that no one thought of it as foreign.

## Thebes: The Kingdom That Waited

The Theban state of the Second Intermediate Period is the least glamorous of the three powers and the one whose internal history matters most, because it is the one that won.

Thebes had been a provincial town of no particular consequence until the First Intermediate Period, when its local rulers fought their way to the crown and produced Mentuhotep II, the king who reunified Egypt and founded the Middle Kingdom, a story told in [the account of Egypt's first collapse and reunification](/2001/10/01/egypt-collapse-and-reunification/). That precedent mattered. The Theban dynasts of the Seventeenth Dynasty were the political descendants of the men who had done this once before, ruling from the same city, buried in the same necropolis, worshipping the same god, and they knew the story. When Egyptian history hands a kingdom the same problem twice, the second solution tends to look like the first.

The Sixteenth and Seventeenth Dynasties are poorly documented and their kings are largely names, recovered from coffins, from a small number of stelae, and from the Turin King List's damaged columns. Whether the Sixteenth was a Theban line running parallel to the Hyksos or a set of minor Delta rulers is one of the reconstruction's open questions, and the Theban reading, argued at length by Ryholt, is the one most current treatments follow. The Seventeenth is firmer. Its kings were buried at Dra Abu el-Naga on the west bank at Thebes, in modest tombs with small brick pyramids, and their coffins are the rishi type. The dynasty's last three rulers are the ones who matter: Seqenenre Tao, Kamose, and Ahmose, and with Ahmose the numbering changes to the Eighteenth Dynasty and the era ends, which is a reminder that the dynasty boundaries in Manetho's scheme are sometimes about what happened rather than about who was related to whom. Ahmose was Kamose's kin and Seqenenre's son. The family did not change. The situation did.

### What kept Thebes alive

Three things, and they are worth naming because they are the structural answer to why a small southern kingdom outlasted a rich northern one.

Geography first. Upper Egypt is a defensible corridor, a strip of floodplain between cliffs with a single river running through it, and an army moving south into it has few options and no flanks. The Hyksos north, whatever its strength, had no easy way to end Thebes, and the surviving evidence gives no sign that it seriously tried for most of the period.

Legitimacy second. The Theban kings held the crown in the form Egyptian tradition recognized, ruled from the city of Amun, and buried their dead in the ancestral necropolis with the old rites. In a society where kingship was a religious office, that mattered enormously, and it gave the Theban house a claim that no amount of Hyksos throne-name orthodoxy could match at the level of Egyptian sentiment.

Grain third. Upper Egypt fed itself and had surplus, and a kingdom that eats is a kingdom that persists. Poverty in imported goods is survivable. Poverty in food is not.

Add the three together and Thebes was a state that could not be easily killed, could not be easily bought, and had a permanent grievance. Give it a generation of competent kings and a military technology transfer and it becomes the most dangerous thing in the country.

### Why did Thebes finally fight?

The proximate causes are the subject of another article and the pillar will not steal them. At the level of the period, the war's underlying logic was in place from the start: the Egyptian theory of kingship could not accommodate a partition, and the Theban kings had spent a century using a titulary that claimed the north while not holding it. What changed at the end was capability rather than desire. By the reign of Seqenenre Tao, Thebes had the chariots, the composite bows, the bronze, and the professional core to make the claim good. The narrative of the campaign that followed, from Seqenenre's death to Kamose's raid to Ahmose's capture of Avaris, is told in [the account of the war that drove the Hyksos from Egypt](/2002/10/01/war-that-drove-out-the-hyksos/), and the reign of the king who finished it is treated in [the profile of Ahmose I, founder of the New Kingdom](/2002/11/01/ahmose-i-founder-of-new-kingdom/).

## Kerma and Kush: The Power Most Accounts Forget

The kingdom of Kush is not a footnote to the Second Intermediate Period. It is the third leg of the stool, and leaving it out produces a fundamentally wrong picture of what Thebes was up against.

Kush was centered on Kerma, a city above the third cataract in Upper Nubia, and it was old. Kerma had been a substantial settlement since the third millennium BCE, and by the Second Intermediate Period it had grown into the capital of a state with monumental architecture, a large cemetery containing royal tumuli of enormous scale, an accomplished metal industry, a distinctive and technically superb pottery tradition, and enough organizational reach to control the Nile from the cataracts deep into Africa. The two great mud-brick structures at the site known as the deffufas are among the largest ancient mud-brick buildings anywhere. This was not a tribal periphery. It was a kingdom, and Egyptian sources treat it as one.

The Middle Kingdom had built the Nubian fortress chain precisely because Kush was a peer competitor worth spending a fortune to contain. When the Middle Kingdom state failed, the containment failed with it, and Kush took the forts. The transfer appears to have been mostly administrative rather than violent: Egyptian garrison families stayed on, kept their titles, and served the ruler of Kush, and one Buhen official's stela records his service to the Kushite king in matter-of-fact terms. Egyptian material at Kerma from this era includes statuary carried south from Egyptian sites, and the Kerma royal burials contain Egyptian objects in quantity. The relationship between Kush and Hyksos Egypt was evidently a working one, with trade moving up and down the river past a Theban kingdom that was in the middle of it and not enjoying the position. The long entanglement between the two countries, from Middle Kingdom conquest through Kushite conquest of Egypt many centuries later, is followed in [the account of Egypt and Kush](/2006/02/01/egypt-and-kush-history/).

The Kushite dimension explains the shape of the New Kingdom's first moves. Ahmose and his successors did not stop at the old frontier once the north was taken. They went south, broke Kerma, and annexed Nubia outright, in a campaign of conquest and colonization that made Nubia an Egyptian province rather than a frontier. That was not opportunism. It was the lesson of a century in which the southern kingdom had been the strategic partner of the northern one and Thebes had been squeezed between them. The Egyptian empire in Nubia is the Second Intermediate Period's most direct policy consequence.

Geography sharpened the squeeze. Thebes sat astride the river between the two other powers, which looks like a commanding position on a map and was nothing of the kind, because the Nile was not the only road. A chain of western oases ran roughly parallel to the valley, and a traveler willing to cross desert could move between the Delta and Nubia without passing a Theban checkpoint at all. Avaris and Kerma could therefore talk to each other over Thebes rather than through it. Whoever held the middle of Egypt held less than the map suggested.

## How the Second Intermediate Period Ended

The end of the era is one event at the level of the period and a long campaign at the level of the fighting. This guide gives the period-level account and routes the campaign to its owner.

The sequence, in outline, is that a Theban king named Seqenenre Tao broke a long quiescence and moved against the north, and died in circumstances the evidence makes unusually vivid: his mummy, recovered in the nineteenth century from the royal cache, carries severe head wounds from multiple weapon types, delivered from angles suggesting he was killed while unable to defend himself, and the injuries are consistent with weapons of the era. Whether he fell in battle, in an ambush, or in some other setting is argued, but he plainly died violently while the war was on. His successor Kamose pressed the attack, sailing north and raiding to the neighborhood of Avaris itself; the stelae recording that campaign are among the few contemporary narrative documents the whole period possesses. Kamose did not finish it. His successor Ahmose, after some years, took Avaris, drove the Hyksos out of Egypt, pursued them into the southern Levant and reduced the stronghold of Sharuhen after a siege, then turned south and broke Kush. The reunified state he left behind is conventionally called the New Kingdom, and the era it opened is the subject of [the complete guide to the New Kingdom of Egypt](/2003/05/01/new-kingdom-of-egypt-guide/).

### What actually happened to the Hyksos?

Not extermination, whatever the triumphal inscriptions imply. The archaeology at Tell el-Dab'a shows the site's Hyksos-era occupation ending and the place continuing under new management rather than being destroyed and abandoned; the population of the eastern Delta did not vanish, and Levantine names, gods, and customs persist in Egypt afterward. What ended was a dynasty and a political arrangement. The likeliest picture is that the ruling house and its retainers withdrew or were expelled to the Levant, some part of the wider population stayed and was absorbed, and the Egyptian state took over the machinery of the port and the trade it carried. Ahmose's soldiers, on the evidence of the autobiographical inscription of the officer Ahmose son of Ebana at el-Kab, took captives and were rewarded with them, which is a real record of what a Bronze Age victory looked like and not a genocide.

The retrospective Egyptian version, which turned a political expulsion into a purification of the land from defilement, is a different kind of document, and reading it as history rather than as ideology is the single commonest error made about this period.

## The Catalyst-Not-Catastrophe Thesis

Here is the pillar's namable claim, stated as plainly as it can be. The Second Intermediate Period was not a hole in Egyptian history. It was the catalyst that turned a kingdom into an empire, and it did so through two mechanisms working together: the technology it delivered and the wound it inflicted. Call it the catalyst-not-catastrophe thesis. Neither mechanism would have been sufficient alone, and the interaction between them is the explanation for why the New Kingdom looks the way it does.

The technological half is the easier half. A century of contact, settlement, and foreign rule brought the chariot, the composite bow, improved bronze, the khopesh, better armor, and the fortification engineering of the Middle Bronze Age Levant into Egypt and left them there. When the Theban kings went to war, they went with the tools of the world outside, and those tools made possible a kind of warfare Egypt had never conducted: fast, projectable, offensive, capable of reaching the Euphrates.

The psychological half is the half that gets treated as soft and is in fact the load-bearing one. Egyptian ideology had rested for a thousand years on a simple proposition: Egypt was the ordered world, and everything outside it was chaos, held at bay by the king and the gods. Foreigners were figures on the soles of sandals and under the feet of statues, a decorative theology of contempt. The Second Intermediate Period broke that proposition in the most humiliating way available, by having the chaos come inside, take the crown, wear the titulary, and stay for a century while the legitimate king sat in Thebes and paid for imports. An ideology that has been falsified must either be abandoned or be defended by force, and Egypt chose force. The New Kingdom's aggressive forward policy in Asia, its permanent army, its garrisons at Levantine cities, its buffer states, and its rhetoric of smiting from the very first reign are the behavior of a state determined that it will never again be surprised by the world.

### Why did the expulsion of the Hyksos launch an empire?

Because the Theban victors concluded that a defensive frontier had failed and only a forward one would hold. Having built a chariot army to expel a foreign dynasty, they kept it, used it, and pushed into the Levant and Nubia, turning a war of liberation into a standing policy of controlling the ground threats came from.

Watch the sequence and the causation is hard to miss. Ahmose does not stop at the border. He pursues into Canaan and takes Sharuhen. His successor Amenhotep I campaigns in Nubia. Thutmose I reaches the Euphrates and sets up a boundary stela there, a gesture with no precedent whatsoever in Egyptian history and no obvious purpose except to announce that the reach of Egyptian arms now extends to the far edge of the known world. Within three generations of the expulsion, Egypt holds a territorial empire from the fourth cataract to northern Syria, administered with garrisons and vassal treaties and hostage-taking, run by a professional army with a chariot arm. No previous Egyptian state had wanted such a thing. This one wanted it badly enough to reorganize its society around it.

The counterfactual makes the point sharper. Suppose the Thirteenth Dynasty had somehow held on, and no foreign dynasty had ever ruled at Avaris. Egypt would eventually have met the chariot anyway, since the technology was diffusing across the whole Near East. But the meeting would have been a border incident rather than an occupation, and the Egyptian response would have been what it always was: build another fort, dig another canal, restate the ideology, wait. The Middle Kingdom's answer to Nubia had been a wall. There is no reason to think its answer to Asia would have been different. What made the New Kingdom's forward policy possible is not that Egypt learned about chariots. It is that Egypt learned that walls do not work, and it learned it in the only way a confident civilization ever learns anything, which is by losing.

## Historiography: How the Hyksos Have Been Judged

The scholarly history of this period is a good short course in how ancient history gets made, unmade, and remade, and any reader who wants to argue about the Hyksos should know the shape of it.

The first stage is the Egyptian tradition itself, which begins immediately after the expulsion and runs for the rest of pharaonic history. Its features are consistent: the Hyksos ruled without Ra, the temples were neglected, the land was defiled, and a king restored order. Hatshepsut's Speos Artemidos text is the fullest surviving statement. Ramesside compositions continue the theme and add color, including a literary tale in which the Hyksos king Apepi provokes the Theban ruler with an absurd complaint about hippopotami, a story that survives in fragmentary form and is fiction rather than record. The tradition's purpose is legitimation, not documentation.

The second stage is Manetho, writing in the third century BCE under the Ptolemies, whose Egyptian history survives only in quotations preserved by later authors, chiefly Josephus, who was quoting him inside an argument of his own about Jewish antiquity. The transmission chain is therefore Egyptian tradition, filtered through a Ptolemaic priest, filtered through a first-century Jewish apologist, filtered through Christian copyists. Manetho supplies the invasion narrative, the shepherd-kings etymology, the name Salitis, and the dynastic scheme. He also supplies an identification of the expelled Hyksos with the Israelites of the Exodus, which Josephus was very interested in and which has no support in Egyptian evidence of any kind. Manetho is not worthless; his dynastic framework is still in use and some of his figures agree with contemporary documents. But he is a source about the Egyptian tradition of the Hyksos, not a source about the Hyksos.

The third stage is nineteenth and early twentieth century Egyptology, which took Manetho's narrative largely at face value and dressed it in the racial theory of its own moment. The result is the shepherd-king horde: nomads, barbarians, destroyers, a Semitic wave crashing over a civilization. That reading enters textbooks, encyclopedias, and the popular imagination, and much of what a casual reader still believes about the Hyksos comes from it.

The fourth stage is the archaeological correction, and it is essentially the work of the Tell el-Dab'a excavations and the parallel reassessment of the Levantine Middle Bronze Age. Once the Hyksos capital could be dug and stratified, the invasion narrative had to face a physical record that showed a Levantine community settling gradually over generations, a trading city rather than a garrison, continuity of Egyptian institutions, and no destruction horizon where the conquest was supposed to be. Ryholt's reconstruction of the political sequence from seals and king lists gave the period a chronology to argue about. Janine Bourriau and others reworked the pottery and the periodization. The isotope and ancient-genetic work forms a fifth strand, putting biological evidence beside the material evidence.

### Where the argument still is

Not everything has been settled by the correction, and it would be a different kind of error to swing from barbarian invasion to peaceful multicultural idyll. Real questions remain open. Whether the Fifteenth Dynasty's accession involved a coup, a war, or a slow accretion of power is not resolved by the absence of a destruction layer at Avaris, since a dynasty can be installed violently at Memphis without anyone burning its own capital. The relationship between the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Dynasties is disputed. The extent of real Hyksos authority in Middle Egypt is inferred from polemical texts and scattered seals. The reign lengths are soft. Whether the Sixteenth Dynasty was Theban is a live question. And the era's absolute chronology is tied to the wider Bronze Age dating debate, where radiocarbon results and the conventional historical chronology have not always agreed. Anyone who tells a reader the Second Intermediate Period is now understood is overstating by a wide margin. What is understood is that the old story was wrong, and the new story is a work in progress.

## The Kings of the Fifteenth Dynasty, As Far As They Can Be Known

A pillar owes its readers the roster, even when the roster is thin, and the Hyksos roster is thin in a way that itself teaches something about the period.

Six kings, a hundred and eight years: that is what the Turin King List appears to give the Fifteenth Dynasty, and it is the only figure of its kind for the whole dynasty. Manetho's transmitted version supplies six names and a longer total, and the two sets do not reconcile cleanly. Contemporary objects supply names that Manetho does not have and omit names he does. Reconciling the three bodies of evidence is a specialist industry, and no reconstruction commands universal assent.

Salitis is the first king in Manetho's list and a problem in every other respect. No contemporary Egyptian object bears the name. The Manethonian tradition credits him with taking Memphis, fortifying Avaris, and garrisoning the east, and the account is coherent enough to be interesting and late enough to be unreliable. Attempts to identify him with a king attested on seals have been made and none has stuck. He may be a real king whose Egyptian name we know under a form we have not recognized, he may be a compressed memory of several rulers, or he may be a literary construction. The honest position is that the first Hyksos king is not securely known, which is a striking gap in the record of a dynasty that ruled a third of Egypt.

Khyan is real, well attested, and the most internationally visible ruler of the period. His throne name Seuserenre appears on seals and monuments; his personal name appears on objects recovered far outside Egypt, including at Knossos and Hattusa. Recent excavation at Tell Edfu in Upper Egypt has produced sealings bearing his name in a context that has forced a reconsideration of when in the dynasty he sits and how far his authority reached, which is a good example of how a single well-stratified find can rearrange a period's internal chronology. What Khyan's material says collectively is that a Delta king had diplomatic or commercial dealings across the eastern Mediterranean and up the Nile, and that the Fifteenth Dynasty at its height was a recognized power in the Bronze Age world rather than an Egyptian anomaly.

Apepi, whose name is also written Apophis, is the best-documented Hyksos king and the one Egyptian tradition remembers, because he is the king the Theban war was fought against. He used more than one throne name across what appears to have been a long reign, which is unusual and has been read as evidence of changing circumstances or changing ambitions. His regnal year thirty-three is the date on the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, which means a reign of at least that length and gives us the one securely dated document of the whole dynasty. He appears in the Kamose material as the antagonist and in the later Ramesside tale as a stock villain. The distance between the king who patronized the copying of Middle Kingdom mathematics and the king who complains about hippopotami in a piece of Ramesside fiction is the distance between the historical Apepi and the remembered one.

Khamudi closes the sequence and is barely more than a name in the Turin King List. He is the king at Avaris when Ahmose took it, or close enough to it that the distinction does not matter to the outcome, and nothing else about him is recoverable.

That roster is what a century of rule by a dynasty of Egypt's Delta has left. Set it beside the documentation available for a comparable stretch of the Twelfth Dynasty, with its dated stelae, its papyrus archives, its temple inscriptions, and its royal annals, and the difference is not a difference in the dynasties. It is a difference in what survived and in who was doing the surviving.

### Why so little survives

Three reasons, and they compound. The Delta is wet, and papyrus does not last in wet soil, so the administrative archives that would have documented Hyksos government are simply gone in a way that Upper Egyptian archives are not. Second, the Eighteenth Dynasty had no interest in curating the memory of the regime whose expulsion justified its own existence, and a good deal of Hyksos monumental material was recut, reused, or removed. Third, the Fifteenth Dynasty seems not to have been a great monument-builder in the first place, which is what one would expect from a commercially oriented state whose capital was a port rather than a ceremonial center. Absence of evidence, in this period more than most, is evidence of absence only for the things that leave traces, and government by tariff leaves fewer traces than government by pyramid.

## The Theban Dynasties Reconstructed

The southern half of the picture has its own reconstruction problems, and they matter because the Theban house is the one that produces the New Kingdom.

The Sixteenth Dynasty's identity is the sharpest of these disputes. The older reading, following one interpretation of Manetho, made it a set of minor Hyksos vassal rulers in the Delta, effectively a shadow of the Fifteenth. The reading that most current treatments follow, argued in detail by Ryholt from the Turin King List's arrangement and from the seal evidence, makes it a Theban line ruling Upper Egypt in parallel with the Hyksos and preceding the Seventeenth. The difference is not cosmetic. On the first reading, Thebes appears as a late and rather sudden challenger. On the second, an independent Theban kingdom exists for most of the era, which is what the three-power map assumes and what the general shape of the evidence supports.

The Seventeenth Dynasty is better attested, largely because its kings were buried at Dra Abu el-Naga on the Theban west bank and because their coffins and funerary equipment have been recovered, some of it in the nineteenth century under circumstances that make provenance a headache. The dynasty's tombs are small brick-pyramid affairs with shafts, a long way from the Middle Kingdom's royal complexes and a long way from the Valley of the Kings that its successors would cut. The rishi coffin, with its enveloping feather pattern, is the dynasty's signature and one of the era's genuine artistic inventions.

The last three Seventeenth Dynasty rulers are where the era's documentation improves sharply, which is entirely a function of the war: fighting generates inscriptions. Seqenenre Tao is known from his mummy, from monuments, and from the Ramesside tale that makes him Apepi's antagonist. Kamose is known chiefly from the stelae recording his campaign, which are among the most important documents of the entire period. Ahmose, who is conventionally the first king of the Eighteenth Dynasty rather than the last of the Seventeenth, is known from a substantial body of material including the officers' autobiographies that describe his campaigns.

One detail from the Theban court deserves mention because it points forward. Royal women in the Seventeenth Dynasty and the early Eighteenth are unusually prominent in the record, holding titles, receiving substantial burials, and appearing in contexts that suggest real authority; Ahhotep, mother of Ahmose, was honored in terms that credit her with holding the country together during the fighting, and her burial equipment included honorific military decorations. The prominence of royal women in the New Kingdom, which eventually produces a female pharaoh, has roots in the Theban war generation, and that is another way the Second Intermediate Period reaches forward into the era it created.

## Why the Dates Are So Hard

A reader who has noticed how often this guide says circa deserves an explanation of why, because the chronology problem in this era is not laziness and it is not solved by looking it up in a better book.

Egyptian chronology is built by counting backward. For the historical periods, the method is to take a reign length from a king list or a dated document, add it to the next, and work back from a fixed point. The trouble is that the fixed points are few and contested, that reign lengths are frequently unknown or given differently by different sources, and that coregencies, in which a king rules alongside his successor for some years, can make two reigns overlap in ways that shorten the total without any document saying so. Every one of those problems is worse in the Second Intermediate Period, because the sources are worse.

The traditional anchors for the second millennium BCE are astronomical: records of the heliacal rising of the star Sirius and of lunar dates, which recur on a known cycle and can in principle pin a regnal year to an absolute year. The problem is that the calculation depends on where in Egypt the observation was made, since the latitude changes the date, and the texts do not always say. That ambiguity alone generates competing chronologies decades apart, and the Second Intermediate Period sits right in the zone where the disagreement bites.

Radiocarbon has entered the argument and has not settled it. Large radiocarbon programs on Egyptian material have broadly supported the conventional historical chronology for much of the pharaonic sequence, which was a significant result, but the fit is not perfect everywhere and the Second Intermediate Period is one of the places where results and conventional dates have been argued over. The volcanic eruption of Thera in the Aegean, which left a datable ash layer and pumice that traveled to Egyptian sites, ought to be the perfect cross-check between Egyptian and Aegean chronology; instead it has become one of the most acrimonious dating disputes in Bronze Age archaeology, with radiocarbon and Aegean-Egyptian synchronisms pointing at dates roughly a century apart. Where the eruption falls relative to the end of the Hyksos era is directly relevant to this period and is not agreed.

### What a careful reader should do with this

Hold the dates loosely and the sequence firmly. The order of events in the Second Intermediate Period is far better established than their absolute dates: the Thirteenth Dynasty precedes the Hyksos ascendancy, Khyan precedes Apepi, Seqenenre precedes Kamose precedes Ahmose, and the expulsion precedes the Nubian campaign. That relative sequence is what the arguments are made of and it is what an exam or an essay actually requires. The absolute figures, circa 1650 and circa 1550, are useful conventional pegs and should be presented as conventional rather than as facts. A reader who says the Second Intermediate Period ran for roughly a century in the middle of the second millennium BCE, on the conventional chronology, and who can name the sequence and the causal chain, is on firmer ground than one who recites the numbers without knowing where they came from.

## Was It a Dark Age? Testing the Charge Properly

The phrase dark age gets thrown at this period constantly, and it is worth taking apart rather than either accepting or rejecting wholesale, because the phrase means at least three different things and the era scores differently on each.

If dark age means a period from which little evidence survives, so that the historian is working in the dark, then the label fits and no one disputes it. The documentary record is genuinely poor. The Delta's soil destroys papyrus. The victors had no interest in preserving the loser's archives. Reign lengths are uncertain, king lists are damaged, and whole dynasties are known mainly from seals. Measured by what we can see, this is one of the darkest stretches in pharaonic history.

If dark age means a collapse of civilization, a loss of literacy, technology, urban life, and organized government, then the label is simply false, and the evidence against it is not marginal. Scribes were copying Middle Kingdom mathematics under Apepi. Avaris was one of the largest cities in the eastern Mediterranean world. Two organized states with royal courts and functioning administrations governed the Nile Valley. Long-distance trade reached from Nubia to Crete. Technology did not regress; it advanced sharply, adding a suite of Bronze Age innovations Egypt had previously lacked. On every measure of civilizational function, the Second Intermediate Period holds or improves.

If dark age means a period of political decline and reduced state capacity, then the label fits partially, and this is the only reading worth arguing about. Egypt was divided, monumental building shrank, the Nubian empire of the Middle Kingdom was lost, and no single authority commanded the Nile. Those are real losses and it is not revisionism to say so. What they are not is decay. A three-way division of a river valley among three functioning states is a different political condition from the fragmentation of the First Intermediate Period, when central authority genuinely dissolved into local rule and famine appears in the record. The Second Intermediate Period is a period of competing states, not of dissolution.

### The right verdict

Diminished in political scale, dark only in our ability to see it, and formative in its consequences. The one-word labels all fail because the era does three things at once: it costs Egypt its unity and its Nubian empire, it delivers the technology and the trade connections of the wider Bronze Age world, and it produces the ideological shock that turned Egyptian foreign policy inside out. A period that leaves a country divided, better armed, and permanently changed in its view of the outside world is not a gap. It is a hinge. That is what the catalyst-not-catastrophe thesis is claiming, and it is why the pillar of this cluster argues the point rather than reporting a sequence of kings.

## How This Era Differed From Egypt's First Fragmentation

Egypt had been through political breakdown before, and setting the two experiences side by side is the fastest way to see what was distinctive about this one. The comparison is orientation rather than debate, and the specialist treatment of the earlier collapse and its repair lives in its own article; the point here is the contrast.

The First Intermediate Period, which followed the Old Kingdom's fall, was a fragmentation from the inside out. Central authority at Memphis dissolved, and power fell to the provincial governors who had already been accumulating it, each running his own district with his own troops and his own granaries. The country broke into many pieces, not three, and the pieces were Egyptian. The evidence includes provincial officials boasting in their tomb inscriptions about feeding their own people during famine, which is a form of advertisement that only makes sense when the state that used to do the feeding has stopped. Whatever else it was, that era was a domestic crisis with domestic causes and domestic actors, and the outside world barely appears in it.

The Second Intermediate Period is structurally different in three ways that matter.

First, the fragmentation is coarse rather than fine. Instead of dozens of provincial power centers, there are three states, each with a capital, a court, a treasury, and a foreign policy. That is a different political condition, and it produces different behavior. Many small competitors fight small wars and make small deals. Three large ones conduct diplomacy, sign what amount to trade arrangements, and eventually fight a war of unification with real armies.

Second, one of the three is foreign in origin. That single fact changes the meaning of everything else. The First Intermediate Period ended with an Egyptian dynasty defeating other Egyptian dynasties, and the resulting ideology treated the era as a lapse in order that a proper king had corrected. The Second Intermediate Period ended with an Egyptian dynasty expelling a dynasty of foreign origin, and the resulting ideology treated the era as a defilement that a purifier had cleansed. The first story leads to restoration. The second leads to revenge, and revenge, institutionalized, is an empire.

Third, the outside world is a participant rather than a backdrop. The First Intermediate Period happened in an Egypt that was still, in its own view and largely in fact, a self-contained world. The Second happened in an Egypt embedded in a Bronze Age international system that ran from Mesopotamia to the Aegean, with trade routes, technology transfer, migration, and diplomacy moving across it constantly. Egypt did not choose to join that system. The system arrived, settled in the Delta, and eventually put a king on a throne.

### What the comparison proves about the pattern

Egyptologists sometimes present Egyptian history as a cycle: unify, flourish, fragment, reunify, repeat. The cycle model has real explanatory value and it collapses at exactly this point. After the First Intermediate Period, the reunified state that emerged was recognizably the same kind of state as the one that had fallen, more bureaucratic and more careful about provincial governors, but still an inward-facing Nile kingdom whose foreign policy consisted of fortifying its borders and extracting what it needed from beyond them. After the Second Intermediate Period, the reunified state was not the same kind of state at all. It had a standing professional army with a chariot arm, a permanent presence in Asia, an annexed Nubian province, an ideology of conquest built into the first reign, and a diplomatic correspondence with every major power of the age. The cycle did not repeat. It broke, and what it broke into is the New Kingdom.

That break is the single strongest piece of evidence for the catalyst thesis, and it is why this era deserves a pillar rather than a paragraph. If the Second Intermediate Period had been merely a gap, Egypt would have come out of it looking like the Middle Kingdom again, because that is what Egypt had always done after a gap. It came out looking like nothing that had ever existed on the Nile before. Something in those hundred years did that, and the two candidates on the table are the technology and the humiliation, which is precisely the pair this guide has argued for throughout.

### The one thing both eras share

Thebes. In both crises, the city that reunified Egypt was the same provincial southern town, ruling the same stretch of valley, worshipping the same god, burying its kings in the same necropolis, and using the same argument: that it alone held the crown in its legitimate form and that the country's division was an offense against order. The Theban house of the Seventeenth Dynasty was doing consciously what the Theban house of the Eleventh had done, and the parallel would not have been lost on them. Egyptian political culture had a template for ending a division, and Thebes owned it. What differed the second time was that Thebes had chariots.

## The Myths Worth Correcting

Several stories about this era circulate far outside the scholarship, and a reader who wants to be useful in a classroom or an exam hall should be able to dismantle them.

The Hyksos invaded with chariots and conquered Egypt in a lightning campaign. This is the composite of Manetho and nineteenth-century imagination, and it fails on the archaeology: there is no destruction horizon corresponding to a conquest, the Levantine population of the Delta predates the Fifteenth Dynasty by generations, and the chariot's arrival in Egypt is not securely attested in a military role until the fighting at the end of the period rather than at the beginning. Whatever the takeover was, it was not a blitzkrieg, and the fuller account of the mechanism belongs to that article rather than to this one.

The Hyksos destroyed Egyptian temples and suppressed the gods. This is Eighteenth Dynasty legitimation propaganda, contradicted by the continuity of Egyptian religious practice through the period and by the Fifteenth Dynasty's own patronage of Egyptian deities in Egyptian forms.

The Hyksos were the Hebrews, and the expulsion is the Exodus. This one comes from Josephus, who had an argument to make about the antiquity of his own people and found Manetho's expelled foreigners convenient. There is no Egyptian evidence connecting the Fifteenth Dynasty to the Israelites, the chronology does not align with any conventional reading of the biblical material, and the identification survives on the strength of narrative resonance rather than evidence. It should be named and set aside.

The Hyksos invented the chariot and the composite bow. They did not. Both technologies developed in the wider Near East before the Fifteenth Dynasty existed. The Hyksos are the channel, not the source.

The Second Intermediate Period was a blank century in which nothing happened. The Rhind Papyrus, the Avaris harbor, the Kamose stelae, the Kerma royal tumuli, and the entire Middle Bronze Age trade network running through the Delta constitute a rather full century, and the era's own artifacts are what refuted the claim.

Egypt threw out every foreigner and closed the door. The opposite happened. The New Kingdom that followed is the most internationally engaged state Egypt had ever been, with Levantine gods in its temples, Asiatic officials in its bureaucracy, foreign wives in its harem, diplomatic correspondence with every major power of the age, and garrisons in Canaan. The expulsion of the Hyksos did not end Egypt's entanglement with Asia. It converted Egypt from an object of that entanglement into its principal actor.

## Sources and Their Limits: What the Evidence Can and Cannot Do

Study-grade understanding of this period requires knowing not only what the sources say but what kind of claims each source can support, so this section sets out the evidence classes honestly.

The Turin King List is the backbone of the chronology and it is a wreck. It is a Ramesside papyrus, so it is already several centuries removed from the events, and it reached the modern world in fragments that were further damaged in transit, leaving scholars to reassemble a jigsaw whose pieces are papyrus scraps with partial names and numbers. Its evidence for this era is precious because it appears to count the Fifteenth Dynasty as a dynasty and give it six kings and a hundred and eight years, and it is fragile because the reading of any given fragment can be argued. Building a chronology on it is unavoidable and uncomfortable in equal measure, and the full account of what the document is and how it should be handled is in its own article.

Scarab seals are the period's most abundant class of inscribed evidence and its most treacherous. A seal bearing a royal name found at a site proves that the object reached the site. It does not prove that the king governed it, that the object arrived during his reign, or that it was not an heirloom, a trade good, or a later copy. The mapping of Hyksos authority by seal distribution is a legitimate method used carefully by specialists, and it is also the single easiest thing in this period to over-read.

The Theban war texts, chiefly the Kamose stelae and the related Carnarvon Tablet, are the closest thing to contemporary narrative the period offers, and they are royal propaganda written to justify and glorify a campaign. They are invaluable for what the Theban court wanted said, for the geography it assumed, and for the strategic picture it painted. They are not neutral reporting, and the specific claims in them about the northern king's conduct and the state of the country must be weighed as claims by an interested party.

The autobiographical tomb inscriptions of officers, above all Ahmose son of Ebana at el-Kab, are a different and unusually valuable kind of source: a participant's account written for his own funerary monument, listing the actions he fought in and the rewards he received. Such texts have their own conventions and their own boasting, but their concreteness about sequence and about the mechanics of reward makes them the best window available onto how the war was actually fought.

The archaeology of Tell el-Dab'a is the period's strongest evidence and it is one site. The temptation to generalize from Avaris to the whole Hyksos realm is powerful and should be resisted; a capital and port is not a typical settlement, and what was true of Avaris need not be true of a village in the western Delta. Other sites, including Tell el-Yahudiya with its distinctive juglets and the Middle Bronze Age towns of the southern Levant, extend the picture, but the density of evidence at Avaris makes it the fixed point around which everything is arranged, with the distortions that always follow from having one very good source and many poor ones.

Later Egyptian literary tradition, from Hatshepsut's inscription to the Ramesside Apepi tale, is evidence for Egyptian memory rather than for the period, and it should be read as a study of how a civilization narrates its own humiliation.

Manetho, at the end of a long transmission chain, is a source about the tradition. His dynastic numbers are useful, his narrative is not testimony, and his etymologies are wrong.

### Why the source problem is the interesting part

A reader who understands this period's evidence understands something general about ancient history that no amount of reading about better-documented eras will teach. The Second Intermediate Period is the case where the written tradition and the material record point in opposite directions, and the historian has to decide which to believe and why. The answer that the field has reached, which is to trust the stratigraphy over the polemic while acknowledging that the stratigraphy cannot answer political questions, is a methodological position with consequences well beyond the Hyksos. It is also, for a student, the most transferable thing in the whole cluster. If a reader can explain why the burned cities of Manetho's account are absent from the ground at Avaris and what that absence does and does not prove, they can handle almost any conflict between a text and a trench.

## The Era's Place in the Whole Arc of Egyptian History

Step back far enough and the Second Intermediate Period sits at a very specific joint in the long story, and seeing the joint clearly is what a pillar is for.

Egyptian history to this point had a rhythm: unify, build, centralize, overreach or ossify, fragment, reunify. The Old Kingdom built the pyramids and collapsed, for reasons worked through in [the account of why the Old Kingdom collapsed](/2001/08/01/why-the-old-kingdom-collapsed/). The First Intermediate Period fragmented the country among local rulers until Thebes reunified it. The Middle Kingdom rebuilt the state on a more bureaucratic model, pushed into Nubia, and then dissolved through succession failure. Each cycle had been internal. Egypt's crises had been Egyptian crises, produced by Egyptian causes, resolved by Egyptian actors, and the outside world had figured in them mainly as a source of raw materials and a supplier of labor.

The Second Intermediate Period breaks the pattern by importing the crisis. For the first time, the question of who ruled Egypt was answered by a dynasty whose origins lay outside it, and the answer stood for a century. That is a categorical change, and Egypt's response to it is a categorical change too. The reunification of 1550 BCE does not restore the Middle Kingdom. It produces something new: a militarized, outward-facing, imperial state that treats the Levant as a zone to be controlled rather than a place to trade with, and that treats Nubia as a province rather than a frontier.

Everything downstream carries the mark. The New Kingdom's empire is the Second Intermediate Period's answer. The prominence of the army and its generals in Egyptian politics, which eventually puts military men on the throne, begins here. The wealth of Amun's priesthood at Thebes, which becomes a political problem large enough to shape the end of the New Kingdom, is built on the tribute and gold of an empire that exists because of this era. Even the Amarna revolution, several centuries later, plays out in a religious landscape that this period's internationalization helped create. And when foreign rule returns to Egypt, again and again, from the Kushites to the Persians to the Ptolemies to the Romans to the Arabs to the Ottomans, the Hyksos are the precedent every Egyptian account reaches for, which is why the question of foreign rule and Egyptian identity, handled across this cluster in [the study of foreign rule and Egyptian identity](/2002/12/01/foreign-rule-and-egyptian-identity/), starts here.

### The study angle

For a student or an exam candidate, this era rewards a specific kind of preparation, because the questions that get asked about it are almost never questions of fact. Nobody asks what year Khyan acceded. The questions are analytical: why did the Middle Kingdom fall, what did the Hyksos contribute, why was the New Kingdom imperial when its predecessors were not, was the Second Intermediate Period a decline. Every one of those wants a causal argument with evidence attached, and every one of them can be answered from the material in this guide if the material is held as a structure rather than as a list of names.

The structure worth holding is the three-power map, the two mechanisms of the catalyst thesis, and the source problem. Given those three, a candidate can build an answer to almost any question this period generates. Given only a list of Hyksos kings, they can answer none of them. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Second Intermediate Period?

The Second Intermediate Period is the modern name for the stretch of Egyptian history, running circa 1650 to 1550 BCE on the conventional chronology, when Egypt was not a single kingdom. Central authority inherited from the Middle Kingdom failed, and the Nile Valley was divided among three powers: a dynasty of West Semitic origin known as the Hyksos ruling the Delta from Avaris, an Egyptian dynasty ruling Upper Egypt from Thebes, and the kingdom of Kush ruling Nubia from Kerma. Egyptologists call it intermediate because it sits between the Middle Kingdom and the New Kingdom, the two eras of unified rule on either side. The period ended when the Theban king Ahmose took Avaris and reunified the country, opening the Eighteenth Dynasty and the New Kingdom.

### Q: Which dynasties made up the Second Intermediate Period?

Manetho's scheme assigns Dynasties Thirteen through Seventeen to this era, but the numbers are not a sequence. The Thirteenth Dynasty is the fading Middle Kingdom state at Itjtawy, which produced roughly fifty short-reigned kings. The Fourteenth is a line of Delta rulers with West Semitic names. The Fifteenth is the Hyksos proper, six great kings ruling from Avaris. The Sixteenth is most likely a Theban line, though an older reading made it a set of minor Delta rulers. The Seventeenth is the Theban dynasty whose last kings fought the war of expulsion. The Fifteenth, Sixteenth, and Seventeenth are contemporaries, not successors, so reading the numbers as a straight line makes the same century happen three times.

### Q: Why is the Second Intermediate Period called intermediate?

The label is a modern convenience with no ancient counterpart. Egyptologists reserve the term kingdom for the eras when one king ruled the whole Nile Valley through a single administration, and they call the gaps between those eras intermediate periods, numbering them in order. The First Intermediate Period sits between the Old and Middle Kingdoms; the Second sits between the Middle and New; the Third follows the New Kingdom. The word describes political fragmentation only. It does not mean the century was less civilized, less literate, or less productive, and the archaeological record of this particular era argues strongly against reading the label that way. No Egyptian scribe ever thought of himself as living in an intermediate age.

### Q: Who were the Hyksos rulers of Egypt?

The Hyksos rulers were the kings of the Fifteenth Dynasty, a line of West Semitic origin that governed the Egyptian Delta from Avaris for roughly a century. The Turin King List gives them six kings and a hundred and eight years. Only two are well attested. Khyan, who used the throne name Seuserenre, left objects bearing his name as far afield as Crete and Anatolia, which indicates a ruler with real standing in the wider Bronze Age world. Apepi, also written Apophis, reigned long enough that a mathematical papyrus is dated to his year thirty-three, and he is the king the Theban war was fought against. Manetho's tradition names a first king, Salitis, whom no contemporary Egyptian object confirms. A last king, Khamudi, is essentially a name in a king list.

### Q: What does the name Hyksos mean?

Hyksos is a Greek rendering of the Egyptian phrase heqau khasut, meaning rulers of foreign lands. It is a title describing an office and a geography, not the name of a people. Egyptian scribes had used the phrase for centuries before the Fifteenth Dynasty existed, applying it to chieftains and princes in Nubia, Libya, and the Levant. Greek writers heard the phrase as an ethnic label and turned a job description into a nation. Josephus, quoting Manetho, added a false etymology in which the syllables meant king and shepherd, producing the shepherd kings of nineteenth-century books and the nomadic-horde imagery that still circulates. The mistranslation is the single most consequential error in the popular understanding of this period.

### Q: Where did the Hyksos come from?

The convergent evidence points to the West Semitic-speaking world of the Levant and, crucially, to the eastern Delta itself. Personal names in the ruling house are West Semitic. The god they promoted was the Egyptian Set identified with the Levantine storm god Baal. The material culture at their capital includes Canaanite pottery, Levantine weapon types, Syro-Palestinian house plans, intramural burials, and donkey burials with clear Levantine parallels and no Egyptian ones. Strontium isotope work on teeth from the site has found that many sampled individuals grew up locally, with non-local individuals disproportionately female, a pattern fitting a resident community established for generations. Whether that community is best described as invaders from outside or immigrants who rose from within is a genuine scholarly debate with its own dedicated treatment in this series.

### Q: What was the Hyksos capital?

Avaris, identified with the site of Tell el-Dab'a in the northeastern Delta and excavated over decades by an Austrian team under Manfred Bietak. The excavation transformed the period, because it replaced a textual argument with a stratified physical record. What emerged was not a fortress of conquest but a port: a settlement founded in the Twelfth Dynasty that gradually took on Levantine character, then grew into one of the largest cities in the eastern Mediterranean world, with a harbor on a branch of the Nile and imported material reaching from Canaan and Cyprus to the Aegean. Avaris sat where the sea route and the land route to Asia met the Nile, and controlling that junction, rather than controlling territory, was the foundation of Hyksos power.

### Q: How long did the Hyksos rule Egypt?

Roughly a century, and the Turin King List's figure of a hundred and eight years for the Fifteenth Dynasty is the closest thing to a hard number the period offers. On the conventional chronology that places their rule at approximately 1650 to 1550 BCE, though both endpoints carry real uncertainty because Egyptian chronology before the first millennium BCE is a reconstruction built from damaged king lists and contested astronomical calculations. It is also worth being precise about what ruled means. The Hyksos governed the Delta directly, held authority around Memphis, and claimed influence south to around Cusae in Middle Egypt, while an independent Theban kingdom held Upper Egypt throughout. They never ruled the whole country.

### Q: What came before the Hyksos took power?

The Middle Kingdom, and then its long unraveling. The Twelfth Dynasty had built the most administratively sophisticated Egyptian state that had yet existed, with a professional bureaucracy, a fortress chain holding Nubia, and a literary golden age. It ended around 1802 BCE, and the Thirteenth Dynasty that followed kept the institutions running while cycling through roughly fifty kings, many reigning only months. No ruler could build a lasting power base, and the crown's grip on distant provinces loosened. Meanwhile the eastern Delta had been absorbing a large Levantine population for two centuries, brought in by Egypt's own trade, campaigns, and labor imports. Local rulers with West Semitic names emerge there before the Fifteenth Dynasty exists. The Hyksos stepped into a political vacancy that had been widening for generations.

### Q: What were the Hyksos known for?

For being the first foreign dynasty to rule any part of Egypt, and for what they brought with them. Their capital was the wealthiest trading port in the region, plugged into networks reaching Canaan, Cyprus, Syria, and the Aegean. Through the Delta they held came a package of Middle Bronze Age technologies Egypt had lacked: the horse-drawn chariot, the composite bow, improved bronze metallurgy, the sickle-shaped khopesh sword, better armor, and advanced fortification engineering, along with civilian imports including new looms, new musical instruments, olives, pomegranates, and zebu cattle. They are also known for the story told about them afterward, in which they burned the temples and defiled the land, which is Eighteenth Dynasty propaganda rather than a record of what happened.

### Q: Who ruled southern Egypt while the Hyksos held the Delta?

A Theban dynasty, most likely the Sixteenth followed by the Seventeenth, ruling Upper Egypt from roughly the Abydos region south to Elephantine at the first cataract. It was the poorer of the two Egyptian states, cut off from Mediterranean trade and therefore from imported copper, tin, silver, and timber, with modest royal tombs at Dra Abu el-Naga and a conservative art style. It was also the more legitimate in Egyptian terms, ruling from the city of Amun with the traditional rites intact, and it held the same ground from which Thebes had reunified Egypt once before after the First Intermediate Period. Beyond Elephantine, the kingdom of Kush held Nubia from Kerma, which meant Thebes sat between two rival powers with reason to cooperate.

### Q: Was Egypt ever united during the Second Intermediate Period?

Not politically, at any point between the Thirteenth Dynasty's collapse and Ahmose's victory. What survived was the idea of unity. Both the Theban and the Hyksos kings used the full titulary of a king of Upper and Lower Egypt, each claiming a whole country neither controlled. That was not scribal carelessness. Egyptian royal ideology had no vocabulary for a legitimate partition: a king was king of the Two Lands or he was a pretender. Because neither side would concede the title, the logic pointed toward a fight from the beginning, even through the long decades when nothing happened militarily and grain moved south while gold and manufactures moved north. The war at the end of the period was the ideology finally cashing its claim.

### Q: Was the Second Intermediate Period a dark age for Egypt?

It depends entirely on what the phrase means. If it means an era we can barely see, the label fits, because the documentary record is genuinely poor: Delta soil destroys papyrus, the victors preserved nothing of the losers, and whole dynasties are known mainly from seals. If it means a collapse of civilization, the label is false. Scribes copied Middle Kingdom mathematics under Apepi, Avaris was among the largest cities in its world, two organized states governed the valley, trade reached from Nubia to Crete, and technology advanced sharply rather than regressing. If it means political decline, the label fits partially: Egypt lost its unity and its Nubian empire, and monumental building shrank. The fair verdict is diminished in political scale, dark only in our visibility, and formative in consequence.

### Q: How did the Hyksos period fit into Egyptian history?

It sits at the joint where Egypt's rhythm changes. Every earlier crisis had been internal: Egyptian causes, Egyptian actors, Egyptian resolutions, with the outside world figuring mainly as a source of materials and labor. The Second Intermediate Period imported the crisis, answering the question of who ruled Egypt with a dynasty whose origins lay outside it, and the answer stood for a century. The reunification that followed did not restore the Middle Kingdom. It produced something categorically new: a militarized, outward-facing imperial state that treated the Levant as ground to control and Nubia as a province to annex. Everything downstream carries the mark, from the political weight of the army and the wealth of Amun's priesthood to the way every later episode of foreign rule was narrated against this precedent.

### Q: Why is the Second Intermediate Period considered a turning point for Egypt?

Because of what this series calls the catalyst-not-catastrophe thesis: the era delivered both the military technology and the ideological wound that together produced the New Kingdom empire. The technology half is straightforward, since the chariot, composite bow, and improved bronze all entered Egypt through this century of contact and foreign rule. The psychological half is the load-bearing one. Egyptian ideology had held for a thousand years that Egypt was the ordered world and everything outside it was chaos held at bay. A foreign dynasty wearing the crown for a century falsified that proposition in the most humiliating way available. An ideology that has been falsified must be abandoned or defended by force, and Egypt chose force, building the permanent army and forward policy that reached the Euphrates within three generations.

### Q: What should students remember about the Hyksos?

Three structures, not a list of names. First, the three-power map: Hyksos at Avaris in the Delta, Thebans in Upper Egypt, Kushites at Kerma in Nubia, each a functioning state with a capital and a foreign policy. Second, the two mechanisms of the catalyst thesis: the technology transfer and the ideological wound, working together to produce an imperial successor state. Third, the source problem: the written tradition says invasion and defilement, the archaeology at Avaris says gradual settlement and a trading city, and knowing why the field trusts the stratigraphy over the polemic is the most transferable lesson in the cluster. Exam questions about this era are almost never factual. They ask for causal arguments, and these three structures answer nearly all of them.
