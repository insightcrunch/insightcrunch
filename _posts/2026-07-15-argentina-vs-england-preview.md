Two records meet in Atlanta and only one of them survives the night. Argentina have played five World Cup semi-finals and lost none of them. England have not reached a World Cup final since the afternoon they won the thing in 1966, sixty years ago this month. The Argentina vs England World Cup 2026 semi-final is the fixture that breaks one of those streaks, and the reason this preview is not simply another rehearsal of the Hand of God is that the history here is not decoration. It is evidence. Five times these two have met at a World Cup, and not once has the better team over ninety minutes been the team that went through. Every chapter turned on a single moment: a dismissal nobody could translate, a hand nobody saw, a kick nobody needed to throw, a penalty nobody expected to be awarded. That is the pattern, and both of these sides have spent the last month proving it still applies.

Call it the moment rule, and it is the spine of this piece. In five previous World Cup meetings between these nations, four were settled by a single goal or a penalty shootout, and none produced more than four goals in total. The margin has never been comfortable and the deciding act has never been a performance. It has always been an incident, usually late, usually contested. Now look at how the two teams arrived here. Lionel Scaloni's holders have trailed, drawn, or been dragged to extra time in every knockout round they have played, and they have answered each time inside the closing twenty minutes. Thomas Tuchel's side have come from behind in the Round of 32, survived with ten men in the Round of 16, and needed the ninety-third minute of a quarter-final to separate themselves from Norway. Two teams whose shared history says the last twenty minutes decide it, both of whom have spent six weeks winning football matches in the last twenty minutes. That is the question Atlanta poses, and everything below is built around it.

## What Argentina vs England Means at World Cup 2026

This is Match 102 of 104, the second semi-final of the tournament, and it is the last of the eight matches Atlanta Stadium has staged across World Cup 2026. The winner goes to New York and New Jersey on Sunday July 19 to play Spain for the trophy. The loser goes to Miami on Saturday July 18 to play France for third place. Neither side has the luxury of a second leg or a group-stage cushion. Ninety minutes, then thirty more if required, then twelve yards.

The first semi-final resolved itself on Tuesday in Dallas, where Spain beat France 2-0 and produced what most observers in the stadium regarded as the most complete performance any team has managed at this tournament. That result matters here for two reasons beyond the obvious. It means the winner in Atlanta gets four days of recovery before the final rather than three, which is a genuine advantage in a tournament where both of tonight's teams have already been taken past ninety minutes. It also means that if England get through, the final becomes a rematch of the Euro 2024 final, which Spain won. Tuchel's players do not need that pointed out to them.

For Argentina the stakes are historical in a way that goes beyond one trophy. No nation has retained the World Cup since Brazil did it in 1962. Italy managed it in 1934 and 1938, Brazil in 1958 and 1962, and nobody in the sixty-four years since. Argentina are two wins from joining that list. A victory tonight would also put them in a fourth consecutive major final, following the Copa America title of 2021, the World Cup of 2022, and the Copa America of 2024, a run of tournament consistency no other nation in the modern game can match. They arrived in North America ranked first in the world and have not lost a match here.

For England the stakes are simpler and heavier. Sixty years without a World Cup final. Sixty years without a major men's trophy of any description. This is their fourth World Cup semi-final, after 1966, 1990 and 2018, and their second in the last three editions of the tournament. They have twice been within one match of a European title in the last five years and lost both finals. The squad knows the shape of this particular disappointment better than any group England have sent to a tournament, which is the argument both for and against them: scar tissue is experience, and it is also scar tissue.

The other frame worth holding is that this is a knockout tie between the two teams that have most consistently refused to be finished off. Neither has lost. Neither has been comfortable. ## The Road to Atlanta: How Both Sides Reached the World Cup 2026 Semi-Finals

### How did Argentina and England reach the World Cup 2026 semi-finals?

Argentina won Group J with a perfect nine points, then survived Cape Verde in extra time, came from two goals down to beat Egypt, and outlasted ten-man Switzerland in extra time. England topped Group L with seven points, came from behind against DR Congo, won 3-2 at the Azteca with ten men, and beat Norway in extra time in Miami.

That is the summary. The detail is where the case for each side lives, because the two routes look similar in the results column and are not remotely similar in what they demanded.

Argentina's group stage was the most straightforward passage either team has had. Three wins, eight goals scored, one conceded: 3-0 against Algeria in Kansas City in the opener, 2-0 against Austria in Dallas, and 3-1 against Jordan to finish. Nine points, a goal difference of plus seven, and a first place that was never seriously threatened. It was the last time the holders looked like the top-ranked team in the tournament rather than a side improvising its way forward.

The knockout rounds have been a different competition entirely. In the Round of 32 in Miami, Cape Verde, the only tournament debutant to reach the knockouts and a side that had gone through the group stage unbeaten, took the champions to extra time before losing 3-2. Scaloni said afterward that the Cape Verdeans' defensive discipline had forced his team into a hundred and twenty minutes they had not planned for, which was true and was also the first hint of the pattern.

Then came Atlanta on July 7 and the match that will define how this Argentina team is remembered regardless of what happens tonight. Egypt led 2-0 with eleven minutes left. Mostafa Shobeir had saved a Messi penalty and then kept out Mac Allister and Alvarez before half-time. Egypt's second goal had a predecessor chalked off by VAR, a decision that produced a formal complaint from the Egyptian federation about the officiating and a fortnight of argument that has not fully subsided. And then Argentina scored three goals in the closing eleven minutes and stoppage time: Cristian Romero heading in a Messi cross in the 79th minute, Messi himself rifling in a first-time finish four minutes later for a record-extending 21st World Cup goal, and Enzo Fernandez completing it deep in added time. The full account of that night sits in our [Argentina vs Egypt preview from the Round of 16](/2026/07/07/argentina-vs-egypt-preview/), and the short version is that a team that had been comprehensively second best for seventy-nine minutes won anyway.

The quarter-final in Kansas City followed the same script with different handwriting. Mac Allister headed in a Messi corner after ten minutes, which was Messi's tenth career World Cup assist and, in a detail that says something about how long he has been doing this, his tenth to ten different players. Argentina then could not add to it. Dan Ndoye equalized in the 67th minute, slipping a finish under Emiliano Martinez. Switzerland went down to ten men in the 72nd, a dismissal that generated its own argument about proportionality, and still Argentina could not break them in regulation. It took a Julian Alvarez strike in the 112th minute and a Lautaro Martinez finish in the first minute of added time at the end of extra time to make it 3-1. We covered the shape of that tie in the [Argentina vs Switzerland quarter-final preview](/2026/07/11/argentina-vs-switzerland-preview/), and the number that carries forward is this: Argentina have played three hundred and thirty minutes of knockout football to get here.

England's group was less serene than the points suggest. They opened by beating Croatia 4-2 in Dallas, a result that flattered nobody's defense, then drew 0-0 with Ghana in Boston in the flattest ninety minutes of their tournament, then beat Panama 2-0 at MetLife to take the group with seven points and six goals.

The Round of 32 at this same Atlanta ground was the first real scare. Brian Cipenga put DR Congo ahead in the seventh minute. Lionel Mpasi made four first-half saves. Marcus Rashford had a shot cleared off the line, England were denied a penalty appeal, and Yoane Wissa hit the post before the break with a chance to make it 2-0. Then Harry Kane headed in a chipped Anthony Gordon cross in the 75th minute and struck the winner shortly after, and England were through 2-1 having been outplayed for an hour.

The Round of 16 was the best thing they have done. At the Azteca, at altitude, against a co-host with an entire country in the stands, England went two ahead through Jude Bellingham, conceded to Julian Quinones, lost Jarell Quansah to a red card, made it 3-1 through a Kane penalty, conceded a penalty that Raul Jimenez converted, and held on with ten men for a 3-2 win. Our [Mexico vs England Round of 16 preview](/2026/07/05/mexico-vs-england-preview/) set that one up as a test of temperament, and England passed it in the least comfortable way available.

The quarter-final in Miami against Norway was the fourth consecutive knockout match in which England trailed or were level deep into the game. Norway scored first. Bellingham equalized just before half-time and completed his brace in the third minute of extra time to win it 2-1. Erling Haaland's side, who had knocked out Brazil in the previous round, were held to four shots on target. The [Norway vs England quarter-final preview](/2026/07/11/norway-vs-england-preview/) framed that tie around whether England could contain Haaland without surrendering the ball, and the answer, narrowly, was yes. Three hundred minutes of knockout football, thirty fewer than Argentina.

## The Rivalry: Sixty-Four Years of Argentina vs England at the World Cup

### What is the World Cup history between Argentina and England?

Tonight is their sixth World Cup meeting. England have won three of the previous five, in 1962, 1966 and 2002. Argentina won the 1986 quarter-final and advanced from the 1998 last-16 tie on penalties after a 2-2 draw. Across all fourteen official matches between the nations, England lead with six wins to three, with five draws.

Those numbers are the frame. The content is stranger and more useful than the numbers suggest, because the five World Cup meetings share a structure that has almost nothing to do with which team played better.

The first came in Chile on June 2, 1962, a group game in Rancagua. Ron Flowers opened from the penalty spot, Bobby Charlton and Jimmy Greaves added the others, Jose Sanfilippo pulled one back, and Walter Winterbottom's England won 3-1. The consequence was that Argentina went out before the knockout rounds. There was no particular edge to it yet. The rivalry as a rivalry did not exist; two good teams played a football match and one of them lost.

Four years later at Wembley, on July 23, 1966, it acquired the edge it has never lost. The quarter-final is remembered in Argentina as el robo del siglo, the theft of the century. Antonio Rattin, the Argentina captain, was sent off by a German referee whose instructions he reportedly could not understand, and refused to leave the field for long enough that the match effectively stopped. Geoff Hurst scored the only goal, disputed at the time for offside. Afterward Alf Ramsey physically prevented his players from swapping shirts and, in the press conference, described the opposition in terms that were reported around the world and have never been forgotten in Buenos Aires. England went on to win the tournament. The incident is also the reason yellow and red cards exist: the communication failure that night pushed FIFA to introduce them at the next World Cup, which is a genuinely remarkable footnote for a fixture to own.

June 22, 1986, the Azteca, is the most famous ninety minutes in the fixture's history and among the most famous in the sport's. It was played four years after Britain and Argentina fought a war over the Falkland Islands, and the atmosphere in Mexico City reflected that; there was fighting between supporters in the streets and inside the ground. The football was goalless and abrasive for forty-five minutes. Then, six minutes into the second half, Diego Maradona pushed a looping Steve Hodge clearance past Peter Shilton with his hand and Ali Bin Nasser allowed it. Four minutes later Maradona collected the ball inside his own half and ran through most of the England team to score the goal that has been voted the best in the tournament's history. Gary Lineker pulled one back late. Argentina won 2-1, went on to lift the trophy, and Maradona later described the win as revenge, while also making a point of praising the English players for not resorting to violence.

Saint-Etienne on June 30, 1998 produced four goals in the first forty-five minutes and then a slow, agonizing collapse. Gabriel Batistuta scored an early penalty, Alan Shearer equalized from the spot, Michael Owen at eighteen ran from halfway and scored the goal that made his career, and Javier Zanetti finished a free-kick routine in first-half stoppage time. Early in the second half David Beckham, on the floor, kicked out at Diego Simeone and was sent off. England played the rest of the tie and thirty minutes of extra time with ten men and did not concede. Carlos Roa saved from David Batty in the shootout, Paul Ince had already missed, and Argentina went through 4-3. One English tabloid ran a front page the next morning that reduced the entire match to Beckham's dismissal, and it took him four years to answer it.

He answered it in Sapporo on June 7, 2002. Mauricio Pochettino brought down Michael Owen, Beckham took the penalty, and England won 1-0 in a group match played, unusually for this fixture, in a good spirit. The result knocked Argentina, who had been among the pre-tournament favorites, out in the first round. It remains the last competitive fixture between the countries. They met once more, in a friendly in November 2005, and then not at all for twenty-one years, which is part of why tonight carries the weight it does. This is a rivalry that is famous out of all proportion to how often it is actually contested.

### Why the political history is present without being the story

The Falklands conflict of 1982 is the reason this fixture is different from England against Germany or Argentina against Brazil, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Sovereignty over the islands remains disputed. In the days before this match an Argentine vice president posted about the war in terms that made international news, and security in Atlanta has been raised beyond the normal level for a semi-final, with the FBI classifying the fixture as the highest-risk match of the tournament. Ticket resale prices have gone to numbers that no other match here has produced.

Both managers have been asked about it repeatedly and both have declined to feed it. Scaloni, asked directly about the rivalry framing, kept returning to the same formulation. "The truth is, this is a football match," he said on Tuesday, adding that he could not mix the two things out of respect for what happened so many years ago and describing that period as a sad one in his country's history. Tuchel took a similar line from the other side, acknowledging that a fixture producing this many iconic moments cannot honestly be called just another match while insisting that a coach's job is to treat it as exactly that and focus on what he can influence. He also noted, correctly, that the two shirts are among the few in world football that are recognized instantly anywhere.

That is roughly where the sensible ground sits. The history is real, it is present in the stadium, and it is not a tactical variable. Neither of these squads was alive in 1982. Messi was not born until 1987, a year after Maradona's two goals. Bellingham and Gordon were born after the last competitive meeting. What the history does is raise the temperature and the stakes of every decision the referee makes, which is a practical consideration rather than a romantic one, and it is worth noting that the officiating crew tonight is itself a first: Ismail Elfath becomes the first American man to referee a World Cup semi-final, leading the first all-American crew appointed to one.

## The Moment Rule: Why This Fixture Is Never Decided by the Better Team

Here is the claim this preview is prepared to defend. Argentina against England at a World Cup has never been decided by superiority over ninety minutes. It has been decided, five times out of five, by a single moment, and in four of those five the moment arrived when the match was level or within one goal. That is not a mystical property of the fixture. It is a consequence of two things that are both true again tonight: these teams are close enough in quality that the game stays tight, and they are both temperamentally inclined to make the game about incidents.

The evidence is in the scorelines. Five meetings have produced thirteen goals between them, an average of 2.6 per match. The 1966 and 2002 ties finished 1-0. The 1986 quarter-final was 2-1. The 1998 tie was 2-2 and went to penalties. Only 1962, at 3-1, has ever been decided by more than one goal, and even that was a group game rather than a knockout. No meeting has produced more than four goals. If you are looking for a reason to expect a low-scoring, tense, incident-driven ninety minutes, the fixture's entire recorded history is that reason.

The second half of the claim is the part that makes it useful tonight rather than merely interesting. Both of these teams have spent World Cup 2026 living in the last twenty minutes.

Take Argentina first, because their case is the more extreme. Going into this semi-final the holders have scored nine goals after the 75th minute at this tournament, a figure the Fox broadcast has been leaning on all week and one that survives inspection. The Egypt comeback was three goals in the last eleven minutes and stoppage time. The Switzerland quarter-final was settled in the 112th and the 121st. The Cape Verde tie required extra time. This is not a team that controls matches and closes them out; it is a team that endures a match and then produces something in the window where opponents' legs and concentration fail. Whether that is a virtue or an alarming habit is a legitimate argument, and the honest answer is that at four consecutive knockout rounds it has stopped being luck and started being a characteristic.

Now England. Kane's equalizer against DR Congo came in the 75th minute and the winner followed. The Mexico tie was won by holding a lead with ten men for most of a second half at altitude. Bellingham's winner against Norway arrived in the 93rd minute. Tuchel's side have trailed in every knockout match they have played and won every one. Their late-game record is not built on the same mechanism as Argentina's, which is worth being precise about: Argentina produce goals late because Messi finds a pass that was not there twenty minutes earlier, while England produce them because Kane and Bellingham are ruthless in the moments when a game finally opens up. Bellingham and Kane have scored twelve of England's thirteen goals at this tournament, which is either concentration of quality or dangerous dependence, and probably both.

So the moment rule, applied: whoever owns the final twenty minutes owns the final. Not whoever controls possession, not whoever wins the expected-goals count, not whoever plays better between the tenth minute and the seventieth. The team that is still standing at 0-0 or 1-1 with twenty minutes left and has one more moment in it goes to New York. Everything tactical below is really an argument about which side is better equipped to reach that window in a state to use it.

## Team News, Selection Calls and the Predicted Lineups

Both managers arrive with fewer problems than the mileage suggests, which is itself a small surprise given that between them these squads have played two hundred and ten minutes of extra time in eleven days.

### What is England's likely lineup for the semi-final against Argentina?

England should line up 4-2-3-1 with Jordan Pickford in goal, Declan Rice and Elliot Anderson screening, Jude Bellingham behind Harry Kane, and Anthony Gordon on the left. Rice is confirmed fit after illness. Jordan Henderson is out with a broken wrist and Jarell Quansah is suspended. The live calls are right-back and the right-sided attacker.

Tuchel confirmed on Tuesday in Atlanta that he has a fully fit squad other than the two absentees, which is more than he could say a week ago. Henderson's wrist injury, sustained in Mexico City, required surgery and has ended his tournament. Quansah serves the second and final game of the two-match ban that followed his red card at the Azteca, which means he is available for the final or the third-place match but not for this. Everyone else trained.

The most significant piece of team news is Rice. He was withdrawn at half-time against Norway having been hit by a stomach illness in the build-up, and his absence for the second half and extra time was visible in how England's midfield lost its shape. He trained fully on Monday in Kansas City, his first complete session since the quarter-final, and Tuchel confirmed he starts. That matters more than any other selection here, and the tactical section below explains why.

The back four contains the tournament's most persistent English question. Nico O'Reilly has effectively owned the left side of the defense since the group stage. The right has moved around: Ezri Konsa deputized there against Norway, and both Reece James and Djed Spence are fit and pressing. Tuchel has options he does not entirely trust, which is the problem. Marc Guehi and John Stones are the likely central pairing, with Konsa able to slide inside if Tuchel prefers a natural full-back on the right. The reason this is not a footnote is that Argentina's best player spends his afternoons in exactly the zone England's right-back has to defend.

The wide areas are the other live debate. Gordon has been the most productive winger in the squad with three assists and has held the left. On the right, Bukayo Saka has started three of the last four and also has three assists, while Noni Madueke started three of the first four without registering a goal contribution and was replaced by Saka at half-time against Norway. Marcus Rashford scored in the opening win over Croatia and has not contributed since. Morgan Rogers is the wildcard, and a genuinely live one: he offers more central drift and more defensive work than Madueke, which is a specific answer to a specific problem in this fixture. Tuchel's recent selections suggest he backs whoever is in form rather than whoever has the reputation, so a Rogers start would not be a surprise.

The England eleven this preview expects, then: Pickford; James, Stones, Guehi, Spence or O'Reilly; Rice, Anderson; Rogers or Saka, Bellingham, Gordon; Kane. Kane earns his 121st cap tonight, which takes him past Wayne Rooney as the most-capped outfield player England have ever had. Only Peter Shilton, on 125, sits ahead of him overall, and Shilton was the goalkeeper Maradona beat with his hand.

### What is Argentina's predicted starting eleven against England?

Scaloni named an unchanged side for the quarter-final and there is little reason to think he tears it up now. Argentina should start Emiliano Martinez behind a back four of Nahuel Molina, Cristian Romero, Lisandro Martinez and Nicolas Tagliafico, with Leandro Paredes screening, Rodrigo De Paul, Enzo Fernandez and Alexis Mac Allister ahead of him, and Messi alongside a striker.

The Argentina team news is fitness rather than availability. Romero was substituted at half-time of extra time in Kansas City through exhaustion rather than injury and was to be assessed before kickoff; Facundo Medina is pushing to start alongside Lisandro Martinez if Romero is short. Molina and Tagliafico were both replaced before the end of extra time and are considered fresh enough to start. There are no reported injuries of consequence. Reports out of Argentina suggested Scaloni trialled a back three in Monday's session, but a wholesale structural change for a semi-final of this size is unlikely, and Scaloni's record is one of small adjustments rather than reinventions.

The genuine call is who partners Messi. Lautaro Martinez started the first four matches of the tournament; Scaloni has preferred Julian Alvarez in the last two. Both scored in extra time against Switzerland, and Lautaro has a goal and an assist across his last two appearances from the bench, which is an argument for keeping him as the substitute rather than promoting him. Alvarez's finish in Kansas City, a strike from distance after an individual run, was the single best piece of football any Argentine produced in that match. Jose Manuel Lopez, the tall centre-forward who set up that goal, is the late option if Argentina need a target man. Thiago Almada and Nico Gonzalez both impressed as substitutes and give Scaloni ways to change the shape of the game without changing its plan.

The midfield is where the age question lives. Paredes, De Paul, Fernandez and Mac Allister have played together for years and read each other without looking, and they are also, collectively, the reason Argentina have looked heavy in second halves. Paredes has been the best of them through the knockout rounds, which is not what anybody predicted a month ago. The trade-off is explicit: this is a group that will not lose the ball in dangerous areas and will not run with England's midfield for ninety minutes either.

The Argentina eleven this preview expects: Emiliano Martinez; Molina, Romero, Lisandro Martinez, Tagliafico; Paredes; De Paul, Fernandez, Mac Allister; Messi, Alvarez. That is a 4-4-2 with a diamond in the middle, which is what it looks like on a team sheet, and something closer to a 4-3-3 with Messi free when Argentina have the ball.

## The Tactical Battle: Four Questions That Decide Atlanta

### What is the key tactical battle in Argentina vs England?

The tie turns on whether England can deny Messi the right half-space between their left-back and their left centre-back without pulling their own shape apart. Everything Argentina do bends toward getting him the ball there facing forward. Everything England do defensively has to account for it while still leaving enough structure to punish a slow, ageing midfield.

That is the headline. Beneath it there are four separable questions, and the outcome is the sum of them.

### Question one: the space Messi lives in

Tuchel has been unusually candid about this. He said publicly that he had considered man-marking Messi with a dedicated shadow, and that everyone knows the areas he wants to appear in. He also said something more revealing about why that is harder than it sounds: that when you analyze the matches you feel as though Messi sees things earlier than anyone else on the pitch, that England had identified patterns in Argentina's play, and that if you close those patterns he simply finds a new one and creates it. That is an honest scouting report from a coach who has clearly watched a great deal of tape and does not have a clean answer.

The specifics matter. Messi at thirty-nine does not press, does not track, and does not run channels. What he does is start high and wide on the right, drift inward into the space between an opposing left-back and left centre-back, and receive the ball facing the goal with his head already up. That zone is the single most dangerous piece of real estate in this match. England's left-back, whether that is O'Reilly or Spence, cannot follow him inside without opening the touchline for Molina's overlap. England's left centre-back cannot step out to meet him without leaving Alvarez a straight run at Pickford. The zone exists because of that geometry, and it exists whether or not England want it to.

There are three ways to attack the problem and England will probably use all three in rotation. The first is Rice dropping into the pocket as a screening presence, which is exactly why his fitness was the biggest team-news item of the week: without him, that space is unguarded. The second is having the ball, because Messi cannot hurt anyone from inside his own half and Argentina's midfield does not press with any conviction. The third is aggression on the first pass into the zone rather than the second, meaning Anderson and Rice stepping to intercept the ball on its way to Messi rather than trying to dispossess him once he has it, which is a losing proposition and has been for twenty years.

The counter-argument is uncomfortable for England. Argentina do not need Messi to be involved for eighty minutes. They need him to be involved twice. A team that has scored nine times after the 75th minute at this tournament is a team whose plan explicitly tolerates seventy minutes of nothing. England's defensive concentration has to hold not for a passage but for the whole match, and it has not done so once in the knockout rounds.

### Question two: England's set pieces against Argentina's box

This is England's clearest edge and the most underdiscussed part of the tie. Rice has taken twenty-six corners and free-kicks at this tournament, comfortably the most of any England player, with Saka, Anderson and others sharing the rest. His delivery onto Kane and Stones is the most reliable goal-generating mechanism either team possesses that does not depend on a moment of individual invention. In a match that history says will be tight and low-scoring, a set piece is not a bonus; it is a plan.

Argentina defend their box well, with numbers and with know-how rather than athleticism, and they are also not immune. Mac Allister headed in a Messi corner against Switzerland, so the holders are entirely capable of scoring this way themselves and entirely aware of what it costs. But Argentina's back line is not tall, Romero is their best aerial defender and may be short of full fitness, and Emiliano Martinez, for all his qualities, is a goalkeeper who commands his area by force of personality rather than by reach.

If this match is 0-0 at the hour, England's most likely route to a goal is a Rice delivery. That is not a criticism of their open play; it is a recognition that against a side that concedes possession happily and defends the center of the pitch with four experienced midfielders, dead balls are where the geometry finally favors the attacking team.

### Question three: legs

Argentina have played thirty more minutes of knockout football than England and have done it with an older spine. Paredes is thirty-two, De Paul is thirty-two, Messi is thirty-nine, Otamendi is thirty-eight and available from the bench. Two of their three knockout ties went to a hundred and twenty minutes, and the second of those finished four days ago. England's extra-time win over Norway was also four days ago, but they came through the Round of 16 in ninety minutes and their squad is younger, deeper and, on the evidence of the last fortnight, better able to change a game from the bench.

England are at their best pressing and moving the ball quickly. If they do that for the first thirty minutes and force Argentina's midfield to defend at pace, the compound interest arrives around the seventieth. That is the theory. The practice is that England have not sustained a high press for a full match at this tournament, and the reason is the same reason the press would work: it costs legs, and they have needed those legs in the last twenty minutes of every knockout tie they have played.

There is a version of this match where England's fitness advantage is decisive and a version where it is irrelevant because Argentina simply do not contest the phase of the game where it would show. Scaloni's side does the simple things at an elite level: control the middle, feed Messi in dangerous areas, and defend the box with numbers. None of that requires running.

### Question four: Kane and Bellingham against a back four that knows them

Scaloni was blunt about the problem in his own press conference. He described England's two as great players, among the best in the world, said his side would try their best to neutralize them, and added that Argentina have their own weapons and would try to prevent them from having a good game. That is a coach naming the threat rather than deflecting it.

Bellingham is the more urgent problem of the two right now. He has scored in back-to-back matches with braces in each, and both of those braces arrived in the phases of the game where Argentina are weakest, which is to say late, in transition, arriving into the box from midfield rather than starting in it. Tuchel called him world-class after Norway, which is a word he does not use casually. Argentina's answer is Paredes, positionally, and it is a reasonable one: Paredes reads the run and fouls it when he has to.

Kane's role in this match is more likely to be architectural than statistical. He drops, he links, he pulls a centre-back out of the line, and he is the target for the set pieces that represent England's clearest route to a goal. He also has six goals here, which is what he managed in 2018 when he won the Golden Boot. Enzo Fernandez, who plays his club football in England and knows this squad as well as any Argentine alive, will have views on all of it.

## The Players Who Decide It

### How important is Lionel Messi for Argentina against England?

He is the plan. Messi leads the World Cup 2026 Golden Boot race with eight goals and has added three assists in six appearances, and Argentina's late-goal habit is largely a description of what he does when a match opens up. Remarkably, at thirty-nine and in his sixth World Cup, tonight is the first time he has ever faced England.

That last fact is worth sitting with, because it is the strangest thing about this fixture. Messi has played more than a thousand competitive matches. He has faced every serious footballing nation on earth, repeatedly, in finals and semi-finals and friendlies. He has never played England. The rivalry's most famous chapter belongs to Maradona, who was born in 1960 and scored those two goals in 1986; Messi was born the following year. The two most famous Argentine footballers in history are separated by a generation and by this: one of them defined the fixture and the other has never been in it.

The numbers he brings are the tournament's most significant. Twenty-one World Cup goals in his career, a record he extended with that finish against Egypt. Ten World Cup assists, each to a different teammate, the tenth being the corner Mac Allister headed in against Switzerland. Double figures for goal contributions at both of the last two World Cups, which no one else has done. Eight goals across five consecutive matches at one point in this tournament. He has also missed two penalties here, including the one Shobeir saved in Atlanta, which is worth stating because the deification tends to sand off the parts that make the story interesting.

What England have to plan for is not a player who will dominate ninety minutes. It is a player who will be almost entirely absent for long passages and then produce two or three actions of a quality nobody else on the pitch can reach. Tuchel's description was the sharpest thing anyone said all week: a silent killer, one who can kill you while he is walking, meaning that the walking is not disengagement, it is scanning. His conclusion was equally direct. "We are here to take the game away from him," Tuchel said, which is the correct ambition and the hard part.

Kane's response to the same line of questioning was the more useful one from England's point of view. He called it a unique occasion, noted how strange it is that a career this long has never intersected with England, and then landed on the thing his manager cannot say as bluntly. "But the game is against Argentina. Not against Lionel Messi," Kane said. That is a captain telling his defenders not to get dragged into a duel they cannot win and to defend the team instead.

Around Messi, Argentina's most likely match-winner is whoever partners him. Alvarez is the more probable starter and the more mobile of the two, a striker who runs the channels Messi cannot and who produced the goal of the quarter-final from nothing. Lautaro is the better pure finisher and has been devastating in the role Scaloni has actually been using him in, which is as a substitute arriving at the seventieth minute against defenders who have been on their feet for an hour. Given the moment rule, the more interesting question may not be which one starts but which one is on the pitch at the eighty-fifth minute.

Enzo Fernandez is the third name to watch and the most likely to be underestimated. He knows this England squad from the inside, he strikes a ball from distance as well as any midfielder in the tournament, and he is the one Argentine who might score the kind of goal that does not require Messi to make it. Emiliano Martinez brings a shootout record that England, of all nations, do not need reminding about. Mac Allister is the most complete midfielder Argentina have and the one who arrives in the box.

For England, Bellingham is the form player of the tournament and the man Argentina have specifically planned for. Six goals, seven in his World Cup career, braces in consecutive knockout matches, and a knack for arriving in the eighteen-yard box at the exact second the defense stops counting. He is twenty-three and already third on England's all-time World Cup scoring list.

Kane is above him on that list and above everyone: fourteen World Cup goals, having passed Gary Lineker's ten earlier in this tournament. Six of those fourteen have come here, matching his 2018 total. He is not the same player he was in Russia, which is the point; he is a better one, and the version of Kane that drops into midfield to receive and turn is the version most likely to unpick a side that defends its box with numbers.

Gordon has three assists and is the most direct thing England have. Pickford is the goalkeeper who has kept England in more than one tournament match already. And if Morgan Rogers starts, he is the pick as the least-priced player on the pitch who could plausibly decide it, because his job would be to occupy exactly the space Argentina's midfield vacates when it shuffles across to Messi's side.

## What Is at Stake and How the Bracket Resolves

### What does the winner of Argentina vs England gain in the final?

The winner plays Spain in the World Cup 2026 final at New York and New Jersey Stadium on Sunday July 19, with four days of recovery. The loser plays France in the third-place play-off in Miami on Saturday July 18, with three days. There is no other scenario. This is single elimination.

Spain's presence on the other side of the bracket changes the calculus for both teams in ways worth spelling out. They beat France 2-0 in Dallas on Tuesday in a performance that looked qualitatively different from anything else at this tournament. For Argentina, a Spain final would be the holders against the European champions, the two best teams in the world by most measures, with Argentina attempting to become the first nation in sixty-four years to retain the trophy. For England it would be a rematch of the Euro 2024 final, which Spain won, with a squad that contains most of the same players and all of the same memory.

The retention question deserves its own paragraph because of how rare it is. Italy did it in 1934 and 1938. Brazil did it in 1958 and 1962. Nobody has done it since, across fifteen tournaments and sixty-four years, and the list of teams that have tried and failed includes some of the best sides ever assembled. Argentina winning tonight puts them ninety minutes from the third entry on that list. It also puts them in a fourth successive major final across five years, which is a run of sustained achievement that the modern international calendar is supposed to make impossible.

England's version is the mirror image and, in its way, heavier. The gap since 1966 is not just about the World Cup; it is sixty years without a men's major trophy of any kind, punctuated by two European finals lost in the last five years and semi-finals in 1990, 2018 and now. Kane and Bellingham are the two players most obviously capable of ending it and they are at exactly the ages, thirty-two and twenty-three, where one of them is running out of tournaments and the other has not started. That asymmetry is part of why this feels like a hinge for England rather than a step.

A practical note on the rules that people search for at this stage and get wrong. If the score is level after ninety minutes there will be thirty minutes of extra time, and if it is still level there will be a penalty shootout; there is no away-goals equivalent and no replay. Yellow-card accumulations were wiped after the quarter-finals, which means no player on either side goes into tonight one booking away from missing a final. That is a genuine tactical variable rather than trivia: in a fixture with this much history and this much friction, and with both managers having named containment as their plan for the other's best player, the tactical foul is available tonight at a lower price than it was a week ago. Expect both midfields to use it.

The third-place match is the other half of the stake and it is not nothing. France, beaten by Spain on Tuesday, will be in Miami on Saturday with a squad that has looked capable of winning this tournament and a bronze medal available. For whichever of these two loses tonight, that fixture will be the least appealing assignment in football: four days after a World Cup semi-final defeat, in Florida heat, against a wounded France.

## Venue, Conditions and How to Watch Argentina vs England

### What time does Argentina vs England kick off at World Cup 2026?

Kickoff at Atlanta Stadium is 3pm Eastern on Wednesday July 15, which is noon on the Pacific coast, 8pm in the United Kingdom, and 4pm in Buenos Aires. In the United Kingdom the match is on BBC One and BBC iPlayer. In the United States it is on the Fox network, which has carried the tournament throughout.

The venue is the most controlled environment either team has played in at World Cup 2026, and that is a real tactical fact rather than a travel note. Atlanta Stadium, known outside the tournament as Mercedes-Benz Stadium, sits in downtown Atlanta beside Centennial Olympic Park with MARTA rail stations within walking distance at Vine City, GWCC and Five Points. Its official World Cup capacity is 68,239, reduced from the roughly seventy-five thousand it holds for other events because FIFA reconfigured the bowl.

The roof is the reason this matters. It is made of eight translucent triangular panels that slide along rails and fan open around a central circular opening, a design the architect took from the oculus of the Roman Pantheon, and it opens or closes in around eight minutes. Atlanta is one of only three venues at this World Cup that can genuinely air-condition its seating bowl, alongside Dallas and Houston, and the roof will be shut tonight. The interior will sit at roughly 72 degrees Fahrenheit, about 22 Celsius, regardless of what Georgia is doing outside, and forecasters do not expect the outdoor temperature to exceed 30 Celsius anyway. There will be no heat, no humidity, no thunderstorm interruption and no wind.

That is worth a paragraph of tactical thought rather than a shrug. Both of these teams have been shaped by conditions at this tournament. England played a Round of 16 tie at altitude in Mexico City and a quarter-final in Miami in July. Argentina played in Miami and Kansas City. Tonight neither side gets an excuse and neither gets an edge. A cooled indoor bowl at 22 degrees is the closest thing to a laboratory that a World Cup semi-final can be played in, and it removes the single most common reason that teams stop pressing after an hour. If England want to run at Argentina's midfield for ninety minutes, the climate will let them. If Argentina want to walk through seventy minutes and strike at the eighty-fifth, the climate will let them do that too. The conditions do not pick a side; they just remove the alibi.

The pitch is a temporary hybrid, natural grass reinforced with synthetic fibers, grown in Colorado and installed after crews removed the artificial turf, the pad beneath it and roughly a foot of asphalt to meet FIFA's requirements. It sits over a SubAir drainage and moisture system with a 27-zone irrigation network and is kept alive under grow lights, because natural grass does not enjoy living indoors. It has been tested through an MLS season's worth of matches and reviewed well. The playing surface is the FIFA standard 105 meters by 68, which the stadium achieves by retracting seating in each corner, a feature designed in from the start because the building was part of the bid for an MLS team.

Atmosphere will not be neutral in the way the temperature is. Atlanta has the largest and loudest soccer support in the United States, the city is a major hub for both Argentine and English travelling fans, and Hartsfield-Jackson is the busiest airport in the world, roughly twenty minutes from the ground. Argentine support has been the loudest of any nation at this tournament. Expect a bowl that is sealed, sold out and extremely hot in the only sense that matters tonight. Gates open three hours before kickoff, the FIFA Fan Fest opens at noon with a closing ceremony afterward since this is Atlanta's last match, and there are official watch parties across the metro area. One more note on the officiating, because in this fixture it is never a footnote. Elfath is a strong appointment and an experienced one, and he is taking charge of a match that both federations, both managers and the FBI have all flagged for different reasons. The first all-American crew to work a World Cup semi-final gets the most combustible fixture in the sport. ## Argentina's Midfield Mileage and the Depth Question

Thirty extra minutes against Cape Verde. Thirty more against Switzerland. Argentina have played three hundred and thirty minutes of knockout football to England's three hundred, and the raw gap of half an hour understates the cost, because the two periods of extra time Argentina have absorbed came in the two matches where they were forced to chase rather than control. Chasing is what empties legs. A team that leads for eighty minutes and closes a game out at walking pace has spent a different afternoon from a team that spent the last quarter of an hour throwing bodies forward and then had to do it again for another half hour.

The players carrying the heaviest load are the ones Scaloni cannot easily replace. Rodrigo De Paul has been the connective tissue of this side for five years, the player who does the running that allows Messi to walk, and he has started every match of this tournament. Alexis Mac Allister has been asked to be both the deepest creator and the first line of the press, and he scored the opening goal against Switzerland in the tenth minute from a Messi corner before playing another hundred and ten minutes. Enzo Fernandez has been the late runner, the man arriving in the box when the ball breaks, and his stoppage-time winner against Egypt came at the end of a match in which he had already covered more ground than anyone in blue and white. That midfield three has been the engine, the brakes and the steering. It has also been on the pitch for almost every minute that has mattered.

Scaloni's response has been characteristically unspectacular. He named an unchanged eleven for the quarter-final, he trialed a back three in Monday's session in a way that read more as a contingency than a plan, and he has been careful to describe the extra time as something his squad absorbed rather than something it suffered. Cristian Romero came off at the interval of extra time against Switzerland through exhaustion rather than injury, and he will be assessed rather than ruled out, with Facundo Medina pushing to start if the assessment goes the wrong way. Nahuel Molina and Nicolas Tagliafico were both withdrawn before the end of the additional period, which Scaloni framed as a decision taken early enough to leave both full-backs fresh for Atlanta rather than one taken because either was struggling. That framing is worth taking seriously. Scaloni has managed load in this tournament with the deliberateness of a man who assumed from the start that he would still be working in the middle of July.

What the mileage does not appear to have touched is the thing Argentina do best, which is arrive. Nine goals after the seventy-fifth minute at this World Cup is not a statistic that belongs to a tired team. It belongs to a team that has decided the last quarter of an hour is where it will live, and that has organized its substitutions, its tempo and its emotional temperature around that decision. Argentina do not sprint for ninety minutes. They sprint for the twenty that count, and they have been able to do it at the end of a hundred and twenty minutes twice.

### Can Argentina's legs last another two hours in Atlanta?

The honest answer is that nobody knows, including Scaloni, and that the question may not be the right one. Extra time in a semi-final is a coin toss that both teams have already survived once, and the roof at Atlanta Stadium changes the arithmetic in a way that favors the older squad. A closed roof and an interior held around seventy-two degrees removes the single biggest accelerant of fatigue in a summer tournament, which is heat. Argentina played their quarter-final in Kansas City in open air. They played their round of sixteen in Atlanta with the roof shut and produced a comeback from two goals down with eleven minutes left. The conditions in which this squad has looked most alive are precisely the conditions they will get on Wednesday.

There is also a version of this argument that runs the other way, and England will be leaning on it. Fatigue does not announce itself in the first hour. It announces itself in the transition moments of the last thirty, when a midfielder who would normally track a runner takes one step and decides against the second. If Declan Rice can get England into the eightieth minute level, with Argentina's midfield three having played three hundred and thirty minutes more football than anyone should have to, the moment England have been waiting for might arrive by simple subtraction. Tuchel has not said this out loud. He does not need to. His team have won three knockout ties by scoring after the seventy-fourth minute, and they will have noticed what everyone else has noticed.

The deeper point about Argentina's squad is that its depth is not the same as its quality. Behind the first eleven there is Jose Manuel Lopez, a targetman who changed the shape of the Switzerland match from the bench and set up Julian Alvarez's goal. There is Thiago Almada, who has looked sharper in twenty-minute cameos than several players who started. There is Nico Gonzalez, who gives Scaloni a direct runner when the game has stretched. None of those players would start a semi-final on merit. All three have altered a knockout match already. That is what a champion's bench looks like, and it is the least discussed reason Argentina keep finding the late goal. The players arriving in the eighty-fifth minute are not survivors. They are fresh specialists, introduced at the exact moment their specialty becomes the only thing that matters.

## England's Set-Piece Machine and Why It Is the Most Likely Goal in Atlanta

Twenty-six corners and free kicks have gone through Declan Rice's right foot at this World Cup. That number is not an accident of who happens to be standing nearest the ball. It is a decision Tuchel made before the tournament started, and it is the clearest statement of intent in England's entire tactical profile. When a coach hands every dead ball in a World Cup to one player, he is telling you where he thinks his goals are coming from.

The mechanics are worth pulling apart, because they explain why this is the specific threat that troubles Argentina rather than a generic observation that England are tall. Rice's delivery is flat and fast, driven into the space between the six-yard line and the penalty spot rather than floated toward the far post. That kind of ball takes the goalkeeper out of the equation, because a keeper cannot come for something arriving at chest height at speed through a crowd. It also removes the defender's ability to attack the ball on his own terms, because there is no hang time in which to find a run-up. What it demands instead is that an attacker gets in front of his marker and applies the smallest possible touch. Harry Kane's winner against DR Congo was not a leap. It was a step across a defender and a nod.

Argentina's box defending has been the least examined part of their tournament, largely because the goals they have conceded have been so dramatic that nobody has bothered to categorize them. Look at them again and a pattern emerges. Egypt's first goal came from a header from a cross. Switzerland's equalizer came from a ball into the area that Argentina cleared badly. Cape Verde scored twice in a match that went to extra time, and neither goal came from a passage of open play that Argentina had controlled. Cristian Romero is an outstanding aerial defender and Nicolas Otamendi has spent fifteen years winning first contacts. The vulnerability is not the first contact. It is the second, and the third, and the scramble that follows when a flat delivery is not cleared properly on the first attempt.

### How dangerous are England from set pieces against Argentina?

Dangerous enough that it is reasonable to call it England's most likely route to a goal, and reasonable to say that if England reach the final it will probably be because of one. John Stones is the tallest center-back in the English side and has scored from corners at every level he has played. Kane has scored from headers at this tournament. Marc Guehi attacks the near post. Rice himself, when England win a corner on the opposite side and Kyle Walker's replacement takes it, becomes an aerial threat rather than a supplier. That is four genuine targets from a single routine, which is one more than most international sides can field.

The counterargument is timing, and it is the counterargument this preview keeps returning to. Set-piece goals cluster in the first hour. They come when teams are still structured, still winning corners from sustained pressure rather than from desperate long balls, still fresh enough to hold their runs. England's set-piece goal, if it arrives, will most likely arrive before the seventy-fifth minute. Argentina's goal, if it arrives, will most likely arrive after it. That is the entire match in two sentences, and it is why the shape of this preview's expectation is England ahead and Argentina chasing rather than the reverse.

There is a second layer to England's dead-ball threat that gets less attention, which is what it does to the game when it does not produce a goal. A team that wins six corners in twenty minutes is a team that has pushed the opposition back into their own third for twenty minutes. Every one of those corners is a rest for England's midfield, a reset of the defensive line, and a chance to get Kane a touch in a dangerous area without asking him to run. Against a side with Argentina's transition threat, the value of a corner is not only the header at the end of it. It is the ninety seconds during which Messi is standing on the halfway line with nothing to do.

Anthony Gordon's three assists and Bukayo Saka's three assists both come substantially from open-play crosses rather than dead balls, and that matters here too, because it means England can threaten the same zone from two different starting points. Gordon's chipped delivery for Kane against DR Congo was, functionally, a set piece taken at speed. Saka has started three of the last four and gives Tuchel a right side that can cut inside and force the corner rather than only whip the cross. The threat is coherent. It has a shape, a supplier, four targets and two entry routes, and it has produced a goal in every knockout round.

## The Benches and the Substitution Battle

Semi-finals are decided by the players who come on more often than anyone admits before kickoff. Both of these teams know it, and both have organized their squads accordingly, but they have organized them around opposite theories of what a substitute is for.

Scaloni's bench is a set of tools. Lopez is the tool you use when the ball needs to stop in the final third and wait for support. Almada is the tool you use when the opposition midfield has stopped stepping out. Nico Gonzalez is the tool for a stretched game with grass to run into. Alvarez and Lautaro Martinez are, remarkably, interchangeable as starter and finisher, and both scored in extra time against Switzerland, which is the strongest possible evidence that Scaloni does not regard the choice between them as a hierarchy. He regards it as a sequencing question. One of them plays the first seventy. The other plays the last fifty. Which order depends on the opponent, and against an England back line that will be sitting deeper than Switzerland did, the case for Lautaro's movement across the front early and Alvarez's running behind late is at least as strong as the reverse.

Tuchel's bench is different in kind. England's substitutes have been used to protect rather than to change. Ezri Konsa deputized at right-back against Norway and did the job without altering the match. Saka replaced Noni Madueke at half-time against Norway and shifted the balance of England's right side, which is the closest thing England have had to a game-changing substitution in the knockout rounds, and it is notable that it came at the interval rather than in the seventieth minute. Marcus Rashford scored the opener against Croatia in the group stage and has not scored since, which leaves Tuchel with a bench forward whose form does not currently justify a chase-the-game introduction.

### Which bench is better equipped to change a semi-final?

Argentina's, comfortably, and the margin is one of the two or three most significant edges in this fixture. It is not that England's replacements are worse footballers. It is that Argentina's replacements have already changed knockout matches at this tournament and England's have not been asked to. Jarell Quansah's suspension removes a defensive option, Jordan Henderson's broken wrist removes the squad's most experienced game-managing midfielder, and the right-back position remains genuinely open between Reece James, Djed Spence and Konsa, which means one of Tuchel's substitutions may be forced rather than chosen if the starter there struggles.

The subtler asymmetry is what each manager will be substituting for. If the match is level at seventy minutes, Scaloni will bring on a forward to win it and Tuchel will face a choice between bringing on a forward to win it and bringing on a midfielder to survive to extra time. Tuchel's team have gone to extra time once and won. Scaloni's have gone twice and won both. Neither manager fears the extra half hour, but only one of them has a bench that reliably improves his team inside it. Against Mexico, England finished the match with ten men and held a one-goal lead by defending in a low block for the final stretch, and Tuchel will know that the version of that plan which works against Mexico is not obviously the version that works against a side who have scored nine goals after the seventy-fifth minute.

There is one England substitution that could rewrite this section entirely, and it involves Cole Palmer or whichever creator Tuchel decides to trust with twenty minutes of a semi-final. England's problem in the last quarter of their knockout ties has been that when Kane drops deep to find the ball, there is nobody making the run he used to make. A substitute who runs beyond Kane changes what Kane's touches mean. Tuchel has not used the tournament to establish who that player is. He has three days to decide, and the decision may be worth more than any tactical adjustment he makes before kickoff.

## The Managers: Scaloni's Continuity Against Tuchel's Rebuild

Lionel Scaloni has been in charge of Argentina since 2018 and has won the Copa America twice and the World Cup once. He took the job as an interim appointment that half of Argentine football considered an insult to the shirt, and he has since constructed the most stable national team environment in the world. His method is not complicated to describe and is apparently impossible to copy. He picks players who understand their function, he asks them to do that function until it is automatic, and he changes almost nothing between matches. The unchanged eleven against Switzerland was the fourth time in this tournament he has named the same side twice in succession.

Thomas Tuchel took the England job in a completely different spirit. He arrived with a mandate to fix something, and the something was the sense that England's talent had never been arranged into a team that could take a tournament by the throat. He has arranged it into a four-two-three-one, kept it there through six matches, given every dead ball to Rice and every penalty to Kane, and produced three knockout wins in which England were second best for meaningful stretches and won anyway. That is not a criticism. Winning while not at your best is the single most valuable trait a tournament team can have, and Tuchel has said his side have not peaked yet with the flat confidence of a man who has done the arithmetic.

The stylistic contrast at Atlanta will be sharper than the two teams' formations suggest. Scaloni's Argentina defend in a compact mid-block and accept that they will not have the ball for long stretches, because the only question that interests them is what happens when they get it back with Messi in space. Tuchel's England want the ball in the final third, want the corner more than the through ball, and want the match to be a series of set-piece opportunities separated by periods of control. Neither of these is the football either nation's supporters imagine their team plays. Both are ruthlessly effective.

### How do Scaloni and Tuchel match up tactically?

The specific chess problem is the right half-space, which is where Messi operates and which is patrolled by whoever England field at left-back, which will be Nico O'Reilly. Tuchel said his plan is to take the game away from Messi, which is a manager telling you he intends to defend the space rather than the man, because defending the man against Messi has never worked for anyone. That means O'Reilly holds his position, Rice shuttles across to double up, and England accept that Messi will get on the ball but will get on it thirty yards further from goal than he wants to. It is the right plan. It is also the plan Switzerland used before Messi's corner produced Mac Allister's goal in the tenth minute, and the plan Egypt used before Messi crossed for Romero's header in the seventy-ninth.

Scaloni's answer to Tuchel is simpler and harder to counter. He will not do anything unusual. He will name his side, he will let England have the ball, he will keep the match at a temperature Argentina find comfortable, and he will wait. Scaloni's press conference line was that this is a football match, which was his way of declining to engage with the rivalry's political weight out of respect for what he called a very sad period in his country's history. It was also, whether he intended it or not, a description of his tactical plan. Argentina are not going to Atlanta to make a statement. They are going to play a football match and to be level with twenty minutes left.

The managerial edge, if there is one, sits with continuity. Scaloni has faced this exact scenario. He has taken this exact group into the last four of a World Cup and out the other side. Tuchel has won a Champions League and understands knockout football as well as anyone alive, but he has been England manager for a short time and this is the first occasion on which his team have been asked to beat a genuinely elite side in a single match with no margin. England beat Croatia in the group stage. They have not yet beaten a team of Argentina's standing when it counted. That is not a prediction. It is the gap in the resume, and Wednesday is the day it gets filled or it does not.

## The Goalkeepers, the Penalties and the Shootout Nobody Wants

Argentina's route to this semi-final includes a missed Messi penalty against Egypt, saved by Mohamed Shobeir, who also stopped efforts from Mac Allister and Alvarez before half-time in a goalkeeping performance that briefly looked like it would eliminate the holders. Messi has missed two penalties at this World Cup. He also has eight goals and three assists in six appearances and leads the Golden Boot race at thirty-nine years old. Both of those facts are true and neither cancels the other, but the first one matters on Wednesday for a reason that has nothing to do with Messi's nerve. It matters because it means Argentina's penalty hierarchy is a live question in a match that could go to a shootout.

England's is not a live question. Kane takes them, Kane scored one against Mexico for three-one, and Kane has been the most reliable penalty taker in international football for a decade. In a shootout, England start with a certainty that Argentina do not have, and that is a genuine and underrated edge given the history. It is also, delightfully, the exact inversion of the stereotype. England have lost World Cup shootouts to Argentina before, in Saint-Etienne in 1998, when Roa saved from David Batty and Paul Ince also missed. Twenty-eight years later England arrive with the better spot-kick profile and Argentina arrive with the goalkeeper who has been beaten less.

### What happens if Argentina vs England goes to penalties?

It becomes a different match with different variables, and both teams have a case. Argentina's case is Emiliano Martinez, whose reputation in shootouts is a psychological event before it is a technical one, and whose presence changes what a taker thinks about during his walk from the halfway line. That is not mysticism. It is a measurable effect, and Argentina have won two major tournament shootouts with him in goal.

England's case is that they have practiced, that they have Kane, that the culture around English penalty-taking has been rebuilt from the ground up since 2018, and that the players walking up on Wednesday have no memory of Saint-Etienne because most of them were not born. Jude Bellingham was not alive in 1998. Neither was O'Reilly. The idea that England carry a curse into a shootout is a story told by people who watched the ones that went wrong, and this squad has won shootouts at major tournaments in the last three years.

The likelier truth is that neither of these teams wants to find out. Argentina have played thirty extra minutes twice and would be walking into a shootout on empty legs. England would be walking in having survived a hundred and twenty minutes against a side whose whole method is the late goal, which is a hundred and twenty minutes of the exact thing they least want.
