The Lokpal Bill holds a record no other Indian measure quite matches. It was introduced in Parliament, in one form or another, for forty-five years without becoming law, and then, inside two years of a street movement forming around it, it was passed by both Houses and signed into the statute book. The easy version of that story credits the movement with writing the law, and the cynical version credits it with nothing but noise. The passage record supports neither. What the movement changed was the political cost of delay: it converted a bill that governments could let die quietly, session after session, into a bill whose failure was televised, counted and punished. What it could not change was the drafting table itself, where its most ambitious clauses were negotiated away by the ordinary machinery of committees, cabinets and floor arithmetic. A reader who finishes this article should be able to say precisely which of the movement's demands survived into the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, which were diluted, and which were dropped altogether, and to explain why the same Parliament that could not pass the bill in four decades passed it in a single week in December 2013.

That distinction, between forcing a vote and dictating a text, is the analytical thread of everything that follows. Legislative history is usually written from the statute backwards, as if the enacted text had been inevitable. The Lokpal's history runs the other way: the text is the residue of a contest, and the contest is only visible if the failed drafts, the abandoned committees and the promises made under pressure are kept in view alongside the Act. The movement's own account of its role, in which a united people imposed its draft on a reluctant state, is part of that record and is treated here as what it is, a participant's account, valuable and contested in equal measure.

## An ombudsman in Indian dress: the idea before the Lokpal Bill

The institution India spent forty-five years failing to create was borrowed. Sweden had appointed a parliamentary ombudsman in the early nineteenth century, and by the middle of the twentieth the device, an independent officer empowered to investigate complaints against the administration, had spread through Scandinavia and into the Commonwealth, with New Zealand adopting it in 1962. The Indian debate began in the same decade. Ashoke Kumar Sen, the Law Minister, proposed a constitutional ombudsman in Parliament in the early 1960s, and in a parliamentary debate in 1963 the member of Parliament L. M. Singhvi gave the proposal its Indian name, coining the word Lokpal, from the Sanskrit lok, the people, and pala, protector or caretaker, with Lokayukta for its state-level counterpart. The naming mattered more than it might seem: the institution was to be presented not as an imported inspector but as a guardian of the citizen against the state, and the name carried that argument into every later draft.

The design itself came from the first Administrative Reforms Commission, set up in January 1966 under the chairmanship of Morarji Desai. The Commission moved quickly on grievances. Its interim report, titled Problems of Redress of Citizens' Grievances and submitted in October 1966, recommended a two-tier machinery: a Lokpal at the Centre and Lokayuktas in the states, modelled on the Scandinavian ombudsman and the New Zealand parliamentary commissioner, and it appended a draft bill to make the recommendation concrete. The Commission's reasoning, quoted in every later debate, was that such an institution was justified not only for removing the sense of injustice from the minds of citizens but also for restoring public confidence in the efficiency of the administrative machinery. Two features of that founding design deserve emphasis, because they recur in every subsequent argument. The ombudsman was to be independent of the executive it investigated, in appointment, tenure and salary, and it was to cover the political executive as well as the permanent bureaucracy, since grievances against administration in a parliamentary system run upward to ministers. The contest of 2011, over whether a Prime Minister could be investigated and who would choose the investigator, was present in embryo in 1966.

The Commission was not writing on a blank page, and the machinery India already possessed explains both the Lokpal's appeal and the resistance to it. The Committee on Prevention of Corruption chaired by K. Santhanam had reported in 1964, and on its recommendation the government had created the Central Vigilance Commission that year as the apex vigilance body for the bureaucracy, an institution that advised on departmental proceedings but answered, in structure and appointment, to the executive it watched. The Central Bureau of Investigation, working under a wartime statute adapted for the purpose, investigated corruption as a police agency under the government's administrative control. The Lokpal proposal was therefore never merely an addition to a crowded field; it was a judgment on the field, an assertion that vigilance machinery housed inside the executive could not credibly police the executive's political head, and that an investigator of ministers had to stand outside the government in a way neither the Commission nor the Bureau did. Every later fight over the Lokpal's appointment process and over control of the CBI is that 1960s judgment being re-argued. One question the Commission's design left open, and which returned in 2011 in an acute form, was whether the ombudsman should be a constitutional authority, entrenched like the Election Commission, or a statutory one, created and alterable by ordinary legislation. Sen had spoken of a constitutional ombudsman; the bills that followed were statutory; and the attempt in December 2011 to give the institution constitutional status by a companion amendment was the old question surfacing at the moment of passage, with consequences the passage narrative will show.

One more feature of the founding design deserves notice, because it explains why the Indian institution proved so much harder to build than its Scandinavian ancestors. The classic ombudsman investigates maladministration: delay, discourtesy, error, the grievances of citizens against a functioning bureaucracy. The Administrative Reforms Commission's Lokpal was that, but it was also something the classic model is not, an anti-corruption authority over the political executive itself, ministers and members of Parliament included within its remit from the first draft. The Indian design thus fused two institutions that other systems keep apart, a grievance commissioner and a corruption prosecutor's overseer, and asked one office to be trusted with both the citizen's complaint about a delayed pension and the investigation of a minister's assets. The fusion was deliberate, born of the Commission's reading of Indian administration, in which maladministration and corruption are not separate pathologies but one continuum of unaccountable discretion. It also guaranteed the forty-five years of argument that followed, since a pure grievance ombudsman might have been enacted without controversy at any point in those decades, while an ombudsman who could investigate the Prime Minister's office was a constitutional event that every government preferred to postpone. When the movement of 2011 inherited the design, it inherited the fusion too, and defended both halves of it, the charter for the citizen and the prosecution of the minister, with the result that its draft was always arguing on two fronts at once.

## The Lokpal Bill from 1968 to 2001: eight introductions, eight failures

The first Lokpal and Lokayuktas Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha in May 1968, in the government of Indira Gandhi. It followed the Administrative Reforms Commission's design closely enough to include Lokayuktas for the states, and it was referred to a joint committee of both Houses, which reported in March 1969. The Lok Sabha passed it on 20 August 1969. It then travelled to the Rajya Sabha, where it was still pending when the Fourth Lok Sabha was dissolved in December 1970, and under the rules governing pending legislation it lapsed. A bill that had cleared the directly elected House, with a committee's scrutiny behind it, died not because any House rejected it but because the parliamentary clock ran out. That manner of death, lapse on dissolution, became the bill's characteristic fate: of the eight bills introduced between 1968 and 2001, seven lapsed and one was withdrawn by the government that had introduced it. None was ever voted down.

### Why did the 1968 Lokpal Bill lapse after the Lok Sabha had passed it?

Because a bill passed by one House and pending in the other lapses when the Lok Sabha is dissolved. The 1968 bill cleared the Lok Sabha in August 1969 but remained before the Rajya Sabha when the Fourth Lok Sabha was dissolved in December 1970, so the passage already won had to begin again from nothing.

The 1977 episode deserves a sentence of its own in any honest telling, because it is the lineage's most inconvenient fact for every later government. The Janata coalition had been elected in March 1977 in the first election after the Emergency, on a platform saturated with the language of accountability, against a government accused of subverting every institution it touched; if any Parliament was ever going to enact an ombudsman, it was that one, with that mandate, in that mood. It introduced its bill within months, sent it to a joint select committee, and then dissolved with the committee's work unconsidered, the coalition having consumed itself in internal quarrels long before its legislative programme matured. The lesson generalises beyond the Lokpal: anti-corruption mandates are perishable goods in Indian politics, strongest in the first season of a government elected against a tainted predecessor and decaying as the new government's own compromises accumulate. The movement of 2011, whatever else it understood, understood this decay curve intuitively, and its insistence on deadlines, a committee by April, a bill by August, passage within the year, was an attempt to legislate inside the window before the mandate rotted. The government's counter-strategy, equally intuitive, was to let the calendar work. The whole of 2011 is those two intuitions in collision.

The successor bills trace the shifting politics of the institution with unusual clarity. The bill of 1971, introduced in August of that year, was not referred to any committee and was never taken up in earnest, dying with the Fifth Lok Sabha. The bill of 1977, introduced in July 1977 by the Janata government that had come to power promising cleaner administration after the Emergency, went to a joint select committee, but the Sixth Lok Sabha was dissolved before the committee's recommendations could be considered. The bill of 1985, introduced under Rajiv Gandhi's government in August 1985 and also sent to a joint select committee, met the lineage's only different fate: the government withdrew it, explaining that it was inadequate to the range of situations it had to cover and promising a more comprehensive measure that did not arrive in that Parliament's lifetime. The bill of 1989, introduced by the National Front government of V. P. Singh, is the one earlier bill that broke new substantive ground, because that government accepted that the Prime Minister should be within the ombudsman's purview; it went to a standing committee and lapsed when the Ninth Lok Sabha was dissolved. The bills of 1996 and 1998 each survived long enough to receive a standing committee's report, in May 1996 and thereafter, and each died the same quiet death, the House dissolving before the government of the day had settled its response to the committee. The bill of 2001, introduced in the Thirteenth Lok Sabha, lapsed on that House's dissolution without approaching a vote.

Read as a sequence, the forty years teach a single lesson about how Indian legislation fails. The Lokpal bills were not defeated by argument; no majority ever assembled to say that India should not have an anti-corruption ombudsman, and most governments introduced a bill precisely in order to be seen supporting one. They were defeated by indifference operating through procedure. A bill that no ministry owns, whose passage creates an institution designed to investigate the very executive that must pilot it, has no natural champion in the legislative timetable, and dissolution, a routine event in India's parliamentary history, reliably arrived before priority did. The design disagreements were real, over the Prime Minister's inclusion, over the judiciary, over the states, but they were never the binding constraint, because the bills rarely survived long enough for the disagreements to be tested on the floor. The constraint was salience. Nothing in the record of those decades punished a government for letting the Lokpal lapse.

Two features of the long failure repay a closer look, because both were misread in 2011. The first is that the lineage was genuinely cross-party, which destroys the comforting theory that the bill failed because one political formation blocked it. Congress governments introduced the bills of 1968, 1971 and 1985; the Janata government introduced that of 1977; the National Front introduced that of 1989; the United Front period produced that of 1996; and the Bharatiya Janata Party-led National Democratic Alliance introduced those of 1998 and 2001. Every governing coalition of the republic tried its hand at a Lokpal bill and every one let it fall, usually while commanding the very majority that could have passed it. The obstacle was not partisan veto but a shared institutional reluctance, common to all who hold power, to create an investigator of the powerful with a statutory claim on the state's own files. The second feature is the slow movement of the Prime Minister question, which functioned across the decades as a thermometer of that reluctance. The bill of 1968 excluded the Prime Minister and members of Parliament altogether; the question then surfaced in committee after committee; the National Front's bill of 1989 accepted the Prime Minister's inclusion and found no time to enact it; and the government drafts of the 2000s settled into the compromise of covering a Prime Minister only after he left office, which is to say at the moment the power to be investigated had departed with him. When the movement of 2011 demanded a sitting Prime Minister's inclusion, it was demanding the one step that forty years of drafting had circled without taking, and the ferocity of that particular argument in the drafting committee cannot be understood without the decades of circling behind it.

While the Centre failed, some states moved, and the contrast is instructive for what happened in 2011. Odisha enacted a Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act in 1970, the first state law of its kind, but did not bring the institution into being under it until 1983. Maharashtra enacted its Lokayukta and Upa-Lokayuktas Act in 1971 and was the first state actually to establish the office and appoint a Lokayukta under it. Over the following decades a majority of states acquired some form of Lokayukta law, of very uneven strength, and a handful acquired working institutions with real records: the Karnataka Lokayukta, held for a decade by Justice N. Santosh Hegde, built a reputation for pursuing corruption in that state's mining sector, and Hegde's report on illegal mining in 2011 preceded the resignation of the state's Chief Minister. That state-level track matters to this history for two reasons. It demonstrated that the institution was administrable in India, refuting the objection that the design was foreign and unworkable, and it supplied the national movement of 2011 with personnel and credibility, since the man who had run a state Lokayukta sat on the movement's drafting committee.

### Which states already had Lokayuktas before the Centre had a Lokpal?

Most of them, on paper at least. Odisha enacted the first state law in 1970 and Maharashtra followed in 1971, becoming the first state actually to appoint a Lokayukta. Over the next four decades a majority of states legislated for the office, although the strength, independence and workload of the resulting institutions varied enormously.

## The quiet decade and the season of scandals

Between the lapse of the 2001 bill and the explosion of 2011, the Lokpal question did not disappear; it subsided into the administrative-reform literature, which is where Indian governments keep proposals they acknowledge and do not intend to enact. The decade was not barren of anti-corruption achievement, and the achievement it did produce shaped the movement to come. The Right to Information Act of 2005, won by the transparency campaign whose methods the Lokpal campaign would inherit and whose veterans it would recruit, demonstrated to a generation of Indian activists that a citizen-facing statute could be extracted from the Indian state by organised pressure and then made to work at scale, because within a few years the information commissions were processing applications in the lakhs and the instrument had entered ordinary administrative life. The demonstration cut both ways for the Lokpal. It proved the genre of campaign viable and supplied it with organisers, lawyers and a tested repertoire, petitions, public hearings, draft bills carried into negotiations. But it also raised the expectation that pressure produces statutes on a campaign's timetable, an expectation the transparency movement itself, fifteen years in the building, would not have endorsed, and the Lokpal campaign's compressed calendar of 2011, its suspicion of committee corridors and its reading of every amendment as betrayal, are unintelligible without the RTI precedent standing behind it as both model and warning. When the two movements' veterans disagreed in 2011 over the Jan Lokpal draft, they were disagreeing, among other things, over the lessons of their own shared victory. The second Administrative Reforms Commission, appointed in 2005 under Veerappa Moily, returned to the subject in its report on ethics in governance and recommended a national ombudsman once more, and a draft Lokpal bill was prepared within the government in 2010. Neither moved the political arithmetic. The decade also contained a quiet institutional experiment that made the events of 2011 less unprecedented than they appeared. The coalition government that took office in 2004 governed alongside a National Advisory Council, chaired by Sonia Gandhi, in which serving activists and academics sat formally inside the policy process and helped shape legislation, most famously the Right to Information Act and the rural employment guarantee. Aruna Roy, whose network would later decline to endorse the Jan Lokpal draft, was among its members. For seven years, therefore, the Indian state had normalised a version of the thing the Hazare campaign would demand: unelected citizens of recognised standing participating, by the government's own invitation, in the preparation of laws. The differences were of degree and of power, an advisory council that recommended against a drafting committee that claimed parity, a chairperson who led the ruling party against nominees chosen by a fasting activist, but the line from the Council to the committee of April 2011 is a real one, and the government's eventual concession is easier to understand against it. The administrative state had already conceded the principle that drafting could be shared; what it had never conceded was sharing it under pressure, on a deadline, with a partner it had not chosen.

What moved the Lokpal itself, in the end, was not a better argument but a change in the weather: a sequence of corruption scandals between 2009 and 2011 that made the cost of the Lokpal's absence visible to voters who had never heard of the Administrative Reforms Commission.

The three episodes that formed the movement's backdrop are worth stating with their confidence levels attached, because the movement's rhetoric often ran ahead of the record. The largest was the allocation of second-generation telecommunications spectrum. The Comptroller and Auditor General's report of November 2010 put the presumptive loss to the exchequer at a figure of Rs 1.76 lakh crore; the word presumptive was part of the finding, the figure was contested from the start, and the criminal proceedings that followed ended years later in acquittals at trial, but the political fact of 2010 and 2011 was that the government's own auditor had attached an enormous number to a discretionary allocation, and the telecom minister, A. Raja, resigned in November 2010. The Commonwealth Games held in Delhi in October 2010 generated a second stream of allegations around contracts and organisation, and the chairman of the organising committee, Suresh Kalmadi, was arrested in April 2011. The Adarsh housing society affair in Mumbai, in which flats in a building promoted for war widows and defence personnel were found allotted to politicians, officials and their relatives, forced the resignation of the Maharashtra Chief Minister, Ashok Chavan, in November 2010. Each episode is distinct in law, and later proceedings revised several of the early conclusions; what they shared, in the public mind of 2011, was a pattern in which discretionary state power over spectrum, contracts and land had been exercised for private benefit while the institutions meant to police it watched. It was against that pattern, and against the forty-year-old promise of an ombudsman, that India Against Corruption organised.

## India Against Corruption and the road to Jantar Mantar

India Against Corruption was the banner under which the 2011 campaign organised, and its nature is worth describing exactly, because the two rival mythologies of the movement both distort it. It was not a party, a registered society with a mass membership, or a spontaneous crowd. It was a campaign platform assembled through 2010 by activists who already knew one another through earlier work, above all the right to information movement: Arvind Kejriwal, a former revenue service officer who had campaigned for the Right to Information Act through his organisations and had received the Ramon Magsaysay Award for that work in 2006, was its chief organiser, and the Jan Lokpal draft that the platform carried into 2011 had been prepared principally by Justice Santosh Hegde, the advocate Prashant Bhushan and Kejriwal. Around that core the platform gathered endorsements from religious figures, retired officials, film personalities and citizen groups, and it built a volunteer network through the internet and mobile messaging that could summon crowds in dozens of cities at short notice. The movement's own account described a leaderless uprising of ordinary citizens. The record shows something both less romantic and more impressive: a small, experienced campaign organisation with a finished legislative draft, a telegenic and morally credible front man, and a distribution network ready before the first fast began. Movements of that kind are not spontaneous, and they are not fake either; they are built, and this one had been under construction for years.

The front man was Anna Hazare, a former soldier from the village of Ralegan Siddhi in Maharashtra, who had spent three decades as a rural development activist, turning his drought-prone village into a frequently cited model of watershed management and prohibition, work recognised by the Padma Shri in 1990 and the Padma Bhushan in 1992. In Maharashtra he had already used the hunger strike, repeatedly and with success, to press state governments for a right to information law and against ministers he accused of corruption, so the method of 2011 was not improvised; it was the national application of a technique he had refined at state level for twenty years. His persona, the white cap, the Gandhian vocabulary, the refusal of party affiliation, supplied the campaign with a moral register that its organisers, lawyers and technocrats by training, could not have generated themselves, and his age and austerity made the government's eventual decision to detain him politically expensive in a way that detaining a younger activist would not have been. The alliance between the organiser and the symbol, Kejriwal's machinery and Hazare's moral authority, was the movement's engine, and its fragility, the two men wanted different endgames, was visible from the start to anyone watching closely.

Through the winter of 2010 and the spring of 2011 the campaign pressed a single, specific institutional demand, and its specificity was a large part of its force. It did not ask the government to end corruption, or to consider a Lokpal, or to introduce some bill. It asked that the Jan Lokpal draft be taken as the basis of legislation and that the drafting be done by a joint committee of five ministers and five nominees of the movement, with a member of the movement as co-chair, so that the text could not be diluted inside a ministry before Parliament ever saw it. Hazare wrote to the Prime Minister, campaign representatives met ministers, and the government offered the ordinary courtesies, a bill in due course, consultation through the usual channels, without conceding the committee. In late March 2011 Hazare announced that if the committee was not constituted he would begin an indefinite fast at Jantar Mantar in Delhi on 5 April. The government calculated, as governments before it had calculated about the Lokpal for forty years, that a fast by an elderly activist was a manageable event. It was the last time in this history that the calculation was safe.

## The fast as a legislative instrument

Because the whole passage turns on two fasts, the instrument itself deserves examination before the narrative resumes: what a hunger strike does, mechanically, inside a parliamentary system, and what it costs the system to answer one. The fast unto death is the extreme member of the family of methods Gandhi developed and named satyagraha, and its Indian legislative record long predates 2011. Potti Sriramulu's fast of 1952, which ended in his death and in rioting that forced the creation of Andhra state, stands as the republic's founding lesson that a fast can redraw the legislative map against a government's will. Hazare had spent twenty years applying the method at state level in Maharashtra, where his campaigns had pressed governments into accepting a right to information law and had forced the resignation of ministers he accused of corruption, so that by 2011 the technique came with a demonstrated record in exactly the policy area in dispute. The method's power is not mysterious. A fast converts an argument, which a government can outwait, into a countdown, which it cannot: each day raises the reputational price of refusal, transfers responsibility for the faster's life to the state in the public mind, and supplies the media with a story that renews itself daily without new facts. It is, in form, the opposite of a parliamentary timetable, and that is why it works on questions where the timetable has been the graveyard.

The method also drew the deepest principled criticism of the entire episode, and the criticism has its own distinguished lineage. In the Constituent Assembly in November 1949, B. R. Ambedkar warned that where constitutional methods of redress are open, resort to civil disobedience, non-cooperation and satyagraha is nothing but the grammar of anarchy, and that the sooner such methods are abandoned the better for the republic. The argument was made against the movement in modern dress throughout 2011: ministers and commentators asked who had elected the five nominees, observed that a fast is a form of duress indistinguishable in structure from a threat, and warned that a Parliament which legislates under a countdown will be asked to legislate under countdowns again, by causes less benign. The movement's reply drew on the same constitutional tradition from the other side: the right to protest peacefully is itself constitutional, a fast harms only the faster, and a demand that Parliament consider a bill, as distinct from enacting a specified text, leaves every legislator's vote formally free. Both positions were honestly held, and the episode resolved neither; what it established is the narrower, empirical proposition that organises this article. In 2011 the fast did not write a single clause. It changed the order of business, the composition of one drafting committee, the content of one resolution and, twice, the dates on which Parliament acted. A legislature's scarcest resource is not votes but time and priority, and the fast is best understood as a machine for seizing both.

## April 2011: the fast that bought a drafting table

The fast that began on 5 April 2011 at Jantar Mantar was, by the standards of Indian protest, small at first: a stage, a few hundred supporters, and one elderly man not eating. Within three days it had become the dominant national story, and the reasons are part of the legislative record, because they explain what the government thought it was conceding and why it conceded so fast. Support marches and candlelight gatherings were reported from cities across the country, organised through the campaign's volunteer networks and amplified by continuous television coverage that treated the fast as a running referendum on the government's integrity. The scandals of the preceding months had loaded the audience; the fast merely gave it a focal point and a deadline. On 8 April, with the fast in its fourth day and the crowds growing, the government gave way. A notification issued by the Ministry of Law and Justice that day constituted a joint drafting committee to prepare a draft Lokpal bill, composed exactly as the movement had demanded, and Hazare ended his fast on 9 April, the morning after the notification appeared in the Gazette.

The four days were not a monologue. Even before the fast began, the government had opened channels to the campaign, and during it the negotiation ran continuously: the Congress president Sonia Gandhi appealed publicly to Hazare to give up the fast, assuring him that the government shared his concern over corruption, and ministers who would sit on the committee were already in contact with the movement's representatives about its composition and terms. The movement, for its part, negotiated as carefully as it protested; its demand had been drafted to be concedable, a committee costs a government nothing it cannot recover later, and its representatives settled the membership, the co-chairmanship and the 30 June deadline in discussion rather than having them announced to it. This matters for the constitutional argument that followed, because the committee was not simply extorted; it was bargained, by a government that believed, not unreasonably, that it was buying peace at a fair price, and by a movement that believed, also not unreasonably, that it had breached the wall. Both beliefs were sincere. The summer was spent discovering that they could not both be right.

What the government conceded deserves a precise description, because both the movement's celebration and its critics' alarm tended to blur it. The notification created a committee of ten: five ministers of the Government of India and five nominees of Hazare, including Hazare himself. It named the Finance Minister, Pranab Mukherjee, as chairperson, and the movement's co-chairperson was Shanti Bhushan, a former Law Minister and senior advocate. The committee was to evolve its own procedure and to complete its work by 30 June 2011, in time for a bill in the monsoon session. Nothing in the notification bound Parliament, bound the Cabinet, or even bound the committee to agree; what it created was a drafting table at which five unelected citizens sat as formal equals of five cabinet ministers in the preparation of a government bill. That had no precedent in Indian legislative practice, and everyone involved knew it.

### Why did the government concede a joint drafting committee in April 2011?

Because the fast converted delay into a daily public verdict on the government's integrity, in a season when spectrum, Games and housing scandals had already damaged it. Conceding a committee cost the government little in law, since Parliament and the Cabinet remained free, and bought an end to the fast on the movement's fourth day.

The speed of the concession also requires the less flattering explanation, which the later record supports. The government did not believe it had surrendered anything structural. A drafting committee, however composed, produces a draft; the draft still had to survive the Cabinet, which could amend it, and Parliament, which could rewrite it entirely, and the ministers who sat on the committee were the government's most experienced negotiators, Pranab Mukherjee above all. Concession was therefore cheap in April precisely because every subsequent veto point remained intact, and the movement discovered across the summer that it had won a seat in the first room of a long corridor. The concession's real significance was symbolic and precedential, and it was large: the state had accepted, in a Gazette notification, that a citizen campaign could claim parity in drafting legislation, and it had done so under the pressure of a fast. Every later argument about the committee, including the constitutional one, was an argument about that acceptance.

## Five ministers, five nominees: the joint drafting committee

The government's five were the Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee, the Home Minister P. Chidambaram, the Law Minister Veerappa Moily, who served as the committee's convenor, the Human Resource Development and Communications Minister Kapil Sibal, and the Water Resources and Minority Affairs Minister Salman Khurshid. The movement's five were Hazare himself; Justice N. Santosh Hegde, the Karnataka Lokayukta and a former judge of the Supreme Court; Shanti Bhushan, the co-chair; his son Prashant Bhushan, an advocate of the Supreme Court with a long record of public interest litigation; and Arvind Kejriwal. The composition contained the whole argument of 2011 in miniature. On one side sat the ministers responsible for finance, internal security, law and communications; on the other, a Gandhian activist, a serving state ombudsman, two generations of one legal family, and a campaign organiser. The committee met nine times between 16 April and 21 June 2011, and its meetings were not the harmonious co-drafting the April notification had imagined. Disputes leaked in real time, each side briefing against the other; and the committee's atmosphere was poisoned from outside as well, when a compact disc surfaced purporting to record the co-chair, Shanti Bhushan, in a conversation suggesting the fixing of a court case, a recording the Bhushans denounced as fabricated and whose authenticity was contested between forensic claims, while separate allegations about land allotments involving the family were aired by political opponents and denied by them. Justice Hegde said publicly that he was hurt by the vilification campaign against his colleagues and was seriously considering leaving the committee, and although nobody resigned, the episode taught the movement that membership of the committee exposed its nominees to a scrutiny of a kind ministers are armoured against by office. By the meeting of 30 May the government members had stated their positions on the questions that mattered most, declining to bring the Prime Minister, the higher judiciary and the conduct of members of Parliament within the House under the Lokpal in the manner the Jan Lokpal draft proposed. The movement's members boycotted one meeting in June in protest. When the committee's work closed on 21 June, ahead of its deadline, it had no joint draft. The government members announced that agreement had been reached on a large share of the basic principles in play, a figure of agreement on most of the forty principles the two sides had listed was given by the government side, and that the remaining differences were fundamental. Both drafts, the government's and the movement's, went forward to the Cabinet, which was free to choose between them, and chose its own.

The committee also fought, instructively, about its own transparency, and the fight deserves recording because it shows the two sides' theories of legitimacy colliding in miniature. The movement's members, drawn from a tradition in which the public hearing and the published record are the activist's natural instruments, sought a process whose drafts and disagreements would be public as they formed, and at one stage demanded access to the audio record of the proceedings; the ministers, formed by a cabinet system in which drafts are confidential until the government owns them, treated the meetings as negotiations whose intermediate positions could not be published without destroying them. Each side was defending the source of its authority: the nominees derived theirs from being watched by the public that had sent them, the ministers from being answerable through a government whose collective responsibility requires a private space in which to change its mind. No rule existed to settle the question, because no such committee had existed, and the improvisation, leaks, counter-leaks and televised accusations that resulted were the sound of two constitutional cultures sharing a table without a common procedure. When the committee failed, both cultures drew the moral that suited them, and the demand for recorded, published drafting became a standing item in the movement's account of what the state owed its citizens, resurfacing in the pre-legislative consultation policy that the government adopted three years later.

The failure of the committee is sometimes written as a betrayal and sometimes as an inevitability, and the historian's task is to see why it was closer to the second. The committee had been designed, by the movement, as a lock: parity of numbers was meant to prevent the ministry from diluting the draft in private, since nothing could issue in the committee's name that the movement's five opposed. But a lock of that kind can only prevent agreement; it cannot compel it, because the ministers retained the exit of reporting disagreement to a Cabinet that the movement could not enter. Parity at the drafting table coexisted with total asymmetry at every later stage, and the movement's discovery of that asymmetry, not any single clause, is what radicalised its tactics over the summer and produced the August fast. For the legislator or staffer reading this record, the transferable lesson is exact: a joint committee with non-legislators can widen the consultation behind a bill and can generate a serious rival text, as this one did, but it cannot transfer the power of decision, and a movement that treats a drafting seat as a share of sovereignty will read every later amendment as theft.

The committee also attracted a serious constitutional criticism, which the brief for any honest account must state at full strength, because it outlasts the episode. The criticism is that lawmaking in a parliamentary democracy is the business of elected representatives, answerable to voters and removable by them, and that a committee half composed of persons with no electoral mandate, constituted by executive notification under the pressure of a fast, sets a precedent in which access to the drafting of laws is allocated by capacity to mobilise crowds and embarrass governments. On this view, associated in the public debate with commentators such as the political scientist Pratap Bhanu Mehta, who described the Jan Lokpal proposal's institutional imagination as at best naive and at worst subversive of representative democracy, the objection is not to consultation but to parity: the movement had not been invited to advise the drafters; it had been seated as a co-drafter, with a co-chair, on terms extracted by a deadline. The counter-argument, made by the movement and by defenders of the arrangement, is that pre-legislative consultation with affected constituencies is normal and healthy, that the committee could bind nobody, that Parliament's authority over the final text was never touched, and that the real objection is therefore not to the form but to the fact that this consultation, unusually, could not be ignored. Both arguments were live in 2011 and remain live whenever such an arrangement is proposed; no court was asked to rule on the committee's constitutionality, and none did, so the question stands resolved politically, by the event, and not judicially at all.

### Was the joint drafting committee constitutional?

No court decided the question. The committee was created by executive notification, its product was only a draft, and Parliament and the Cabinet remained legally free throughout, so the arrangement breached no provision that a court identified. The criticism pressed at the time was one of democratic propriety and precedent, not of adjudicated illegality.

## Not two drafts but three: the other blueprints of 2011

The public argument of 2011 was framed as a duel between the government's bill and the Jan Lokpal draft, but the drafting record contains a third position, and no honest account of the passage can omit it, because it influenced the eventual text and because the movement's internal critics are part of its history. The National Campaign for People's Right to Information, the network built by the transparency movement's veterans, among them Aruna Roy, declined to endorse the Jan Lokpal draft and proposed instead a package of separate instruments: a Lokpal confined to high public functionaries, a distinct and decentralised grievance redressal law for the citizen's everyday encounters with the state, a judicial accountability measure for the higher judiciary, and protection for whistleblowers. The reasoning, argued in public through 2011, was that a single ombudsman loaded with every grievance of a billion citizens would collapse under its own intake, and that the Jan Lokpal's concentration of functions repeated, inside one office, the design error of expecting one institution to do what only a system can. Jayaprakash Narayan, the founder of the Lok Satta movement and a former civil servant, circulated his own set of proposals in the same period, arguing for a strong but bounded Lokpal within a wider programme of institutional reform. When the Prime Minister addressed the Lok Sabha in the August debate, he proposed that Parliament discuss the Jan Lokpal draft alongside the versions prepared by Aruna Roy and Jayaprakash Narayan, and the ensuing debate ranged across all three.

The existence of the third position matters to the verdict this article must reach, in two ways. It shows that the movement's claim to speak for a united civil society was itself contested from within civil society, by activists whose credentials in the transparency struggle were at least as deep as the drafting committee's nominees; the government's negotiators knew it, and used it, arguing that the five nominees represented one campaign rather than a consensus. And it explains some features of the enacted text that the duel framing cannot: the separation of grievance redressal from the Lokpal, enacted as a distinct bill that later lapsed, follows the package logic of the Roy proposals more closely than it follows either the government's August draft, which had ignored grievances entirely, or the Jan Lokpal draft, which had folded them into the ombudsman. Legislative texts are sedimentary; the Lokpal Act's structure preserves the imprint of an argument that the headlines of 2011 largely ignored.

## Two drafts on the table: Jan Lokpal against the government bill

By the monsoon session of 2011 there were two complete texts in public circulation, and the differences between them, catalogued at the time by the legislative research organisation PRS Legislative Research, define what the rest of the year's struggle was about. The substantive offences at stake were those of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, the statute that defines bribery and criminal misconduct by public servants and whose complete guide is the companion to this history, [the Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 explained](/2011/12/15/prevention-of-corruption-act-1988/); the fight was never over what counted as corruption but over who could be pursued for it, by whom, and under whose control.

On jurisdiction the drafts divided at the top. Both covered ministers and members of Parliament for acts outside the House, and both covered senior, Group A, officers. The Jan Lokpal draft covered a sitting Prime Minister, with the safeguard that prosecution of the Prime Minister, ministers, members of Parliament and senior judges could begin only with the permission of a seven-member bench of the Lokpal; the government bill covered a Prime Minister only after he had left office. The Jan Lokpal draft covered the higher judiciary; the government bill excluded judges altogether. The Jan Lokpal draft reached even an MP's speech or vote in Parliament, the territory the Constitution's Article 105 protects, which the movement defended on the ground that a vote sold for a bribe is not a speech; the government bill kept parliamentary conduct outside. And the Jan Lokpal draft covered every government employee down to the lowest grade, while the government bill stopped at Group A, leaving the junior bureaucracy, the clerks, inspectors and constables with whom citizens actually transact, to departmental processes and the existing vigilance machinery. In one curious inversion, it was the government's bill that reached further into civil society, covering officers of non-governmental organisations receiving public or foreign funds, where the movement's draft did not.

On the investigating agency the drafts embodied two different theories of the Indian state. The government bill gave the Lokpal an investigation wing of its own and a prosecution wing of its own, leaving the Central Bureau of Investigation where it stood, under the executive. The Jan Lokpal draft placed the CBI's anti-corruption work under the Lokpal, so that the agency investigating corruption would answer to the ombudsman and not to the government of the day, and had the CBI's prosecution directorate conduct prosecutions on the Lokpal's behalf. This was, in the movement's own hierarchy of demands, close to the core: a Lokpal that must ask the executive's police to investigate the executive was, on its account, the old system with a new signboard. The government's answer, pressed by its ministers through the summer, was that detaching the CBI would create an unaccountable investigative empire and disrupt an agency with many functions beyond corruption. The selection process divided the drafts as sharply. The Jan Lokpal draft built a two-stage mechanism, a search committee anchored in retired holders of high constitutional office who would co-opt civil society members, and then a large selection committee including senior judges, the Chief Election Commissioner and the Comptroller and Auditor General, designed to minimise the government's voice in choosing its own investigator. The government bill used a smaller selection committee of the familiar kind, chaired by the Prime Minister and including the Leaders of the Opposition, a Supreme Court judge, a High Court chief justice, an eminent jurist and an eminent person, with any search committee optional. Finally, the Jan Lokpal draft carried a whole second statute inside it: it required every public authority to publish a citizens' charter of its commitments, made the victimisation of whistleblowers and the repeated violation of a charter punishable, and built a grievance redressal machinery into the Lokpal law, on the theory that petty corruption is experienced by citizens as failed services. The government bill contained nothing on grievance redressal at all.

The scale of the Jan Lokpal design is part of the comparison, because it shaped how parliamentarians received it. The draft imagined an institution of a chairperson and ten members, served by the CBI's machinery, reaching through state Lokayuktas of the same pattern to the citizen's local grievances, working to fixed clocks, investigation to be completed within a year and trial within the year following, with the corrupt official's ill-gotten assets confiscated on conviction. Its supporters presented the scale as the point: corruption in India is not a boutique offence, and an ombudsman built small would be a monument, not a remedy. Its opponents, in the Cabinet and later in the Houses, presented the same scale as the refutation: an unelected body of that reach, commanding the premier investigative agency, supervising every grade of official and armed with its own prosecution, would constitute a parallel state, and the price of creating it in a hurry, under a deadline, would be paid in constitutional friction for decades. The government bill's answering smallness, an advisory-toned body of a chairperson and up to eight members, confined to senior officials and dependent on the existing agencies, was in its authors' presentation the virtue of feasibility. The year's argument was thus never only about corruption; it was about how large a check on the executive a parliamentary executive can be persuaded to build against itself, and the forty years before 2011 had already supplied the base rate for that persuasion.

The Cabinet approved the government's draft in July 2011, and on 4 August 2011 the Minister of State V. Narayanasamy introduced the Lokpal Bill, 2011 in the Lok Sabha, the ninth introduction of a Lokpal bill in the House's history. The introduction itself showed the new politics of the question: the Leader of the Opposition, Sushma Swaraj, objected at the threshold to the exclusion of the Prime Minister, and Narayanasamy told the House that the Prime Minister had personally favoured bringing his office within the bill but that the Cabinet had decided otherwise. Hazare, who had demanded the Jan Lokpal draft and threatened a further fast from 16 August if a strong bill was not passed, burned copies of the government bill in protest, and the bill was referred to the departmental Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice. The movement had its drafting seat behind it and a parliamentary committee ahead of it, and it judged, correctly on the record of forty years, that the committee corridor was where Lokpal bills went to sleep. It returned to the street.

## The argument over the design, clause by clause

Between the two drafts stood a small number of arguments, repeated in every forum from the committee room to the television studio, and the passage history is unintelligible unless each is given its strongest form, because the final text is best read as the scorecard of these arguments rather than as anyone's draft.

On the Prime Minister, the government's case was institutional. A serving Prime Minister investigated by an unelected body, its ministers argued, would carry a permanent instrument of destabilisation over the executive: foreign governments, markets and adversaries would read an inquiry as a verdict, complainants with political motives would file strategically, and the conduct of international relations and national security could not be disentangled from the office's ordinary work. Manmohan Singh's own position complicated his government's brief, since he had let it be known that he personally favoured his office's inclusion, and the Cabinet had decided against him. The movement's case was one of equality and of evidence. No other office concentrates as much discretionary power over the allocations, contracts and appointments in which grand corruption occurs, an ombudsman forbidden to look at the summit would investigate only the foothills, and the safeguards in its draft, a seven-member bench's permission before prosecution, answered the destabilisation fear without conceding the principle. The eventual Act split the argument in a characteristic legislative way: the Prime Minister came in, and whole subject areas, international relations, external and internal security, public order, atomic energy and space, were carved out around him, so that each side could claim its essential point.

On the investigating agency, the movement's case rested on a structural observation that the government's ministers never fully answered: the Central Bureau of Investigation investigates at the sufferance of the executive it may be required to investigate, its director serves at the government's pleasure in practice, and a Lokpal reduced to requesting that agency's services would inherit the very dependence it was created to break. The government's case was equally structural: the Bureau is a general investigative agency with functions far beyond corruption, detaching its anti-corruption branch into the Lokpal would split command, strand cases, and create a second police force answerable to a body with no minister in Parliament to defend its estimates or answer for its errors. The select committee's package, collegium appointment of the Director, Lokpal superintendence over referred cases, approved panels of prosecutors, was the negotiated middle: the Bureau remained the government's, but its leadership and its referred work were partially insulated. Readers of the later record will note that the argument did not close in 2013; in May of that year, in the separate proceedings over coal block allocations, the Supreme Court described the Bureau as a caged parrot speaking in its master's voice, a remark from a different case that entered the Lokpal debate as evidence for the movement's original diagnosis.

On the lower bureaucracy, the argument was about arithmetic against experience. The movement insisted, with the citizen's daily life behind it, that corruption is not experienced at the level of secretaries but at the level of the clerk, the inspector and the constable, and that a Lokpal for the senior grades alone would protect the many by prosecuting the few. The government's answer, which the Standing Committee substantially adopted, was that the Union's junior grades number in the millions, that no single commission in Delhi could inquire into them, and that the honest route was to arm the Central Vigilance Commission with analogous powers over Groups C and D rather than to pretend one office could be everywhere. On selection, the movement argued that any committee the government dominates will choose a Lokpal the government can live with, which is the definition of failure; the government argued that the movement's sprawling selection college, reaching to the Election Commissioner and the Auditor General and a search committee of retired officeholders, diffused responsibility until nobody could be held to account for a bad appointment. On the citizens' charter the division was between those who held that a grievance answered is corruption prevented, the movement and, in committee, the dissenting members, and those who held that mixing service delivery into an anti-corruption statute would drown the investigator in complaints about pensions and ration cards, the position the Standing Committee's majority took in recommending a separate law. Each argument had a winner, and the ledger section records them; what the clause-by-clause view adds is that the victories were distributed by institutional logic as much as by pressure, since the arguments that prevailed were generally those a working Parliament could administer, and the movement's defeats came where its design asked a single office to be a system.

## August 2011: arrest, Ramlila Maidan and the sense of the House

The August fast began with a miscalculation by the state that repeated, in miniature, the pattern of the whole history: an assumption that the Lokpal agitation could be managed by procedure. Hazare had announced an indefinite fast from 16 August unless the Jan Lokpal bill was introduced and passed. The Delhi Police, whose permission was required for the fast site, imposed conditions on duration and numbers that the movement rejected, and prohibitory orders were in force around the intended venue. In the early morning of 16 August, before the fast could begin, police detained Hazare at his residence in Delhi and took more than a thousand supporters into preventive custody. Hazare was produced before a magistrate, refused to sign the personal bond offered for his release, and was remanded to seven days' judicial custody in Tihar Jail, where he began his fast in his cell. The detention achieved the opposite of its purpose. Protests spread across the country within hours, Parliament itself was disrupted by the opposition's condemnation, and by the night of the same day the government had ordered his release. Hazare refused to leave the jail until permission for an unconditional fast at an adequate venue was granted, negotiating, in effect, from a cell he was free to walk out of, and on 19 August he left Tihar for the Ramlila Maidan grounds in central Delhi, where he continued the fast before crowds that grew day by day and coverage that rarely left the story.

The fast at Ramlila Maidan lasted until 28 August, and its endgame is the episode's constitutional novelty, a parliamentary resolution extracted by negotiation between a government and a fasting citizen. Through the second week the government negotiated with the movement's representatives, an all-party meeting was held, and the movement's position narrowed to an achievable demand: it no longer insisted that Parliament pass its bill in that session, but that Parliament debate the rival drafts and adopt a resolution committing itself, in principle, to three propositions drawn from the Jan Lokpal draft. On 27 August both Houses debated the Lokpal question under the rule for discussions without a vote, the Prime Minister told the Lok Sabha that he saluted Hazare and that the House should find a way to give effect to his sense of frustration with corruption, and at the close the Finance Minister, Pranab Mukherjee, as Leader of the House, moved the formulation that both Houses adopted by acclamation, without a division: "This House agrees in principle on the following issues: Citizens Charter, Lower Bureaucracy under Lokpal through an appropriate mechanism, and establishment of Lokayuktas in the States." Mukherjee asked that the proceedings be transmitted to the Standing Committee then examining the bill, and the next morning, 28 August, Hazare ended his fast.

Precision about what that resolution was matters, because it is the element of the story most often inflated in retrospect. It was not a vote on a bill, not an amendment, and not a commitment in law; it was an expression of the sense of the House, agreeing in principle to three propositions, each of which contained its own escape route. A citizens' charter could be enacted inside the Lokpal law or outside it. The lower bureaucracy would come under the Lokpal through "an appropriate mechanism", a phrase that could describe the Jan Lokpal draft's direct jurisdiction or the Central Vigilance Commission model the government preferred. Lokayuktas would be established in the states, but by whom and on what design the resolution did not say. The choice of instrument was itself negotiated, and the negotiation is a small masterpiece of parliamentary improvisation that repays attention. The movement had demanded a resolution voted upon; a voted resolution committing the House to specified legislative content was more than the government would accept, both because it might lose and because it would set the precedent of the floor being bound by an outside deadline. What emerged instead was a discussion under the rule that permits debate without a formal motion, closed by the Leader of the House stating the sense of that discussion in agreed words, adopted without division. The form protected everyone: the government conceded no vote it could lose, the opposition associated itself with the popular points without owning the bill, and the movement received words spoken on the floor of both Houses, in the presence of the Prime Minister, which it could and did treat as Parliament's pledge. Hazare's negotiators accepted the form because the alternative was a fast continuing into medical danger with diminishing returns; the government offered it because the alternative was a fast continuing into political danger with diminishing control. Instruments of this kind, solemn, public and legally weightless, are how parliamentary systems pay debts they acknowledge but cannot yet settle, and the sense of the House of 27 August 2011 is the outstanding modern Indian example of the genre.

What the resolution indisputably did was raise the political price of abandoning those three points to a level no committee could quietly pay, and it converted a fasting activist's demands into the declared position of Parliament itself. That is a weaker thing than a statute and a stronger thing than a promise to the press, and the drafting history of the next two years is exactly the record of Parliament honouring it in the third column's way rather than the movement's.

The debate of 27 August also deserves its place in the record for the range of positions it put on the parliamentary file, because the eventual Act drew on more of them than the movement liked to admit. The Bharatiya Janata Party, speaking through its leaders in both Houses, supported the three points and a strong Lokpal while prosecuting its case against the government's credibility, a dual posture it maintained to the end. The Left parties supported the resolution while pressing for the CBI to be brought under the Lokpal and for corporate corruption to be addressed. Rahul Gandhi intervened to propose that the Lokpal be given constitutional status, on the model of the Election Commission, an idea the government adopted within months as its own companion amendment, with the fate recorded below. Several members across parties recorded their unease at legislating in the shadow of a fast even as they voted with the sense of the House, and that unease, Ambedkar's grammar-of-anarchy argument in contemporary dress, remained on the record as the standing objection whenever the movement next reached for the instrument. Parliament, in short, did not merely yield in August 2011; it argued, adopted what it could defend, reformulated what it could not, and left minutes of the disagreement that the committees then worked from. The movement's account of a Parliament dragged unwillingly to virtue is as incomplete as the government's account of a Parliament serenely in control.

## The difficult autumn: a movement under attrition

Between the resolution of August and the session of December, the movement that had dominated the year began to spend the capital it had accumulated, and the state of its health when Parliament reconvened is part of the explanation for what Parliament felt able to do. In October 2011 the campaign carried its agitation into electoral politics for the first time, asking voters in the Hisar Lok Sabha by-election in Haryana to defeat the Congress candidate in order to punish the ruling party for its Lokpal record; the step was defended by Kejriwal as pressure and attacked by opponents, and by Hazare's own earlier declarations that the movement stood apart from parties, as the beginning of the politicisation that would divide it within a year. The same month brought a public assault on Prashant Bhushan, beaten in his Supreme Court chamber by men who cited remarks he had made about Kashmir, an episode that exposed how quickly the year's discourse could move from drafting clauses to punishing the drafters. The government's departments, meanwhile, pursued the movement's organisers through ordinary process that was widely read as retaliation: Kejriwal received a demand from his former service for dues he disputed, relating to his resignation from the revenue service, and members of the campaign faced a season of notices, audits and hostile briefing of a kind that established parties absorb routinely and a volunteer campaign feels as attrition.

None of this stopped the legislative machinery, and the movement's leaders continued to negotiate and to testify before the Standing Committee, but the autumn changed the terms on which December would be fought. The government had learned that the movement's crowds, enormous in August, were harder to summon on schedule and divided in counsel; the movement had learned that the state's repertoire extended well beyond argument, into the administrative weather that surrounds any citizen who becomes inconvenient. Both lessons are part of the passage history because both sides carried them into the winter session: the government with a greater willingness to let the Rajya Sabha clock run, the movement with a correspondingly greater fear, vindicated at midnight on 29 December, that anything not nailed down in that session would be renegotiated in the quiet of the following year. That is, in fact, what the select committee year did, though with a better outcome for the bill than the movement feared, and the autumn's attrition is therefore best read not as a defeat for either side but as the normalisation of the conflict, the point at which an extraordinary movement became one interest among others in a legislative process that processes interests for a living.

## The committee winter: a new bill, one House, and a midnight adjournment

The Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice, chaired by the Congress member Abhishek Manu Singhvi, reported on the government's bill in December 2011, and its report supplied the architecture the eventual Act would largely follow. The committee declined to settle the Prime Minister's inclusion itself, leaving that question to Parliament with the arguments on both sides recorded. It recommended bringing Group B officers within the Lokpal alongside Group A, and placing the vast Group C and D workforce under the Central Vigilance Commission, armed with powers analogous to the Lokpal's, rather than loading a single national body with millions of employees; it recommended a separate law for grievance redressal and citizens' charters, outside the Lokpal bill; and it recommended a single central law providing for Lokayuktas in the states. Its dissent notes are as instructive as its consensus: three members, Meenakshi Natarajan, P. T. Thomas and Deepa Dasmunshi, dissented to demand, among other things, that the Central Vigilance Commission be placed under the Lokpal, that CBI investigations be subject to the Lokpal's superintendence, and that Group C officers be included directly in the Lokpal's jurisdiction, which is to say that the movement's central demands had acquired parliamentary sponsors inside the committee room even as the committee's majority declined them.

The government then did something that showed how far the politics had moved since August: it withdrew its own bill and started again. On 22 December 2011 a new and comprehensive Lokpal and Lokayuktas Bill, 2011 was introduced in the Lok Sabha, drafted on the Standing Committee's broad pattern but going beyond it on the contested summit, bringing the Prime Minister within the Lokpal subject to safeguards and subject-matter exclusions around international relations, security, public order, atomic energy and space, and carrying, alongside it, a Constitution (One Hundred and Sixteenth Amendment) Bill to give the new institution constitutional status. The Lok Sabha debated and passed the Lokpal bill on 27 December 2011. The constitutional amendment, in the same sitting, failed to secure the special majority that Article 368 requires, a defeat for the government in the House it controlled, and a fact worth holding onto when the following night's events are judged: the government's floor management that week was demonstrably imperfect even where its majority was not in doubt.

The December sittings also place the Lokpal in the wider anti-corruption package that the government, under the year's pressure, was assembling in parallel, and the package's fate is a control experiment for this article's thesis. On the same day the Lok Sabha passed the Lokpal bill, it also passed the Whistle Blowers Protection Bill, a measure the movement supported but had not fasted for; that bill waited in the Rajya Sabha for more than two further years before enactment. The citizens' charter, the first of the three points of August, travelled in a separate grievance redressal bill introduced in the Lok Sabha in the same December week, which never completed its passage and lapsed with the Fifteenth Lok Sabha. The Judicial Standards and Accountability Bill, the vehicle meant to answer the judicial-corruption half of the movement's case after the judiciary was excluded from the Lokpal, likewise stalled. Of the package, only the measure with a fast behind it, a parliamentary resolution recording its three points, and an activist prepared to resume fasting on a date named in public, completed the course. The comparison is not flattering to the legislative process, but it is exact: in the India of 2011 to 2013, the difference between a bill that passed and bills that lapsed was rarely the quality of the drafting; it was the presence or absence of a constituency able to make delay costlier than passage.

The federal argument that wrecked the night deserves its strongest statement, because it was a real constitutional objection and not merely an obstruction. The bill as passed by the Lok Sabha did not confine itself to the Union's ombudsman; its later chapters prescribed the establishment of Lokayuktas in the states, on a design the central text supplied. Public order and the machinery of state administration sit in the State List of the Seventh Schedule, and the governments of states ruled by parties other than the Congress, joined by the Trinamool Congress from within the ruling coalition itself, objected that a central statute designing state institutions inverted the federal scheme for the sake of a headline. The Union government's answer rested on the treaty-implementing power: India had ratified the United Nations Convention against Corruption in 2011, and Article 253 of the Constitution empowers Parliament to legislate for the whole country to give effect to an international agreement, notwithstanding the distribution of powers. Critics replied that a general anti-corruption convention could not honestly be read as a blank authority to design state ombudsmen, and that the argument proved too much. The select committee's eventual settlement, an obligation on states to establish Lokayuktas by their own laws within a year, with the design surrendered to the states, was the federal objection winning in substance while losing in form, and it is the clearest instance in this history of the Rajya Sabha performing the function its composition intends, as the chamber in which the states' governments can amend what the Lok Sabha's majority has passed.

The bill reached the Rajya Sabha on 29 December 2011, and there it met the arithmetic that the Lok Sabha figures concealed. The ruling coalition did not command the upper House; more than 180 amendments had been moved by members, including a large set from the Trinamool Congress, the government's own ally, whose objections centred on the bill's imposition of a Lokayukta model on the states and the federal impropriety of the Centre legislating the design of state institutions; the Bharatiya Janata Party and the Left pressed their own amendments, several aimed at bringing the CBI closer to the Lokpal. Debate ran through the day and into the night. The Parliamentary Affairs Minister, Pawan Kumar Bansal, sought time for the government to consider the amendments; the Leader of the Opposition, Arun Jaitley, charged that a government which lacked the numbers had consciously choreographed the debate so that it could not conclude before midnight; and at midnight the Chairman, Hamid Ansari, adjourned the House sine die. The bill had not been passed, had not been rejected, and had not even been voted on. The government's account was procedural, that 187 amendments could not responsibly be processed in a night; the opposition's account was that the government, facing defeat on amendments it opposed, had run out the clock. Both accounts are recorded because the passage history turns on which is believed: what is not in dispute is that the session ended, the cameras left, and the Lokpal bill entered its forty-fifth year, saved from lapse only because a bill passed by the Lok Sabha and pending in the Rajya Sabha survives the lower House's eventual dissolution, and sent, in May 2012, to a select committee of the Rajya Sabha for the examination the winter had not completed.

## The select committee year and the December 2013 passage

The select committee route is where this history makes its most useful contact with the general study of how Parliament scrutinises bills, a subject taken up in the series' account of [parliamentary committees and the scrutiny of bills](/2022/04/01/parliamentary-committees-bill-scrutiny/), because the Lokpal select committee did what the theory of committees promises: it slowed the bill down, isolated the clauses on which the House was genuinely divided, and rewrote several of them into forms that opponents could accept. The committee, chaired by the Congress member Satyavrat Chaturvedi, reported on 23 November 2012. Its most consequential recommendation answered the federal objection that had wrecked the bill in the Rajya Sabha: the detailed Lokayukta structure written into the central bill should come out, so that states would be obliged to establish a Lokayukta within a year of the Act's commencement but would decide its nature and design for themselves. On the CBI it proposed a package that stopped well short of the movement's merger and went well beyond the status quo: the Director of the CBI should be appointed by a collegium of the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha and the Chief Justice of India; the Lokpal should hold superintendence over the CBI in cases the Lokpal referred to it; officers investigating referred cases should not be transferred without the Lokpal's approval; and for referred cases the CBI should be able to engage prosecutors from a panel approved by the Lokpal. It tidied the eligibility language for members, so that a person "affiliated" to a political party, rather than the vaguer "connected", would be disqualified, and it left the committee-stage position on the Prime Minister's subject-matter exclusions undisturbed. The Cabinet, on 31 January 2013, accepted nearly all of this, declining only the requirement of Lokpal approval for transferring CBI officers on referred cases.

### What did the Rajya Sabha select committee change in the bill?

It removed the central bill's detailed Lokayukta blueprint, leaving states to design their own institutions under a one-year obligation; built a collegium for appointing the CBI Director and gave the Lokpal superintendence over cases it refers; and sharpened the bar on party-affiliated members, moving the bill from a text parties opposed to one they could pass.

Even with an agreed text, the calendar did not move for most of 2013, which is the final evidence for this article's claim about what moves Indian legislation. The bill waited through two sessions while governments calculated. Through the budget and monsoon sessions of 2013 it was repeatedly listed for consideration in the Rajya Sabha and repeatedly crowded out, in a Parliament whose working days were being consumed by disruptions over other questions, from statehood for Telangana to the allocation of coal blocks; the episode is a useful corrective to any account in which the December passage was a simple change of heart. Legislatures do not lack opinions on bills like this; they lack hours, and hours are allocated by the same political calculus that had starved the Lokpal since 1968. The select committee had removed the textual obstacles by January 2013. What December added was not agreement but urgency, and urgency in a legislature is a perishable commodity manufactured by events outside it. What changed the calculation in December 2013 was a conjunction. Hazare began another fast at Ralegan Siddhi on 10 December 2013, announcing that he would not end it until the bill passed, restoring the movement's deadline pressure in a reduced but real form. The results of the Delhi assembly election, declared on 8 December, had just delivered a shock that every party in Parliament could read: the Aam Aadmi Party, contesting its first election, won 28 of the 70 seats in the capital on an anti-corruption platform built from the movement's own material, and the Congress, in the words its own Law Minister Kapil Sibal used during the Rajya Sabha debate, had received a drubbing at its hands. A general election was due within months. In that atmosphere the Rajya Sabha took the bill up, debated it on 17 December 2013 with a degree of cross-party consensus absent two years earlier, Arun Jaitley for the opposition supporting the bill while recording his objection that its provision for representation of religious minorities among the members carried a constitutional vice, the Samajwadi Party opposing and absenting itself, and passed it with the select committee's amendments. The Lok Sabha adopted the amended bill the next day, 18 December 2013, by voice vote, over the walkout of the Samajwadi Party and the Shiv Sena. Hazare ended his nine-day fast, thanked Parliament and, in a judgment that captures his wing of the movement's settled position, said the law could control a substantial share of corruption, which he put at forty to fifty per cent, while warning that vigilance would decide what it achieved. Kejriwal and the Aam Aadmi Party took the opposite position in public, dismissing the enacted text as too weak to send the corrupt to jail, so the movement's two wings delivered opposite verdicts on the statute their joint campaign had produced. The opposition the bill did meet in December 2013 is worth recording accurately, because it defines the constituency the institution would have to live with. The Samajwadi Party's leader, Mulayam Singh Yadav, argued in the Lok Sabha that the bill was dangerous, that it would paralyse decision-making by exposing every official and legislator to investigation at a constable's instance, and he appealed to the Congress leadership to withdraw it; his party walked out in both Houses. The argument, that an ombudsman strong enough to matter is strong enough to frighten honest administration into inaction, had been made against every Lokpal bill since 1968 and would be made against the institution's working thereafter; in December 2013, for the first time, it was made and lost. The President, Pranab Mukherjee, the same minister who had chaired the drafting committee and moved the sense of the House, gave his assent on 1 January 2014, and the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 came into force on 16 January 2014.

## The demand-to-statute ledger

The cleanest way to see what the movement won and lost is to follow its demands through the four texts that successively carried them: the Jan Lokpal draft, the sense of the House resolution of August 2011, the Act as enacted in December 2013, and the position after enactment. The enacted scheme itself, its composition, jurisdiction and procedure, is set out in the series' guide to [the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013 explained](/2011/11/15/lokpal-lokayuktas-act-2013-explained/); what follows is concerned only with the movement from demand to outcome. Readers assembling the rival drafts for a lecture, a classroom or a debate can keep the committee-stage texts and presentations together and [open the presentation files in the ReportMedic viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) while working through the ledger row by row. Read down the third column and the narrowing performed by the legislative process is visible; read down the fourth and a second, quieter attrition appears, the attrition of implementation, in which a statute can be complete on paper for years before the institution it creates exists.

| Demand | The Jan Lokpal draft | The sense of the House, August 2011 | The 2013 Act | Position after enactment |

| The lower bureaucracy | Every central employee, Groups A to D, under the Lokpal directly | Agreed in principle, the lower bureaucracy to be brought under the Lokpal through an appropriate mechanism | All groups within the Lokpal's jurisdiction, with complaints against Group C and D officers routed through the Central Vigilance Commission for preliminary inquiry | The routing survives; the Commission, not the Lokpal, is the first forum for most complaints against junior staff |

The fourth column contains the passage history's last and most sobering lesson. The Act commenced in January 2014 with an obligation on the selection committee to appoint the first Lokpal, and the appointment did not happen for five years. The immediate obstacle was textual and political at once: no party had won the seats needed for a recognised Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha elected in 2014, and the government of the day maintained that appointments should wait for an amendment substituting the leader of the largest opposition party. Litigation, not mobilisation, moved the question: in Common Cause v. Union of India, decided on 27 April 2017, a bench of Justices Ranjan Gogoi and Navin Sinha held that the Act as it stood was an eminently workable piece of legislation, that the absence of a Leader of the Opposition was no justification for keeping its enforcement in suspension, and that the proposed amendment was a streamlining measure rather than a precondition. Even then, the first Lokpal, the retired Supreme Court judge Pinaki Chandra Ghose, was selected only on 17 March 2019 and sworn in days later, more than five years after the statute commenced and eight after the fasts. The demand-to-statute ledger therefore ends with a row that no column can hold: the movement won the law in two years, and the state took five more to staff it, at a pace set not by crowds, of which there were none left, but by a court judgment and an election calendar.

The states wrote the same lesson in smaller print. Section 63 of the Act obliged every state to establish a Lokayukta by its own law within one year of commencement, and the year passed with the obligation honoured unevenly: some states legislated, some delayed for years, and some moved in the opposite direction, diluting institutions they already possessed. Karnataka, whose Lokayukta had supplied the movement with its Exhibit A of a functioning ombudsman and with one of its five drafters, amended its Lokayukta law in 2016 to strip the institution of the independent police wing that had given its inquiries force, after a controversy over the conduct of the then Lokayukta's household had damaged the office. The episode is the fourth column in miniature: a legislature that had been content to maintain a strong institution while it embarrassed others discovered, when the institution became an embarrassment to itself, that statutory strength is only ever on loan from the politics of the moment. For the reader of this series, the generalisable rule is that enactment settles the text of an institution and nothing else; its staffing, its budget, its investigative machinery and its survival are re-decided continuously by the same political process that the text was meant to constrain, which is why passage histories that end at assent mislead.

The institution's subsequent biography continued in the same register. The rules governing the form in which a citizen may complain to the Lokpal were notified only in 2020, six years after commencement, and the office's public record in its first years was one of large numbers of complaints received and a small proportion taken to full inquiry, many closed at the threshold for defects of form, a pattern its critics read as the fourth column fulfilling the third column's cautions and its defenders as the ordinary infancy of a body building procedure from nothing. Justice Ghose completed his term and was succeeded as chairperson in March 2024 by Justice A. M. Khanwilkar, another retired judge of the Supreme Court, through the same selection machinery whose composition the movement had contested in 2011. Nothing in that record is dramatic, and the absence of drama is itself the data point: the Lokpal became, within a decade, a working part of the administrative landscape, neither the engine of purification its drafters promised nor the threat to governance its opponents forecast, and the distance between those two forecasts is the measure of what the legislative process did to the movement's design between 2011 and 2013.

The ledger method is worth a word of defence, because it disciplines arguments that rhetoric otherwise wins by default. A demand is a measurable thing only when it is followed across texts: what the movement's draft said, what Parliament said in principle, what the enrolled Act says, and what the institution did once staffed. Most public argument about 2011 compares the first column with an imagined version of the third, or celebrates the third without reading the fourth, and both errors flatter their makers. The record assembled here supports a colder and more useful habit. Where the columns agree, as they broadly do on the Prime Minister's formal inclusion, the movement may claim a victory of principle; where the second column's generous phrases narrow in the third, as with the lower bureaucracy's "appropriate mechanism", the legislative process has done its ordinary work of translation and loss; and where the fourth column diverges from the third, as with the five unstaffed years, the failure belongs neither to the movement nor to the drafters but to the continuing politics that every statute must survive after its historians have closed the file.

## Two mythologies: what the movement did and did not do

The first mythology is the movement's own triumphalism, the claim that the people wrote the law. The ledger refutes it clause by clause. Of the demands the movement ranked highest, control of the investigating agency was refused outright, the citizens' charter was exported to a bill that died, the judiciary was excluded, the selection process was returned to a committee of officeholders of the familiar kind, and the lower bureaucracy reached the Lokpal only through the vigilance machinery the movement distrusted. What Parliament enacted in 2013 was recognisably descended from the government's drafts and the two committees' reports, not from the Jan Lokpal text, and its genealogy runs through the Standing Committee's architecture and the select committee's amendments. A movement that had dictated its text would not have split over whether to accept the result.

The second mythology is the dismissal, common among the movement's critics, that it achieved nothing but a political party. That is equally untenable against the record. A statute that had failed for forty-five years, under governments of every complexion, was enacted; a resolution committing Parliament in principle to three of the movement's propositions was adopted by acclamation in both Houses; a select committee rewrote the bill in directions, on the CBI's directorship and the Lokpal's superintendence, that no pre-2011 draft had contemplated; and the Prime Minister of India stands within an ombudsman's jurisdiction, with safeguards, because the politics of 2011 made his exclusion untenable, the government having been forced to concede in its December 2011 bill the very point its August bill had refused. The honest verdict is the one the evidence supports and this article has argued throughout: the movement changed what it was politically possible to leave undone, and the legislative process changed almost everything else.

A third account of the movement's legacy, neither triumphalist nor dismissive, holds that its most durable product was not the statute at all but the demonstration that corruption could be converted into electoral capital by a new political entrant. On this reading, the Aam Aadmi Party's first Delhi campaign, fought within a year of its formation on the movement's own themes and personnel, and its victory over both national parties in the capital in 2015, taught every Indian party that the anti-corruption constituency was real, organisable and available to whoever credibly claimed it; the general election of 2014 was then fought by the winning side substantially on the corruption record of the incumbent coalition. The statute-first reading and the party-first reading are not easily separated, since the party was itself an argument about the statute, a claim that only electoral power could finish what pressure had started; but they predict different futures, and the subsequent record, in which the party governed Delhi while its own promised Jan Lokpal legislation for the capital stalled in disputes with the Centre, suggests that the conversion of movement capital into governing power obeys the same law as the conversion of demands into statutes. Pressure, in office as on the street, moves calendars more readily than texts.

Two further contested stories belong to the record rather than to either mythology. The first is the allegation that the movement was not what it claimed to be but an engineered operation. The Congress general secretary Digvijaya Singh alleged publicly that the Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh stood behind the campaign, publicising a letter in which the Sangh's general secretary Suresh Joshi had extended support to Hazare's efforts against corruption, and a party spokesman wondered aloud about a foreign hand in the protests. Team Anna rejected the allegations, Kejriwal responding that Hazare was an agent of the country and that the charges were a diversion from the bill; the Sangh's documented position was one of declared support for anti-corruption efforts, and no evidence of the Sangh directing the campaign, choosing its demands or funding its operations was produced in the controversy. The allegation is recorded here as an allegation, made by named political actors for intelligible political reasons, and it has never been adjudicated into a finding in any forum. The second story is the mirror image, the claim of a spontaneous mass awakening, which the organisational record contradicts: the campaign rested on a decade of prior right-to-information work, a pre-drafted bill, professional organisers and media management of a high order, and the comparison with the transparency campaign makes the point best, because [the legislative history of the Right to Information Act](/2011/09/15/rti-act-legislative-history/) shows a movement that spent fifteen years building state laws village by village before it won a central statute, while the Lokpal campaign compressed its ascent into a single year of national mobilisation and paid for the compression in the drafting room. Which of the two movements achieved more, and by which instrument, is the question the series takes up directly in [the comparison of the RTI and the Lokpal](/2012/01/15/rti-vs-lokpal-comparison/).

There remains the hardest question, which the brief of this history requires to be asked rather than evaded: whether the movement's diagnosis was ever right, whether a single powerful ombudsman is an instrument that addresses corruption in a state of India's scale. The critics' case, put by Pratap Bhanu Mehta at the height of the agitation, was that the Jan Lokpal imagined an institution concentrating investigative, prosecutorial and grievance functions in a body answerable to no electorate, replicating at a higher level the unaccountability it was built to cure, and that corruption rooted in discretionary licensing, political finance and weak local institutions would not yield to a super-watchdog in Delhi. The movement's answer was the ARC's original one, that citizens facing a corrupt administration need one address of undeniable independence, and that the proliferation of agencies answerable to the executive is precisely the disease. The passage record cannot settle that dispute, but the implementation record leans against the stronger version of the movement's hope: the enacted Lokpal, shorn of the CBI and the charter, is a narrower institution than the diagnosis required, and even it stood unstaffed for five years, which suggests that the binding constraint on Indian anti-corruption was never the absence of a statute alone.

The comparative evidence the movement itself marshalled deserves a hearing in this argument, because it was the empirical core of its case and its critics' silence about it was conspicuous. The movement's speakers repeatedly cited Hong Kong's Independent Commission Against Corruption, created in 1974, as proof that a single, independent, well-armed agency could transform the integrity of an administration within a generation, and Singapore's older anti-corruption bureau in the same sense. The critics' replies, when they came, were about scale and structure: Hong Kong is a city, India a subcontinent of governments; the Hong Kong agency operated within a colonial and then a special administrative executive that willed its success; and neither example involved an ombudsman set over an elected head of government in a parliamentary federation. Both the citation and the reply are recorded here because they mark the honest limits of the comparative method in institutional design: foreign success proves an institution can work somewhere, under someone, and nothing about whether it will work in the soil into which a particular Parliament, in a particular December, transplants it. India's Lokpal would be tested, as it has been, on Indian evidence alone.

## What the episode changed in Indian lawmaking

Beyond the statute, the passage left procedural residue that outlasts the Lokpal itself, and a legislative history should record it. The joint drafting committee, whatever its constitutional critics established, demonstrated that a draft prepared outside the ministry system could be completed, published and debated clause by clause against the government's text, and the experiment normalised the public circulation of rival drafts as an instrument of legislative politics: thereafter, campaigns on subjects from land acquisition to data protection arrived with their own texts in hand, expecting to negotiate from them. The government's side of the ledger moved too. In February 2014 the Union government adopted a pre-legislative consultative policy requiring that draft legislation be published for public comment before introduction, with the department's responses to the comments recorded; the policy's enforcement has been uneven, but its existence is a direct institutional descendant of the argument of 2011, in which the state's defence, that consultation happens through its own channels, had collapsed in public. The sense of the House resolution likewise entered the repertoire as a device for closing a confrontation with a movement without enacting its text, available to future governments facing future fasts. And the select committee's rescue of the bill, after its midnight failure, strengthened the standing of the committee system as the place where Indian legislation is actually finished, a development traced across subjects in the article linked earlier. Movements are usually credited or blamed for laws; this one should also be debited and credited with procedures, since the way Indian bills get drafted, published and rescued bears its fingerprints in places the statute book does not show.

## How this history is documented, and why the accounts quarrel

A passage this contested leaves a documentary record with traps in it, and the reader who uses this article's anchors should know where they lie. The firmest stratum is the parliamentary one: the texts of the successive bills, the reports of the joint, standing and select committees with their dissent notes, and the debates of August and December 2011 and December 2013, all of which fix what was proposed, promised and enacted, and on which questions of date, composition and wording this article has relied throughout. The second stratum is the contemporaneous analytical record, of which the comparisons prepared by PRS Legislative Research are the most useful, because they were written before the outcome was known and therefore record the drafts as they stood rather than as memory improved them. The third stratum is the participants' literature, the movement's own published accounts, the memoirs and interviews of ministers and campaigners, and the journalism of the fasts, which is indispensable for motive and atmosphere and unreliable in predictable directions: the movement's accounts enlarge its unity and the purity of its mandate, the government's accounts enlarge its reasonableness and the movement's intransigence, and both compress the forty-five years before 2011 into a darkness from which their own year emerges as dawn. Even the elementary facts quarrel across these strata, as the reader has seen: the number of failed bills varies with the counter's convention, the day of the first bill's introduction in May 1968 is given differently by respectable sources, and the authorship of the first civil-society draft of 1968 is claimed in accounts written long afterwards. The discipline this article has followed, and which it recommends to any writer who follows it into this material, is to take dates and texts from the parliamentary stratum, mechanisms from the analytical stratum, and colour from the participants, attributed to its maker every time. A legislative history built the other way round, colour first and texts afterwards, is how the mythologies this article has tried to separate are manufactured.

## Verdict: pressure moves the calendar, not the text

The Lokpal's passage is the clearest demonstration in this series that the distance between a demand and a statute is where legislative history actually lives. Forty-five years of failure and two years of passage were both produced by the same institutions working in their ordinary way; what differed was the price of inaction. The movement's achievement was real and precisely bounded: it made delay expensive, extracted a parliamentary commitment in principle, forced the Prime Minister's inclusion onto the government's own bill, and kept the question alive through a midnight adjournment and a select committee year until a changed electoral season let the text through. Its limits were equally real: on the investigating agency, the charter, the judiciary and the selection process, the clauses it cared about most were settled in committee rooms by the ordinary methods of Indian lawmaking, and largely against it. A reader measuring any future movement should therefore ask the question this history has asked of 2011: not whether a law was passed, but which clauses survived, and then check the fourth column, because a statute's biography does not end at assent.

The passage record also predicts, with uncomfortable precision, the kind of institution the Act would produce. A law enacted at the end of a forty-five-year queue, by a Parliament acting under deadline in the last winter of its term, on a text assembled from two committee reports and a resolution adopted to end a fast, will be strong where the committees were strong and thin where the politics were thin: in this case, comprehensive in jurisdiction, elaborate in safeguards, cautious in machinery, and dependent for its life on appointments that the political class had no movement left to fear for delaying. That is the Lokpal India received. Whether a differently sequenced campaign, slower, state by state, clause by clause, on the transparency movement's model, would have produced a stronger institution is a genuine question the record leaves open; the Lokpal campaign chose speed, and speed is what it got, together with everything speed costs. For the three readers this history most concerns, the yield is different in each case, and worth stating separately. The historian gets a controlled experiment in Indian lawmaking: the same text, in substance, failing for forty-five years and passing in two, with the variable isolated, which is as close as political history comes to laboratory conditions. The legislator or staffer gets a manual of the corridor: where bills actually die in India, in the timetable rather than on the floor; what a committee can rescue and what it cannot; what a resolution can promise and what it cannot bind; and what it costs a government, in precedent, to buy peace from a movement at the drafting table. The journalist gets a set of anchors against the compression that retrospectives practise on this story, the distinction between the ninth introduction and the eight failures, between a drafting committee that advised and a Parliament that decided, between a resolution in principle and a statute in force, between a law's enactment in 2013 and an institution's staffing in 2019. What follows in these pages is legal and legislative history, offered for understanding rather than as guidance on any individual grievance, for which the institutions described, and professional advice on the facts of the case, are the proper channels.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many times did a Lokpal Bill fail before 2013?

Eight times on the strictest count, and the count is the first thing to be careful about. Counting only bills formally introduced in the Lok Sabha, there were eight before 2011: in 1968, 1971, 1977, 1985, 1989, 1996, 1998 and 2001. Seven of them lapsed when the Lok Sabha of the day was dissolved, and the 1985 bill was withdrawn by the government that had introduced it. The bill of 2011 was therefore the ninth introduction, and it is the one that eventually passed. Higher figures in circulation, of ten or eleven failures, reach their totals by also counting drafts that were prepared inside government but never introduced, or by counting the replacement bill of December 2011 as a separate attempt.

### Q: What was the Jan Lokpal Bill and how did it differ?

The Jan Lokpal Bill was the complete draft statute prepared by the Hazare campaign, principally by Justice Santosh Hegde, Prashant Bhushan and Arvind Kejriwal, and offered to the government as the text it wanted enacted. It differed from the government's 2011 bill on nearly every contested point. It covered a sitting Prime Minister, the higher judiciary, members of Parliament even for speeches and votes, and every government employee down to the lowest grade, where the government bill covered the Prime Minister only after he left office, excluded judges and parliamentary conduct, and stopped at Group A officers. It placed the CBI's anti-corruption work under the Lokpal, where the government bill gave the Lokpal its own investigation wing and left the CBI with the executive. And it wrote citizens' charters and grievance redressal into the law, which the government bill omitted entirely.

### Q: Who sat on the 2011 joint drafting committee for the Lokpal Bill?

Ten people, named in the Gazette notification of 8 April 2011 that constituted the committee after Hazare's April fast. The five ministers were Pranab Mukherjee, the Finance Minister, who chaired it; P. Chidambaram, the Home Minister; Veerappa Moily, the Law Minister, who was its convenor; Kapil Sibal; and Salman Khurshid. The five nominees of Hazare were Hazare himself; Justice N. Santosh Hegde, the Karnataka Lokayukta and a former Supreme Court judge; Shanti Bhushan, the former Law Minister, who was co-chairperson; his son Prashant Bhushan, the advocate; and Arvind Kejriwal. The committee met nine times between April and June 2011 and closed without an agreed draft, each side sending its own version to the Cabinet.

### Q: What did the sense of the House resolution on the Lokpal Bill promise?

Less than the celebrations suggested, and more than a press statement. Adopted by both Houses on 27 August 2011, after a day of debate and without a vote, the resolution recorded that the House agreed in principle on three issues: a citizens' charter, the lower bureaucracy to be brought under the Lokpal through an appropriate mechanism, and the establishment of Lokayuktas in the states. Finance Minister Pranab Mukherjee, who moved it, asked that the proceedings be sent to the Standing Committee examining the bill. It was not legislation and it bound no one in law; the phrases "in principle" and "appropriate mechanism" left the design of each commitment open, which is why the following two years were spent arguing over what honouring it required.

### Q: Why did the Lokpal Bill stall in the Rajya Sabha in 2011?

Because the government could pass the bill in the Lok Sabha but could not control the Rajya Sabha, and on the night of 29 December 2011 the two facts collided. The ruling coalition lacked a majority in the upper House; more than 180 amendments stood moved against the bill, including a large set from its own ally the Trinamool Congress, which objected on federal grounds to a central law designing state Lokayuktas; and opposition parties were pressing amendments on the CBI that the government did not want to lose on. The Parliamentary Affairs Minister sought time to process the amendments, the Leader of the Opposition Arun Jaitley accused the government of choreographing the debate to avoid a vote it would lose, and at midnight the Chairman adjourned the House sine die with no vote taken.

### Q: What finally forced the Lokpal Bill through in 2013?

No single event, but a conjunction that changed every party's calculation in the same fortnight. The Rajya Sabha select committee had already rewritten the bill into a form its opponents could accept, removing the central Lokayukta blueprint and adding the CBI safeguards, so the text was no longer the obstacle. In December 2013 Hazare began another fast at Ralegan Siddhi, restoring the movement's deadline pressure. On 8 December the Delhi election results showed the Aam Aadmi Party, born of the movement, winning 28 seats in the capital on the anti-corruption issue, weeks before a general election was due. With the electoral cost of further delay suddenly measurable, the Rajya Sabha passed the bill on 17 December and the Lok Sabha on 18 December 2013.

### Q: Who were the main figures in the anti-corruption movement?

The public core was the group the press called Team Anna. Anna Hazare, the former soldier and Ralegan Siddhi activist, was its face and its fasting instrument. Arvind Kejriwal, the former revenue officer and right to information campaigner, was its chief organiser and strategist. Shanti Bhushan, a former Law Minister, and his son Prashant Bhushan, the Supreme Court advocate, supplied its legal drafting and argument. Justice N. Santosh Hegde, the Karnataka Lokayukta, brought the credibility of a working ombudsman. Kiran Bedi, the former police officer, was a leading campaigner and public voice, though not a member of the drafting committee. Around them stood a wider circle of volunteers, religious figures and supporting activists who built the crowds in cities across the country.

### Q: Did the final Lokpal Act match what the movement demanded?

Only in part, and the two wings of the movement answered the question oppositely, which is itself the evidence. The Act delivered a Lokpal covering the Prime Minister, though with subject-matter exclusions and safeguards the Jan Lokpal draft had not proposed; it covered all grades of central employee, though with junior staff routed through the Central Vigilance Commission; and it required every state to establish a Lokayukta. It refused the movement's demands for control of the CBI, for the higher judiciary, for members' conduct in Parliament and for citizens' charters inside the Act, and its selection committee was far narrower than the draft's. Hazare welcomed the Act and ended his fast; Kejriwal's Aam Aadmi Party dismissed it as too weak to jail the corrupt.

### Q: What was India Against Corruption?

India Against Corruption, usually shortened to IAC, was the campaign platform under which the 2011 movement organised. It was not a political party or a membership organisation with branches in the ordinary sense; it was a banner and a network, assembled through 2010 around the demand for a Jan Lokpal law by activists who already knew one another from the right to information movement and allied campaigns. Kejriwal and his associates supplied its organisation, volunteer lists and media operation, Hazare supplied its public face, and endorsements from religious leaders, retired officials and citizen groups widened its reach. Its own description of itself was a people's movement against corruption; the record shows a small, experienced campaign core that had prepared its draft and its networks well before the fasts began.

### Q: What led to Anna Hazare's April 2011 fast in Delhi?

A specific refusal, on top of a season of scandal. Through 2010 and into 2011 a series of corruption episodes, the spectrum allocation report of the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Commonwealth Games allegations and the Adarsh housing affair, had made corruption the dominant public question. The India Against Corruption campaign had used those months to press one precise institutional demand: that its Jan Lokpal draft be taken up through a joint drafting committee of ministers and movement nominees, so the text could not be diluted inside a ministry. Hazare wrote to the Prime Minister and campaign representatives met ministers, but the government offered only the ordinary promise of a bill in due course. Hazare announced that without the committee he would fast at Jantar Mantar from 5 April 2011, and he did.

### Q: What happened during the August 2011 fast at Ramlila Maidan?

The fast began in a jail cell. On the morning of 16 August 2011, Delhi Police detained Hazare before he could start his announced fast, after he rejected the conditions imposed on the protest site; a magistrate remanded him to Tihar Jail for seven days when he refused a release bond, and he began fasting there. Protests spread nationally, the government ordered his release the same night, and Hazare refused to leave until an unconditional venue was granted. On 19 August he moved to Ramlila Maidan and continued the fast before growing crowds. Negotiations and an all-party meeting followed; on 27 August both Houses debated the question and adopted the sense of the House resolution on his three points; on 28 August, after twelve days, Hazare ended the fast.

### Q: Where did the idea of the Lokpal come from?

From the Scandinavian ombudsman, by way of a commission report. Sweden created a parliamentary ombudsman in the early nineteenth century, and the institution, an independent officer investigating citizens' complaints against the administration, spread through northern Europe and the Commonwealth. In India the Law Minister Ashoke Kumar Sen proposed a constitutional ombudsman in the early 1960s; the member of Parliament L. M. Singhvi coined the name Lokpal for it in a debate in 1963; and the first Administrative Reforms Commission, in its interim report of October 1966 on the redress of citizens' grievances, recommended a Lokpal at the Centre and Lokayuktas in the states, appending a draft bill. Every Lokpal bill from 1968 to 2013 descends from that report.

### Q: What happened to the Lokpal Bill introduced in 1989?

It broke new ground and then died the family's usual death. The bill was introduced by the National Front government of V. P. Singh, which had come to power at the end of 1989 on a platform that made corruption a central charge against its predecessor, and it was the first Lokpal bill to bring the Prime Minister within the ombudsman's purview, a step the 1968 bill had declined and most later government drafts resisted. The bill was referred to a parliamentary standing committee for examination. Before the process could produce a law, the Ninth Lok Sabha was dissolved amid the collapse of the National Front experiment, and the bill lapsed with it, leaving the Prime Minister's inclusion as an argument for later decades rather than an enacted fact.

### Q: Why do accounts differ on how many times the Lokpal Bill failed?

Because the counters are counting different things. The firm record is of eight bills formally introduced in the Lok Sabha before 2011, in 1968, 1971, 1977, 1985, 1989, 1996, 1998 and 2001, each of which lapsed or was withdrawn. Totals of ten or eleven failures appear when writers also count drafts that were prepared within government but never introduced in Parliament, of which a 2010 draft is the clearest example, or when they treat the government's replacement bill of December 2011 as a separate attempt from the bill introduced that August, or when they count the Jan Lokpal draft itself as a failed bill although it was never introduced at all. The discrepancy is one of bookkeeping, not of substance: on any count, no introduced Lokpal bill became law before 2013.

### Q: What corruption scandals formed the backdrop to the 2011 movement?

Three episodes dominated the season, and they should be stated with their outcomes kept separate from their political effect. The Comptroller and Auditor General's report of November 2010 on second-generation spectrum allocation put a presumptive loss of Rs 1.76 lakh crore on a discretionary licensing process; the figure was contested from the outset, the telecom minister A. Raja resigned, and the criminal trial that followed ended in acquittals years later. Allegations around the contracts and organisation of the Delhi Commonwealth Games of October 2010 led to the arrest of the organising committee chairman Suresh Kalmadi in April 2011. And the Adarsh housing society affair in Mumbai, involving flats allotted to politicians and officials from a project promoted for defence families, forced the resignation of Maharashtra's Chief Minister Ashok Chavan in November 2010.

### Q: How was Baba Ramdev's 2011 protest different from Anna Hazare's movement?

They shared a season and an enemy, corruption, and differed in almost everything else. Ramdev, a yoga teacher with a large television following and his own organisation, campaigned principally on black money held abroad, demanding its declaration as a national asset and its repatriation; the Hazare movement campaigned for one institution, the Lokpal, on the basis of a finished draft. Ramdev began his own fast at Ramlila Maidan on 4 June 2011, between Hazare's two fasts, and the state's response was the opposite of August: police cleared the ground by force in the middle of the night of 4 and 5 June, injuring supporters, and Ramdev was flown out of Delhi, an episode of which the Supreme Court later took critical notice. His campaign had no drafting committee, no bill and no parliamentary endgame.

### Q: How did the anti-corruption movement lead to the formation of a political party?

Through a disagreement about means that the movement's failure to dictate the bill's text made unavoidable. After the December 2011 stall, Kejriwal and his colleagues concluded that pressure from outside had reached its limit and that the movement should contest elections itself; Hazare held that the movement must remain above parties, both to preserve its moral claim and because he distrusted electoral politics as a solvent of movements. A further fast in the summer of 2012 ended with an announcement that the campaign would offer a political alternative; in September 2012 the two camps publicly parted; Kejriwal announced the party's formation on 2 October 2012; and the Aam Aadmi Party was formally launched on 26 November 2012, with Hazare outside it and figures such as Kiran Bedi and Santosh Hegde also declining the political route.

### Q: Why did the government table its own Lokpal Bill in August 2011 rather than the Jan Lokpal draft?

Because, in its stated position, drafting law is the government's and Parliament's function, and the joint committee had been a consultation, not a transfer of that function. The committee closed in June 2011 without agreement, each side sending its own draft to the Cabinet; the Cabinet in July adopted the government's text, which its ministers defended as the workable version, leaving the Prime Minister out while in office, keeping the judiciary and the CBI outside the Lokpal, and omitting the charter. The government also had parliamentary reasons: it doubted a bill on Jan Lokpal lines could pass either House, and it preferred to negotiate from its own text through the Standing Committee. The movement read the choice as the dilution it had predicted, and answered with the August fast.

### Q: How did television and social media shape the 2011 Lokpal movement?

They set its tempo. The movement was the first Indian campaign of its scale to run simultaneously as a street protest, a continuous television event and an online mobilisation: news channels carried the fasts as running stories with daily deadlines, which converted each day of government inaction into a broadcast verdict, and the campaign's networks used the internet and mobile messaging to summon supportive crowds in city after city without owning any organisation there. The visual language, the white Gandhi cap, the tricolour, the slogan identifying the supporter with Hazare himself, was designed for cameras and travelled through them. Time magazine counted the campaign among the ten leading news stories of 2011 worldwide. The same machinery had a cost the movement discovered later: attention of that intensity is fast to gather and fast to move on.

### Q: Who was Anna Hazare before the 2011 movement?

A regional figure of considerable standing whom most of urban India had not yet heard of. Kisan Baburao Hazare, called Anna, an elder brother's form of address in Marathi, served as a soldier before returning to his village of Ralegan Siddhi in Maharashtra's Ahmednagar district, where from the mid-1970s he led a transformation built on watershed development, water conservation, prohibition of alcohol and village self-discipline that made the village a standard citation in development literature; the Padma Shri followed in 1990 and the Padma Bhushan in 1992. Within Maharashtra he was equally known for agitation: through the 1990s and 2000s he used fasts and campaigns against ministers he accused of corruption and in pressing the state for a strong right to information law, refining the method he later applied in Delhi.
