In the winter of 639 a force of somewhere between three and four thousand men crossed into Egypt from the east. Egypt at that moment was the richest province of the Byzantine empire, the source of grain that fed a capital of hundreds of thousands, defended by fortifications, garrisons and a fleet, held by a state that had just won a twenty six year war against Persia. Within three years the province had changed hands permanently, and it has never returned to the empire that lost it. Explaining how that happened is the task of this article, and the explanation that fits the evidence is not primarily a military one.

The claim defended here has a name: the unloved Byzantium thesis, that the conquest succeeded as fast as it did because imperial rule in Egypt had spent two centuries alienating the Christian majority over doctrine and had exhausted the province through war, occupation and taxation, so that when the invasion came there was very little will to defend the existing arrangement. That is not the same as saying Egyptians welcomed the invaders, a claim examined and rejected below. It is the weaker and better supported proposition that a state which has forfeited the loyalty of its subjects can hold a province only for as long as its army holds the field, and that the Byzantine army in Egypt was in no condition to hold anything.

The most important thing to fix before beginning is the distinction between two events that popular accounts routinely merge. The military conquest was fast, taking three years, and it was decisive. The transformation of Egypt into a majority Muslim, Arabic speaking country was slow, taking somewhere between four and seven centuries depending on which measure is used, and it happened through mechanisms almost entirely unconnected to the campaign of 639 to 642. Confusing the two produces the single commonest error in this subject, which is the assumption that an army arriving in 640 explains what Egypt looked like in 1000.

## Dating the Conquest

The chronology is firmer here than for most subjects in this series, because the events fall in a period with documentary evidence, several narrative sources and a datable papyrus, and because the campaign was short enough to be tracked season by season.

The invasion began in late 639 with the crossing of the eastern frontier. The first target was the fortified town at the eastern edge of the Delta that guarded the approach from Palestine, and it held out for a period of weeks before falling early in 640. The force then moved westward and southward through the Delta's eastern margin, taking smaller places as it went.

The decisive field engagement came in the summer of 640 near the ancient site at the apex of the Delta, after reinforcements had roughly tripled the invading force. The Byzantine field army was defeated there, and with it went any realistic prospect of stopping the campaign in open country.

The siege of the great fortress at the head of the Delta, the position that controlled the junction of the river and the routes into both Upper Egypt and the Delta, ran from the autumn of 640 to the spring of 641 and ended in surrender. This was the hinge of the whole campaign.

Negotiations for Alexandria followed through 641, complicated by the death of the emperor in Constantinople and by the recall and subsequent return of the imperial official conducting them. A treaty was agreed late in 641 providing for a truce of about eleven months, the payment of tribute, and the evacuation of Byzantine forces. The city was handed over in the autumn of 642 without a final assault.

A Byzantine expedition retook Alexandria in 645, and the city was recovered by the Arab commander in 646, after which no further imperial attempt was made.

### How firm are these dates?

Firmer than for most of the earlier periods in this series. The events fall within a few years, several narrative sources give datable sequences, and a bilingual receipt on papyrus from 643 fixes the administration in operation. Disagreements between sources are about months and details rather than about the framework.

The documentary anchor deserves particular mention because it is the sort of evidence that settles arguments. A bilingual receipt written in Greek and Arabic, dated to 643 and issued in Middle Egypt for the requisition of sheep, survives on papyrus. It is among the earliest dated Arabic documents in existence, and it shows the new administration operating routinely in the province within a year of the fall of Alexandria, in the language of the old administration alongside the language of the new. A single scrap of paperwork establishes more about the practical reality of the transition than any chronicle.

The reason the dates matter is that the compression is the story. Three years from the eastern frontier to the handover of the second city of the empire, against a state that had just defeated Persia, is an outcome requiring explanation, and the explanation cannot be that the invading force was large.

## Egypt on the Eve

Understanding why the province fell so fast requires knowing what had happened to it in the preceding forty years, and the answer is that it had been comprehensively wrecked.

The long war between the Byzantine and Persian empires, running for more than a quarter century, drained both. Egypt was not a peripheral theatre in it. Persian forces occupied the province for roughly a decade, taking Alexandria and holding the country until the peace settlement returned it. An occupation of that length disrupts administration, damages infrastructure, displaces populations and empties treasuries, and the province that returned to imperial control was not the province that had been lost.

The restoration was recent when the invasion came. Byzantine authority had been reestablished for barely a decade, and the reconstruction of the administrative and military apparatus was incomplete. Officials were new, arrangements were provisional, and the memory of how quickly the province had changed hands once already was fresh.

Plague had struck repeatedly across the eastern Mediterranean from the middle of the previous century, in waves that recurred for two hundred years, and the demographic effect on the empire's manpower and revenue was severe even where it cannot be quantified.

Taxation had been heavy throughout, and the demands of war finance made it heavier. The grain requisition that fed the capital continued regardless of local conditions, because the political survival of the imperial government depended on feeding the city.

The military position was thin. Egypt had not been a frontier province for centuries and was not garrisoned as one. Its forces were dispersed among fortified points, its field strength was limited, and reinforcement from the east was impossible because the eastern provinces were themselves being lost in the same years. Syria fell before Egypt, which meant that by 639 there was no reserve to draw on and no relieving army that could plausibly arrive.

Set against that background, the arrival of a small force from the east looks less like an improbable upset and more like the last push against a structure already close to collapse.

## The Religious Estrangement

The factor that distinguishes Egypt from a simple story of military exhaustion is the state of the relationship between the imperial government and the Egyptian Christian majority, and it had been deteriorating for nearly two centuries.

The doctrinal settlement of the middle of the fifth century produced a formula that the great majority of Egyptian Christians rejected, and the empire committed itself to enforcing it. The mechanism was ecclesiastical: patriarchs who accepted the settlement were installed with imperial backing, the alternative hierarchy was treated as illegitimate, and periods of severe pressure alternated with periods of attempted compromise. Egypt maintained its own line of patriarchs regardless, producing two parallel church structures, one recognized by the empire and one commanding the loyalty of the population. That history is treated in the article on [the Council of Chalcedon and Egypt](/2009/06/01/chalcedon-and-egypt-church-split/).

The decade immediately before the invasion was among the worst. The imperial government pursued a new compromise formula and sent to Alexandria an official who combined ecclesiastical and civil authority, charged with securing conformity. Egyptian tradition remembers that period as harsh, with pressure applied to clergy and monasteries and the Egyptian patriarch driven into hiding for more than a decade. Byzantine sources present the same period very differently, which is normal for this material.

### Did religious grievance decide the outcome?

Not by itself. It removed the will to defend the existing arrangement rather than producing active support for the invaders. A population that will not volunteer, will not inform, will not fund and will not fight for its government leaves that government dependent entirely on its regular forces, which in Egypt were insufficient.

The practical consequences of alienation in a military campaign are worth spelling out, because they are frequently left vague. A hostile or indifferent population does not supply intelligence about enemy movements. It does not raise local levies. It does not volunteer labour for fortification. It does not conceal or feed retreating soldiers. It does not resist requisition by the invader with the energy it would show toward an occupier it hated more than its own government. None of this requires anyone to change sides, and all of it degrades a defence severely.

The counter argument deserves airing because it is serious. The Egyptian Christian population had no reason to expect better treatment from an unfamiliar power arriving from the desert, the terms of the eventual settlement could not have been predicted in advance, and the sources record Egyptians fighting the invaders in several places. Estrangement from a government is not the same as preference for its enemy, and treating the two as equivalent is exactly the overreach this article warns against.

The reconciliation between these positions is the position taken here. The evidence supports an absence of loyalty rather than a presence of enthusiasm, and that absence is sufficient to explain the speed of the collapse without requiring any claim about welcome.

## The Campaign

The military narrative is short and its shape is informative, because it shows a force that was small, mobile and dependent on the enemy's inability to concentrate.

The invading force entered through the eastern approach, the traditional invasion route into Egypt used for millennia, and took the fortified town guarding it after a siege of some weeks. That first operation is revealing: a force of a few thousand needed weeks to reduce a frontier town, which establishes both that the invaders had no significant siege capability and that Byzantine fortifications still functioned.

Movement thereafter was rapid and avoided the strongest positions. The force pushed along the eastern edge of the Delta, taking or bypassing smaller places, and reached the region at the apex of the Delta where the river divides. The choice of route was sound: it avoided the fortified Delta interior with its waterways and its Byzantine naval advantage, and it aimed at the point that controlled communication between Upper Egypt and the Delta.

Reinforcement was decisive. A second contingent roughly tripled the invading strength before the main field engagement, and its arrival is the moment the campaign became viable. Even at full strength the force numbered in the low tens of thousands at most, and probably considerably less.

The field battle near the ancient sun temple site north of the old capital broke the Byzantine field army in the summer of 640. Its consequences were structural rather than dramatic: after it, the remaining Byzantine forces were shut up in fortified places, and a defence conducted entirely from behind walls, without a field army to relieve sieges or threaten a besieger's rear, is a defence that can only lose slowly.

Upper Egypt was secured with comparatively little fighting, which is itself evidence about the state of the province. A country with a hostile population and an intact administration does not fall apart behind an invading army; a country with an alienated population and a collapsing administration does.

## The Siege of Babylon

The fortress at the head of the Delta is the pivot of the whole campaign and deserves the fullest treatment, because everything before it was preliminary and everything after it was consequence.

The position was old. A Roman fortress had stood there since the early imperial period, built to control the point where the Nile valley opens into the Delta and where the routes east toward Sinai and Palestine met the river. Its walls were massive, its towers substantial, and parts of them still stand within the modern city, sunk below the present ground level because the city has risen around them. It was the strongest fortification in Egypt outside Alexandria and it held the strategic key to the country.

The siege ran through the winter, roughly seven months from the autumn of 640 to the spring of 641. That duration is the strongest evidence available about the invading force's capabilities: an army that could take a first class fortress quickly would have done so, and one that needed seven months and eventually obtained a negotiated surrender was operating at the limit of what it could do. The picture of an irresistible force sweeping through Egypt does not survive contact with this siege.

Negotiations ran alongside the fighting, conducted from the Byzantine side by the official who combined ecclesiastical and civil authority in the province. His conduct is one of the most argued questions in the whole subject. Egyptian and later Arabic sources present him as pursuing accommodation, and imperial authorities recalled him at one point on suspicion of exceeding his instructions, which suggests Constantinople took the same view. Whether he was a realist assessing an unwinnable position or a man who had lost confidence in the empire he served is not recoverable from the sources.

The fortress surrendered in the spring of 641 on terms. With it went the centre of the country, the crossing point, and any coherent connection between the Byzantine positions in the Delta and anything upriver.

The immediate consequence was the establishment of the invaders' camp beside the fortress, on ground that would become the new capital of the province and eventually grow into the modern city. That story belongs to the article on the founding of Fustat, and what matters here is the strategic logic: the new administration planted itself at the same point the Romans had fortified, because the geography that made the site important had not changed.

## Alexandria: Why the Greatest City Was Not Stormed

The fall of Alexandria is the least military event in a military campaign, and understanding why illuminates the whole conquest.

Alexandria was, on paper, close to impregnable to the force besieging it. Its walls were formidable, its position between the sea and a lake made encirclement difficult, and above all it could be supplied indefinitely by sea. The Byzantine fleet was uncontested; the invaders had no navy. A city that can be resupplied by water cannot be starved by an army on land, and an army without siege engineering cannot break walls of that class. By every ordinary measure Alexandria should have held.

It was handed over by treaty instead, and the reasons were political rather than military. The emperor died early in 641, and the succession in Constantinople was contested and unstable for months, which meant that the person on the spot had no clear authority to appeal to and no prospect of a relief expedition being organized. The official negotiating had already concluded that the province could not be held. And the terms available were survivable: tribute, evacuation of the imperial forces, and guarantees for the population.

### Why did a defensible city surrender?

Because holding a city requires a state behind it. Alexandria could have been supplied by sea indefinitely, but the empire was in a disputed succession, no relief force was being prepared, the field army had been destroyed and the rest of the province was lost. A fortress without a strategy is a delay, not a defence.

The truce is the most distinctive feature of the arrangement and it is well attested. The agreement provided for a delay of roughly eleven months before the handover, during which the Byzantine forces and any inhabitants who wished to leave could evacuate in an orderly manner. Terms of that kind are the product of negotiation between parties who both want to avoid a fight, and they are not what a triumphant army imposes on a helpless city.

The handover in the autumn of 642 was accordingly undramatic. There was no storm, no sack, and no destruction layer of the sort a violent capture leaves in the archaeology. The city continued to function, its population largely remained, and its churches continued to operate.

The recapture four years later is the coda and it matters for the argument. A Byzantine expedition arrived by sea and retook the city, which demonstrates that seaborne recovery had been feasible all along and that the failure to attempt it in 642 was a failure of political capacity rather than of military possibility. The Arab commander retook the city in 646, and the empire never tried again. That second loss, and the decision not to contest a third time, marks the point at which Constantinople accepted that Egypt was gone.

## Why It Succeeded

The causes can be ranked, and ranking them is more useful than listing them, because popular accounts tend to seize on whichever one they find most congenial.

Imperial exhaustion comes first. A state that has just fought a twenty six year war to a standstill, lost the province to enemy occupation for a decade, regained it barely ten years earlier, and is simultaneously losing Syria has no capacity for a serious defence of anything. This is the largest single factor and it is structural rather than local.

The absence of a field army after the summer of 640 comes second and follows from the first. Fortified places without a mobile force to relieve them fall in sequence, and the only question is how long each takes. Everything after the battle at the Delta apex was the working out of that arithmetic.

Alienation of the population comes third, in the specific form described earlier: absence of the will to defend rather than support for the invader. Its effect was to deny the defenders intelligence, labour, levies and local cooperation, which does not lose a war on its own and makes losing one much faster.

Political collapse at the centre comes fourth. The emperor's death in the middle of the campaign, followed by months of contested succession, removed the possibility of a coordinated imperial response at exactly the moment one was needed. Alexandria's surrender is directly attributable to it.

Arab military qualities come fifth, and belong on the list without being inflated. The force was mobile, cohesive, well led and highly motivated, operating on interior lines from a secure base in the east, and it exploited opportunity effectively. What it was not was numerous, technically superior or capable of reducing first class fortifications quickly, and the seven month siege of the inland fortress proves the last point.

Geography comes sixth and cuts both ways. Egypt's approaches are narrow and its heartland is a river valley, which historically made it defensible, but the same narrowness meant that losing the eastern approach and the Delta apex left nothing to fall back on.

What is absent from this list is any claim about religious enthusiasm as a military multiplier, which appears in both hostile and admiring accounts and explains nothing that the structural factors do not explain better.

## Did the Copts Welcome the Arabs?

This is the most repeated claim about the conquest and it needs handling with precision, because a version of it is defensible and the popular version is not.

The popular version holds that the Egyptian Christian population, oppressed by Byzantine religious policy, actively welcomed the invaders as liberators, assisted them, and preferred Muslim rule from the outset. It appears in Arabic sources written centuries later, in some modern accounts, and in polemical writing on several sides.

The evidence does not support it. What the contemporary and near contemporary material shows is more complicated and, in places, directly contradictory. Egyptians fought the invaders in several places, and the fullest Egyptian account of the conquest, written by a bishop within living memory of the events, describes the invasion in hostile terms, records violence and requisition, and does not read as the work of a liberated man. Local officials cooperated with the new authority in some places and resisted in others, which is what always happens during a change of regime and tells us little about sentiment.

The stronger and better supported claim is negative. There is no evidence of the Egyptian population mobilizing to defend Byzantine rule, no sign of popular levies, no record of towns holding out from local determination rather than garrison presence, and no indication that the loss of imperial control provoked resistance movements. Nobody fought for Byzantium who was not already a soldier of Byzantium.

### What does the evidence actually support?

Absence of loyalty rather than presence of welcome. Egyptians did not mobilize to defend imperial rule and in places fought the invaders anyway. The defensible statement is that the empire had no popular support to draw on, not that the population preferred its enemy.

The return of the Egyptian patriarch after the conquest is often cited as evidence of welcome, and it is better read as evidence of the new administration's policy. He had been in hiding for more than a decade under imperial pressure and returned under a guarantee of safety from the new commander, resuming leadership of his church. That is a fact about what the conquerors offered rather than about what the Egyptians had wanted, and the distinction matters. A new government seeking to stabilize a province has obvious reasons to conciliate the majority community's leadership, and doing so tells you about its calculations rather than about prior sentiment.

There is also a chronological problem with the welcome story. Most of the sources that state it most strongly were written well after the events, in a period when a narrative of providential and popular acceptance served identifiable purposes. Contemporary material is both thinner and cooler.

The honest summary is therefore the one this article's thesis is built on. Byzantine rule in Egypt had exhausted its credit with the population it governed, which meant that when it was attacked it fought with its army alone. That is a sufficient explanation and it does not require anyone to have welcomed anybody.

## The Terms of Settlement

What the conquerors actually imposed matters more for the following centuries than the campaign does, and the arrangements were pragmatic rather than transformative.

The basic framework granted the existing Christian population protected status in exchange for payment. A poll tax fell on adult non Muslim males, with exemptions for the poor, the elderly, women, children, the disabled and in most readings the clergy and monks. A land tax fell on agricultural property. In exchange the community retained its churches, its internal legal autonomy, its right to worship, and protection.

Conversion was not required and was not the immediate objective. This is the single most important point for anyone trying to understand why Egypt did not become Muslim quickly, and it is also perfectly comprehensible from the conquerors' side: a protected non Muslim population is a taxable population, and a converted one is not taxable on the same basis. The fiscal logic pointed away from mass conversion in the first generations.

Administrative continuity was substantial. The existing machinery of provincial government continued to operate, staffed largely by the same people, using the same procedures, in the same languages, with the new authority sitting on top of it. Tax registers, receipts and correspondence from the first Islamic decades show the ordinary apparatus running with a change of ultimate authority above it. The bilingual receipt from 643 is the emblem of this arrangement.

The Byzantine forces evacuated under the treaty, and a proportion of the Greek speaking population associated with the imperial administration and the imperial church left with them or in the following years. That departure removed a layer of the province's population and much of the constituency for imperial religion.

Grain was redirected. The province's surplus had fed Constantinople for three centuries, and it now fed the new political centre in Arabia instead, carried through a reopened canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea. This is a change with consequences for the whole eastern Mediterranean, since the capital that had depended on Egyptian grain had to find it elsewhere.

The settlement's terms are also the reason the following centuries look the way they do, and their operation is the subject of the article on [Copts under early Islamic rule](/2010/05/01/copts-under-early-islamic-rule/), which owns that question.

## What Changed Immediately

The short list of things that were different within a few years is worth setting out precisely, because it is much shorter than most readers expect.

The government changed. Egypt ceased to be a province of an empire ruled from Constantinople and became a province of a caliphate ruled from Arabia and later from Damascus. Its governor was appointed from there, its revenue went there, and its strategic orientation reversed from north toward the Mediterranean to east toward Arabia and the Red Sea.

The capital changed. Alexandria, a Mediterranean city facing the sea, ceased to be the seat of government, and the new administrative centre was established inland at the head of the Delta beside the old fortress. This is one of the most consequential changes of the whole event, because it reoriented the country's centre of gravity for the next fourteen centuries and every subsequent capital of Egypt has stood in the same area.

The garrison changed. An Arab military settlement was established with its own quarters, its own administration and its own relationship to the land, and it constituted a new population element in a country that had not received one for a long time.

The religious hierarchy changed. The imperially backed church lost its state support and much of its constituency departed, while the Egyptian patriarch returned from hiding and resumed leadership. This was a genuine and immediate reversal for the majority community.

The grain flow changed direction, as described above.

Taxation changed in framework though not necessarily in weight. Whether the burden rose or fell in the first generations is disputed and the evidence is mixed, since assessments varied by region and period and the comparison depends on which taxes are counted.

What did not change is the longer list. The population, the language of daily life, the religion of the great majority, the agricultural calendar, the administrative personnel at working level, the legal practice governing contracts and inheritance, the churches and monasteries and their property, and the ordinary texture of village life all continued.

### How quickly did Egypt become Muslim?

It did not. Conversion took centuries, with the crossover to a Muslim majority generally placed somewhere between the ninth and twelfth centuries and actively debated. The conquest changed the government in three years and the religion of the population over roughly half a millennium, by mechanisms largely unconnected to the campaign.

## What Took Centuries

Separating the fast from the slow is the analytical core of this article, and the slow changes deserve naming even though each belongs to a specialist article.

Religious change was the slowest and the most consequential. The mechanisms were administrative advantage, fiscal incentive, intermarriage, employment and generational drift rather than compulsion, and the process ran for centuries. The debate about its pace and its causes belongs to the comparison article on conquest or conversion.

Language change ran on a similar timescale and by similar mechanisms, beginning with the switch of the administration to Arabic around the turn of the eighth century and ending with the disappearance of Egyptian as an ordinary spoken language at a date scholars dispute. That story belongs to the article on how Egypt became Arabic speaking, and the linguistic side of it to the article on [the Coptic language](/2009/07/01/coptic-language-and-its-history/).

Administrative Arabization was the earliest of the slow changes and the trigger for much else. For roughly two generations the province was governed in Greek and in Egyptian at local level, by the existing staff. The move of the central accounts to Arabic around the turn of the eighth century removed the largest professional incentive to be literate in anything else and redirected the ambitious toward the new language.

Legal and social integration developed gradually as Islamic law and institutions took root, replacing or overlaying existing practice in some domains while leaving others alone for a long time.

The demographic and settlement pattern changed slowly through Arab settlement, urban growth around the new capital, and the long decline of Alexandria as a great city.

The point of listing these together is the contrast with the three year campaign. A reader who takes away one thing from this article should take away that the conquest and the transformation are different events with different durations and different causes, and that the second one is not explained by the first.

## The Sources and Where They Disagree

This is a subject where the answer to almost any detailed question depends on which source is preferred, so the source situation should be understood before any specific claim is assessed.

The Egyptian Christian account written closest to the events is the work of a bishop who lived through the period, and it is the single most valuable narrative for the conquest. Its transmission is peculiar: the original does not survive, and the text exists in an Ethiopic version translated from an Arabic version translated from the original, which means every reading passes through two intermediaries. Portions covering the crucial years are damaged or missing. Despite these problems it supplies detail available nowhere else and a perspective from inside the conquered population.

The Arabic conquest narratives were composed considerably later, from the ninth century onward, drawing on earlier transmitted material. They are detailed, they preserve much that is plausible, and they were written in a milieu with clear interests in how the conquest was remembered. They frequently disagree with each other about sequences, numbers and the conduct of individuals, and their accounts of negotiations are shaped by later concerns about the legal status of conquered territory, which is a technical question with real consequences in Islamic law.

Byzantine chronicles supply the imperial perspective, are generally brief on Egypt, and were mostly written later than the events from earlier material.

Documentary papyri are the most reliable material and the narrowest. Receipts, requisition orders, tax documents and letters show the administration functioning, the languages in use, the demands being made and the personnel involved. They do not narrate and they cannot be argued with, which makes them the control against which the narratives are tested.

Archaeology contributes mainly negatively and usefully. The absence of destruction layers at Alexandria corresponding to a storm supports the treaty account. The standing remains of the inland fortress confirm its scale. Continuity of occupation at most sites supports the picture of a change of government rather than a devastation.

The principal disagreements worth knowing about concern numbers, which are unreliable in every narrative source and should not be quoted precisely; the conduct and motives of the negotiating official, on which the sources are irreconcilable; the extent of violence during the campaign, described very differently by different traditions; and the terms of the settlement, where later legal concerns have shaped the accounts.

The practical rule for a reader is that the framework of events is secure, the details are contested, and any confident specific figure about army sizes, casualties or tribute should be treated as a source's claim rather than as a fact.

## Myths Worth Correcting

Several claims about this conquest circulate widely and each is wrong in an instructive way.

The first is that the Arabs burned the great library of Alexandria. The story appears only in sources written six centuries after the event, in a form that includes a memorable but implausible anecdote, and it is rejected by essentially all specialists. The great library's decline is a long and complicated story with no single destructive moment, treated in the article on [the library and lighthouse of Alexandria](/2007/10/01/library-lighthouse-of-alexandria/), and by the seventh century there was no great library left to burn.

The second is that Egypt was Islamized by the conquest. The conquest changed the government. The religion of the population changed over centuries afterward through entirely different mechanisms, and no serious account places a Muslim majority anywhere near the seventh century.

The third is that the Copts welcomed the invaders as liberators. The defensible version is the absence of any will to defend Byzantine rule, which is well supported. Active welcome is not, and Egyptians fought the invaders in several places.

The fourth is that the conquest was accomplished by an overwhelming force. The invading army numbered a few thousand at the outset and in the low tens of thousands at most after reinforcement, and it needed seven months to reduce a single fortress. Overwhelming is precisely what it was not.

The fifth is that Alexandria was stormed and sacked. It was handed over by treaty after a negotiated truce, and the archaeology shows no destruction consistent with a violent capture.

A sixth belief is less a myth than a persistent framing error: treating the conquest as the beginning of Egyptian history's Islamic chapter in a way that implies discontinuity of population. Nobody was replaced. The Egyptians of 700 were the Egyptians of 600, under different management, and the changes that followed happened to them rather than around them.

## The Counter-Attack and Why It Failed

The Byzantine attempt to recover Alexandria in 645 is usually treated as a footnote and deserves better, because it is the clearest test of the explanations offered for the conquest.

An imperial expedition arrived by sea and retook the city, which established immediately that the sea route remained open and that Byzantine naval superiority was intact. Whatever else had gone wrong, the empire could still put a force ashore in Egypt whenever it chose. This is important because it eliminates the simplest explanation for the original loss, namely that recovery was impossible.

The recapture succeeded and then failed to spread. The expedition held the city and pushed inland with limited success, and the Arab commander returned with a field force and defeated it. Alexandria was retaken in 646, and after that the empire made no further serious attempt.

Two conclusions follow. The first is about the province rather than the city: recovering Alexandria did not recover Egypt, because the rest of the country was under a functioning administration that had already established itself and had no reason to change back. A seaborne expedition can seize a port; it cannot re-establish authority over a river valley a thousand kilometres long against a government already in place.

The second is about popular sentiment, and it is the single most useful piece of evidence on the question. The arrival of an imperial force in 645 was an opportunity for anyone who preferred Byzantine rule to act on the preference, and no general rising occurred. The population that had not defended the empire in 640 did not restore it in 645 either. Whatever the Egyptians thought about their new rulers by that point, they were not attempting to bring the old ones back.

The decision to stop trying is the last element. The empire that had spent twenty six years fighting Persia over the eastern provinces accepted the loss of its richest province after two attempts, which is a measure of how comprehensively its capacity had been exhausted and of how firmly the new authority had established itself. Constantinople reoriented toward the defence of Anatolia and the capital itself, and Egypt passed permanently out of its world.

There is a longer consequence worth noting. The loss of Egyptian grain forced a fundamental change in how Constantinople fed itself, and the loss of Egyptian revenue permanently reduced the empire's resources. A conquest that took three years shaped Byzantine history for the following eight centuries.

## Egypt, Syria and Persia Compared

Setting the Egyptian conquest beside the other campaigns of the same decades clarifies which features were general and which were specific to Egypt.

Syria fell first and its conquest shares most of the same structural features: an exhausted empire, a newly reconquered province, a population divided from the imperial church over doctrine, and a field army destroyed in a decisive engagement. The pattern of a battle followed by the sequential reduction of fortified places repeats. If anything, the Syrian case makes the Egyptian one look less exceptional, since the same explanations apply with the same force.

Persia is the instructive contrast. The Sasanian empire was not conquered by a fast campaign against an alienated province but by the destruction of a state, and the process took considerably longer, involved much larger battles, and ended with a dynasty extinguished rather than a frontier moved. Egypt lost a government; Persia lost a civilization's political framework.

The comparison illuminates what Egypt's situation actually was. It was not a state being destroyed. It was a province being transferred from one imperial system to another, retaining its administration, its personnel, its population and most of its institutions. That is why the conquest looks so undramatic in the documentary record: from the perspective of a village clerk, the tax demands continued to arrive and the name at the top of the paperwork changed.

There is a further comparative point about religious dissent. The provinces the empire lost fastest were the ones where a substantial population had been in dispute with the imperial church for generations, and the provinces it held were the ones that were doctrinally aligned with the capital. That correlation has been noticed for a very long time and it can be overread, since those provinces also happened to be the exposed frontier ones. The careful version is that religious alienation was one factor among several and that its main effect was to remove the reserve of local support that a defence requires.

Egypt's distinctive contribution to the comparison is the documentation. The papyri allow the transition to be watched at the level of individual receipts and village registers in a way no other conquered province permits, and the models built from Egyptian evidence are applied outward to regions that left nothing comparable.

## The Reorientation of a Country

The most permanent consequence of the conquest is geographical, and it is easy to overlook because it happened without anyone announcing it.

For roughly a thousand years before the conquest, Egypt had faced the Mediterranean. Its capital was a Greek city on the coast, its governing class looked north, its surplus went by sea to feed a European capital, and its intellectual life belonged to a Mediterranean world. Alexandria was the point where Egypt met that world, and everything of consequence in the province's external relations ran through it.

After the conquest, Egypt faced east and south. The capital moved inland to the head of the Delta. The surplus went by canal and Red Sea to Arabia. The governing class looked toward Damascus and then Baghdad. The intellectual and religious centre of the world that mattered to the new rulers was in another direction entirely, and the Mediterranean became a frontier rather than a highway.

Alexandria's decline follows directly. A city built to connect Egypt to the Mediterranean loses its purpose when Egypt stops facing the Mediterranean, and the city's long contraction from a metropolis to a provincial port is one of the more dramatic urban declines in the region's history. The departure of the imperial administration and much of the Greek speaking population accelerated it.

The rise of the new inland capital is the mirror image, and it belongs to the article on the founding of Fustat. The relevant point here is the strategic logic. A settlement at the apex of the Delta controls the whole country because everything moves on the river, it sits at the junction of the routes east and south, and it is defensible against a naval power in a way a coastal city is not. Every capital of Egypt since has stood within a few kilometres of that spot, which is the strongest possible evidence that the choice reflected geography rather than preference.

The reorientation also changed which parts of Egypt mattered. A Mediterranean facing Egypt values the Delta, the coast and the Alexandrian hinterland. An east facing Egypt values the routes to the Red Sea, the desert roads, the Sinai approaches and the Nile valley as a corridor to the south. The pattern of investment, fortification and settlement shifted accordingly over the following centuries.

Two aspects of the older orientation survived. The grain economy remained the foundation of Egyptian wealth regardless of where the grain was sent, and the country's role as a granary for whoever ruled it continued unbroken. And the Mediterranean trade never stopped entirely, reviving substantially in later Islamic centuries when Egypt became the hinge of the eastern trade routes, a role treated in the articles on the Fatimid period.

## What Happened to the Greek Population

The fate of the province's Greek speaking element is a neglected part of the story and it explains several later developments.

The Greek speaking population of Egypt had never been large in proportion to the whole, but it was concentrated where it mattered: in Alexandria, in the administration, in the imperial church, in commerce and among landowners. Its position depended on the connection to the empire.

The evacuation clause in the surrender treaty allowed those who wished to leave to do so during the truce, and a proportion took the opportunity. The military forces departed under the terms, and with them went the families and dependents attached to garrison life. Officials of the imperial administration whose careers were imperial rather than local had obvious reasons to go, as did clergy of the imperially backed church, whose position in Egypt had rested entirely on state support.

Those who stayed were absorbed over generations. Greek remained an administrative language for two generations after the conquest, so Greek literacy retained value in the bureaucracy until the switch to Arabic around the turn of the eighth century removed it. Commercial and landowning families with local roots had less reason to leave and more to lose by leaving.

The consequence for the Egyptian church was significant and favourable from its own perspective. The imperially backed hierarchy lost its state support and much of its constituency at once, which effectively resolved a two centuries old competition in favour of the Egyptian church. That community had spent generations under pressure from a rival backed by imperial power, and that pressure ended abruptly.

The consequence for Alexandria was demographic as well as economic. A city that loses its administrative function, a portion of its population and its connection to its principal market contracts, and the archaeology of the following centuries shows exactly that.

There is a broader lesson about conquests here. The population that leaves after a change of regime is rarely the general population; it is the layer whose position depended on the departing power. Identifying which layer that was in any given case explains a great deal about what the new arrangement looked like, and in Egypt it explains why the imperial church vanished as a force while the country's Christianity did not.

## Egypt in the Caliphate

Understanding what Egypt became after 642 requires seeing it from the perspective of the state that acquired it, and from that perspective it was the single most valuable acquisition of the conquests.

Egypt was a revenue province of the first rank. Its agricultural surplus was reliable in a way that rain fed agriculture is not, its tax base was well documented by an administration with centuries of experience assessing it, and its population was large. A new state with an expanding military establishment to pay found in Egypt exactly what it needed, and the province's revenue supported operations far beyond its borders.

It was also a grain province, and grain is strategic rather than merely valuable. The reopening of a canal connecting the Nile to the Red Sea allowed Egyptian grain to reach the Arabian heartland directly, which mattered enormously to a region that could not feed its growing urban populations from local production. That link is one of the most consequential infrastructural decisions of the early Islamic period.

Strategically Egypt was the base for expansion westward along the North African coast, and campaigns in that direction were mounted from it within a few decades. It was also the naval base from which the new state contested the Mediterranean, which it could only do because it had acquired the shipyards, timber supply chains and maritime expertise of a province that had been building ships for centuries.

Politically the province was governed by an appointed governor, and the pattern of appointments, dismissals and revenue extraction reflects the centre's priorities rather than local ones. Egypt was administered as an asset, and the tension between what the province generated and what the centre took became a recurring feature of the following centuries, eventually producing the first attempts at autonomous rule under a dynasty based in Egypt itself.

The status of the province in Islamic legal terms was a live question with practical consequences, because the rules governing land, taxation and the rights of the conquered population differed according to whether a territory had been taken by force or by treaty. Egyptian evidence is disputed on this point, with different sources supporting different classifications for different places, and the later legal literature has an obvious interest in the answer. This is one of the reasons the Arabic narratives about the negotiations are more careful than a modern reader expects: they are addressing a legal question as much as a historical one.

That legal dimension is worth flagging for anyone reading the sources, because it explains an otherwise puzzling feature of them. Details about whether a particular town surrendered or was stormed, which look like antiquarian minutiae, carried real consequences for land tenure and taxation centuries later, and accounts were shaped accordingly.

## Studying the Conquest

For a student, this topic organizes around a manageable set of things worth holding.

Be able to give the five ranked causes of success, with imperial exhaustion first and Arab military qualities fifth. Be able to name what changed immediately and what took centuries, and to explain why merging them is the commonest error. ## The Frontier Town and the First Weeks

The opening of the campaign is usually compressed into a sentence and repays expansion, because the first weeks establish everything about the invading force's capabilities.

The entry route was the traditional one, the coastal road across northern Sinai that had carried armies in both directions since the Bronze Age. It is the only practical land approach to Egypt from the east, constrained by desert on one side and sea on the other, with a small number of water points that determine where a force can stop. Any army coming from Palestine takes this road, and any defence of Egypt from the east begins by holding it.

The fortified town at its Egyptian end was the frontier position, and it was garrisoned. Its resistance lasted weeks, which is the first hard datum about the campaign. A force capable of quick reduction of fortifications would have taken it in days; a force that needed weeks against a frontier town was going to need months against a major fortress, which is exactly what happened later.

The advance after its fall was rapid and selective. The force pushed inland rather than turning to clear the Delta, bypassing places it could not take quickly and aiming at the strategic centre. This is the behaviour of a commander who understands that the province falls with its centre and that time spent on secondary positions is time the enemy uses to concentrate.

The pause for reinforcement is the campaign's most revealing decision. Rather than press on with the original force, the invaders waited for a second contingent before accepting a field battle. A commander confident of victory with what he has does not wait; one who has assessed the odds does. The reinforcement roughly tripled the strength available, and only then was the Byzantine field army engaged.

The supply position through these months depended on the countryside, and the campaign's viability rested on being able to feed several thousand men and their animals from districts they were passing through. This works when local production is intact and local resistance is not organized, and it fails when either condition breaks. That it worked is a quiet but significant piece of evidence about the state of the province.

What the opening phase establishes, taken together, is a picture of a competent, cautious, resource limited operation rather than an irresistible advance. Every subsequent phase of the campaign is consistent with that picture, and the popular image of a sweeping conquest is contradicted by the campaign's own tempo.

## The Counterfactual and Its Uses

Asking what would have happened had the conquest failed is not history, but it is a useful way of isolating which of its consequences were contingent and which were structural, so it is worth doing carefully and briefly.

Had the invasion been repelled in 640, the empire would have retained a province it could barely administer, with a majority population estranged from its church, a wrecked economy and no capacity to reinforce it. The pressures that made the conquest possible would have remained, and another attempt from the same base would have been likely. This suggests that the loss of Egypt to the new power was close to structural rather than a matter of chance.

What was contingent is the timing and the terms. An emperor who lived through 641, or a succession that resolved quickly, might have produced a defence of Alexandria rather than a negotiated handover, and a city held for another decade would have changed the shape of the following century in ways that cannot be specified. The settlement's generosity toward the Christian community was a policy choice by the conquerors rather than an inevitability, and a harsher settlement would have produced a different Egypt.

The deepest consequence, the reorientation of the country away from the Mediterranean and inland to a new capital, was less contingent than it appears. Any power ruling Egypt from the east rather than from Europe would have found the head of the Delta the natural centre, and any power without naval superiority would have avoided a coastal capital. The site chosen reflects geography and strategy rather than preference.

The value of this exercise is that it separates the layers of causation. Structural factors made the loss of the province highly likely. Contingent factors determined when and on what terms. Policy choices by the new authority determined what the following centuries looked like for the population. Merging the three, which is what a simple narrative does, produces the impression that everything followed inevitably from an army crossing a frontier.

The corresponding caution is that counterfactual reasoning has no evidence behind it and cannot be tested. Its only legitimate use is the diagnostic one applied here: identifying which parts of an outcome were fixed and which were open. Anything beyond that is fiction, however plausible.

## Upper Egypt and the Countryside

Most accounts of this conquest are accounts of the Delta and Alexandria, and the rest of the country deserves attention because its experience was different and better documented.

Upper Egypt was secured with comparatively little fighting after the fall of the inland fortress, which is unsurprising once the strategic position is understood. The fortress controlled the corridor between the valley and the Delta, and a valley a thousand kilometres long with no field army and no communication with the coast has no means of organizing a defence. Detachments moved south, took surrenders and established authority, and the process appears in the sources as a series of arrangements rather than as a campaign.

The countryside experienced the conquest primarily as a change in who collected. Village archives from the decades on either side of 642 show the same categories of document, the same officials, the same crops and the same disputes. What changed was the destination of the surplus and, gradually, the identity of the people at the top of the administrative chain.

Requisition was the sharpest immediate experience. An army in the field needs grain, fodder, animals and transport, and it takes them from the districts it passes through. The bilingual receipt from 643 is precisely a requisition document, recording sheep taken for the new authority's use, and its existence tells you the practice was routine and documented within a year of the campaign's end.

The monasteries were significant institutions in the countryside and their experience is partly recoverable. Monastic property was substantial, monastic communities were large, and monastic archives continue across the conquest without a break in most cases. Their protected status under the new arrangement, and the taxation applied to them, developed over the following decades and belongs to the article on the Christian community under Islamic rule.

Regional variation was considerable and is under studied. The Delta's experience differed from the valley's, the oases were peripheral to the campaign entirely, and the frontier regions in the far south had their own dynamics with the peoples beyond the border. Generalizing from Alexandria to Egypt is the standard error, and generalizing from the Delta to the valley is the next one.

The wider point is that the conquest was, for the great majority of Egyptians, an administrative event punctuated by the passage of an army. That is not a dismissal of its importance, which was enormous over the long run. It is a description of what it felt like at the time to the people who experienced it, and the gap between that experience and the dramatic narrative in the chronicles is one of the most instructive things about the whole subject.

## How the Conquest Was Remembered

The afterlife of this event in memory is a subject in its own right and it shapes almost everything a general reader is likely to encounter.

Within the Egyptian Christian community the conquest was absorbed into a framework already in place. A community that read pressure through the template of persecution and endurance treated the change of rulers as one more episode in a long series, and the immediate experience of having imperial doctrinal pressure lifted made the new arrangement compare favourably with the last decade of Byzantine rule. Later, as the community's position changed over centuries, its memory of the conquest changed with it, and accounts written in different periods carry very different tones.

Within the Islamic tradition the conquest became part of a larger narrative of the early expansion, with the commander a significant figure and the details of the settlement carrying legal weight. That is why the Arabic accounts are so attentive to the terms of surrenders: the classification of territory determined tax rules and land rights for centuries, and getting the history right had consequences.

Within Byzantine memory the loss was absorbed as one element of a general catastrophe, and Egypt received comparatively little attention in a literature preoccupied with the survival of the capital and the defence of Anatolia.

In modern writing the conquest has been used for a wide range of purposes. Nationalist historiography in Egypt has read it variously as the beginning of the country's Arab identity or as an interruption of a longer Egyptian continuity, depending on the current. Colonial era European scholarship frequently framed it through contemporary preoccupations. Confessional writing on several sides has read it as either liberation or catastrophe.

The practical consequence for a reader is that almost every account of this conquest encountered outside specialist literature is doing something in addition to describing what happened, and the tells are usually visible: an emphasis on welcome, an emphasis on devastation, a compression of the conquest and the conversion into one event, or a precision about numbers that the sources cannot support.

The corrective is not to seek an account with no perspective, which does not exist, but to know what the evidence can and cannot establish. The sequence and the dates are secure. The causes can be argued for from structural evidence. The numbers cannot be established. The sentiments of the population can be established only negatively. Anything asserted beyond that is interpretation, and interpretation is legitimate as long as it is labelled.

## The Administration That Did Not Stop

The single most revealing thing about this conquest is how little the machinery of government changed, and the papyri let that be observed in detail.

Egypt had been governed for centuries by a layered administration: a provincial apparatus at the top, district officials below it, and village level officers responsible for assessment, collection and the endless paperwork of a tax state. That system was staffed by local men, operated in Greek at the higher levels and in Egyptian at the lower, and was extremely good at what it did, which was extracting a predictable surplus from a large agricultural population.

The new authority kept it. There was no attempt to replace the apparatus, no importation of an alternative bureaucracy, and no dismantling of the assessment system. The governor sat on top of the existing structure and the demands continued to flow down it. This was not generosity; it was the only practical option. A conquering force of a few thousand cannot administer a province of millions, and the machinery that already worked was the machinery available.

The consequences are visible in the documents. Receipts continue in the same forms. Requisition orders use the same procedures. The same categories of officials appear, with the same responsibilities. The bilingual receipt from 643 shows the new language appearing alongside the old within a working document rather than replacing it, which is exactly how administrative change proceeds when a small elite governs through an existing staff.

For an ordinary Egyptian, this is what the conquest meant in practice. The tax collector who arrived was the same kind of person as before, using the same procedures, issuing the same kind of receipt, in a language the local officials still used. The name of the ultimate authority had changed. Almost nothing else about the transaction had.

The switch of the central accounts to Arabic around the turn of the eighth century is therefore the real administrative revolution, and it came two generations after the conquest rather than with it. That change did what the conquest did not: it made the language of the new rulers the language of advancement, which redirected the ambitions of every family with a son to educate. The consequences of that decision run through the language article and the conversion debate.

There is a general point here worth carrying to other conquests. The moment of military victory frequently changes very little in the daily operation of a state, because the incoming power lacks the personnel to change it. The transformative decisions usually come a generation or two later, when the new authority has consolidated enough to reorganize rather than merely to collect, and those decisions are far less memorable than the battles.

## The Question of Violence and Its Limits

Assessing how violent this conquest was requires the same care applied elsewhere in this series, and the sources disagree sharply.

Violence certainly occurred. Towns were taken by force, a field battle was fought, sieges were conducted, and the Egyptian account written closest to the events describes killing, plunder and the seizure of property in terms that leave no room for a sanitized reading. Requisition of supplies for an army in the field is coercive by nature, and a population subjected to it experiences it as violence whether or not anyone is killed.

At the same time the campaign's structure limited it. A small force that preferred negotiated transfers to assaults, that took its principal objectives by treaty, and that needed the countryside to remain productive in order to feed itself had strong practical reasons for restraint. The two most important places in the country, the inland fortress and Alexandria, both changed hands by agreement.

The archaeological record supports the limited reading in the aggregate, with continuity of occupation at most sites and no destruction horizon corresponding to the conquest. Populations do not continue living in places that have been destroyed.

The later Arabic sources describe the campaign in terms shaped by legal interests, as noted, and tend to emphasize agreements. The Egyptian account emphasizes suffering. Byzantine sources are brief. None of these is disinterested and the honest position holds them together: the conquest was violent in the ordinary way that seventh century warfare was violent, and it was not a catastrophe on the scale that popular tellings on either side sometimes suggest.

The distinction that matters most for the following centuries is between violence during a campaign and violence as a system of rule. The first ended in 642. The second did not occur: the settlement that followed was a tax arrangement rather than a persecution, and the community that had been under pressure from the previous regime found that pressure lifted. That contrast, more than any battle, is what shaped how the conquest was remembered by the people who lived through it.

## Egypt and Arabia Before the Invasion

The conquest did not come out of nowhere, and the connections between Egypt and the Arabian peninsula before 639 are worth setting out because they explain how the campaign was possible at all.

Contact was old and continuous. The Red Sea had been a trade corridor for millennia, carrying goods between Egypt, the Arabian coast, the Horn of Africa and further east, and Egyptian ports on that coast had functioned since pharaonic times. Overland routes through Sinai connected the Nile Delta to Palestine and onward to the peninsula. Arabic speaking populations had lived on Egypt's eastern margins and in the eastern desert for a very long time, and the frontier was a zone rather than a line.

The invaders were therefore not arriving from an unknown place. They came by a route that had carried traffic for thousands of years, into a country that peninsular Arabs knew as a trading partner, a granary and an imperial power. Whatever else the campaign was, it was not an expedition into terra incognita.

Byzantine policy toward the desert frontier had relied on subsidized allied tribes to police the approaches, an arrangement of long standing across the whole eastern frontier. The disruption of those arrangements during the Persian war and the reorganization afterward left the frontier less effectively managed than it had been, and the loss of Syria removed the buffer entirely.

The immediate strategic context is the decisive part. Syria had fallen before Egypt, which meant that by 639 the new power held Palestine and had a secure base directly adjoining Egypt's eastern approach. The invasion of Egypt was a short move from an established position rather than a leap across a hostile void, and the supply line ran back through territory already under control.

There is also a question about whether the invasion was authorized, which the sources treat at some length. Several accounts present the commander as having pressed for the campaign against caution at the centre, and as having moved with permission that was ambiguous or reluctant. Whether that reflects what happened or a later interest in dramatizing the commander's initiative is not determinable, and the question belongs to his own article. What can be said is that the campaign's small initial force and the substantial reinforcement that followed are consistent with an operation that began as a probe and was upgraded when it proved viable.

Understanding this background removes the sense of improbability that hangs over popular accounts. A neighbouring power with a secure adjoining base, using a traditional invasion route into an exhausted province with no field reserves, achieving success in three years, is a comprehensible military outcome rather than a marvel.

## The Fortress Remains and What Archaeology Adds

Physical evidence for the conquest is thinner than for most subjects in this series, and what exists mostly works by absence, which is instructive in itself.

The inland fortress survives in part. Its round towers and stretches of its walls stand within the modern city, sunk below present ground level because centuries of urban accumulation have raised the surroundings. The remains confirm the scale of the position and make the seven month siege comprehensible: these were not walls a small force with no engineering could rush.

Alexandria supplies the most significant negative evidence. There is no destruction layer corresponding to a violent capture in 642, which is exactly what the treaty account predicts and what a storm and sack would have left. Cities that are taken by assault in this period leave archaeological traces, and this one does not.

Continuity of occupation across the conquest is the general pattern at excavated sites. Settlements were not abandoned, churches were not systematically destroyed, and the material culture of the decades after 642 continues that of the decades before with no sharp break. This is consistent with a change of government rather than a devastation, and it is the strongest archaeological support for the account this article gives.

The new settlement at the head of the Delta is archaeologically important and difficult, since it lies under a continuously occupied city and its earliest layers are hard to reach. Excavation has recovered material illustrating the early Islamic city's development, and its interpretation belongs to the article on the founding of the capital.

Coinage provides a useful independent index. The transition in Egyptian coinage from Byzantine types through transitional issues to distinctively Islamic ones happened gradually over decades rather than immediately, which is another indication that the change of authority did not produce an instant rupture in ordinary economic life.

The documentary papyri are technically archaeological evidence and have already been discussed, and they remain the most valuable material by a wide margin. A tax receipt is a better guide to what the conquest meant for an Egyptian villager than any chronicle, precisely because it was written without any intention of telling anyone about the conquest at all.

## Numbers, and Why They Cannot Be Trusted

Every popular account of this conquest quotes figures, and almost none of them should be quoted, so it is worth setting out why and what can be said instead.

The narrative sources give army sizes, casualty figures and tribute amounts, and these are unreliable for reasons that apply generally to premodern military history. Nobody counted. Estimates were made by observers with no method, transmitted through generations of oral and written retelling, and shaped by the rhetorical needs of the accounts they sat in. A small force is more impressive if the enemy was large; a defeat is more excusable if the enemy was larger still. Both parties to a conflict have reasons to inflate the other side.

What can be said about the invading force rests on inference rather than on the quoted numbers. A force that crossed the frontier and needed weeks to reduce a frontier town was small. A force that was substantially reinforced before it would risk a field battle was small at the outset. A force that took seven months over a fortress had no siege train and limited manpower. A force that could not blockade Alexandria by sea had no fleet. Those constraints, taken together, put the invading army in the low thousands initially and in the low tens of thousands at most after reinforcement, and they do so without relying on any figure any source gives.

The Byzantine side is harder still. The province's garrison was dispersed, its field strength is unknown, and the sources describing its defeat have no better information than those describing the invaders. What can be said is structural: the force was insufficient to hold the province, no reinforcement arrived, and after one battle it ceased to exist as a field army.

Tribute figures suffer from an additional problem, which is that the later Arabic legal literature had a strong interest in what was agreed and on what basis, since the classification of territory determined tax rules for centuries afterward. Figures reported in that context are not neutral reporting.

Population figures for Egypt in this period are equally unavailable. Estimates exist and range widely, and any specific number for the province's population, its Christian proportion or the size of its cities in the seventh century is a modern reconstruction resting on assumptions rather than on counts.

The practical rule follows. Use the sequence, the durations and the outcomes, which are reasonably secure. Treat every number as a claim by a source rather than as a measurement, and be suspicious of any account that presents precise figures without saying where they come from.

## What a Seventh Century Siege Involved

The seven months outside the inland fortress are the longest single episode of the campaign and describing what that actually meant makes the whole conquest more comprehensible.

A besieging army in this period had three ways to take a fortified place: break the walls, get over them, or wait. Breaking walls required engineering, materials, skilled personnel and time, and the invading force gives every sign of lacking the first three. Getting over them required numbers sufficient to overwhelm defenders at a chosen point and to accept heavy losses, which a small force cannot afford. Waiting required the besieger to remain supplied longer than the besieged, which in Egypt meant living off the surrounding countryside through a winter.

That last point is where the campaign intersects with the population question. An army of several thousand camped for seven months has to eat, and it eats what the surrounding district produces, by purchase or by requisition. Whether the local population cooperated, resisted or simply endured is not directly recorded, but the fact that the siege was sustainable at all tells you the countryside was not organized against the besiegers.

For the defenders, a siege of that length is a test of supply, morale and the expectation of relief. A garrison that believes a field army is coming holds; one that knows none exists calculates differently with every passing month. The surrender on terms in the spring of 641 is what that calculation produces.

Disease was a constant factor on both sides and is rarely quantified in sources of this period. A besieging camp in one place for months, with poor sanitation and a nearby river, is an epidemiological problem, and so is a crowded fortress. Losses to sickness in operations of this kind routinely exceeded losses to fighting.

The negotiation running alongside the fighting is characteristic rather than exceptional. Sieges in this world were usually resolved by agreement rather than by assault, because assault was expensive for the attacker and catastrophic for the defenders, and both sides generally preferred a deal. The terms typically involved the garrison departing, the population being spared and a payment being made, which is broadly what happened here and again at Alexandria.

Understanding this changes how the campaign reads. It was not a series of storms and massacres but a sequence of negotiated transfers punctuated by one significant battle, conducted by a small force that could not afford to fight if it could arrange a handover instead.

## The Fleet Question

Naval power is the missing element in most accounts of this conquest, and its absence explains several features of the campaign that otherwise look strange.

The invading force had no fleet. This is the single most important military fact about the campaign after the size of the army. It meant that Alexandria could not be blockaded, that the Delta's waterways were a Byzantine advantage rather than an obstacle, that coastal positions could be resupplied indefinitely, and that the empire retained the ability to land forces anywhere on the coast at will.

The consequences show throughout. The campaign route avoided the Delta interior, where waterborne mobility favoured the defenders. Alexandria fell by treaty rather than by siege, because a siege could not work. And the counter-attack of 645 was possible precisely because nothing prevented an imperial force arriving by sea.

The strategic response was to acquire a navy, and Egypt was where it was acquired. The province had shipyards, timber supply arrangements, sailors and centuries of accumulated maritime expertise, all of which passed to the new authority with the territory. Within a few decades the caliphate was contesting the eastern Mediterranean, and Egyptian resources were central to that capability.

That development reverses the strategic picture in a single generation. A power that could not blockade Alexandria in 641 was fighting fleet actions in the eastern Mediterranean within thirty years, and the naval balance that had let Byzantium retake Alexandria in 645 did not persist. The empire's ability to project force onto the Egyptian coast declined accordingly, which is part of why no third attempt was made.

There is a wider point about conquest here. What an invader lacks at the moment of conquest, it frequently acquires with the territory. Egypt supplied not only revenue and grain but a maritime industry, and the acquisition of that industry altered the balance of power in a sea the conquerors had not previously contested. Assessing a conquest by the capabilities of the invaders at the start therefore understates its consequences considerably.

## Fortifications, Cities and the Shape of the Province

A short survey of Egypt's defensive geography helps explain why the campaign unfolded exactly as it did, and it applies well beyond this conquest.

Egypt is unusually easy to defend and unusually easy to lose, and the two facts have the same cause. The country is a corridor: a narrow valley for a thousand kilometres, opening into a delta at the north, with desert on both sides and only a handful of practical approaches. An invader has very few choices about where to enter, which means a defender needs to hold very few places. But the corollary is that once those places are lost there is nothing behind them, because a corridor has no depth.

The eastern land approach across northern Sinai is the historic invasion route and the one used in 639, constrained by water availability to a single practical road. Holding its Egyptian end holds the country against a land attack from the east, and losing it opens everything.

The Delta interior is a different kind of terrain: waterlogged, cut by branches and canals, difficult for a land army and favourable to a defender with boats. That is why the invading force avoided it and pushed inland instead, and why the Byzantine advantage there was never brought to bear once the field army was gone.

The apex of the Delta is the strategic key and always has been, which is why a Roman fortress stood there and why every capital of Egypt since the conquest has stood within a few kilometres of it. A force at that point controls river traffic in both directions and can move against either the valley or the Delta.

Alexandria is the exception to all of this, being a maritime city whose defensibility depends on sea power rather than on the river system. Its impregnability in 641 was real and irrelevant, since a port that cannot be relieved by a state that no longer has the capacity to organize relief is only a delay.

The valley south of the apex has fortified points but no defensive depth, because a line of towns strung along a river cannot support one another without a mobile force. Its rapid submission after the fall of the fortress follows from geography rather than from sentiment.

Reading the campaign against this geography removes most of its apparent strangeness. The invaders did the obvious things in the obvious order, and the defenders lost the two positions that mattered.

## What the Conquest Did Not Touch

A final inventory, of the things that carried on entirely unaffected, because it is the shortest route to understanding what a seventh century conquest actually was.

The flood came, as it had every year for as long as anyone had recorded anything, and the agricultural year turned on it exactly as before. Basins were filled and drained on the same schedule, sowing and harvest happened at the same times, and the calendar that governed all of it continued unchanged.

Villages continued as villages, with the same families holding the same land under the same arrangements, litigating the same disputes about boundaries, water and inheritance, in front of officials drawn from the same class as before.

Contracts continued in the same forms. Sales, leases, loans, marriage settlements and wills were drawn up according to established practice, in the established languages, by the same notaries, and the documentary record shows no break at 642.

Monasteries continued their routine, their landholding and their copying. The manuscript production that preserved the literature of Egyptian Christianity continued through the conquest without interruption, which is why so much of that literature survives.

Craft production continued in the same workshops using the same techniques, and the textile industry in particular carried on with a change in patronage rather than in method.

Trade continued along the same routes with adjustments, and the Red Sea traffic that had run for millennia acquired new importance rather than a new existence, since the corridor and its ports had been in use since pharaonic times.

Language continued, since nothing about the conquest changed what anyone spoke at home, and Egyptian remained the ordinary speech of the great majority for centuries afterward, with Greek still serving the administration for two generations more.

Religion continued for the overwhelming majority, in the same buildings, under the same hierarchy, with the same calendar and the same festivals.

Setting this list beside the list of what changed produces the clearest statement of the article's argument. A conquest changes who rules and where the surplus goes. It does not, by itself, change who lives somewhere, what they speak, what they believe, how they farm or how they organize their families. Those things change afterward, slowly, through mechanisms that a conquest can set in motion but cannot accomplish. The three years from 639 to 642 decided the first set of questions permanently and left the second set entirely open.

## The First Governors and the Shape of Rule

The two decades after the handover established the pattern of how Egypt would be governed, and they were less settled than the neatness of a conquest date suggests.

Authority in the province rested with a governor appointed from the centre, responsible for revenue, security and the military settlement, and answerable to a caliph a long journey away. That distance is a structural fact with consequences: a governor of Egypt controlled a rich province with its own resources at a remove from oversight, which created both opportunity and suspicion, and the pattern of appointments and dismissals across the early decades reflects a centre managing that tension.

The relationship between the governor and the revenue was the standing issue. Egypt generated a large surplus, the centre expected it, and the mechanisms for verifying what had been collected against what was remitted were imperfect. Disputes on this point recur throughout the early period and are the origin of much of the friction between province and centre.

The military settlement was the other pillar of rule. The force that had taken the province settled at the new capital, drew stipends from the revenue, and constituted a distinct population with its own quarters and its own relationship to the administration. Managing that community, paying it and keeping it from becoming a political factor in its own right was a permanent task.

The civil administration below this level remained the inherited one, as described, staffed by local men using established procedures. The result was a two layer arrangement in which a small Arabic speaking military and governing elite sat above a large Greek and Egyptian speaking bureaucracy that actually ran the country.

The instability of the caliphate itself in these decades affected Egypt directly. The civil conflicts of the middle of the century involved Egyptian participants and Egyptian revenue, and control of the province was contested as part of wider struggles. A province that had changed hands once by conquest changed governors repeatedly by politics.

What emerged by the end of the century was a settled provincial system with an Arabic speaking administration at the top, which is the moment the slow transformation described elsewhere in this article properly begins. The conquest opened the door; the administrative reorganization two generations later was what walked through it.

## The Province the Conquerors Inherited

It is worth describing what the new authority actually took possession of, because the inventory explains why Egypt mattered so much to the caliphate and why the settlement took the shape it did.

They took an agricultural system of extraordinary productivity, dependent on an annual flood, managed through basin irrigation with dykes and canals maintained by organized labour, and capable of generating a reliable surplus in a way rain fed agriculture never is. That system required continuous maintenance, which meant the new rulers had every incentive to keep the rural population in place and working rather than to disrupt it.

They took a tax apparatus of centuries' refinement, with land registers, assessment procedures, collection mechanisms and an experienced staff. Assessing an agricultural province accurately is difficult and Egypt's administration had been doing it since the pharaohs. Inheriting it intact was worth more than any plunder.

They took cities, of which Alexandria was the largest in the province and among the largest anywhere, along with provincial towns with markets, workshops and administrative functions.

They took an industrial base of real significance: textile production of high quality and enormous volume, papyrus manufacture which was effectively a monopoly and supplied writing material across the Mediterranean, glass, ceramics, metalwork and shipbuilding.

They took a maritime capability, as discussed, which changed the strategic balance of the eastern Mediterranean within a generation.

They took a large Christian population with its own hierarchy, its own institutions, its own extensive landholdings in the form of churches and monasteries, and its own recent history of conflict with the previous government.

And they took a set of physical assets whose maintenance was itself a governing task: the irrigation infrastructure, the canal to the Red Sea which was reopened, the roads, the harbours and the fortifications.

Listing these together explains the character of the settlement better than any statement of principle. A conquering power that has acquired a functioning revenue machine does not smash it. It keeps the staff, keeps the procedures, protects the productive population, taxes what it can and changes as little as possible until it has the capacity to change things safely. That is exactly what happened, and it is why the conquest looks so different in the papyri from how it looks in the chronicles.

## The Verdict

The argument this article has defended is that the Arab conquest of Egypt was fast because the province was undefendable rather than because the invaders were formidable, and that the undefendability was made of several distinct components which can be named and weighted.

The empire was exhausted by a generation of war, a decade of enemy occupation and a recent, incomplete reconstruction. It had no reserves, no relieving force and no prospect of one, because the provinces that might have supplied them were being lost at the same time.

The field army was destroyed in a single summer engagement, after which the defence consisted of fortified places falling in sequence with no possibility of relief. That is not a war; it is a schedule.

The population had no stake in the outcome. Two centuries of doctrinal enforcement had produced a majority community whose own hierarchy operated in hiding and whose relationship with imperial authority was adversarial. Nobody who was not a soldier fought for the empire, and the practical effects of that, in intelligence, labour, levies and local cooperation, accelerated everything.

The imperial centre collapsed politically at the decisive moment. The emperor's death in the middle of the campaign, and the disputed succession that followed, removed any possibility of coordinated response precisely when the fate of Alexandria was being negotiated.

The invaders were good and were not overwhelming. A force that needed seven months to reduce a single fortress and that obtained the second city of the empire by treaty rather than assault was operating at the edge of its capability throughout.

The qualifications matter and were made as they arose. Egyptians did fight the invaders in places, and the claim that the Copts welcomed the Arabs is not supported by contemporary evidence. The numbers in every narrative source are unreliable and none should be quoted precisely. The conduct and motives of the official who negotiated are irrecoverable. And the legal shaping of the later Arabic accounts means their details about surrenders and terms should be read with that interest in view.

What follows from the verdict is the point this article has pressed throughout. The conquest was a change of government accomplished in three years, and it was not the transformation of Egypt into an Arab and Muslim country, which happened over the following five to seven centuries through mechanisms that had almost nothing to do with the campaign. The soldiers of 640 did not make the Egypt of 1000. Tax policy, employment, intermarriage, administrative language and five hundred years of ordinary generational drift did that, and each of those has its own article in this series because each is a genuinely different subject.

The deepest consequence of the three years is the one nobody chose. A country that had faced the Mediterranean for a thousand years turned to face east, moved its capital inland to a spot it has never left, and joined a world whose centre lay in another direction. That reorientation was decided by where an army made its camp, and it has held for fourteen centuries.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Arab conquest of Egypt known for?

For being extraordinarily fast against an unexpectedly weak defence, and for being the hinge between Byzantine Christian Egypt and the Arab and Islamic world. A force of a few thousand entered the empire's richest province in late 639 and the province had changed hands permanently by the autumn of 642. It is also known for what it was not: it was not a devastation, since the two principal objectives both changed hands by negotiated agreement, and it was not the Islamization of Egypt, which took centuries afterward through entirely different mechanisms. Its most permanent consequence was geographical, since it moved Egypt's capital inland to the head of the Delta and turned a country that had faced the Mediterranean for a thousand years to face east instead.

### Q: What year was the Arab conquest of Egypt?

The campaign ran from late 639 to the autumn of 642, with a coda in 645 and 646. The invading force crossed the eastern frontier in December 639, took the frontier town early in 640, defeated the Byzantine field army in the summer of 640, besieged and took the great inland fortress between the autumn of 640 and the spring of 641, negotiated a treaty for Alexandria late in 641 that provided for a truce of roughly eleven months, and received the city in the autumn of 642. A Byzantine seaborne expedition retook Alexandria in 645 and the Arab commander recovered it in 646, after which no further imperial attempt was made. These dates are secure within months.

### Q: How did the Arab conquest of Egypt happen?

In four phases. The force entered by the traditional eastern land route and reduced the frontier town after several weeks. It then advanced inland selectively, bypassing places it could not take quickly, and paused for a reinforcement that roughly tripled its strength. It defeated the Byzantine field army in a single engagement near the apex of the Delta in the summer of 640, after which the defence consisted of fortified places with no prospect of relief. It besieged the great fortress at the head of the Delta for roughly seven months and took it on terms in the spring of 641. Alexandria was then obtained by treaty, with a truce of about eleven months allowing Byzantine forces and departing inhabitants to evacuate before the handover.

### Q: Why did the Arab conquest of Egypt succeed?

Five factors, in order of weight. Imperial exhaustion after a twenty six year war with Persia, a decade of Persian occupation of Egypt itself and an incomplete reconstruction left no reserves and no relieving force. The destruction of the Byzantine field army in the summer of 640 reduced the defence to fortified places falling in sequence. Two centuries of doctrinal enforcement had estranged the Christian majority, removing the intelligence, labour, levies and local cooperation any defence requires. The emperor died in the middle of the campaign and the disputed succession that followed removed any coordinated response at the decisive moment. And the invading force was mobile, cohesive and well led, though small and incapable of reducing first class fortifications quickly.

### Q: Who led the Arab conquest of Egypt?

Amr ibn al-As commanded the campaign, operating under the caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab, with a reinforcing contingent brought by a second prominent commander before the decisive field battle. Amr subsequently governed the province, founded the settlement that became its new capital, negotiated the arrangements with the Christian community including safe conduct for the Egyptian patriarch who had been in hiding, and recovered Alexandria after the Byzantine counter-attack. His career, his relationship with the caliphs, the question of whether the invasion was fully authorized, and the assessment of his conduct as commander and governor all belong to his own article in this series, which owns those questions rather than this one.

### Q: How long did the Arab conquest of Egypt take?

Roughly three years from the crossing of the frontier in December 639 to the handover of Alexandria in the autumn of 642, with the loss and recovery of Alexandria in 645 and 646 as a coda. The internal distribution of that time is informative: several weeks for the frontier town, a few months of advance and a pause for reinforcement, one summer engagement, seven months besieging the inland fortress, and then roughly a year of negotiation and truce before Alexandria changed hands. More than half the campaign's duration was spent either besieging one fortress or waiting out an agreed truce, which is not the profile of an irresistible advance.

### Q: Did Egyptians resist the Arab conquest?

In places, yes, and the fullest Egyptian account of the conquest, written by a bishop within living memory, describes the invasion in hostile terms and records fighting, violence and requisition. What did not happen was any general mobilization to defend Byzantine rule. There were no popular levies, no towns holding out from local determination rather than garrison presence, and no resistance movement after the province fell. The clearest test came in 645, when a Byzantine expedition retook Alexandria and no general rising followed. The evidence supports absence of loyalty to the empire rather than either enthusiastic welcome for the invaders or determined popular resistance to them.

### Q: What changed after the Arab conquest of Egypt?

Immediately: the government, from an empire ruled from Constantinople to a caliphate ruled from Arabia; the capital, from a Mediterranean city to a new inland settlement at the head of the Delta; the destination of the grain surplus, redirected through a reopened canal to the Red Sea; the position of the imperially backed church, which lost state support as the Egyptian patriarch returned from hiding; and the tax framework, which granted protected status in exchange for payment. Not changed for centuries: the population, the language of daily life, the religion of the great majority, the agricultural calendar, the administrative personnel at working level, the legal practice governing contracts, and the churches and monasteries with their property.

### Q: What was the Babylon Fortress and why did it matter?

A Roman fortress at the head of the Delta, on the site now within Old Cairo, built to control the point where the Nile valley opens into the Delta and where the routes east toward Sinai met the river. Parts of its massive walls and round towers still stand, sunk below modern ground level because the city has risen around them. It was the strongest fortification in Egypt outside Alexandria and it held the strategic key to the country, since whoever controlled it controlled communication between Upper Egypt and the Delta. Its siege ran roughly seven months from the autumn of 640 and ended in surrender on terms, and the invaders established their camp beside it, which became the new provincial capital.

### Q: How did Alexandria fall to the Arabs?

By treaty rather than assault, because a siege could not have worked. The city's walls were formidable, its position between sea and lake made encirclement difficult, and it could be supplied indefinitely by a Byzantine fleet the invaders had no means of contesting. What defeated it was political. The emperor died early in 641, the succession in Constantinople was disputed for months, no relief expedition was being organized, the field army had already been destroyed and the rest of the province was lost. The provincial official conducting negotiations concluded the province could not be held and agreed terms late in 641 providing for tribute, evacuation and a truce of about eleven months. The handover in the autumn of 642 left no destruction layer.

### Q: How big was the Arab army that took Egypt?

Small, and the figures quoted in narrative sources should not be trusted. What can be established comes from inference rather than from any source's numbers. A force that needed several weeks to reduce a frontier town was small. A force that waited for reinforcement before risking a field battle was small at the outset. A force that took seven months over one fortress had no siege train. A force that could not blockade Alexandria had no fleet. Those constraints together place the invading army in the low thousands initially and in the low tens of thousands at most after reinforcement. Premodern military figures are unreliable generally, since nobody counted and both sides had reasons to inflate the other.

### Q: Why was Byzantine Egypt so poorly defended?

Because the empire had been comprehensively drained. A twenty six year war with Persia had exhausted both empires. Persian forces had occupied Egypt itself for roughly a decade, and imperial authority had been restored for barely ten years when the invasion came, leaving reconstruction incomplete and arrangements provisional. Plague had struck repeatedly across the eastern Mediterranean for a century, damaging manpower and revenue. Egypt had not been a frontier province for centuries and was not garrisoned as one, its forces being dispersed among fortified points with limited field strength. Above all, Syria fell before Egypt, which meant no reserve existed to draw on and no relieving army could plausibly arrive from anywhere.

### Q: Who was Cyrus the patriarch and what did he negotiate?

The official sent from Constantinople who combined ecclesiastical and civil authority in Egypt, charged with securing conformity to the imperial religious formula in the decade before the invasion, and remembered in Egyptian tradition as the author of a harsh period of pressure. He conducted the negotiations with the invaders, was recalled to Constantinople at one point on suspicion of exceeding his instructions, returned after the emperor's death, and agreed the treaty for Alexandria late in 641 providing for tribute, evacuation and a truce. Whether he was a realist assessing an unwinnable position or a man who had lost confidence in the empire he served is not recoverable, and the sources describing him are irreconcilable on the point.

### Q: What terms did the Egyptians get after surrender?

Protected status in exchange for payment. A poll tax fell on adult non Muslim males, with exemptions generally covering the poor, the elderly, women, children, the disabled and in most readings clergy and monks, and a land tax fell on agricultural property. In exchange the community retained its churches, its internal legal autonomy, its right to worship and protection. Conversion was not required and was not the immediate objective, which is comprehensible from the conquerors' side, since a protected non Muslim population is a taxable one. Administrative continuity was substantial, with the existing machinery operating under new ultimate authority. The Egyptian patriarch returned from more than a decade in hiding under a guarantee of safety from the new commander.

### Q: Did Alexandria have to be captured twice?

Effectively three times, counting the negotiated handover. The city passed by treaty in the autumn of 642. A Byzantine seaborne expedition retook it in 645, which proved that recovery by sea had been feasible all along and that the failure to attempt it in 642 was a failure of political capacity rather than military possibility. The Arab commander returned with a field force and recovered the city in 646, after which the empire made no further attempt. The 645 episode is the most useful single piece of evidence about popular sentiment, since the arrival of an imperial force was an opportunity for anyone preferring Byzantine rule to act on it, and no general rising followed.

### Q: Was Egypt still Christian after the conquest?

Overwhelmingly, and it remained so for centuries. The conquest changed the government, not the religion of the population. Conversion proceeded slowly through administrative advantage, fiscal incentive, intermarriage, employment and generational drift, and the crossover to a Muslim majority is generally placed somewhere between the ninth and twelfth centuries, with the pace actively debated among scholars. Nothing in the conquest settlement required or encouraged rapid conversion, and the fiscal logic pointed the other way in the first generations, since a protected non Muslim population was taxable on a basis a converted one was not. The immediate religious consequence was favourable to the Egyptian church, which had its imperially backed rival removed.

### Q: How did the conquest change Egypt's grain trade?

It reversed its direction. Egyptian grain had fed Constantinople for three centuries, and the political survival of the imperial government had depended on that flow continuing regardless of local conditions. After the conquest the surplus was redirected to the new political centre in Arabia, carried through a canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea that was reopened for the purpose. This mattered enormously to a region that could not feed its growing urban populations locally, and it is one of the most consequential infrastructural decisions of the early Islamic period. For Constantinople the loss forced a fundamental change in how the capital fed itself, and the reduction in resources shaped Byzantine history for centuries afterward.

### Q: What sources describe the Arab conquest of Egypt?

Four categories with very different reliability. The Egyptian Christian account written closest to the events, by a bishop who lived through the period, is the most valuable narrative but survives only through two successive translations with portions damaged. The Arabic conquest narratives were composed from the ninth century onward, are detailed, disagree with each other, and were shaped by legal interests, since the classification of territory as taken by force or by treaty determined tax rules for centuries. Byzantine chronicles are brief on Egypt and mostly later. Documentary papyri are the most reliable and the narrowest, showing the administration functioning, and a bilingual Greek and Arabic receipt from 643 is the key document.

### Q: Was there a Byzantine attempt to retake Egypt?

Yes, in 645, and it succeeded initially. An imperial expedition arrived by sea and retook Alexandria, establishing that Byzantine naval superiority was intact and the sea route open. The expedition then failed to spread its success inland, and the Arab commander returned with a field force and recovered the city in 646. After that the empire made no further serious attempt. Two conclusions follow: recovering the port did not recover the province, because a functioning administration was already established along a river valley a thousand kilometres long, and the absence of any popular rising in support of the expedition is the clearest available evidence that the population was not seeking the restoration of imperial rule.

### Q: How does the Arab conquest of Egypt compare with Syria and Persia?

Syria is the close parallel and makes Egypt look less exceptional: an exhausted empire, a newly reconquered province, a population divided from the imperial church over doctrine, a field army destroyed in one engagement, and fortified places reduced in sequence. Persia is the instructive contrast, since it involved the destruction of a state rather than the transfer of a province, took considerably longer, and ended with a dynasty extinguished. Egypt lost a government while retaining its administration, personnel, population and most institutions, which is why the conquest looks so undramatic in the documentary record. Egypt's distinctive contribution to the comparison is its papyri, which allow the transition to be watched at the level of individual receipts.
