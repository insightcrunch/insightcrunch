For fifty years the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971 was amended once, in a modest housekeeping exercise, and otherwise left to be stretched by courts deciding one desperate petition at a time. The MTP Amendment Act 2021 is the amendment that ended that arrangement. It moved the outer gestational limit from twenty weeks to twenty four for a prescribed list of women, created a Medical Board in every State and Union territory to decide cases of substantial foetal abnormality beyond any limit, rewrote the confidentiality position with a criminal penalty, and replaced the words "married woman" and "her husband" with "any woman" and "her partner" in the ground that has carried the largest share of lawful terminations. It did all of this while deliberately refusing the simpler course of raising the limit for everyone, and that refusal is the fact about the amendment from which most of its later history, including a major Supreme Court judgment and an unusual recall proceeding, has flowed.

This article follows the amendment as a trail: what the statute said before, what pressure accumulated against it, what the amending Act changed clause by clause, what the rules made under it added within weeks of commencement, and what the courts have done with the new structure since. The base statute itself, its grounds, its place and consent provisions and its interaction with the criminal law, belongs to [the pillar article on the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act](/2015/08/01/mtp-act-abortion-law-india/), and the account here concentrates on the 2021 changes and their consequences. Where the trail reaches contested ground, the contest is stated in the terms its participants use, because in this field the disagreement is not a distraction from the law. It is the environment in which the law operates.

## The Act as originally enacted, in brief

The Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, Act 34 of 1971, received the assent of the President on 10 August 1971 and came into force on 1 April 1972. It was, and remains, an exception carved out of the criminal law: causing a miscarriage was an offence under the Indian Penal Code, and the Act specified the conditions under which a registered medical practitioner could terminate a pregnancy without incurring that liability. Its architecture was a gatekeeping one. Section 3 permitted termination where a practitioner formed an opinion in good faith that the continuance of the pregnancy would involve a risk to the life of the pregnant woman or of grave injury to her physical or mental health, or that there was a substantial risk that if the child were born it would suffer from physical or mental abnormalities so as to be seriously handicapped. Two explanations converted the most common hardships into presumptions: the anguish of a pregnancy alleged to have been caused by rape was presumed to be a grave injury to mental health, and the anguish of a pregnancy caused by the failure of a contraceptive method used by a married woman or her husband to limit the number of children could be presumed to be such an injury.

The counting structure was the part everyone remembered. Up to twelve weeks, the opinion of one registered medical practitioner sufficed. Between twelve and twenty weeks, the concurring opinions of two were required. Beyond twenty weeks the Act supplied no route at all, subject to a single emergency provision, Section 5, which lifts the length and opinion requirements where termination is immediately necessary to save the woman's life. Termination had to take place at a government hospital or a place approved for the purpose under Section 4, and Section 3(4) governed consent: the consent of the pregnant woman herself was the rule, with the written consent of a guardian substituted where she had not attained majority or was mentally ill. Around this core sat the machinery of certification, records and inspection that the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Rules and Regulations of the following decades elaborated.

Two features of the original design matter for understanding what happened in 2021. The first is that the Act distributed decisional authority to doctors rather than to the woman. The woman consented; the practitioner opined. Every later reform debate, including the debate over the 2021 amendment, has been a debate about where that authority should sit. The second is that the twenty week ceiling was a product of the medical assumptions of 1971, when the Shantilal Shah Committee, whose report preceded the Act, weighed the safety of the procedures then available. As diagnostic technology moved the detection of serious foetal abnormalities to, and often beyond, the twenty week anatomy scan, the ceiling began to cut across precisely the cases the Act's own abnormality ground existed to cover. The statute's internal tension, a ground that could frequently be established only after the deadline for acting on it had passed, is the single most important fact in the amendment's prehistory.

### What grounds must the doctors' opinion rest on?

The opinion must rest on one of two statutory grounds: that continuance would involve a risk to the life of the pregnant woman or of grave injury to her physical or mental health, or that there is a substantial risk the child, if born, would suffer from a serious physical or mental abnormality. The amendment left both grounds untouched.

The grounds deserve attention because public discussion of the amendment often proceeds as though the grounds changed. They did not. What changed was the counting structure around them, the classes of women who may invoke the second band, and the institution that decides the hardest cases. A practitioner forming the opinion may also take account of the pregnant woman's actual or reasonably foreseeable environment, a direction carried in Section 3(3) since 1971, which is how social and economic circumstances enter an assessment that is framed in the language of health. The two explanations operate within the first ground. A pregnancy alleged by the woman to have been caused by rape carries a statutory presumption that its anguish is a grave injury to her mental health, so the opinion does not have to be constructed from psychiatric evidence. A pregnancy caused by the failure of a contraceptive device or method carries the same presumptive assistance, and the 2021 amendment widened the class of women who may rely on it, a change examined clause by clause below.

## The drift: one minor amendment and a growing docket

Between 1971 and 2021 the principal Act was amended once. The Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Act, 2002, Act 64 of 2002, decentralised the approval of places where terminations may be performed, substituting a district level committee for the earlier state level process, and stiffened the punishment for the owner of an unapproved place. It touched nothing in Section 3. The gestational structure that the Shah Committee's era had produced therefore survived intact into a period in which its premises had dissolved. Ultrasound diagnosis of structural abnormalities became routine, and the standard window for detecting many of them settled at eighteen to twenty weeks, with confirmatory testing pushing decisions later still. Forensic and clinical practice in sexual assault cases produced pregnancies discovered late. The petitions began to arrive.

The widely reported beginning of the modern line was the case of Nikita Mehta in 2008, where a congenital cardiac defect in the foetus was detected after the twenty week line had passed and the Bombay High Court declined to permit a termination, reasoning within the statute as it stood. The case fixed public attention on the gap between the Act's abnormality ground and its ceiling, and it was followed by a lengthening procession of writ petitions in the Supreme Court and the High Courts: survivors of rape whose pregnancies were discovered late, minors, women carrying foetuses with abnormalities incompatible with life or involving severe suffering, women whose own health collapsed mid-pregnancy. Courts responded by building an unwritten extension of the Act. They constituted medical boards of doctors at public hospitals to examine the woman and report, and then, case by case, permitted or refused terminations beyond twenty weeks by writ. Permission was granted in many cases involving grave abnormality or assault and refused in others, and the outcomes turned on the medical report, the gestational stage reached by the time the court decided, and the bench's assessment of viability and risk. From about the middle of the following decade the Supreme Court itself regularised one element of the practice, constituting medical boards at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences and other public institutions to examine the woman and advise the Court before it decided, so that the board, which had begun as an evidentiary improvisation, became the standard instrument of the writ jurisdiction years before the statute adopted it. Legal scholars who have traced the practice note that the Court's boards were advisory and case-specific, constituted for the petitioner before the Court and dissolved with her case, and that their reports acquired a near-decisive weight, so that in substance the medical board was already deciding late terminations in India before Parliament wrote the institution into the Act. It was, in substance, a jurisdiction of exception administered at litigation speed in a field where every week of delay changes the medical facts.

The Union government's own account of why amendment became necessary adopted this description. The Statement of Objects and Reasons appended to the 2020 Bill recorded that several cases had been filed before the Supreme Court and the various High Courts seeking permission for terminating pregnancies beyond the twenty week limit under the Act, usually on the ground of the diagnosis of foetal anomalies or because the pregnancy was caused by rape, and that with the advancement of medical technology there was scope for increasing the upper gestational limit, especially for vulnerable women and for pregnancies with substantial foetal anomalies detected late. The Cabinet approved the Bill for introduction on 29 January 2020, describing its purpose as expanding access to safe and legal abortion services on therapeutic, eugenic, humanitarian and social grounds and as ensuring dignity, autonomy, confidentiality and justice for women who need to terminate a pregnancy. The litigation docket, in other words, was not merely the background to the amendment. It was the stated reason for it, and the design of the amendment, a judge-like board inside the statute, bears the docket's imprint throughout.

An earlier attempt deserves its place in the trail because the 2021 Act is partly defined against it. In 2014 the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare circulated a draft amendment bill prepared after stakeholder consultation. That draft proposed raising the limit to twenty four weeks, extending the contraceptive failure presumption, and, most controversially, widening the provider base beyond registered medical practitioners of the allopathic system to include categories of mid-level providers for early terminations. The draft drew support from public health advocates who pointed to the shortage of doctors in rural India and opposition from medical associations protective of the provider boundary, and it was never introduced in Parliament. The episode matters because the 2021 Act silently dropped the provider widening while enacting much of the rest, so the enacted amendment is narrower than the reform its own ministry had designed seven years earlier. The trail, as usual, records not only what changed but what was considered and abandoned.

## Passage of the MTP Amendment Bill and the MTP Amendment Act 2021

The Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Bill, 2020 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 2 March 2020 by the Minister of Health and Family Welfare, Dr Harsh Vardhan, and was passed by that House on 17 March 2020. Its consideration in the Rajya Sabha extended across the interrupted sessions of the pandemic year, and the upper House passed it on 16 March 2021, against a demand from opposition members that the Bill be referred to a Select Committee for closer examination, a demand the government did not accept. The Bill received the assent of the President on 25 March 2021 and was published as the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Act, 2021, Act 8 of 2021. Section 1 of the amending Act left commencement to notification, and the Central Government brought the Act into force on 24 September 2021. The amended rules followed within weeks: the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Rules, 2021 were notified on 12 October 2021, amending the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Rules, 2003, and it is in those rules that the categories of women eligible for the extended limit and the working procedure of the Medical Boards are found.

The upper House passed the Bill by voice vote, and the demand for a Select Committee, which would have taken the text apart clause by clause with evidence from stakeholders, was pressed and lost in the same sitting. The debates in both Houses rehearsed the positions that have structured commentary ever since. Members supporting the Bill emphasised the cruelty of the petition route, the position of rape survivors and minors, and the anomaly of a statute that recognised foetal abnormality as a ground while making the ground unusable in the cases where the diagnosis came late. Members pressing for wider change objected that the Bill preserved a doctor-centred framework, that it left the gestational decisions of the majority of women untouched beyond twenty weeks, that it made no room for transgender persons who can be pregnant, and that the Medical Board mechanism risked substituting an administrative queue for the judicial one. Women's health organisations that had campaigned on the issue, including coalitions that commented publicly on the Bill's introduction, recorded a parallel double response: welcome for the raised limit and the confidentiality clause, and criticism that the Bill was a need-based rather than a rights-based measure which left the decisive authority with doctors and boards rather than with the pregnant woman. The Federation of Obstetric and Gynaecological Societies of India, speaking for the profession that carries the Act's daily operation, welcomed the raising of the limit as aligning the law with clinical reality. These positions are set out at greater length in the assessment sections below; the point for the trail is that the enacted text was understood at the moment of passage to be a compromise, narrower than the 2014 draft behind it and narrower than the court practice in front of it.

## Commencement and the interval before the rules

The amending Act's own commencement clause deserves a paragraph, because the interval it created is a small lesson in how this statute works. The Act received assent on 25 March 2021 but, by its Section 1, came into force only on a date the Central Government appointed by notification, and the appointment chosen was 24 September 2021, some six months later. During that interval the unamended Act continued to govern, and petitions filed in it were decided under the twenty week law by courts exercising the same writ discretion they had exercised for a decade. The delay between assent and commencement is a common Indian drafting practice, defended as time for the machinery to be readied, and in this case the machinery argument had real content: Boards had to be notified by every State and Union territory, and the rules prescribing categories, Board functions and practitioner norms had to be made. Yet the interval also meant that for half a year the statute book carried an enacted promise that no woman could use, and the cases that arrived in that window were answered by the old law and the old discretion.

A second, shorter interval followed commencement. The Act as commenced referred in Section 3(2)(b) to categories "as may be prescribed by rules", and the prescribing rules were notified only on 12 October 2021, eighteen days after commencement. In strictness, the second gate had no categories to operate on during those eighteen days, and the Board's powers and functions awaited Rule 3A in the same instrument. No reported catastrophe attended the gap, because practitioners and hospitals sensibly worked to the Bill's well-publicised scheme and the courts stood behind them, but the episode exposes the structural truth this article returns to repeatedly: an amendment drafted to live in its rules is only as complete as the notification that finishes it, and the completeness date of the 2021 reform is 12 October 2021 rather than the assent date printed on the Act. Students of the series' other statutes will recognise the pattern, in which commencement, notification and rule-making distribute a single reform across three dates that public reporting habitually collapses into one.

The commencement history also fixes the reform's relationship to the pending caseload. A beneficial amendment to a procedural and permissive statute invites the question whether cases already in the system take its benefit. The courts that decided petitions straddling September and October 2021 worked with the amended framework for cases still before them, which is consistent with the general approach to remedial legislation, and the Supreme Court's own major engagement with the amended Act, the marital status appeal, concerned a pregnancy and a High Court refusal that fell squarely within the amended period. The trail therefore has no transitional jurisprudence of consequence to report, and that absence is itself worth recording in a series where other statutes, the criminal codes among them, generated years of savings-clause litigation.

## Section 3 rewritten: the changes clause by clause

The amending Act works principally on Section 3 of the principal Act, and the surest way to state the amendment is to follow the substituted text. Section 3(2), as substituted, provides that a pregnancy may be terminated by a registered medical practitioner where the length of the pregnancy does not exceed twenty weeks, if such medical practitioner is of the opinion, formed in good faith, that one of the two grounds is made out; and where the length of the pregnancy exceeds twenty weeks but does not exceed twenty four weeks, in the case of such category of woman as may be prescribed by rules made under the Act, if not less than two registered medical practitioners are of that opinion. The twelve week internal line, which for fifty years doubled the number of opinions required mid-pregnancy, disappears. In its place there is a single threshold at twenty weeks, below which one good faith opinion on a statutory ground suffices for any woman, and a band from twenty to twenty four weeks in which two opinions are required and eligibility is filtered by category.

Around this core the amendment builds its ancillary provisions. A new sub-section (2A) provides that the norms for the registered medical practitioner whose opinion is required at different gestational ages shall be as prescribed by rules, which is the hook on which the amended rules hang the experience and training qualifications for practitioners acting at each band, including the conditions under which termination by medical methods up to nine weeks may be performed. New sub-section (2B) disapplies the length provisions altogether where termination is necessitated by the diagnosis of any of the substantial foetal abnormalities diagnosed by a Medical Board. New sub-section (2C) obliges every State Government and Union territory administration, by notification in the Official Gazette, to constitute a Board to be called a Medical Board for the purposes of the Act, to exercise such powers and perform such functions as the rules prescribe. New sub-section (2D) fixes the Board's composition in the Act itself: a Gynaecologist, a Paediatrician, a Radiologist or Sonologist, and such other number of members as may be notified in the Official Gazette by the State Government or Union territory. The definitions inserted into Section 2 belong to the same design: "Medical Board" means the Board constituted under sub-section (2C), and "termination of pregnancy" is defined for the first time in the Act's history as a procedure to terminate a pregnancy by using medical or surgical methods.

The explanations to Section 3(2) were recast as part of the substitution. Explanation 1 provides that for the purposes of clause (a), where a pregnancy occurs as a result of the failure of any device or method used by any woman or her partner for the purpose of limiting the number of children or preventing pregnancy, the anguish caused by such pregnancy may be presumed to constitute a grave injury to the mental health of the pregnant woman. Explanation 2 provides that for the purposes of clauses (a) and (b), where a pregnancy is alleged by the pregnant woman to have been caused by rape, the anguish caused by the pregnancy shall be presumed to constitute a grave injury to her mental health. Two features of this drafting carry large consequences. The contraceptive failure presumption speaks of any woman and her partner, where the 1971 text spoke of a married woman and her husband, so the statutory text itself no longer conditions this ground on marriage, a point whose significance the Supreme Court would later seize upon. And the presumptions reach differently across the bands: the contraceptive failure presumption is written for clause (a) alone, the first twenty weeks, while the rape presumption is written for both clauses, so a survivor's statutory route into the twenty to twenty four week band runs through the rules' categories and the rape presumption together.

What the substitution did not touch is equally part of the record. The two grounds in Section 3(2) were carried over without alteration, including the abnormality ground's wording of a substantial risk that the child, if born, would suffer from any serious physical or mental abnormality. The consent provision, Section 3(4), was not amended: the consent of the pregnant woman remains the requirement, with a guardian's written consent substituted for a woman who has not attained majority or who is mentally ill. The place provision, Section 4, was not amended, so terminations remain confined to government hospitals and approved places. The life-saving provision, Section 5, was not amended. And no provision of the amending Act creates an entitlement exercisable against an unwilling practitioner: the whole structure continues to operate through opinions formed in good faith by registered medical practitioners, a continuity that the critics of the framework place at the centre of their case and that the defenders of the framework defend as the statute's medical character.

### Can a pregnancy be terminated at any stage to save the woman's life?

Yes. Section 5 of the Act, which the 2021 amendment left untouched, provides that the restrictions in Section 3 on length of pregnancy and on the number of practitioners whose opinion is required do not apply where a registered medical practitioner forms the opinion in good faith that termination is immediately necessary to save the life of the pregnant woman.

Section 5 is the pressure valve of the whole scheme, and its survival unamended through the 2021 exercise is a useful index of the amendment's caution. Even in the new architecture of gates and boards, the emergency route requires no second opinion, no category and no board, because the legislature of 1971 and the legislature of 2021 agreed on one thing: where the woman's life is immediately at stake, the decisional structure must collapse to the single doctor at the bedside. The provision's limits are equally instructive. It is keyed to saving life, not to grave injury short of mortal danger, and practitioners asked to act at advanced gestation in harrowing but not immediately life-threatening circumstances must find their authority elsewhere, in the board route or in a court's writ. Several of the hardest reported cases turn on exactly this gap between grave injury, which Section 3 covers within its limits, and immediate danger to life, which is all Section 5 covers beyond them.

### Did the amendment change who may perform a termination?

No. A termination may still be performed only by a registered medical practitioner, and the amendment did not adopt the 2014 draft's proposal to widen the provider base to mid-level practitioners. What it changed was the rule-making hook: Section 3(2A) leaves the norms for the practitioner whose opinion is required at each gestational age to the rules.

Those norms, as prescribed, continue to demand prescribed experience and training in gynaecology and obstetrics, and the amended rules spell out the conditions for termination by medical methods at the earliest stage, including a short supervised experience pathway for practitioners acting up to nine weeks. The practical consequence is that the amendment expanded what the existing provider class may lawfully do while leaving the composition of that class where it found it. Public health researchers who supported the 2014 draft regard this as one of the amendment's significant omissions, since the districts where boards and specialists are scarcest are the same districts where registered practitioners with the prescribed training are fewest. The medical associations that opposed the widening regard the boundary as a safety measure. The text itself simply does not address the question, and the silence is one reason the trail's next amendment, whenever it comes, is widely expected to reopen the provider issue.

## The amending Act read as a whole

Before descending into the categories, it is worth reading the amending Act as its drafters arranged it, because its five sections disclose the reform's theory of itself. Section 1 gives the short title and leaves commencement to notification, the two-stage commencement examined above. Section 2 works on the definitions in the principal Act, inserting the definition of "Medical Board" by reference to Section 3(2C) and the Act's first definition of "termination of pregnancy" as a procedure to terminate a pregnancy by using medical or surgical methods. The definitional change is easy to pass over and should not be: for fifty years the Act had regulated a procedure it never defined, and the new definition, by naming medical methods alongside surgical ones in the Act itself, aligns the statutory language with the pharmaceutical reality in which a large share of early terminations are effected by drugs rather than by operation, and removes any argument that a medical method termination sits outside the Act's protection or its discipline. Section 3 of the amending Act then performs the central substitution in Section 3 of the principal Act, and Section 4 inserts Section 5A. Section 5 completes the machine by amending Section 6, the rule-making power, to add the three heads under which the operative detail would be prescribed: the category of woman, the norms for practitioners at different gestational ages, and the powers and functions of the Medical Board.

Arranged this way, the amending Act is a study in legislative economy and legislative delegation in equal measure. Everything Parliament decided for itself is in the substituted Section 3 and the new Section 5A: two numbers, a board, a disapplication, a confidentiality duty. Everything that determines who actually benefits, the category list, the Board's procedure and clock, the practitioner's required norms, is assigned in a single section to rules that the executive would make and could remake. A reader comparing this amendment with the great substituting amendments elsewhere in this series, which carry their schedules and categories within the four corners of the enacted text, will see the difference of technique at once. The 2021 Parliament legislated the architecture and delegated the allocation, and the merits and defects examined in the rest of this article flow from that allocation decision more than from any other single feature of the Act.

## The categories: Rule 3B and the delegation at the heart of the amendment

Section 3(2)(b) extends the limit to twenty four weeks only for "such category of woman as may be prescribed by rules made under this Act". The amending Act inserted the corresponding heads into the rule-making power in Section 6, authorising rules on the category of woman under clause (b), on the norms for practitioners under sub-section (2A), and on the powers and functions of the Medical Board. The operative list then arrived with the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Rules, 2021, which inserted Rule 3B into the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Rules, 2003. Rule 3B provides that the following categories of women shall be considered eligible for termination of pregnancy for a period of up to twenty four weeks: survivors of sexual assault or rape or incest; minors; women whose marital status changes during the ongoing pregnancy through widowhood or divorce; women with physical disabilities, with major disability measured by the criteria laid down under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016; mentally ill women including mental retardation; cases of foetal malformation that has a substantial risk of being incompatible with life or, if the child is born, of the child suffering from such physical or mental abnormalities as to be seriously handicapped; and women with pregnancy in humanitarian settings or in disaster or emergency situations as may be declared by the Government.

Read closely, the list is a map of the petition docket that produced the amendment. Every category answers a recurring fact pattern from the writ years. The assault survivor discovered her pregnancy late. The minor's pregnancy came to light through a school, a hospital or a police report months after the event. The widow or the divorced woman found her circumstances transformed mid-pregnancy by an event she did not choose. The woman with a disability faced barriers of access and communication that consumed the early weeks. The anomaly scan at twenty weeks returned a result that required confirmation after twenty weeks. Parliament and the rule-maker between them converted the case law of exception into a schedule of types. That is the amendment's genuine achievement, and it is also its structural choice: eligibility for the second band is a property of the woman's situation as the rules describe it, not of the pregnancy's medical facts alone. Two women at identical gestational stages, with identical medical findings, may stand on opposite sides of the twenty four week line because one falls within a Rule 3B description and the other does not. The brief for this article calls that design "access by category, not by stage", and the sections below test the claim against the record.

The category that has attracted the closest attention is the third, change of marital status during the ongoing pregnancy, expressed in the rule by the parenthetical examples of widowhood and divorce. The drafting poses an immediate question: is the category the genus, change of marital status, illustrated by two instances, or is it exhausted by the two instances named? On the narrow reading, a woman whose unmarried relationship ends during pregnancy, or whose partner abandons her, experiences a change in her circumstances but not in her marital status, since she had none in law to change. On the wider reading, the parenthetical is illustrative and the genus is a change in the woman's relational circumstances during pregnancy. That question, which is a question about the construction of a rule that Parliament did not write and cannot be answered from the Act, reached the Supreme Court within a year of commencement and produced the judgment discussed below. It is worth pausing on the institutional fact: the single most litigated phrase in the amended law is a parenthesis in a rule, made by the executive under a power the Act conferred in one line. The series' recurring thesis, that in Indian legislation the operative content so often sits in subordinate legislation that the parent Act merely enables, has no clearer instance in the health cluster.

The disability category carries its own precision requirement. Rule 3B measures the physical disability limb by the criteria of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, the statute that defines benchmark disability and the schedule of specified disabilities, which is why the disability analysis in this field cannot be separated from that Act's framework (the disability critique of the abnormality ground is a different matter, addressed in the assessment below, and the pillar treatment of the 2016 Act is at [the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 explained](/2017/08/01/rpwd-act-2016-explained/)). The mental illness limb speaks of mentally ill women including mental retardation, using the vocabulary the 2003 Rules carried, a usage that sits awkwardly beside the modern statutory language of intellectual disability and beside the consent position in Section 3(4), where a mentally ill woman's termination requires her guardian's written consent. The awkwardness is not merely terminological: the same woman whom Rule 3B treats as a member of an eligible category is a woman whose own consent the Act displaces, and the interaction between her eligibility and her displaced consent has had to be worked out by courts attentive to her expressed wishes, in a line of cases of which the Supreme Court's decision in Suchita Srivastava against the Chandigarh Administration, though delivered under the unamended Act, remains the reference point for the principle that a woman's reproductive choice is an aspect of her personal liberty and that the best interests inquiry cannot be used to override the choice of a woman capable of expressing one.

## The categories at work: how Rule 3B operates case by case

A list of categories is best understood by watching it sort cases. The assault survivor's category is the list's engine, because it combines with Explanation 2's presumption: her allegation that the pregnancy was caused by rape is enough to raise the presumption of grave injury to mental health, and her category membership carries her into the second band, so the two practitioners' task is certification of the ground rather than an inquiry into the assault. No first information report is a statutory precondition, and the Supreme Court's 2022 judgment reinforced the point in the marital context by holding that a woman need not set the criminal process in motion to be believed for the Act's purposes. The practical frictions lie elsewhere: in hospitals that demand documents the statute does not, in the medico-legal examination habits that treat every such case as evidence collection first and care second, and in the interaction with the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act where the survivor is a minor, considered below.

The minor's category answers the discovery problem. Pregnancies of minors are characteristically discovered late, through a school medical check, a hospital visit for unrelated symptoms, or a disclosure months after the event, and under the old law the discovery date frequently sat beyond twenty weeks, which is why minors recur so often in the writ reports. Rule 3B moves the ordinary case inside the statute: the minor is eligible to twenty four weeks on two opinions, and the guardian's written consent under Section 3(4) is the operative formality. What the category cannot do is move the cases discovered later still, and a minor found pregnant at twenty six weeks remains dependent on the abnormality route or a court, which is why the minor's category, for all its clarity, has not emptied the courts of minor cases. The marital status category has been transformed by construction, as the 2022 judgment section explains, from a widowhood and divorce provision into a broader changed-circumstances provision. Its operation in hospitals nevertheless continues to depend on how certifying practitioners read it, and the distance between the judgment's construction and a cautious district practitioner's reading is one of the amended Act's live implementation problems.

The disability and mental illness categories expose the list's internal tensions most sharply. A woman with a major physical disability, measured by the 2016 Act's criteria, is eligible to twenty four weeks: the legislative judgment is that the barriers of access, transport, communication and institutional delay that disability interposes justify the longer window, a judgment disability advocates have generally received as recognition rather than condescension, because the category advantages the woman herself. The mental illness category sits beside a consent rule that displaces the woman's own consent in favour of her guardian's, so the Act simultaneously treats her as a person whose situation merits extended access and as a person whose authorisation is not the one the law requires. The Supreme Court's older authority in Suchita Srivastava, distinguishing a woman's expressed wishes from a best interests assessment made over her head, is the standing judicial corrective to reading the two provisions together mechanically. The foetal malformation category is the bridge between the second and third gates: within twenty four weeks it operates as an ordinary Rule 3B category on two opinions, and beyond twenty four weeks the same diagnosis, when made by the Board at the level of substantial abnormality, engages Section 3(2B) and the ceiling falls away. The humanitarian category is the list's only inbuilt flexibility for the unforeseen: it awaits a government declaration about a setting or an emergency, and it therefore operates as a power held in reserve rather than as a standing entitlement, a design that has drawn the obvious criticism that a category dependent on declaration will rarely be declared in time for the women inside the emergency it describes.

Three illustrations, drawn from the recurring fact patterns of the reported cases and stated as illustrations rather than as reports, show the sorting at work. Take first a woman of twenty four whose pregnancy by a long-term partner is discovered at twenty one weeks, the partner having withdrawn from the relationship. Before the amendment she had no route: the contraceptive failure presumption was confined to married women, and twenty one weeks was beyond every line. Under the amended Act she passes the first gate only if she is within twenty weeks, which she is not; at the second gate she needs a category, and after the 2022 judgment the changed-circumstances construction of Rule 3B's third category is the one her advisers will invoke. Take secondly a girl of sixteen found pregnant at twenty three weeks. Before the amendment, a writ petition was the standard route and the courts granted many such permissions on board reports. Under the amended Act she is within her category, her guardian consents in writing, two practitioners certify, and no court is involved at all: the amendment's cleanest success. Take thirdly a woman whose twenty week anatomy scan suggests a cardiac abnormality, confirmed by foetal echocardiography at twenty five weeks as severe and incompatible with sustained life. Before the amendment she petitioned, and courts granted or refused on per-case boards. Under the amended Act the standing Board diagnoses substantial foetal abnormality, Section 3(2B) disapplies the ceiling, and the termination proceeds on the Board's Form D opinion: the amendment working precisely as designed, in the class of case that did most to produce it. The contrast among the three is the amendment in miniature. Where the case fits a category or a Board diagnosis, the statute answers it. Where it fits neither, nothing has changed since 1971 except the ceiling the case must be argued against.

### What does "substantial foetal abnormality" mean in the amended Act?

The Act does not define the phrase further. Section 3(2B) speaks of "any of the substantial foetal abnormalities diagnosed by a Medical Board", and Rule 3A states the test the Board applies: a substantial risk that the malformation is incompatible with life, or that the child, if born, would suffer such physical or mental abnormalities as to be seriously handicapped.

The absence of a schedule of qualifying conditions is deliberate in structure even if its wisdom is debated. No list of named conditions could track foetal medicine, in which prognosis turns on severity, combination and progression rather than on diagnosis alone, so the legislature committed the judgment to a specialist body applying a described standard. The cost is variance: two boards in two States, reading the same scans, may assess substantial risk differently, and the woman whose board reads narrowly must then persuade a court to read it again. The phrase also carries the amendment's deepest value dispute within it, because "seriously handicapped" is the statutory language through which the disability critique enters, and the Board members who apply the words are clinicians, not philosophers, working a standard whose moral content the legislature chose not to examine in the text.

## Section 5A: the confidentiality offence

The fifth change made by the amending Act is the insertion of a new Section 5A, headed "Protection of privacy of a woman". Section 5A(1) provides that no registered medical practitioner shall reveal the name and other particulars of a woman whose pregnancy has been terminated under this Act except to a person authorised by any law for the time being in force. Section 5A(2) provides that whoever contravenes the provision shall be punishable with imprisonment which may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both. Before 2021, confidentiality for women using the Act rested on the admission register regime under the regulations, which treats the register as a secret document and serialises entries without names in the returns, and on the general law of medical confidence. The amendment converted the practitioner's duty into a statutory prohibition backed by a criminal penalty, and it did so in the language of privacy that the Cabinet's approval note had used when it listed confidentiality among the values the Bill was to ensure.

Two readings of the provision competed from the start, and both should be recorded because both continue. On the first reading, Section 5A is among the amendment's most consequential provisions, precisely because it is the least discussed. Fear of exposure is a documented driver of delay and of resort to unqualified providers, particularly for unmarried women, minors and survivors, and a woman deciding whether to enter the lawful system weighs the risk that her name will travel. A prohibition with a penalty speaks to that fear in a way that a departmental instruction cannot, and it binds the practitioner who is the person best placed to leak the particulars. On the second reading, pressed by critics of the Bill during its passage, the exception for disclosure "to a person authorised by any law" is wide enough to swallow much of the protection, because other laws authorise a great deal of disclosure: mandatory reporting obligations under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act for minors, police requisitions in assault investigations, and court orders all pass through the exception, so the woman's particulars remain available to the state through several doors even as the practitioner's casual disclosure is criminalised. The tension is sharpest for minors, whose pregnancies the criminal law treats as evidence of an offence and whose confidentiality under Section 5A coexists with a reporting duty under another statute that the practitioner ignores at the cost of prosecution. The provision also allocates its penalty asymmetrically: the prohibition in terms binds the registered medical practitioner, while the punishment clause speaks of "whoever", a drafting breadth that commentators have noticed without the point having been tested. What can be said on the record is that the amendment made confidentiality a statutory feature of termination care for the first time, that the Supreme Court's privacy jurisprudence, from the nine-judge decision in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) against the Union of India onwards, supplies the constitutional frame in which the provision will be read when it is tested, and that the [Puttaswamy privacy judgment and its Aadhaar sequel](/2019/05/15/puttaswamy-privacy-aadhaar-judgment/) is the necessary background for any reader who wants to understand why a one-section confidentiality offence was thought worth enacting at all.

## The Medical Board and the three gates

The amended Act distributes decisions across three gates, and a reader who keeps the gates separate will not be misled by the commonest errors in reporting, which treat the twenty four week figure as a general limit and the Medical Board as a body that supervises all late terminations. The first gate is the ordinary one: up to twenty weeks, one registered medical practitioner, acting on a statutory ground, in good faith, at an approved place, with the woman's consent. The second gate governs the band from twenty to twenty four weeks: two practitioners, a statutory ground, and a woman who falls within a Rule 3B category. The third gate is not a band at all. Where a Medical Board diagnoses any of the substantial foetal abnormalities, Section 3(2B) disapplies the length provisions, so a termination necessitated by that diagnosis may proceed beyond twenty four weeks without any statutory ceiling. The Board's province is therefore diagnosis, not general permission: it has no power to authorise a post-twenty four week termination on social or economic grounds, however compelling, and no power to refuse one within the first two gates, where it has no role.

The Board's composition is fixed by Section 3(2D): a Gynaecologist, a Paediatrician, a Radiologist or Sonologist, and such other members as the State Government or Union territory notifies. The amended rules, in the inserted Rule 3A, prescribe its powers and functions: to allow or deny termination beyond twenty four weeks under sub-section (2B) only after due consideration, ensuring that the procedure would be safe for the woman at that gestational age and whether the foetal malformation has a substantial risk of being incompatible with life or of the child, if born, suffering such physical or mental abnormalities as to be seriously handicapped; to co-opt other specialists and call for additional investigations where required; to examine the woman and her reports; to provide its opinion in Form D, the prescribed form, within three days of receiving the request; and, where it advises termination, to ensure the procedure is carried out with all safety precautions and appropriate counselling within five days of the receipt of the request. The design consciously mirrors the ad hoc boards the courts had been constituting for a decade, with two deliberate improvements on the writ model: the Board is standing rather than improvised per case, and the rules impose a decision clock, three days for the opinion and five days for the procedure, of a kind no court order had standardised.

### Can the Medical Board overrule two doctors who are willing to terminate?

Within twenty four weeks, the question cannot arise, because the Board has no jurisdiction there: the first two gates belong to the treating practitioners, and a Board neither approves nor vetoes their opinions. The Board's authority begins only where the practitioners' authority ends, beyond twenty four weeks and only on the abnormality diagnosis.

The converse question is the one that reaches the courts: whether a court's power survives a Board's refusal. It does. A Board's negative opinion is an administrative and clinical determination under the Act, and the woman refused by it may invoke the writ jurisdiction, where the court will ordinarily obtain a fresh medical report and decide on the whole material. The practical hierarchy is therefore the reverse of the formal one. The Board is the statute's final authority at the third gate, but the constitutional courts stand behind it as they stood behind the twenty week ceiling before 2021, and a well-advised woman treats a Board refusal as the beginning of a litigation decision rather than as the end of her case.

## The Board in practice: delay inside a mechanism built to end delay

The case for the Board was administrative. A standing specialist body, deciding within days under a rule-imposed clock, would relieve women of the writ petition, with its filings, listings and constitutions of ad hoc boards, and would relieve courts of a docket for which the judicial process is medically clumsy. The case against the Board, made during the Bill's passage and repeated since, was that the design multiplies decision-makers precisely where time is the scarcest resource, that a board of senior specialists is an urban institution in a country where radiologists and paediatricians are unevenly distributed, and that an adverse or delayed board opinion simply returns the woman to the court the mechanism was meant to spare her. Both cases can be tested against the operating record, and the record, stated with the care the material requires, supports parts of each.

The recurring reported difficulties are of constitution, composition and convening. Section 3(2C) obliges every State and Union territory to constitute its Board by gazette notification, yet in the period after commencement, courts in individual cases continued to direct hospitals to constitute boards for the woman before them, which is difficult to explain unless the standing mechanism was unavailable or incomplete in that place at that time. High Courts have recorded, in terms, their frustration at the pattern: the Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh High Court, in a case where a couple was told at thirty one weeks that nothing could be done without the court's intervention, criticised doctors in the associated hospitals of medical colleges and district hospitals for not shouldering their statutory responsibilities and leaving women to approach the court as a last resort, and directed the superintendent of the Srinagar hospital to constitute a competent board and have the woman examined at once. Composition is the second difficulty. The Act names three specialists and leaves the remainder to notification; a Board that lacks its radiologist or its paediatrician on the day cannot sit with the authority the design assumes, and the specialists named are precisely those in shortest supply outside the large teaching hospitals. Convening is the third. The rule's three day opinion clock assumes the woman arrives with a complete record; additional investigations, which Rule 3A expressly empowers the Board to require, restart the practical timetable even where the formal one is honoured, and a co-opted specialist's report can take longer than the opinion it feeds.

The consequences of delay in this field are not the ordinary consequences of administrative slowness, and the amendment's critics and defenders agree on that much. A delayed decision does not preserve the status quo. Each week that passes moves the pregnancy to a later gestational stage, narrows the clinical options, increases the medical risk of the procedure the Board exists to assess, and can extinguish the entitlement altogether where the Board's eventual answer is no and the court route must then be taken at a still later stage. Delay inside this mechanism is therefore a form of decision, a point made in Parliament during the Bill's passage, made by advocates who have carried these cases, including Anubha Rastogi, a lawyer who has represented women in termination petitions and who argued that the Board layer would add delay and complicate access when specialists are unavailable in many districts, and made implicitly by every court that has complained of receiving a case at an advanced stage through no fault of the woman. The defenders of the design answer that the comparison must be with the writ system the Board replaced, not with an imagined frictionless alternative: the writ system also consumed weeks, also depended on medical boards constituted per case, and added the cost and publicity of litigation on top, so a standing board with a statutory clock is an improvement even where its performance is imperfect. The fair verdict on the record is that the Board is a genuine institutional advance over per-case improvisation in the places where it has been properly constituted and staffed, and a new bottleneck in the places where it has not, and that the amendment's central weakness is that it made the third gate depend on state administrative energy without providing a default mechanism, a reporting duty or a sanction for the State that lets its Board exist on paper only.

Documentation is the Board system's connective tissue, and it is worth a clinician's attention in its own right. The opinion of two practitioners is certified in the prescribed forms, the woman's consent is recorded in the prescribed consent form, the Board's opinion is rendered in Form D, and the admission register is maintained as a secret record whose serial numbers, rather than names, travel into official returns. Hospitals that administer the amended Act at any volume keep these instruments as working files, and practitioners reviewing a facility's records will commonly open the spreadsheet and document files in which registers and returns are maintained through an [office file viewer for the facility's termination records and returns](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) rather than through the originating application. The discipline matters legally and not merely administratively: in the litigation that continues to reach the courts, the case is usually won or lost on the quality and dating of the paper record, and a Board opinion that records its reasoning against the statutory test is worth more to the woman who must rely on it, and to the court that later reads it, than a bare conclusion.

## The nine week line: medical methods inside the amended rules

One change in the 2021 rules package has had practical consequences out of proportion to the attention it receives, because it concerns the stage at which the largest number of terminations occur. The amended rules prescribe, for termination by medical methods up to nine weeks of gestation, the experience and training a registered medical practitioner must hold, including a pathway of three months' experience in obstetrics and gynaecology at a hospital, or the independent performance of ten cases of termination by medical methods under the supervision of a registered medical practitioner in a government hospital or approved training institute. Read with the new statutory definition of termination of pregnancy, which speaks of medical or surgical methods in one breath, the rules complete a quiet reorientation of the Act's centre of gravity. The statute of 1971 was written around a surgical procedure performed in an approved place by a specialist. The framework after 2021 expressly accommodates a pharmaceutical procedure, begun on a practitioner's prescription within a supervised training standard, which is how early termination is in fact delivered across much of the country.

The accommodation is partial, and its limits mark another of the amendment's chosen boundaries. The Act's place requirement still governs, the certification and consent discipline still applies, and the practitioner prescribing must hold the prescribed experience, so the medical methods pathway widens what qualified practitioners may do without creating the self-managed or pharmacy-led model that some other jurisdictions recognise and that public health advocates continue to press for in the Indian debate. The nine week line also interacts with the counting structure in a way worth noticing: it sits well inside the first gate, where one opinion suffices, so the amendment's early-pregnancy regime, one practitioner, a medical method, a supervised training standard, is the most liberalised part of the whole framework, while its late-pregnancy regime is the most heavily mediated. That gradient, permissive early and gated late, is the amendment's real philosophy of gestation made operational, and it is stated nowhere in the Act's text. It has to be assembled from the rules, which is where this statute keeps its philosophy.

## The practitioner's side of the amendment: opinions, protection and exposure

The amendment is usually read from the woman's side of the consulting room door, but its daily operation is determined on the practitioner's side, and the trail is incomplete without the provisions that govern the doctor's position. The opinion the Act requires must be formed in good faith, and the certification regime gives that phrase its paperwork: the practitioner's opinion is recorded and certified in the prescribed form, the woman's consent is taken in the prescribed consent form, and the admission register is maintained at the place of termination as a record treated as secret, with entries serialised so that the returns sent to the district authorities carry numbers rather than names. The amended rules' insertion of Form D for the Board's opinion extends the same discipline to the third gate. A practitioner whose papers are in order is a practitioner who can show, to an employer, an inspector or a court, that each statutory element was addressed at the time, and the cases in which doctors have been harassed or prosecuted despite lawful work are characteristically cases in which the record, rather than the medicine, was incomplete.

Against the exposure stands a protection the amendment did not need to create because the original Act supplied it. Section 8 of the principal Act protects a registered medical practitioner from suit or other legal proceeding for any damage caused or likely to be caused by anything done or intended to be done in good faith under the Act. The good faith standard does double duty in the scheme: it is the quality the opinion must have to be valid, and it is the quality that earns the practitioner immunity, so the doctor who honestly forms and honestly records the statutory opinion is protected even if the opinion is later second-guessed. The criminal exposure that frames the whole field sits outside the Act, in the Penal Code provisions on causing miscarriage, which the Act displaces only for terminations that comply with it, and within the Act in the offence provisions addressed to those who operate outside it: termination by a person who is not a registered medical practitioner, and termination at a place that is not approved, are punishable with rigorous imprisonment for terms running from two to seven years. The 2021 amendment did not recalibrate any of these penalties, which is itself a datum about its character: it was an access amendment, not an enforcement one, and the penal perimeter of lawful practice in 2022 is the perimeter drawn in 1971 and stiffened in 2002.

One interface on the practitioner's side has generated more operational difficulty than the amendment itself, and a complete account must record it. Where the pregnant woman is a minor, the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 obliges any person, including the treating doctor, who has knowledge or apprehension of a sexual offence against the child to report it to the police, and makes failure to report an offence. The obligation sits in visible tension with the confidentiality the amended Act promises in Section 5A, whose own exception for persons authorised by law is the textual bridge between the two statutes. The Supreme Court confronted a cognate tension in the 2022 proceedings and in later directions concerning minors seeking termination, and the working position practitioners have extracted is that reporting obligations are complied with while identifying particulars are guarded within the space the statutes leave, a compromise that satisfies neither statute's text completely and that awaits legislative tidying. The episode is the confidentiality provision's first stress test, and it arrived from another statute rather than from within the Act, which is where the stresses on Indian health legislation usually originate.

### What protects a doctor who acts on a good faith opinion?

Section 8 of the Act provides that no suit or other legal proceeding shall lie against a registered medical practitioner for any damage caused or likely to be caused by anything done or intended to be done in good faith under the Act. The protection follows the quality of the opinion and the honesty of its recording.

The limits of the protection are as instructive as its breadth. It covers acts under the Act, so a termination that strays outside the statutory conditions, a wrong gestational band, an unapproved place, a missing consent, is outside the shelter and inside the Penal Code's ordinary provisions. It protects against proceedings for damage caused by the act done in good faith, not against departmental or professional-conduct inquiry into whether the faith was good. And it does not protect the institution's owner for breaches that are the owner's own, which is why the approval status of the place and the constitution of its records are matters of legal substance for hospital administrators and not merely of housekeeping.

## Access by category: the design choice examined

The amendment's central design choice can be stated exactly. Parliament had before it two ways of relieving the petition docket. The first was to raise the ceiling, from twenty weeks to twenty four or beyond, for every woman whose practitioners formed the statutory opinion, leaving the Act's grounds to do the filtering. The second was the course taken: raise the ceiling for a list. The Statement of Objects and Reasons framed the extension as being "especially for vulnerable women", and the vulnerability framing did the decisive work. It allowed the amendment to be presented as a targeted protection for women in defined hardship rather than as a general enlargement of reproductive choice, and it preserved the framework's medical-opinion character, because the filter operates on the woman's category rather than on any assessment of her reasons. The namable claim of this article is that the 2021 amendment extended access by category rather than by stage, so that the legal position of a woman at twenty two weeks depends on characteristics of her situation that may have nothing to do with the pregnancy's medical facts, and every major dispute the amendment has generated, in the courts and outside them, has been a dispute about the boundaries of the list rather than about the limit itself.

The strongest case for the design is administrative and political at once. A universal extension would have required the legislature to defend twenty four weeks as a line for all cases, including cases with no element of hardship, and to confront directly the question of foetal viability that a general line raises at its upper end. A categorical extension directs relief to the fact patterns that had actually filled the courts, which were patterns of hardship, late discovery and late diagnosis. It also gives practitioners a checklist in place of a judgment call, and checklists are what risk-averse clinical practice runs on: a doctor who must later justify an opinion to an employer, an insurer or a court can point to a rule and a category rather than to a construction of grave injury. The design's defenders within government made a further point during passage, that the categories are in rules rather than in the Act so that experience can correct the list without fresh primary legislation, and the point is a real one about legislative technique. Subordinate legislation can be amended by notification, and a list that proves under-inclusive can be repaired by the executive in weeks rather than by Parliament in years.

The delegation carries a risk its defenders acknowledged less readily, which is that a list held in rules can move in both directions without parliamentary scrutiny of the same order. A future executive persuaded that a category is too wide may delete or qualify it by notification, laid before Parliament under the ordinary subordinate-legislation procedure, which in practice attracts a fraction of the attention a bill receives. The history of the list so far runs the other way, toward expansion by judicial construction rather than executive revision, but the structural point stands and is the standard caution of this series about delegated legislation in rights-bearing fields: what the executive gives by notification, the executive can qualify by notification, and a protection that women hold at the level of a rule is held at one remove from the protection a section of an Act would give. The counter-consideration is real and should be recorded. Had the categories been written into the Act in 2021 in the form the ministry first proposed, the marital status boundary that the Supreme Court repaired in 2022 would have required either a constitutional strike-down of primary legislation or a fresh amendment to fix, and the repair the Court achieved by construing a rule would have been harder, slower and more confrontational. Delegation made the design flexible in exactly the place where it proved to be wrong.

The strongest case against the design is that it constitutionalises a hierarchy of deservingness among women in identical medical positions, and does so along lines that correlate with stigma. The woman at twenty two weeks whose contraceptive failed and whose long-term partner has left her is outside the list as first prescribed, while the woman whose husband has died is inside it, and the difference between them is not medical, not a difference in the gravity of injury the pregnancy inflicts, and not a difference either woman chose. Critics from the reproductive rights movement, including the Common Health coalition in its commentary on the Bill, objected as well to the process by which the list was made: a stakeholder consultation they regarded as inadequate for a measure allocating rights among women, and a drafting process conducted within the ministry rather than in public. The disability analysis of the list belongs to the next assessment section, because it raises a distinct objection that its holders direct at the abnormality provisions rather than at categorisation as such. For the categorisation question itself, the record since commencement supplies a natural experiment. Within eighteen months, the Supreme Court had to decide whether the list's most socially sensitive category could be confined to married women, and its answer, which rewrote the category's practical content without amending a word of the rule, suggests that a list drawn by marital and situational type was always going to be unstable under a Constitution that forbids the state to classify by stereotype. The fuller architecture of the health legislation within which this amendment sits, including the statutes on clinical establishments, mental healthcare and transplantation that allocate decisional authority in comparable ways, is mapped in the [complete guide to health legislation in India](/2015/06/01/health-laws-india-complete-guide/), against which the MTP framework's doctor-and-board model can be compared.

## The courts after commencement: the 2022 judgment on marital status

The leading judicial event in the amendment's life is the Supreme Court's judgment of 29 September 2022 in X against the Principal Secretary, Health and Family Welfare Department, Government of NCT of Delhi, Civil Appeal No. 5802 of 2022, delivered by a bench of Justices D. Y. Chandrachud, A. S. Bopanna and J. B. Pardiwala, in an opinion authored by Chandrachud J. The appellant was a twenty five year old unmarried woman, twenty three weeks pregnant, whose partner had refused to marry her. The Delhi High Court had declined to permit termination, holding that she fell outside Rule 3B. The Supreme Court allowed the appeal, and the reasoning proceeded on several distinct levels, which must be kept separate because later reporting has frequently run them together into a general declaration the judgment did not make.

At the level of construction, the Court read Rule 3B purposively. The rule's third category, change of marital status during the ongoing pregnancy, could not in the Court's view be confined to the married, because the mischief the category addresses is the transformation of a woman's circumstances during pregnancy, and an unmarried woman whose relationship ends, or whose partner abandons her, undergoes that transformation no less than a widow or a divorcee. The Court observed that the distinction the rule on a literal reading would draw between married and unmarried women was artificial, and that a construction that excluded unmarried women from the benefits of the amendment while including them in its burdens would not survive the equality guarantee of Article 14. At the level of constitutional principle, the judgment placed reproductive decision-making within the personal liberty and privacy protected by Article 21, in the line of the Court's earlier reasoning in Suchita Srivastava and in the Puttaswamy decision, holding that the right of every woman to make reproductive choices without state interference is an inseparable part of her personal liberty and that the decisional autonomy at stake belongs to the woman alone. At the level of the individual case, the Court permitted the termination, having before it a medical opinion that the procedure could be carried out without danger to the appellant's life.

The judgment's further holding requires the precision the brief for this article demands, because it is the holding most often overstated. The Court held that, for the purposes of the MTP Act and the rules made under it, the expression "rape" in the provisions that presume grave injury to mental health, and the corresponding category of survivors in Rule 3B, must be understood to include a married woman's allegation of forced sexual intercourse by her husband. The Court was explicit about the boundary of the proposition. Exception 2 to Section 375 of the Indian Penal Code, which removes intercourse by a man with his own wife from the definition of rape, is a creature of the criminal law, and reading "rape" inclusively for the purposes of the termination statute does not abolish or alter that exception in its own field. The holding is therefore a statute-specific construction: within the MTP framework, a married woman need not be turned away on the ground that what she alleges cannot in criminal law be rape, while in the criminal law itself the exception stands until Parliament or a constitutional bench displaces it. Readers tracing the criminal provision should note that the offence of rape, defined in Section 375 of the Penal Code with its marital exception in Exception 2, is carried in the successor code into Section 63 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, a renumbering whose full consequences are traced in the [comparison of the Indian Penal Code and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/). Within its own field, the construction matters practically: a married woman alleging forced intercourse may invoke the survivor category and the rape presumption without first establishing a criminal charge, and a practitioner may act on her allegation, which is what the statutory word "alleges" has always contemplated.

The procedural history of the appeal carries a lesson of its own about the system's response times. The Delhi High Court declined the appellant's petition in the middle of July 2022, at which point she was some twenty three weeks pregnant and the statutory window itself was closing. The Supreme Court, moved at once, first dealt with her case on an interim footing: after a medical board at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences reported that the pregnancy could be terminated without danger to her life, the Court modified the High Court's order and permitted her to undergo the procedure, reserving the questions of law for the full judgment that followed on 29 September 2022. The sequence is worth dwelling on because it shows the amended system and the old judicial safety valve operating in harness: the statute, as the High Court read it, excluded her; the Court's interim equity admitted her; and the final judgment then rewrote the reading so that the next woman in her position would not need the equity. Judgments that merely decide the case before them leave the gatekeeping structure untouched. This one adjusted the structure, which is why it, rather than any of the hundreds of individual permissions in the writ line, is the amendment's leading case.

The judgment also ranged beyond the rule it was construing, in passages that practitioners quote because they describe the system's ordinary failures rather than its exceptional ones. The Court addressed the phenomenon of providers refusing terminations the statute permits, observing that registered medical practitioners who decline on grounds the Act does not recognise, including moral objection and demands for documents or consents the Act does not require, force women into the courts and into unsafe alternatives, and that the object of the amended Act is defeated when its own gatekeepers will not operate its gates. It situated the statute against the background fact that unsafe abortion remains a significant cause of maternal death, treating access to the lawful system as a public health question and not only a private one. And it read the Act's "woman" in the light of the judgment's autonomy reasoning in a passage noting that persons other than cisgender women may also require access to safe termination, while deciding nothing on that question, which the text of the Act leaves where it found it. These passages are reasoning, not holdings on the questions they touch, and the careful reader will keep the distinction: the ratio of the case is the construction of Rule 3B and the statute-specific reading of rape, and the remainder is the Court instructing the system on how the amended Act is to be approached.

What the judgment did not do is as important to the trail. It did not strike down Rule 3B, did not declare a general right to termination up to twenty four weeks, and did not disturb the category structure of the amendment, with whose boundaries it was content to work by construction. Its method, an equality-driven reading of a rule rather than a reframing of the Act, left the design choice of 2021 intact while removing its most vulnerable application. The judgment also spoke to administration, urging that women not be forced to approach courts for what the statute already permits, a direction whose premise is that refusals at the clinical level, driven by caution, unfamiliarity or moral objection, were generating petitions the law never required. The Bombay High Court later ordered wide publicity of the judgment within Maharashtra for exactly that reason, directing that its substance reach the practitioners and officials whose refusals had been sending women to court. The 2022 judgment thus operates on two planes: as a construction of a rule, and as an instruction to the system that the amended Act, properly read, already answers more cases than the system's gatekeepers had been allowing.

## Advanced gestation after the amendment, and the recall of 2023

The Board route did not end the courts' involvement in late cases, for three structural reasons. First, the Board's province is confined to substantial foetal abnormality, so every late case resting on the woman's circumstances, her health short of immediate mortal danger, or an assault discovered after twenty four weeks still has no statutory destination other than the writ. Secondly, a Board's refusal is not insulated from review, and a woman refused by a Board may petition, whereupon the court typically obtains its own medical board's report and decides substantially on that material. Thirdly, the pre-amendment culture of the writ petition, in which lawyers and judges alike treated the court as the natural forum for hard cases, persisted by habit on all sides. The result is a continuing jurisdiction that is fact-sensitive in the strict sense: outcomes turn on the diagnosis, the gestational stage at the moment of decision, the assessed viability of the foetus, and the presence or absence of danger to the woman, rather than on any rule broader than those factors. Courts have permitted terminations well beyond twenty four weeks where boards reported abnormalities incompatible with life or involving severe suffering, and have declined permission where the foetus was reported healthy and viable and the woman's case fell outside the statutory grounds, and the two lines of outcome are not in tension. They are the same test applied to different medical facts.

The episode that defines the jurisdiction's limits is the Supreme Court's recall proceeding of October 2023, which must be described as what it procedurally was: not an appeal and not a review in the ordinary course, but an application by the Union of India to recall an order the Court itself had made days earlier. The petitioner was a married woman of twenty seven with two children, who had discovered her pregnancy late, at about twenty weeks, because of lactational amenorrhoea, and who sought termination at about twenty six weeks on the ground, among others, of postpartum psychosis following her second delivery and her assessed inability to raise another child. A bench of Justices Hima Kohli and B. V. Nagarathna directed her examination by a medical board at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences and, by order dated 9 October 2023, permitted the termination, recognising her decisional autonomy over her body and her physical, psychological and socioeconomic position. The Union then applied for recall of that order, relying on the medical board's report that the foetus was viable and had a favourable chance of survival, and on the difficulty the AIIMS doctors recorded, that a termination at that stage would require the foetal heartbeat to be stopped. The two judges of the original bench divided on the application: Justice Kohli held that her judicial conscience did not permit the termination to proceed in the light of the viability report, while Justice Nagarathna found no reason to interfere with the order the bench had made. The matter was accordingly placed before a larger bench.

On 16 October 2023 a bench headed by the Chief Justice, Dr D. Y. Chandrachud, sitting with Justices J. B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra, declined to permit the termination. The reasoning was statutory and narrow. The pregnancy had reached twenty six weeks and five days, beyond the twenty four week ceiling. Neither of the two routes beyond that ceiling was available: there was no diagnosis of substantial foetal abnormality to engage Section 3(2B), the AIIMS board having reported a healthy foetus, and there was no situation of immediate necessity to save the woman's life to engage Section 5. The Court observed that to direct the termination would be to direct the stopping of the heartbeat of a viable foetus, which it was not prepared to do on the material before it, and it directed instead that the delivery take place at AIIMS at the appropriate time with the State bearing the medical and incidental expenses, leaving the woman the choice thereafter of keeping the child or offering the child for adoption. The challenge to the constitutional validity of the amended provisions, which had been raised in the proceedings, was expressly left to be dealt with in separate proceedings, and it remains a pending question rather than a decided one.

The proceeding is also the leading exhibit for a shift in the state's litigation posture that commentators across positions have noticed. In the writ era the Union and the States appeared before the courts most often as respondents directed to constitute boards or to permit what a board advised, and their stance was facilitative or neutral in the run of cases. In October 2023 the Union appeared as an applicant, intervening of its own motion to undo a permission already granted to an identified woman whose procedure was imminent, on the strength of a medical viability assessment. Whatever view is taken of the merits, the institutional fact is durable: the government is a practised and willing litigant on the restrictive side of late termination cases, practitioners and petitioners must expect the state's position to be argued even after an initial order, and the interval between a permission and its execution has acquired a litigation risk of its own. Boards and hospitals that understand this will treat the execution of permissions as time-critical in a way the pre-2021 practice rarely required.

The recall repays careful reading because it is so easy to misread in both directions. It is not a decision that the 2022 judgment was wrong: the benches, the questions and the gestational stages were all different, and the 2023 bench worked within the amended statute rather than against its grain. Nor is it a decision that viability operates as a freestanding legal barrier written into the Act, because the Act contains no viability test: the outcome follows from the absence of either statutory route beyond twenty four weeks on the facts found, and the viability of the foetus entered the reasoning as part of the medical material on which the Court declined to exercise an extraordinary discretion. What the episode establishes, at the level of practice, is the shape of the boundary the amendment built. Inside twenty four weeks, and inside the categories, the statute speaks and practitioners decide. Beyond twenty four weeks, the statute speaks only to abnormality and to mortal danger, and everything else is an appeal to a court's discretion, which the court will exercise on medical evidence and which, as October 2023 showed, the state may contest even after an initial order in the woman's favour. A mechanism enacted to take hard cases out of court has, in the hardest class of case, left them there, with the additional feature that the government of the day is a practised litigant on the other side.

## Did the amendment empty the courts? The docket after 2021

The amendment's stated purpose was to retire the petition docket, and an amendment trail should test the purpose against the outcome with whatever evidence exists, stated at the confidence the evidence supports. The figures in this field are not published as an official series, so the honest account works from studies of the case law and from the reported decisions themselves. The Pratigya Campaign's analysis of High Court judgments in the years immediately before the amendment found that foetal abnormality had become the predominant ground on which women reached the courts, which reflected the twenty week ceiling cutting across the diagnostic calendar. A later study of one thousand one hundred and fourteen termination cases decided by the Supreme Court and the High Courts across the period from 2019 to 2024 found that permission was granted in about eighty five per cent of the cases, and that in nine hundred and forty nine of them the court had the opinion of a medical board before it and ordinarily worked from that opinion. The pattern those findings describe is consistent with what the reported decisions show: the volume of petitions did not collapse after October 2021, but its centre of gravity shifted. Cases that the categories and the standing Boards could answer, the minor discovered at twenty two weeks, the widow, the survivor within her window, increasingly end inside the statute. Cases that remain in court are concentrated where they were always going to remain: beyond twenty four weeks without a Board diagnosis of abnormality, Board refusals under challenge, and institutional failures, refusals by practitioners and missing Boards, that push statutory cases into writs.

Two conclusions follow for the trail. The first is that the amendment changed the question the courts are asked. Before 2021 the standard petition asked the court to supply a permission the statute lacked. After 2021 the standard petition asks the court to correct a failure in the statutory machinery, a refusal, a delay, an absent Board, a category read too narrowly, which is a supervisory question rather than a legislative one, and courts answer it with directions to the machinery as often as with permissions to the woman. The second conclusion is less comfortable for the design's defenders. The medical board, which the amendment adopted from the courts, has become in the courts' own practice the near-decisive voice even in cases the statute reserves to courts, because a judge asked to override a specialist board's report on substantial abnormality or viability will rarely do it. The board system therefore governs late termination in India twice over, once by statute and once by judicial deference, and the quality, composition and speed of boards is the single variable on which the practical law most depends. A reform that began as an escape from case-by-case medical adjudication has settled into a system of standing medical adjudication with a judicial backstop, and whether that is the amendment's success or its quiet defeat depends on the moral premises the next section states.

## The amendment and the States: one Act, many Board systems

Health sits in the State List's neighbourhood and hospitals are run by States, so a central Act that works through state machinery inherits the federation's unevenness, and the 2021 amendment is unusually exposed to it because its third gate is a state institution. The categories in Rule 3B are central rules and uniform across India. The approved places where terminations occur are approved through the district level machinery the 2002 amendment created. But the Medical Board is constituted by each State Government and each Union territory administration for itself, its additional members are those the State notifies, its seat is where the State places it, and its working resources, the specialists it can summon, the investigations it can order at speed, the distance a woman must travel to reach it, are functions of the State's health system. Two women with identical diagnoses, one in a State whose Board sits weekly at a metropolitan teaching hospital and one in a State whose Board is a paper notification serviced by a district hospital without a resident radiologist, hold the same statutory entitlement and face different laws in every sense that matters to them.

The amendment contains no equalising device: no model composition beyond the statutory trio, no central reporting of Board decisions, no appellate Board, no funding line. The Union's role after commencement has been exhortatory and evidentiary, answering parliamentary questions and filing affidavits in litigation, rather than supervisory. Defenders of the arrangement regard it as the ordinary federal distribution of health administration, in which the Centre legislates the entitlement and the States run the hospitals, and point out that the first two gates, which carry nearly all cases, depend on practitioners rather than on state machinery. Critics answer that the third gate is precisely where the cases are hardest and the women least able to travel, litigate or wait, so that locating the reform's most fragile institution at the level of government least able to standardise it was a design decision with foreseeable consequences. The federalism analysis of the wider health cluster belongs to the cluster's own articles; for this trail the point is narrower. The amendment's text is national, its Boards are provincial, and every evaluation of how the amendment has worked that does not specify which State's Board it is describing is an evaluation of an abstraction.

## The disagreement stated fairly, and the criticisms both sides can share

The moral disagreement underneath this body of law must be stated in its participants' own terms before any assessment is credible, and the discipline of this article, as in the pillar article on the parent Act, is to state each position at full strength and adopt neither. One position holds that a pregnancy is a human life in formation whose moral claim strengthens as it develops, that the state's duty to protect the vulnerable extends to the unborn, and that a legal framework for termination is justified, if at all, as a bounded exception for grave cases, policed by medical judgment precisely because the interests at stake exceed the preferences of any single decision-maker. From this position, gestational limits are not bureaucratic cruelty but the legal form of a moral gradient, and the question in any amendment is whether the boundary has been placed where the gradient justifies it. The opposing position holds that the decision whether to continue a pregnancy is an exercise of the pregnant woman's bodily autonomy and personal liberty, that compelling a woman to carry a pregnancy to term is a state imposition on her body of a kind imposed in no other medical context, and that medical gatekeeping converts a right into a permission. From this position, gestational limits administered by third parties are objectionable in principle, and the acceptable framework is one in which the woman's decision governs, with clinical involvement addressed to safety rather than authorisation. Between and around these poles sit the positions most legislators actually hold, which accept termination as lawful within limits and argue about where the limits and the decision-makers should be. The 2021 amendment is a product of that middle ground, which is why it satisfies neither pole and why both poles can describe it, accurately from their premises, as a failure.

A further objection was pressed during the parliamentary passage and belongs to the record, though it is directed at a risk rather than at the amendment's text. Members in both Houses asked whether a higher gestational limit would be misused for sex-selective termination, given that the anatomy scan that detects abnormality also discloses sex, and that the twenty to twenty four week window opens after that scan. The government's answer, and the position the framework maintains, is that sex selection is policed by its own statute, the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, which criminalises the disclosure and the use of sex determination and which the amendment did not touch, so that the two regimes run in parallel: the MTP framework asks whether a ground for termination exists, and the diagnostic techniques framework polices how the information feeding that decision was obtained. Whether parallel policing is adequate in practice is a question about the enforcement of the other statute, and the pillar article on the parent Act takes up the interaction in full. The objection is recorded here because it shaped the politics of the Bill's passage and because it explains part of the legislature's preference for categories: a list of eligible women was easier to defend against the misuse charge than a general extension would have been.

Superimposed on the moral disagreement are criticisms of the amendment that do not depend on resolving it, and these the article can evaluate on the record. The first is the boundary problem examined above: a categorical list allocates a medical entitlement by situational type, and its edges, marital status above all, have already required judicial repair. The second is the delay problem: a board mechanism whose statutory clock is three days operates inside health systems where its own members are scarce, and in this field delay is not neutral. The third is the provider problem: the amendment enlarged the lawful field of the existing provider class without enlarging the class, leaving the rural supply of lawful services where it found it. The fourth is the coverage problem: the Act speaks of women throughout, and pregnant transgender and gender-diverse persons stand outside its text, an exclusion raised in Parliament and in commentary and not addressed by the amendment. The fifth is an asymmetry inside the abnormality provisions themselves, which reserve the only uncapped route to cases of foetal diagnosis, so that a survivor of assault whose pregnancy is discovered after twenty four weeks has no statutory route at all, a gap that the critics of the list regard as its least defensible edge and that no amount of rule amendment within the present Act can close, because the confinement of Section 3(2B) to abnormality is in the Act and not in the rules.

The disability question requires its own statement, conducted with the care the brief for this article requires, because it concerns a ground that predates the amendment, which the amendment enlarged into the only uncapped route in the Act. The defence of the abnormality ground, as its proponents state it, is that it spares a woman and her family the continuation of a pregnancy in which the diagnosis establishes that the child, if born, would suffer gravely or could not survive, that the judgment involved is a judgment about suffering and about the family's capacity to sustain a life of total dependence in a country where public support for such care is thin, and that the decision is in substance the parents' own, with the law merely refusing to force the alternative on them. The criticism, as disability rights advocates state it in their published critiques of the amendment, is that a statute which singles out the predicted disability of the future child as the one ground on which the ordinary limits fall away embeds a public judgment that lives with disability are of lesser worth, that the language of serious handicap echoes the eugenic reasoning with which such provisions entered the law in the last century, that viability and suffering are being used to do work that a social judgment about disability is actually doing, and that the state's energy would be better spent on the support whose absence is then cited as the reason the ground is needed. An analysis by the Pratigya Campaign of High Court judgments found that foetal abnormality had become the predominant ground on which women were reaching the courts at all, which both sides of this argument can read consistently with their position: the defence, as proof that the ground answers the commonest hard case; the criticism, as proof that the law channels its compassion through a diagnosis. The article records the disagreement without resolving it, because the disagreement is about what a diagnosis means, and that is a question of value on which the statutory text is silent and on which people directly affected hold opposed views in complete sincerity.

## What the trail predicts

An amendment trail is read for what it predicts, and this one points in two directions at once. The textual direction is toward the rules. The Act has twice been outpaced by its own subordinate legislation in practical importance: the categories that decide the second gate live in Rule 3B, the Board's clock lives in Rule 3A, the practitioners' norms live in rules under Section 3(2A), and the 2022 judgment was a judgment about a rule's construction. Any future widening or narrowing of access that the executive can achieve within the present Act will be attempted there first, and observers of this field should read the gazette notifications under the MTP Rules with the attention usually reserved for bills. For the reader who wants to follow this statute's movement rather than merely know its position, three indicators are worth watching, because between them they will show movement before any formal reform is announced. The first is the gazette itself: amendments to Rules 3A and 3B, additions to the category list, and notifications constituting or reconstituting State Boards are the instruments through which this framework actually changes, and they pass with little of the attention that a bill commands. The second is the composition and output of the Boards in the larger States, because the courts' continuing willingness to supervise the third gate will rise or fall with the Boards' visible competence, and a run of well-reasoned Form D opinions is the surest protection the Board system can earn itself. The third is the fate of the constitutional challenge reserved in the recall proceedings, which is the one pending vehicle capable of reopening the questions the amendment answered by category: if it is heard and decided on the equality and autonomy grounds the 2022 judgment mapped, the list itself may be re-examined as a whole rather than at its edges. None of the three requires prophecy to watch, and between them they cover the executive, administrative and judicial channels through which every change in this field since 1971 has in fact arrived.

There is a fourth indicator, quieter than the other three, in the behaviour of the gatekeepers themselves. The amended Act works only if practitioners form opinions at the first two gates without demanding documents, consents or categories the law does not require, and the Supreme Court's 2022 observations on refusals were directed at exactly this layer of practice. Hospital protocols, medical college circulars and professional guidance that bring institutional practice into line with the judgment are the unglamorous instruments by which the amendment's paper entitlements become clinical ones, and their spread, State by State and institution by institution, will determine the experience of the large majority of women who never meet a Board and never see a courtroom. An amendment trail that ends at the gazette misses this layer; the trail of the 2021 Act runs through it, and the distance between the Act as construed in New Delhi and the Act as administered at a district hospital remains the truest measure of how much of the reform has actually happened.

The structural direction is toward the points the amendment could not reach by rule: the confinement of the uncapped route to abnormality, the absence of any statutory route for the late-discovered assault case, the provider base, and the position of pregnant persons the Act's language does not reach are all embedded in the parent Act, and each will require Parliament if it is to move. The pending constitutional challenge noted in the recall proceedings may yet decide some of these questions from the bench, and until it is decided the honest statement of the position is the one this article has maintained throughout: the Act of 2021 settled the numbers, twenty and twenty four, and a Board for abnormality beyond them, and left the allocation of those numbers among women to a list, and the list is where the argument lives. This article is legal information about that statute and its amendment, not advice on an individual case, and any person whose position turns on days or on a category should take advice on the facts of that case, because in this field the procedural clocks are short and unforgiving.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the 2021 amendment change about gestational limits?

Before the amendment, the Act worked on a twelve and twenty week structure: one registered medical practitioner could terminate a pregnancy up to twelve weeks, and two were required between twelve and twenty weeks, beyond which the Act supplied no route except the life-saving provision in Section 5. The Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Act, 2021 substituted a new structure in Section 3(2). One practitioner's good faith opinion on a statutory ground suffices up to twenty weeks for any woman. Between twenty and twenty four weeks, two practitioners must hold the opinion, and the woman must fall within a category prescribed by the rules. Beyond twenty four weeks there is no general limit at all where a Medical Board diagnoses substantial foetal abnormality, because Section 3(2B) disapplies the length provisions in that class of case.

### Q: Which categories of women can seek termination up to twenty four weeks?

The categories are prescribed in Rule 3B of the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Rules, 2003, inserted by the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Rules, 2021. They are survivors of sexual assault or rape or incest; minors; women whose marital status changes during the ongoing pregnancy, through widowhood or divorce; women with physical disabilities, measured by the major disability criteria of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016; mentally ill women including mental retardation; cases of foetal malformation carrying a substantial risk of incompatibility with life or of the child being seriously handicapped; and women pregnant in humanitarian settings or in disaster or emergency situations declared by the Government. Falling within a category is necessary but not sufficient: two registered medical practitioners must also form the opinion, in good faith, that a statutory ground in Section 3(2) is made out.

### Q: What is the Medical Board and when must it be approached?

The Medical Board is a body that every State Government and Union territory administration must constitute by gazette notification under Section 3(2C) of the Act. It consists of a Gynaecologist, a Paediatrician, a Radiologist or Sonologist, and such other members as the government notifies, and it may co-opt further specialists. It must be approached where a termination is sought beyond twenty four weeks on the ground of substantial foetal abnormality, because Section 3(2B) removes the gestational ceiling only where the Board has made that diagnosis. Under Rule 3A of the rules, the Board examines the woman and her reports, gives its opinion in Form D within three days of receiving the request, and, where it advises termination, ensures the procedure is carried out with safety precautions and counselling within five days of the request. The Board has no role in terminations within twenty four weeks.

### Q: Did the 2021 amendment extend termination access to unmarried women?

Within the first twenty weeks, unmarried women stood in the same position as married women both before and after the amendment, because the Act's grounds never depended on marriage except in one respect. That respect was the contraceptive failure presumption, which the 1971 text confined to a married woman and her husband. The 2021 amendment rewrote the presumption to cover any woman and her partner, so the statutory text itself ceased to condition that ground on marriage. For the twenty to twenty four week band, the position turned on Rule 3B, whose marital status category was drafted around widowhood and divorce. The Supreme Court's judgment of September 2022 read that category so as not to exclude unmarried women whose circumstances change during pregnancy, holding the married and unmarried distinction artificial. The practical answer is therefore that unmarried women have access on the same terms, through the amended text at the first gate and through the Court's construction at the second.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide about marital status and termination?

In X against the Principal Secretary, Health and Family Welfare Department, Government of NCT of Delhi, decided on 29 September 2022, a three-judge bench held that the distinction drawn between married and unmarried women in the operation of Rule 3B could not be sustained. The Court read the category of change of marital status during pregnancy purposively, so that an unmarried woman whose relationship ends or whose partner abandons her during the pregnancy is not excluded from the twenty to twenty four week band, and it held that excluding her would offend the equality guarantee of Article 14 and the reproductive autonomy protected by Article 21. The Court also held that, for the purposes of the MTP Act alone, "rape" includes a married woman's allegation of forced intercourse by her husband, while expressly leaving the marital exception in the criminal law untouched.

### Q: Why do Medical Board referrals cause delay?

The design assumes a standing Board of named specialists deciding within the rule's clock of three days for the opinion and five days for the procedure. Practice departs from the design at three points. Constitution: some States and Union territories were slow to notify fully composed Boards, and courts in individual cases have had to direct hospitals to assemble one. Composition: the Board requires a gynaecologist, a paediatrician and a radiologist or sonologist together, specialists who are scarce outside large teaching hospitals and whose absence prevents a sitting. Convening: the Board may require additional investigations and co-opted specialists, each of which takes time the formal clock does not count. Because gestation advances while the process runs, delay in this field does not preserve the position. It narrows the clinical options and can extinguish the entitlement, which is why delay is the most persistent operational criticism of the 2021 design.

### Q: Did the 2021 amendment create a right to abortion?

No, in the legal sense the word carries. The amendment enlarged a permission structure; it did not convert termination into an entitlement the woman can demand. The Act continues to work through the good faith opinion of registered medical practitioners on statutory grounds, and no provision obliges an unwilling practitioner to perform or approve a termination. What the amendment changed was the reach of that structure: a higher ceiling for prescribed categories, an uncapped route for Board-diagnosed abnormality, a contraceptive failure presumption no longer tied to marriage, and a confidentiality offence. The Supreme Court's 2022 judgment described reproductive choice in the language of constitutional rights under Articles 14 and 21, and that language governs how the statute is construed, but the operative law remains a conditional statutory permission administered by doctors and, at the third gate, by a Board, rather than a right exercisable on request.

### Q: What do critics say the termination amendment left unresolved?

The criticisms that recur across otherwise opposed camps are these. The categorical design allocates the extended limit by situational type, so women in identical medical positions stand differently, and the list's edges required judicial repair within two years. The Board mechanism has no statutory fallback where a State fails to constitute or staff it, so the delay the amendment was meant to end reappears inside the mechanism. The provider base was left unchanged, so the rural shortage of practitioners qualified to act persists. The uncapped route is confined to foetal abnormality, leaving a survivor of assault whose pregnancy is discovered after twenty four weeks with no statutory destination except a writ petition. The Act's language reaches women only, leaving pregnant transgender persons outside its text. And the disability critique, that the abnormality ground embeds a judgment about which lives are worth living, is one the amendment enlarged rather than answered.

### Q: When did the MTP Amendment Act 2021 come into force, and when were the amended Rules notified?

The amending Act is Act 8 of 2021. Parliament passed the Bill in two stages separated by the pandemic sessions: the Lok Sabha on 17 March 2020 and the Rajya Sabha on 16 March 2021. The President's assent followed on 25 March 2021, the date from which the Act is dated, but assent did not itself commence the Act, whose Section 1 left commencement to notification. The Central Government brought the Act into force on 24 September 2021. The subordinate legislation that makes the amendment workable followed on 12 October 2021, when the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Rules, 2021 were notified, inserting Rule 3A on the Medical Board's powers and functions and Rule 3B on the categories eligible up to twenty four weeks into the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Rules, 2003. The sequence matters practically, because between commencement and the rules there was a short interval in which the Act referred to categories that had not yet been prescribed.

### Q: How did the twelve and twenty week structure of the original Act work?

From the commencement of the principal Act on 1 April 1972 until September 2021, the structure stood as the 1971 legislature had built it. Up to twelve weeks, the opinion of one registered medical practitioner, formed in good faith on a statutory ground, sufficed. Between twelve and twenty weeks, the concurring opinions of two registered medical practitioners were required on the same grounds. Beyond twenty weeks the Act provided no route by which a pregnancy could be terminated on the ordinary grounds, and the only statutory exception was Section 5, which lifts the limits where termination is immediately necessary to save the woman's life. It was the absence of any route beyond twenty weeks that generated the writ jurisdiction of the constitutional courts, in which judges, advised by medical boards constituted for the individual case, permitted or refused late terminations as an exercise of constitutional discretion rather than under any provision of the Act.

### Q: Did the 2021 amendment widen the class of practitioners who can perform a termination?

No. The amendment did not widen the class of persons who may lawfully perform a termination, which remains confined to registered medical practitioners with the prescribed experience and training in gynaecology and obstetrics. This was a deliberate omission rather than an oversight. The draft amendment bill circulated by the Health Ministry in 2014 had proposed extending the provider base to categories of mid-level practitioners for early terminations, a proposal supported by public health researchers who pointed to the scarcity of doctors in rural districts and opposed by medical associations. The 2020 Bill dropped the proposal, and the enacted amendment instead worked on the norms side: a new Section 3(2A) leaves the qualifications required of the opinion-forming practitioner at each gestational age to the rules, and the amended rules prescribe the training and the supervised experience pathway for termination by medical methods at the earliest stage, up to nine weeks. The provider boundary the Shah Committee's Act drew therefore survived the amendment intact.

### Q: How many doctors must approve a termination at eighteen weeks after the amendment?

One. Eighteen weeks falls in the first band of the substituted Section 3(2), which runs to twenty weeks and requires the opinion of a single registered medical practitioner, formed in good faith, that continuance of the pregnancy would involve a risk to the woman's life or of grave injury to her physical or mental health, or that there is a substantial risk of the child being born with a serious physical or mental abnormality. Under the pre-amendment structure the same case required two opinions, because the old twelve week line doubled the requirement for the whole of the second trimester. The removal of that internal line is one of the amendment's least reported and most used changes, since it halves the authorisation burden for the large class of terminations sought between twelve and twenty weeks. The practitioner's opinion must still be certified in the prescribed form, the woman's consent obtained, and the procedure carried out at a government hospital or an approved place.

### Q: Can a survivor of rape terminate a pregnancy after twenty-four weeks under the amended Act?

Not through the ordinary statutory machinery. Rule 3B carries a survivor of sexual assault, rape or incest up to twenty four weeks, with two practitioners' opinions, and Explanation 2 to Section 3(2) presumes that the anguish of a pregnancy caused by rape is a grave injury to her mental health, so the ground is not in issue. Beyond twenty four weeks, however, the only route the Act itself opens is Section 3(2B), which is confined to substantial foetal abnormality diagnosed by a Medical Board, and a pregnancy caused by assault does not enter that route unless an abnormality is also diagnosed. Section 5 remains for the case where termination is immediately necessary to save her life. In every other case the survivor's remedy is the one that existed before the amendment: a writ petition to a High Court or the Supreme Court, which decides on medical evidence. Critics across the spectrum identify this as the categorical scheme's least defensible edge.

### Q: What is the punishment under Section 5A for revealing the identity of a woman whose pregnancy was terminated?

Section 5A, inserted by the 2021 amendment under the heading "Protection of privacy of a woman", provides in sub-section (1) that no registered medical practitioner shall reveal the name and other particulars of a woman whose pregnancy has been terminated under the Act, except to a person authorised by any law for the time being in force. Sub-section (2) makes contravention punishable with imprisonment which may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both. The provision converted confidentiality from a matter of record-keeping discipline under the regulations, where the admission register is treated as a secret document, into a statutory duty owed by the practitioner and backed by a criminal penalty. Two qualifications are part of the text rather than commentary on it: the exception for persons authorised by law admits disclosures that other statutes require, including reporting duties that arise in cases involving minors, and although the prohibition is addressed to the registered medical practitioner, the punishment clause speaks of "whoever" contravenes it.

### Q: Can a minor use the twenty to twenty-four week window without approaching a court?

Yes, provided the other conditions are met. A minor is herself one of the Rule 3B categories, so a pregnancy between twenty and twenty four weeks may be terminated on the opinions of two registered medical practitioners formed on a statutory ground, without any application to a court. The feature of her case that differs from an adult's is consent. Section 3(4)(a) of the Act, which the amendment did not alter, provides that the pregnancy of a woman who has not attained majority shall not be terminated except with the consent in writing of her guardian. In practice the cases that reach courts involving minors usually arrive for a different reason: the pregnancy is discovered after twenty four weeks, which takes it outside the window altogether and into the Board route, available only for abnormality, or into the writ jurisdiction. Practitioners also have to reckon with the reporting obligations that the criminal law attaches to sexual activity involving a minor, which operate alongside the Act and are not displaced by it.

### Q: Who constitutes a Medical Board, and can it bring in specialists beyond its named members?

The duty to constitute the Board lies on each State Government and Union territory administration, which must do so by notification in the Official Gazette under Section 3(2C). The composition is fixed by Section 3(2D) of the Act itself rather than left to the rules: a Gynaecologist, a Paediatrician, a Radiologist or Sonologist, and such other number of members as the State Government or Union territory notifies. Beyond its constituted membership, Rule 3A of the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Rules expressly empowers the Board to co-opt other specialists and to call for additional investigations where required for deciding on the termination. Co-option matters clinically, because the abnormalities that reach the Board are frequently cardiac, neurological or genetic, and the named trio may need a foetal medicine specialist, a cardiologist or a geneticist before a responsible opinion can be formed. It matters legally as well, because time spent awaiting a co-opted report is the commonest reason the rule's three day opinion clock is not met in practice.

### Q: What is the difference between the Medical Board route and the court route after twenty-four weeks?

The Board route is statutory, standing and diagnosis-bound. A Medical Board constituted under Section 3(2C) decides, on the medical record and its own examination, whether the case involves a substantial foetal abnormality of the kind Section 3(2B) describes, and if it does, the gestational ceiling falls away and the termination proceeds within the rules' five day discipline without any court being involved. The Board cannot authorise a termination on any other ground, however strong the circumstances. The court route is constitutional, episodic and discretion-bound. A woman petitions a High Court under Article 226 or the Supreme Court under Article 32, the court obtains a medical board report of its own, and it decides as a matter of constitutional discretion on the whole of the material, which allows courts to consider grave injury, assault and changed circumstances that the statutory Board must ignore. Its costs are delay, publicity and uncertainty of outcome, and its decisions bind as precedent only in the loosest sense, because each turns on its medical facts.

### Q: Why did Parliament extend the limit by prescribed categories instead of for all women?

The Statement of Objects and Reasons framed the extension as being especially for vulnerable women, and the enacted design follows that framing: relief was directed to the fact patterns that had filled the courts, late-discovered pregnancies after assault, minors, women whose circumstances changed through widowhood or divorce, women with disabilities, and late-diagnosed foetal abnormality. A categorical extension also avoided two decisions a universal extension would have forced. It avoided defending twenty four weeks as the line for cases involving no element of hardship, and it avoided confronting in general terms the viability question that a universal line raises at its upper end. There was a legislative-technique reason as well, stated by the design's defenders during passage: placing the categories in rules rather than in the Act allows the list to be corrected by notification as experience accumulates, without fresh primary legislation. The cost of the design is the boundary litigation it predictably generated, of which the marital status judgment of 2022 is the leading instance.

### Q: What did the 2014 draft amendment bill propose, and why was it never enacted?

The draft bill circulated by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare in 2014, after stakeholder consultation, proposed the core of what was eventually enacted in 2021, including raising the gestational limit to twenty four weeks for categories of women and extending the contraceptive failure presumption beyond married women. Its distinctive proposal, which the later Bill abandoned, was to widen the provider base: categories of mid-level practitioners, including practitioners of the recognised Indian systems of medicine and trained mid-level providers, were to be permitted to perform early terminations, on the reasoning that the shortage of allopathic doctors in rural India was itself a barrier to lawful care. That proposal drew the sharpest opposition, from medical associations that treated the provider boundary as a safety line, and the draft was never introduced in Parliament, lapsing with the consultation politics around it. The 2020 Bill revived the gestational and presumption reforms while dropping provider widening entirely, which is why the enacted amendment enlarges what the existing class of practitioners may do without enlarging the class.

### Q: What happens if a State has no functioning Medical Board when a case beyond twenty-four weeks arises?

The Act gives the woman no alternative statutory forum, because Section 3(2B) makes the Board's diagnosis the key that lifts the ceiling, and a State's failure to constitute or staff its Board does not transfer the function to anyone else. What happens in practice is the reappearance of the pre-amendment pattern in a new form. The woman, or the hospital treating her, approaches the High Court, and the court directs the constitution of a board for her case, ordinarily at a government medical college hospital, obtains its report and decides. Courts have recorded their displeasure at being used as a constituting authority for a mechanism the statute makes standing, and at doctors declining to act within their own powers and leaving women to litigate. The deeper defect is structural: the amending Act imposed the duty to constitute Boards on States and Union territories without a default mechanism, a reporting obligation or a consequence for default, so the third gate's reliability varies with state administrative energy in a way the first two gates, which depend only on practitioners, do not.
