The farmer protests that began against three Union farm laws in the autumn of 2020 ended, fourteen months after the laws received Presidential assent, with the laws themselves repealed by the Parliament that had passed them. Between the ordinances of 5 June 2020 and the Farm Laws Repeal Act of 2021 stands a sequence that this closing article reads as citizen-renegotiation: a federation of farm unions converted three statutes into a year-long negotiation conducted at the borders of the capital, inside a courtroom, across eleven rounds of ministerial talks, and finally on the floor of the two Houses. The reading is the last application of the movements framework that organises this pillar, and it closes the series by testing that framework against the largest sustained mobilisation of the period the corpus covers.

## The Ordinances of June 2020 and the Passage of September 2020

The three farm laws began as ordinances promulgated on 5 June 2020, during the nationwide lockdown imposed on 25 March 2020 against the COVID-19 pandemic, a governing condition examined in the Shaheen Bagh article of this pillar for the protest politics that immediately preceded the farm agitation. The ordinances proposed to redraw the legal geography of agricultural trade: where a farmer could sell, on what terms a buyer could contract for a harvest before it was sown, and which foodstuffs the state could subject to stock limits and movement controls in ordinary times. The Union Cabinet presented the package as the completion of a reform agenda that agriculture ministers had discussed since the model marketing Acts circulated to states in 2003 and 2017, and the ordinance route placed the texts before Parliament at its monsoon session in September 2020.

The Lok Sabha passed the three Bills on 17 September 2020. Harsimrat Kaur Badal of the Shiromani Akali Dal resigned from the Union Cabinet that day, and her party, the Bharatiya Janata Party's oldest ally, withdrew from the National Democratic Alliance on 26 September 2020. The Rajya Sabha passed the Bills on 20 September 2020 by voice vote. Opposition members had demanded a division, a recorded vote, and the demand was not granted; members trooped into the well of the House, the Deputy Chairman Harivansh presided over the passage amid the disorder, and eight opposition members were suspended for the remainder of the session on 21 September 2020. Opposition parties then boycotted the rest of the session. President Ram Nath Kovind gave assent on 27 September 2020, and the three ordinances became three Acts of Parliament on that date.

The manner of passage entered the protests' own record as evidence. Union statements, pamphlets and speeches returned repeatedly to the voice vote of 20 September, treating the absent division as a procedural grievance that stood beside the substantive objection to the texts. The government's stated case, given by Agriculture Minister Narendra Singh Tomar on the floor and in interviews through the autumn, held that the Bills had followed the practice of the House, that the demand for a division had come after the moment for it had passed, and that the laws answered demands farmers' organisations had themselves raised for decades. The opposition's stated case held that a division was demanded in time and refused. The record carries both claims, and the dispute over the division remained unresolved by any authoritative ruling through the protest year. What the record settles is the sequence: ordinances on 5 June, Lok Sabha passage on 17 September, Rajya Sabha passage by voice vote on 20 September, assent on 27 September 2020.

In Punjab the agitation preceded the Delhi march by months. Farm unions in Punjab began local protests after the ordinances, escalated them after the September passage, and sustained a rail roko that halted freight and passenger trains across the state from late September into November 2020. A Bharat Bandh called for 25 September 2020 by the All India Kisan Sangharsh Coordination Committee drew participation across Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh and scattered action elsewhere. The Punjab Vidhan Sabha answered the Union laws on 20 October 2020 with state amendment Bills designed to nullify their effect within Punjab, a federal counter-move whose assent the Governor reserved for the President. Haryana unions held parallel mobilisations, and the decision to carry the protest to the capital was taken by the Punjab unions in November, then widened into a joint call. The 2020 to 2021 farmer protests therefore arrived at Delhi's borders as an already organised movement with a settled demand, repeal of the three Acts, rather than as a spontaneous gathering that formed its programme at the barricades.

## The Three Farm Laws by Their Texts

The Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act 2020 created a legal space called trade area, defined as any place outside the yards and sub-yards of a market committee constituted under a state Agricultural Produce Market Committee Act, and freed trade within that space from state market fee, cess and levy. A farmer could sell to any buyer holding a permanent account number, electronic trading platforms could operate across state lines, and disputes went to the Sub-Divisional Magistrate for conciliation rather than to a civil court, whose jurisdiction the Act barred. The Act's stated purpose, in its preamble and in the government's exposition, was one nation one market: a barrier-free trade in farm produce that would let a grower in Punjab sell to a processor in another state without paying the mandi charges of either.

The Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act 2020 built the contract-farming frame. A farming agreement between a farmer and a sponsor could run from one crop season to five years, had to state a price or a pricing formula with a guaranteed amount and a bonus or premium above it, and barred the sponsor from acquiring ownership of the farmer's land or erecting permanent structures on it. Agreements were exempt from state contract-farming regulation and from stock limits under the Essential Commodities Act. Disputes again went to conciliation boards and the Sub-Divisional Magistrate, with appeal to the Collector, and the Act barred recovery of dues from the farmer's land. The government's stated case held that the written price assurance protected the grower against the price risk that open-market sale carried. The unions' stated case read the same provisions differently: a conciliation ladder ending in a revenue officer, they argued, placed an individual grower against a corporate sponsor with the state as arbiter, and the bar on civil courts removed the forum in which a small party could hope to match a large one.

The Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act 2020 removed cereals, pulses, oilseeds, edible oils, onion and potatoes from the list of commodities that the Union government could regulate as essential in ordinary conditions. Stock limits on these items could be imposed only in extraordinary circumstances, defined as war, famine, extraordinary price rise or natural calamity of grave nature, with price-rise triggers fixed at one hundred percent for horticultural produce and fifty percent for non-perishables over a reference price. The government's stated rationale was that predictable deregulation would draw private investment into storage, cold chains and processing, whose absence the Shanta Kumar committee of 2015 and successive Economic Surveys had recorded as a cause of post-harvest loss. The unions' stated reading held that removing stock limits outside emergencies licensed hoarding by large buyers and weakened the public distribution and procurement system that rested on state purchase at announced prices.

Underneath all three texts lay the institution none of them named. The minimum support price, announced for twenty-two mandated crops on the recommendations of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, had no statutory basis; it operated as an administrative commitment, effective where procurement machinery existed, principally for paddy and wheat in Punjab, Haryana and parts of western Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh. The three Acts neither abolished the support price nor the mandis, and the government repeated that both would continue. The unions' core inference, stated in their charters from September 2020 onward, was that trade drained out of the fee-bearing mandi into the free trade area would hollow the mandi system and, with it, the procurement on which the support price depended in practice. The protests' second demand, a legal guarantee of the support price for all crops, grew directly out of that inference. Sukhpal Singh, the agricultural economist at Punjab Agricultural University whose essays on the Punjab farm economy were cited across the protest literature, documented the depth of Punjab's dependence on the paddy and wheat procurement cycle, and that documented dependence explains why the movement's centre of gravity held in the two states where the mandi and procurement system was most complete.

## The Punjab Stakes: Procurement, Debt and the Mandi Economy

The movement's geography was not accidental, and its explanation lies in the political economy the three laws entered. Punjab's farm economy had been rebuilt since the late 1960s around the Green Revolution package: high-yielding wheat and paddy, assured irrigation, and a state procurement system that purchased the harvest at the announced support price through the mandis of the state marketing board and the Food Corporation of India. By 2020 Punjab contributed a share of the central foodgrain pool far beyond its share of the country's farmland, and the paddy and wheat rotation had become the state's economic spine. The arhtiya, the commission agent who financed the farmer, aggregated the harvest and mediated the mandi sale, stood inside that system as creditor, broker and political broker at once. The unions' defence of the mandi was therefore a defence of an entire credit and marketing settlement, not of a fee schedule.

Sukhpal Singh's scholarship on the Punjab economy, produced over two decades at Punjab Agricultural University and cited throughout the protest literature, documented the settlement's costs alongside its securities: groundwater drawn down by the paddy rotation, farm household debt carried at levels his surveys recorded among the highest in the country, holdings shrinking across generations, and a generation of educated rural youth for whom neither the farm nor the state offered employment of the kind the settlement had once promised. The laws arrived in a countryside already arguing about its future. The unions' reading of the trade area provisions drew its force from that context: a farmer carrying debt to the arhtiya, facing a buyer outside the mandi with no auction, no crowd of competing bidders and a magistrate's conciliation as the only remedy, heard in the statute not liberation from the intermediary but exposure without one. The government's counter-reading, pressed by Tomar and by economists who supported the reform, held that the mandi system itself taxed the farmer through fees and restricted buyers, that the arhtiya's credit was the most expensive in the village economy, and that choice of buyer was the precondition of any price improvement. Both readings rested on the same documented settlement. They divided on whether that settlement was a floor to be defended or a cage to be opened, and the protest year was, among other things, that argument conducted at national scale.

Haryana shared the procurement geography in its northern and central districts and added the politics of the Jat farming belt that runs into western Uttar Pradesh. The khap panchayats of that belt, clan councils with no formal legal standing and deep convening power, endorsed the movement through the winter and supplied the mahapanchayat form in which its 2021 expansion was organised. Western Uttar Pradesh contributed the sugarcane economy, whose grievance, the state advised price for cane and its delayed payments, predated the farm laws and ran alongside them; Tikait's Bharatiya Kisan Union carried the institutional memory of his father Mahendra Tikait's mobilisations of the late 1980s, including the 1988 siege of the Boat Club lawns in Delhi, a precedent the son invoked and the press recalled throughout the year. The movement's three-state core thus rested on three distinct farm economies, paddy and wheat procurement, the khap belt's mixed farming, and cane, united by a single statutory object. That unity across distinct economies is one of the facts the citizen-renegotiation reading is built on: the federation was an achievement of organisation laid over divergent material interests, and it held for fourteen months.

## Delhi Chalo and the Border Camps of November 2020

The Samyukta Kisan Morcha was formed on 7 November 2020 as a joint front of farm organisations, approximately forty unions at its formation, growing out of the coordination the All India Kisan Sangharsh Coordination Committee had built and the Punjab unions' own joint action committee. Its leadership was collective and its decisions issued in the Morcha's name. Rakesh Tikait spoke for the Bharatiya Kisan Union in western Uttar Pradesh; Joginder Singh Ugrahan led the Bharatiya Kisan Union (Ekta Ugrahan), the largest Punjab union, which kept its own distinct positions inside the front; Balbir Singh Rajewal, a veteran of Punjab farm politics since the 1970s, headed his own Bharatiya Kisan Union faction; Darshan Pal of the Krantikari Kisan Union served as a principal coordinator; Gurnam Singh Charuni led the Bharatiya Kisan Union in Haryana; and Hannan Mollah, general secretary of the All India Kisan Sabha and a former member of Parliament, linked the front to the older all-India peasant movement. Yogendra Yadav, a founder of Swaraj India who had moved from electoral politics to farm activism, worked in the Morcha's coordination and public argument, and his essays on the movement's discipline and programme were among its most cited public texts.

The march on the capital, called Delhi Chalo, began on 26 November 2020, the day of a general strike called by central trade unions against labour and farm legislation. Haryana police met the converging columns with water cannon, tear gas and barricaded highways; farmers removed barricades, crossed the points where national highways had been dug up, and reached the Delhi border on 27 November. Delhi Police fired tear gas at the Singhu crossing on National Highway 44 and offered the Nirankari ground at Burari as the designated protest site. The Morcha refused Burari, judging an enclosed ground a containment, and settled at the borders themselves. Singhu on the Punjab and Haryana approach became the largest camp. Tikri, on the highway from Bahadurgarh, held a second large encampment. Ghazipur, on the Uttar Pradesh border along National Highway 9, became the seat of Tikait's Bharatiya Kisan Union. Smaller presences held at Shahjahanpur on the Rajasthan and Haryana border and at Palwal on the highway from Faridabad.

The camps acquired the institutions of settled towns. Community kitchens, the langars, fed residents and visitors on a scale the movement's chroniclers numbered in tens of thousands of meals daily. Libraries, medical camps, laundry arrangements and stage programmes filled the trolleys and tents that lined the highways for kilometres. A newspaper, the Trolley Times, began publication from Singhu in December 2020 to carry the movement's own account of itself. The camps' durability through the Delhi winter became a political fact in its own right: a protest that could feed, house and govern itself at the capital's gates could not be waited out on the assumption of dispersal. The Morcha's stated programme remained two demands, repeal of the three Acts and a statutory guarantee of remunerative prices, and its stated method remained a disciplined occupation of the border sites with the capital's supply lines left open. The government's stated position through December held that the laws were passed by Parliament after discussion, that amendments could be considered, and that repeal was not on the table. Between those two stated positions the year of negotiation unfolded.

The march's passage through Haryana was its first trial, and it fixed the movement's method. Columns leaving Punjab on 25 and 26 November met barricades at the state line, trenches dug across national highways, and barricaded bridges; at several points police used water cannon and tear gas in cold weather that the marchers' own accounts made a byword of the journey. The columns answered with the repertoire the year would make familiar: barricades lifted aside by tractors, trenches filled, routes diverted through village roads where residents fed the marchers and opened their homes, and an unbroken refusal, recorded in the press of those days and in the Morcha's statements, to answer force with force. When the first columns reached Delhi's border on 27 November and were met with tear gas at Singhu, the refusal of the Burari ground converted a march into a settlement within hours: tents rose on the highway, the first langars were established, and the Morcha announced that it had come to stay until repeal. The Haryana government's stated position, given by Chief Minister Manohar Lal Khattar, held that the march was politically sponsored and that the laws would benefit Haryana's farmers; the state's police action and its chief minister's statements were answered, in the movement's subsequent geography, by Haryana's own unions holding the Tikri and Shahjahanpur sites and the khap panchayats' endorsements through the winter. The march had been planned as a Punjab action. It arrived as a three-state one, and the widening that occurred on the road, village by village and barrier by barrier, is the first documentary evidence for the reading this article defends: the renegotiation's carrier was assembled in motion, and it did not disassemble for fourteen months.

## The Camps as Institutions: Langar, Print and Discipline

The border camps deserve treatment as institutions because their internal order was the movement's principal exhibit. The langar, the Sikh community kitchen, operated at each major site at industrial scale, staffed by rotating village contingents and supplied by convoys of provisions from Punjab and Haryana villages that treated the supply of the camps as a collective obligation. The rotation system, in which villages sent batches of residents for fixed spells and replaced them in an unbroken relay, solved the problem that defeats most occupations: the harvest calendar. A protest that could rotate its population could outlast a season, and the camps' population in fact survived the wheat harvest of April 2021, the moment at which the government's waiting strategy expected them to thin to nothing. Medical camps staffed by volunteer doctors, libraries stocked in tractor trolleys, schools for the children of resident families, and a daily stage programme of speeches, poetry and religious observance filled the camps' calendar. The Trolley Times, a newspaper produced from Singhu in Punjabi, Hindi and English editions, carried the movement's record of itself, and a constellation of social media accounts, several of them suspended and restored in running disputes with the platforms and the government, carried its argument outward.

Discipline was the camps' stated constitution. The Morcha's code prohibited alcohol at the sites, assigned sanitation and security to its own volunteers, and routed every consequential decision through announced meetings whose outcomes were declared from the stage. Women participated in large numbers, as residents, speakers and tractor drivers in the parades, and the movement marked 18 January 2021 as Mahila Kisan Diwas, women's farmer day, with women conducting the stages at all three principal sites. The presence of elderly protesters through a Delhi winter, and the deaths at the sites from cold and illness that the Morcha's list recorded, formed part of the movement's moral presentation of itself: a population visibly paying costs for its demand. The government's stated criticism of the camps ran on different lines, the obstruction of arterial highways, the hardship of the neighbourhoods sealed behind the barricades, and the economic cost to Delhi's trade, and the residents of the border districts carried burdens the movement's record acknowledges only in passing. Both descriptions are true of the same sites. An occupation that is an institution to its residents is a blockade to the city it faces, and the politics of the year turned in part on which description held the larger public.

The camps also functioned as the movement's parliament. National decisions, the rejection of the amendment package in December, of the suspension offer in January, of the committee throughout, were announced as decisions of the Morcha's general body taken after consultation at the sites, and the form mattered to the movement's self-understanding: the governed, assembled, deliberating a statute of the Union and returning a verdict on it. That is the citizen-renegotiation mechanism in its most literal form, and the camps' institutional completeness, kitchen, press, rotation, code, assembly, is what allowed the form to persist long enough for the verdict to prevail.

## Eleven Rounds of Talks and the January 2021 Suspension Offer

Formal negotiation between the Union government and the farm unions ran in eleven rounds from 14 October 2020, when Agriculture Secretary Sanjay Agarwal met Punjab union representatives, to 22 January 2021. From the third round on 1 December 2020 the Union side was led by Agriculture Minister Narendra Singh Tomar, with Railways and Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal and Minister of State Som Parkash, himself from Punjab. The unions sent delegations drawn from the Morcha's constituents and reported each round back to the border stages. The government offered amendments in writing on 9 December 2020: a written assurance that the existing support price system would continue, equalisation of the fee position between the trade area and the mandis through state powers, a registration regime for traders stronger than the permanent account number threshold, and access to courts alongside the magistrate ladder. The Morcha rejected the package on the day it arrived, holding that anything short of repeal left the architecture intact.

### What did the government offer in January 2021 to end the standoff?

At the tenth round on 20 January 2021 the government offered to suspend the three Acts for a period of twelve to eighteen months and to constitute a joint committee of government and union representatives to examine the demands, including the statutory support price.

The Morcha's general body considered the offer on 21 January and rejected it, restating repeal as the precondition of any settlement. The eleventh round on 22 January 2021 ended without a date for a twelfth, and formal talks then stopped for the remainder of the protest year.

The rounds themselves traced the narrowing. In the first two rounds, on 14 October and 13 November 2020, officials heard the Punjab unions' objections without ministers at the table, and the unions read the absence as a measure of the government's seriousness. The third round on 1 December brought Tomar, Goyal and Som Parkash to Vigyan Bhawan, and the unions answered the ministerial presence with a discipline of their own: they carried their own food to the talks, declining the official lunch, a gesture recorded across the press of the day and intended, as union leaders stated, to mark that they had come to negotiate, not to be hosted. The fourth round on 3 December ended with the government undertaking to consider amendments; the fifth, on 5 December, with the unions observing a period of silence inside the meeting and holding placards demanding repeal; the sixth, set for 8 December, was overtaken by the Bharat Bandh of that day. The written amendment package followed on 9 December and was rejected within hours. The seventh round on 30 December produced the year's only partial agreement: the government accepted the unions' demands on excluding farmers from stubble-burning penalties under the air quality ordinance and on shelving the proposed changes to electricity subsidy through the Electricity Amendment Bill, while the two principal demands were carried forward. The eighth round on 4 January 2021 and the ninth on 15 January narrowed to the repeal question and adjourned on it. The tenth, on 20 January, produced the suspension offer; the eleventh, on 22 January, produced nothing but the record of its failure. No twelfth round was scheduled. The negotiation's documentary trail, offers in writing, a partial agreement minuted, a final offer refused, is complete enough to support the article's central inference: the state moved across eleven weeks from implementation to amendment to abeyance, and stopped, each time, one step short of the legislature.

The suspension offer matters to the reading defended in this article because it shows the state's own bargaining position moving under the pressure of the camps. In October 2020 the government would discuss implementation. By December it offered written assurances and amendments. By January 2021 it offered to hold its own enacted statutes in abeyance for up to eighteen months, an executive suspension of primary legislation that constitutional lawyers across opinion noted as an extraordinary instrument. Each movement of the state's position followed an escalation of the movement's: the march, the camps, the threatened Republic Day parade. Each movement stopped short of the single word the unions required, repeal, and the unions' rejection of the January offer stated their reasoning plainly: a suspended Act could be revived by the same executive that suspended it, while a repealed Act had to be enacted again by Parliament. The negotiation had narrowed to a question about which institution would hold the settlement, and the unions insisted on the legislature. That insistence, and its eventual vindication in November 2021, is the clearest documentary trace of the renegotiation this article names: the governed party declined an executive settlement and held out for a parliamentary one.

## The Supreme Court Stay and the Committee of January 2021

The Supreme Court entered the dispute through petitions challenging the Acts and petitions against the protests' blockade of the highways. On 12 January 2021 a bench of Chief Justice S.A. Bobde with Justice A.S. Bopanna and Justice V. Ramasubramanian stayed the implementation of the three farm laws until further orders and constituted a four-member committee to hear the grievances of the agitating farmers and the views of the government and to make recommendations. The Court recorded its hope that the stay and the committee would create an atmosphere in which the protest could be wound up. The order decided nothing about the constitutional validity of the Acts; it was an interim arrangement in pending proceedings, and the challenges to the Acts, raising the Union's legislative competence over agriculture as a state subject and the ordinance and passage history, remained unheard on their merits through the repeal and were overtaken by it.

The committee's composition decided its reception. Its four members were Bhupinder Singh Mann of the Bharatiya Kisan Union, Ashok Gulati, the agricultural economist and former chairman of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, Pramod Kumar Joshi of the International Food Policy Research Institute, and Anil Ghanwat of the Shetkari Sanghatana. Each had publicly defended the reform direction of the Acts before appointment, a fact the unions stated in their refusal to appear. The Morcha announced it would not participate in the committee process, while welcoming the stay as a judicial acknowledgement of the deadlock.

### Why did Bhupinder Singh Mann leave the Supreme Court committee?

Bhupinder Singh Mann recused himself on 14 January 2021, two days after the committee was constituted. In his statement Mann wrote that, in view of the sentiments and apprehensions among the farm unions and the public, he was ready to sacrifice the position so as not to compromise the interests of Punjab and of the farmers of the country.

Mann, a former member of the Rajya Sabha and himself a farmer and union leader, closed his statement with the pledge that he would always stand with his farmers and Punjab.

The remaining three members held consultations with farm organisations that were not part of the agitation and with industry and academic bodies, and submitted their report to the Court in a sealed cover on 19 March 2021. The report stayed sealed and unpublished through the rest of the protest year and after the repeal. Anil Ghanwat pressed for its release and, on 21 March 2022, made the report public himself, stating that a document bearing on public policy should not remain suppressed. The released report recorded that the majority of the organisations the committee consulted had supported the Acts with amendments, recommended against repeal, and proposed measures including a statutory backing for the support price announcement process and greater state flexibility in implementation. By the date of its release the Acts it assessed had ceased to exist, and the report survives as a residue of the judicial track: an expert verdict delivered into a sealed cover, overtaken by the political settlement it was constituted to inform. For the reading defended here, the committee episode carries a precise significance. The unions refused a settlement forum whose composition they had not negotiated, and the refusal cost them nothing in the eventual outcome; the renegotiation proceeded through the channel the movement itself had built, direct confrontation and negotiation with the executive, and ended in the legislature.

## The Federal Question the Court Never Answered

Beneath the political contest ran a constitutional one that the repeal left permanently undecided. Agriculture sits in the State List of the Seventh Schedule, and the state governments and the petitioners before the Supreme Court argued that the three Acts were, in pith and substance, laws on agriculture and land that Parliament had no competence to enact. The Union's defence rested on the Concurrent List entry for trade and commerce in foodstuffs and on the Union List entry for inter-state trade, arguing that the Acts regulated the marketing of produce rather than its cultivation. The Court's stay of 12 January 2021 was granted without a ruling on competence, and the repeal extinguished the challenge before any bench heard it to conclusion. The question therefore survives the episode as open doctrine: the extent of Parliament's power to legislate the conditions of agricultural trade over a State List subject remains where the Constituent Assembly's distribution of entries left it, tested by argument and never by judgment.

The states contested the laws legislatively as well. Punjab's Vidhan Sabha passed its amendment Bills on 20 October 2020, and Rajasthan and Chhattisgarh followed with state Bills of their own, each designed to restore within the state the regulatory position the Union Acts had displaced, and each reserved by its Governor for the President's consideration, where they rested without assent. The farmers' own federalism was practical rather than doctrinal: the movement was built from state-level unions, spoke in the idioms of three state countrysides, and made its demands on the Union because the statutes were the Union's. The episode thus displayed the federal tension in both directions at once, the Union legislating over a state field by ordinance in a lockdown, and a movement of the states' countrysides compelling the Union's legislature to reverse itself. The GST settlement examined elsewhere in this corpus renegotiated the fiscal federation by council and consensus; the farm laws episode renegotiated the legislative federation by siege and repeal. The constitution the founding pillar describes was built to hold both kinds of pressure, and in 2021 it held the second.

The Court returned to the protests once more before the repeal, in a register different from the stay. Hearing a petition by residents seeking the removal of the blockades, a bench of the Supreme Court observed in October 2021 that the right to protest could not mean a perpetual occupation of public highways, that commuters' rights stood on the same constitutional footing, and that the agitation's continuation after the laws had been stayed called for explanation. The unions' counsel answered that the blockades at the borders were the state's own barricades as much as the movement's camps, that the farmers had sought entry to the capital's designated grounds and been refused the sites they requested, and that the right to protest at the seat of government was the substance at stake. The exchange is the judicial track's second document, and it belongs with the first: a court that had frozen the laws in January without deciding them spent the year declining, in both directions, to become the settlement's author. The repeal came from the channel the Court had left open and the movement had never left, the political one, and the petitions challenging the Acts were closed as infructuous in its wake, the competence question carried forward undecided into whatever future statute tests it.

## The Tractor Parade of 26 January 2021 and the Red Fort

The Morcha's Republic Day tractor parade was negotiated with Delhi Police through January 2021; routes on the capital's periphery were agreed, and the police issued a no-objection arrangement for a parade along designated corridors after the official parade on Rajpath concluded. On 26 January 2021 the columns moved earlier than the agreed hour at several points, barriers at the borders were broken, and the parade diverged from its corridors into central Delhi. A section of protesters reached the Red Fort, where the actor Deep Sidhu and the wanted gangster turned activist Lakha Sidhana were present among those who entered the complex; a protester hoisted the Nishan Sahib, the Sikh religious flag, on a flagpole in the fort complex, alongside the Khalsa flag, on a pole distinct from the main rampart pole from which the Prime Minister addresses the nation on Independence Day. Clashes spread across the ITO crossing and other points. Navreet Singh, a young farmer from Rampur in Uttar Pradesh, died near ITO; Delhi Police stated that his tractor overturned after striking a barricade and that he died of the resulting injuries, while his family alleged police firing, a dispute over cause that remained contested in the public record. Delhi Police reported that more than three hundred of its personnel were injured. The police registered cases against named union leaders, including Tikait and Yadav, under charges that included sedition and attempted murder, and issued lookout notices in the days that followed.

The Morcha's stated position, issued the same evening, condemned the violence, dissociated the movement from the Red Fort entry, noted that the route deviations had begun before the agreed hour at points it had not authorised, and held the government responsible for allowing infiltrators to redirect a section of the march. The government's stated position held the unions answerable for a parade conducted under their call that had breached its agreed terms. The events of 26 January divided public sympathy measurably in the days that followed, and the movement's own leadership recorded the day as its gravest setback. What the sequence shows for the reading defended here is the discipline problem of a coalition movement at scale: the Morcha's authority over its own mobilisation was tested on the one day its formations entered the capital's ceremonial core, and the movement's survival after that test is the subject of the Ghazipur episode two days later.

### What revived the movement at Ghazipur on 28 January 2021?

On 28 January 2021 the Uttar Pradesh administration served notice on the Ghazipur camp and moved to clear it; Rakesh Tikait, refusing to surrender or vacate, addressed the gathered farmers and press in tears, declaring that he would rather die than leave and that he would drink water only if it was brought from his village.

The address travelled across western Uttar Pradesh that night, and farmers arrived at Ghazipur in thousands by morning, restoring the camp and the movement's momentum.

The Ghazipur night is the best documented instance in the protest year of the movement's moral economy operating as a political instrument. Tikait's pledge converted an eviction into a mobilisation: the administration's notice, intended to shrink the protest after its worst day, produced its largest reinforcement since November. In the weeks that followed, mahapanchayats across western Uttar Pradesh, Haryana and Rajasthan, at Muzaffarnagar on 29 January and in a rolling series through February and March, rebuilt the movement's public standing and extended its geography. The Morcha also recalibrated its tactics: a chakka jam of highway blockades on 6 February, conducted outside Delhi and with emergency services exempted, demonstrated reach without repeating the capital entry. The movement that entered the spring of 2021 was smaller at the borders than in December and broader in the countryside than at any earlier point.

## The Long Year: Mahapanchayats, Lakhimpur Kheri and the Counting of the Dead

Through 2021 the Morcha converted endurance into a political calendar. A mahapanchayat at Muzaffarnagar on 5 September 2021 drew a gathering the organisers numbered in lakhs and launched a campaign, named Mission Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand, aimed at the Bharatiya Janata Party in the assembly elections due in early 2022. At Karnal on 28 August 2021 police charged a farmers' gathering at the Bastara toll plaza; the district officer Ayush Sinha was recorded instructing police to strike protesters on the head, and a siege of the Karnal mini secretariat ended on 11 September with the government agreeing to a judicial inquiry and Sinha's transfer. A Bharat Bandh on 27 September 2021, marking a year since assent, closed markets and transport across several states. The movement's demands remained the two of November 2020, and its border camps, reduced in number, held Singhu, Tikri and Ghazipur through the year.

On 3 October 2021, at Tikunia in Lakhimpur Kheri district of Uttar Pradesh, a convoy of vehicles associated with Ajay Mishra, the Union Minister of State for Home, and his son Ashish Mishra drove into a body of protesting farmers. Four farmers and a journalist died in the vehicle impact; in the violence that followed, three occupants of the convoy were killed by the crowd. The Morcha stated that the vehicles had deliberately run down peaceful protesters returning from a demonstration against the minister's visit. The Mishra family stated that the convoy had been attacked with stones and sticks and that the driver lost control. A Special Investigation Team of the Uttar Pradesh Police chargesheeted Ashish Mishra and others, and its report described the incident as a pre-planned conspiracy rather than an accident; charges were framed by the trial court in December 2022. The case occupies a distinct place in the protest record: it is the year's deadliest single episode, and its competing accounts were partly adjudicated by the chargesheet's language while the trial itself proceeded on its own calendar.

The dead of the protest year became a disputed count, and the dispute itself belongs to the record. The Samyukta Kisan Morcha maintained a running list of those it recorded as having died during the agitation, at the camps, in travel to and from them, and in episodes including Lakhimpur Kheri; on 19 November 2021 the Morcha stated the figure as nearly seven hundred, and on 4 December 2021 it transmitted a list of 702 names to the Union government in support of its demand for compensation. The Union government's stated position, given by Agriculture Minister Narendra Singh Tomar in a written answer in the Lok Sabha in the winter session of 2021, was that the Ministry of Agriculture held no record of deaths in the agitation and that the question of compensation therefore did not arise. The two figures, 702 named dead on the movement's list and no record on the government's, were never reconciled, and neither was withdrawn. The article records them as their holders stated them, unaveraged: a named list maintained by the movement and an absence of record stated by the ministry. The Punjab government, for its part, paid compensation to families within Punjab and gave government employment to members of bereaved families under its own scheme, a state-level acknowledgement that coexisted with the Union's stated absence of record.

The winter's toll was recorded by the movement with the same documentary care it gave its demands. The Morcha's list of the dead, maintained from December 2020 onward and read aloud at the sites in commemoration, assigned each name a village, an age and a circumstance, and the word it used for the dead, shaheed, martyr, placed the protest year's losses inside a vocabulary of sacrifice that Punjab's political culture supplied. State governments answered parts of the list administratively: Punjab's government paid compensation to the families of farmers from the state who died during the agitation and offered government employment to one member of each bereaved family, and Haryana's government faced parallel demands it answered in part. The list's function in the renegotiation should not be missed. A movement that names and numbers its dead converts endurance from a claim into an account, and the account was presented, name by name and list by list, at every stage of the negotiation: in the Morcha's rejection of the claim that the protests lacked a human stake, in its statement on the repeal's announcement, which recorded the victory as purchased at the cost the list enumerates, and in the December settlement, where compensation stood in the government's letter because the list had made the dead a head of the negotiation. The Union government's stated absence of record, set against the movement's named seven hundred and two, remains the protest year's most quoted documentary dissonance, and this article has preserved it unresolved because the record preserves it so.

M.S. Sriram, the scholar of agricultural markets and finance whose essays on marketing reform accompanied the debate through the year, argued in his writings that the support price's effective geography, confined to a few states and crops, made the protests as much a defence of an existing procurement settlement as a resistance to a new market one, and that any durable settlement would have to address the states and crops the procurement system never reached. That analysis, from a writer broadly sympathetic to marketing reform, identifies the structural limit the repeal left untouched, and the article returns to it in assessing the residue.

## February 2021: The Toolkit Case and the Contest of Characterisations

The weeks after the tractor parade brought the contest over the movement's character to its sharpest point, and the record of that contest requires the allegations to be stated as their holders stated them. On 2 February 2021 the singer Rihanna posted a link to a news report on the protests with a question to her followers, and the Swedish climate activist Greta Thunberg posted in support, sharing a document described as a toolkit for the campaign. The Ministry of External Affairs issued a statement the next day holding that the temptation of sensationalist social media hashtags and comments, especially when resorted to by celebrities, was neither accurate nor responsible, and that the protests were being conducted by a very small section of farmers in parts of the country. Delhi Police registered a case over the toolkit document, alleging a conspiracy to wage war against the government of India, and on 13 February 2021 arrested Disha Ravi, a young climate activist from Bengaluru, on charges including sedition, for editing and sharing the document. A Delhi court granted her bail on 23 February, the judge recording that citizens are conscience keepers of government in a democratic polity and that the evidence of sedition in the material before the court was scanty.

The separatist characterisation ran on a parallel track. Leaders of the ruling party and aligned commentators alleged through the winter that Khalistani separatist elements had infiltrated the movement, pointing to posters and slogans at the sites honouring figures of the Punjab insurgency's past and to the Nishan Sahib hoisting at the Red Fort. The Morcha's stated answer, repeated by Tikait, Rajewal and others, was that the movement's programme was two demands about statutes and prices, that its stages carried the flags of its unions and the national flag, and that the allegation was a device to discredit a farmers' movement by renaming it. The government also suspended mobile internet at the border sites at intervals in late January and February 2021, a measure it defended on public order grounds and the movement condemned as an attempt to isolate the camps. Each of these episodes, the toolkit prosecution, the separatist allegation, the internet suspensions, belongs to the documented record of the year, and each carried a contested characterisation at its centre. The article records them as such: the allegations with their holders named, the answers with theirs, and the judicial residue, a bail order's reasoning, stated as the court stated it. The renegotiation reading does not need the movement to have been beyond criticism; it needs only the observation that the delegitimation campaign of February failed of its object, since the camps it targeted were still standing, and still the address to which the state eventually carried its repeal, in November.

## The Opposition Parties and the Parliament of 2021

The movement's relation to the party system was deliberately arm's length, and the record of 2021 shows why. The Morcha barred political parties from its stages from the first week at the borders, a rule its statements defended on the ground that the movement's charter belonged to the unions and that party platforms would divide what the charter united. Opposition leaders who visited the sites came as individuals at the margins of the camps rather than as speakers at them. The rule was tested repeatedly: the Congress party's national leadership supported the repeal demand, Rahul Gandhi led a tractor rally to Parliament in July 2021 and was detained, and the Aam Aadmi Party's Delhi government supplied the camps with water, sanitation and wireless internet facilities that the movement accepted as municipal services rather than as patronage. The Morcha's discipline held through all of it, and its stated reason stands as one of the year's most consequential political judgements: a movement that becomes a party's front loses the non-partisan breadth that made its federation possible, including the participation of farmers who voted for the governing party and said so at the sites.

Inside Parliament the laws' afterlife ran through the sessions of 2021. The Budget session of February and March 2021 carried opposition demands for debate on the protests, adjournments, and the government's position that the laws were settled and the talks were the forum. The monsoon session of July and August 2021 was consumed by disruptions in which the farm laws stood beside the Pegasus surveillance allegations as the opposition's stated grounds; the session passed legislation amid continuous protest in both Houses and ended two days early, with the opposition charging that Parliament itself had been reduced to the voice-vote condition of September 2020. The winter session opened on 29 November 2021 with the repeal Bill itself, passed in the Lok Sabha within minutes of its introduction and in the Rajya Sabha the same afternoon, in each case over opposition demands for discussion that the presiding officers did not grant. The symmetry is part of the record and was remarked across the press of the day: the laws that entered by a contested voice vote in a disorderly House left by an uncontested, undebated passage in a quiet one, and the opposition, which had demanded repeal for fourteen months, voted for it while protesting the manner of its arrival. The movement's own reading, stated from the Singhu stage, was simpler and is recorded as its holders stated it: the repeal had been won at the borders and was being registered in the House, and the House's procedure in registering it was the House's affair. For the framework this article defends, the parliamentary history of 2021 completes the evidence. The renegotiation was conducted outside the legislature by design, its settlement was executed inside the legislature by necessity, and the parties of the system were present at every stage as witnesses, amplifiers and claimants, but at no stage as the settlement's authors. The authors were a federation of unions and a government that finally answered it, and the documents of the answer are the Prime Minister's address and the one-page Act.

## The Repeal of November 2021 and the Suspension of December 2021

On 19 November 2021, the festival of Gurpurab marking the birth anniversary of Guru Nanak, Prime Minister Narendra Modi addressed the nation on television and announced the repeal of the three farm laws. His stated reasoning was precise and is recorded here in its own terms: the government had brought the laws for the benefit of farmers, especially small farmers; a section of the farming community had remained unconvinced despite the government's efforts to explain; and, in his words, "we could not convince some farmers". He apologised to the country for that failure of persuasion, announced that the constitutional process of repeal would be completed in the Parliament session beginning later that month, and appealed to the protesters to return home. He also announced a committee on the support price, comprising Union and state government representatives, farmers and agricultural scientists, to work toward transparency and effectiveness in the price system.

The announcement's timing sat inside a political calendar the record documents without needing to assign it a motive: assembly elections in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh were due within three months, and both states carried the protest's strongest geographies. Commentators who attributed the repeal to the electoral calendar and commentators who attributed it to the government's stated reasoning divided along predictable lines, and the article records the stated reasoning and the calendar as facts of the same record, leaving the causal weighting where the public argument left it, contested. What is not contested is the institutional form the repeal took. The Farm Laws Repeal Bill was passed by the Lok Sabha and by the Rajya Sabha on 29 November 2021, in each House without discussion, over opposition demands for a debate; the President gave assent on 30 November 2021, and the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 was published in the Gazette on 1 December 2021. It repealed the three Acts and omitted the provision the Essential Commodities amendment had inserted. The single demand the unions had defended through eleven rounds and a judicial committee, repeal by Parliament rather than suspension by the executive, was met in the exact institutional form they had specified.

The Morcha's response to the announcement was guarded in its own documented terms. Its statement of 19 November welcomed the decision, recorded the deaths it attributed to the agitation as the cost at which the decision had arrived, and announced that the movement would continue until the repeal took effect through parliamentary procedure and the remaining demands, the statutory price guarantee and withdrawal of cases and of the Electricity Amendment Bill, were addressed. A mahapanchayat at Lucknow on 22 November restated the remaining programme. The repeal Act's passage shifted the negotiation to the residue. On 9 December 2021 the Union government sent the Morcha a formal letter recording its decisions: a committee on the support price in which the Morcha would have representation, withdrawal of the cases registered against protesters in the Union territories and an appeal to the states to withdraw theirs, compensation to the families of the dead by the state governments concerned on the Punjab pattern, and no prosecution of farmers for stubble burning under the air quality legislation. On the strength of that letter the Morcha announced the suspension of the agitation on 9 December 2021, and the border camps were emptied in a farewell procession on 11 December 2021, three hundred and seventy-eight days after the march reached the borders.

## The Electoral Reading and the Punjab Verdict of 2022

The repeal's consequences ran into the election season that followed it, and the record of that season disciplines any simple electoral explanation of November 2021. Punjab and Uttar Pradesh voted in February and March 2022. In Punjab the Aam Aadmi Party won ninety-two of one hundred and seventeen seats, displacing the Congress government, reducing the Shiromani Akali Dal to three seats and the Bharatiya Janata Party, contesting separately from its former ally, to two. The farm movement's own electoral vehicle, the Sanyukt Samaj Morcha, a political front formed by Balbir Singh Rajewal and allied unions in December 2021, contested the Punjab election and won no seats, a result its leaders attributed to the movement's late and divided entry into electoral politics and the unions' own decision, in large part, to remain a movement rather than become a party. In Uttar Pradesh the Bharatiya Janata Party returned to power with a reduced majority; the movement's Mission Uttar Pradesh campaign had targeted it openly, and analysts divided over the campaign's effect in the western districts, where the party lost ground in several seats and held its statewide position on its strength elsewhere.

The electoral record therefore refuses both of the tidy readings offered for it. The repeal did not purchase the ruling alliance a Punjab recovery: the Akali Dal's exit from the alliance in September 2020 and the Congress government's collapse into the Aam Aadmi Party's victory proceeded on their own logic, and the party of the repeal gained almost nothing in the state whose farmers had besieged the capital. Nor did the movement convert its victory into seats: its own front's failure in Punjab is the clearest evidence in the record that the renegotiation the movement practised was institutional rather than electoral, a pressure applied to a legislature from outside the party system by a federation that declined, at the moment of its triumph, to enter that system. The one durable electoral consequence lay at the level of calculation. After November 2021 every national party pricing a reform that touched an organised constituency had the farm laws episode in its arithmetic: a completed statute, a year's siege, a televised apology and a one-page repeal Act. The episode entered the repertoire of Indian politics as the demonstration case of the costs of legislating against a federation that can hold a border.

## The Residue: The MSP Committee, Delhi Chalo 2.0 and the Unfinished Settlement

The repeal closed the statutes and left the settlement's other half to committees and later marches. The committee on the support price announced in the Prime Minister's address and promised in the December letter was constituted by the Agriculture Ministry on 18 July 2022 under the chairmanship of Sanjay Agrawal, a former agriculture secretary, with the Morcha invited to nominate three representatives. The Morcha declined to nominate members, stating that the committee's composition and terms of reference, which included examining the support price's future alongside natural farming and crop diversification, did not embody the December understanding as the Morcha read it. The committee continued without the Morcha's nominees and submitted its deliberations through its own course. The statutory guarantee of the support price, the demand the unions had ranked with repeal itself, remained unenacted through the period this article covers: recommended in principle by committees across decades, demanded by a year-long siege of the capital, promised a committee in the repeal settlement, and still, at the record's end, a demand rather than a law.

In February 2024 the unsettled half produced a second march. Two formations outside the original Morcha's discipline, the Samyukta Kisan Morcha (Non-Political) and the Kisan Mazdoor Morcha, under the leadership of Sarwan Singh Pandher and Jagjit Singh Dallewal, called Delhi Chalo 2.0 from 13 February 2024, with a charter headed by the statutory price guarantee on the Swaminathan Commission's C2 plus fifty percent formula. Haryana police sealed the Punjab border at Shambhu and Khanauri with concrete barriers, iron nails and tear gas; the columns halted at the border and camped there. On 21 February 2024 Shubhkaran Singh, a young farmer from Bathinda district, died at Khanauri during a confrontation; the circumstances of his death were disputed between the protesters, who attributed it to police firing, and the Haryana authorities. Dallewal undertook a fast unto death at Khanauri from 26 November 2024, ended it in January 2025 after the Union government invited the unions to talks, and negotiations resumed in February 2025. In March 2025 the Punjab Police cleared the Shambhu and Khanauri camps and detained the leaders, closing the second encampment without the march having reached Delhi and without the price guarantee enacted.

The residue therefore cuts both ways, and the article weighs it without trimming. The repeal stands: three Acts of Parliament, defended by their authors as the most consequential agricultural reform in decades, were withdrawn in their entirety within fifteen months of assent, the only complete statutory reversal of a flagship reform in the period the corpus covers. The price guarantee does not stand: it survived the repeal as the movement's carried-forward demand, visible in the 2024 march's charter and unmet at that march's dispersal. Sriram's structural point returns here with force. The procurement settlement the movement defended covered paddy and wheat in a handful of states; the guarantee it demanded would have universalised a price floor the fiscal and market systems had never carried at that scale; and the state's refusal, consistent across the repeal and after it, marks the boundary between what the renegotiation could win and what it could not. The movement won the restoration of the status quo ante in full and the transformation of that status quo not at all. Both halves of that sentence belong to the record.

## The Economics Each Side Cited

The argument over the laws was conducted with rival bodies of figures, and the figures deserve recording with their holders attached. The unions' charter arithmetic rested on the Shanta Kumar committee's 2015 finding, quoted across the movement's literature, that only about six percent of farmers obtained the support price for their produce, the committee having been appointed by the same government that later enacted the laws. The movement turned that finding in two directions at once: the support price system was too narrow to be trusted to an announcement, and the farmers inside it, the six percent concentrated in Punjab, Haryana and a few other states, had the most to lose from its erosion. The Swaminathan Commission's formula, cost C2 plus fifty percent, supplied the guarantee demand with its number: the government calculated the support price at A2 plus family labour plus fifty percent, and the difference between the two cost bases was itself a technical dispute with a political charge, since adopting C2 would raise the announced prices substantially and the procurement bill with them.

The government's figures answered from the other side. The Agriculture Ministry's statements through the year recorded that procurement at the support price had risen to record levels in the 2020 to 2021 season, that the number of farmers reached by paddy and wheat procurement had grown, and that the laws changed neither the price announcement nor the procurement machinery; Tomar told Parliament that the support price had been raised across crops on the A2 plus family labour formula and would continue. Economists supporting the reform, Ashok Gulati most prominently among them, argued in their columns that the trade area's freedom would raise farm-gate prices by admitting competition the mandi's licensed cartel suppressed, and that a statutory price guarantee for all crops was fiscally and logistically impossible, since the state cannot purchase, store and dispose of the entire marketable surplus of twenty-two crops. Economists critical of the laws answered that the impossibility argument assumed procurement of everything, where the guarantee could operate through deficiency payments or a legal floor enforced on private trade, instruments other jurisdictions use and the Bhavantar scheme in Madhya Pradesh had piloted in imperfect form. The article does not adjudicate the fiscal arithmetic, which turns on design choices never legislated; it records that both sides argued from commission reports and ministry data, that the numbers in dispute were real numbers with named sources, and that the political contest was decided by neither side's arithmetic. The farmers who held the borders were, on the movement's own account, defending a known settlement against a projected one, and projected gains have never in this corpus outbid a held settlement when the holders could organise.

## The Counter-Arguments at Their Strongest

The evenhandedness this series practises requires the case against the movement to be stated as its holders made it, and that case had four heads. The first was constitutional propriety of method: a statute enacted by a Parliament in which the governing party held a large majority, the argument ran, should be amended or repealed by the same route and on the floor, not under the pressure of a highway blockade; the government's defenders held that conceding repeal to an occupation taught every organised interest that a siege outranks a vote. The second was the blockade itself: the residents and traders of the border districts, and the Delhi administration in its court filings, recorded closed highways, diverted traffic, lost trade and obstructed emergency movement across a full year, costs borne by people who were not parties to the dispute. The third was representation: the movement spoke for the procurement belt of three states, its critics noted, while the laws' intended beneficiaries, the smallholders of the eastern and central states whom the mandi system barely reached, had no comparable federation at the borders, and the committee's consultations recorded farm organisations outside the agitation supporting the Acts with amendments. The fourth was the reform's substance: that Indian agriculture's marketing restrictions were a documented drag on farm incomes, that every expert committee for three decades had recommended loosening them, and that a repeal won by endurance returned the sector to a status quo its own defenders, Sukhpal Singh's debt and groundwater figures among them, described as a crisis.

The movement's answers are also on the record. To the first, that it had sought the floor throughout: its demand was a parliamentary act, and its rejection of executive substitutes was a defence of the legislature against the executive, not of the street against the House. To the second, that it kept emergency passage open at its sites, fed the neighbourhoods its kitchens served, and carried costs of its own that its list of the dead enumerates. To the third, that its charter's demands, a price guarantee for all farmers and the defence of a public procurement open in law to every state, were framed universally even if its federation was regional, and that the absence of other regions' farmers from the borders measured their organisational poverty rather than their consent. To the fourth, that it had never defended the status quo as just: its charter demanded the guarantee precisely because the existing settlement reached too few, and it opposed the laws because they weakened the floor under the reached few without building one under the rest. The closing adjudication of this article does not require either case to collapse. It requires only the observation, documented across the eleven rounds and the year's sequence, that the state's four arguments, whatever their force, were never converted into a settlement, while the movement's two demands were converted into one statute repealed and one promise recorded; and in a contest of renegotiation, conversion is the metric.

## The Five Features as One Sequence

The five features can be read as a single sequence rather than as episodes. The first feature, the legislative sequence of June to September 2020, set the terms: statutes made by ordinance, passed in the upper House by voice vote over a refused division, and defended thereafter as settled law. The second feature, the Delhi Chalo march and the border camps from 26 November 2020, answered the legislative fact with a physical one: an organised federation at the capital's gates that could sustain itself indefinitely and could not be dispersed without a political cost the state declined to pay. The third feature, the tractor parade of 26 January 2021 and the Ghazipur revival of 28 January, tested the movement's discipline and its depth in the same week: the coalition's control failed at the Red Fort and its countryside roots held at Ghazipur, and the second fact outweighed the first. The fourth feature, the Supreme Court's stay of 12 January 2021 and its committee, opened a judicial track that the movement refused and that events bypassed: the stay froze the laws without deciding them, and the committee reported into a sealed cover. The fifth feature, the repeal announced on 19 November 2021 and enacted by 1 December 2021, closed the sequence in the institution where it had opened, Parliament, in the form the movement had demanded since the December rejection of amendments.

Read together, the features describe a transfer of initiative. In September 2020 the state set the agenda and the unions reacted. By December the unions had fixed the agenda's single question, repeal or no repeal, and the state's every offer was measured against it. The amendment package of 9 December, the suspension offer of 20 January, and the judicial committee were successive attempts to move the question onto ground the state could manage, amendments, abeyance, expertise. Each failed against the same refusal. The repeal succeeded because it finally answered the question in the terms in which the movement had posed it. The archival trace of this transfer is unusually complete: eleven rounds of talks with dated offers, a court order with a dated committee, a televised address with a stated rationale, a one-page repeal Act. For a researcher archiving such a sequence for later verification, the practice recommended in [the VaultBook guide to protecting sources and records](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) is the preservation of the primary documents themselves, the Acts, the order, the address and the letter of 9 December 2021, against the commentary that accumulates around them; the sequence this article reconstructs is only as strong as those documents, and they are all public.

The sequence also disciplines the reading against exaggeration. The movement did not write new law; it unwrote law, which is a rarer and narrower achievement. It did not shift the underlying political economy of procurement, prices or state finances; the residue section of this article records the guarantee unmet and the second march dispersed. What it did was to demonstrate that a sufficiently organised, sufficiently patient federation of the governed could reverse a completed act of the governing majority by extra-parliamentary pressure sustained to the point of parliamentary surrender. Every element of that demonstration is dated and documented in the features above.

The episode's documentary residue is unusually rich, and its shape matters to any later reading. The movement archived itself as it went: the Trolley Times from the Singhu site, the Morcha's dated press statements and letters to the government, the pamphlets printed for distribution at the camps, and the correspondence through which forty organisations coordinated a single line are preserved in part at the movement's own collections and in the Punjab archives its chroniclers assembled. The state's record is equally dated and public: the ordinances and Acts, the parliamentary debates of September 2020, the written answers in which the Agriculture Ministry stated its positions, the Supreme Court's order of 12 January 2021, the repeal Act of 2021 and the letter of 9 December 2021. Between those two archives sits a third, the press record of a year in which the camps were among the most reported sites in the country. A reader testing this article's reading against the record need not rely on any participant's memoir: the offers, refusals and instruments on which the renegotiation claim rests are documents with dates, and the claim stands or falls on whether the sequence they form shows what the article says it shows. That is the standard the series has applied to every episode it has treated, and its closing article is content to be judged by it.

## The Pillars in Retrospect: A Series-Closing Synthesis

The farmer protests close the pillar of movements, and the pillar closes the series. The movements framework, set out at this pillar's opening in [the movements-as-citizen-renegotiation framework](/2021/01/09/indian-movements-citizen-renegotiation/), reads collective action as the governed renegotiating the terms of their governance in public. The pillar's ten movements test that reading across a century's range of forms. The JP movement, traced in [the account of the movement for total revolution](/2021/01/14/jp-movement-total-revolution/), sought the renegotiation of an entire political order and was answered by the Emergency. The Chipko movement renegotiated the terms on which a forest economy would be governed, and the Narmada movement renegotiated, and partly arrested, the terms of a river valley's transformation. The Naxalite and Maoist movements carried renegotiation into armed refusal, and the women's movement renegotiated the terms of representation itself. The Telangana movement renegotiated the map, and Shaheen Bagh, examined in [the citizen-renegotiation through specific architectural moments and content framework](/2021/02/22/shaheen-bagh-caa-protests-india/), renegotiated the terms of belonging for a community that sat down in the street and stayed. The farmer protests inherit every one of these forms in part: the mass moral claim of the JP movement, the resource defence of Chipko and Narmada, the representational demand of the women's movement, the federal grievance of Telangana, the sit-in's patience of Shaheen Bagh, and a discipline that renounced the armed path of the Naxalite tradition. No earlier movement in the pillar combined all of them, and none closed with a statute repealed.

### Which earlier movements does this pillar place beside the farmer protests?

The synthesis sets the farmer protests beside nine predecessors treated in this pillar: the JP movement, the Chipko movement, the Narmada Bachao Andolan, the Naxalite and Maoist movements, the women's movement, the Anna Hazare movement and the party it produced, the Telangana statehood movements, and the Shaheen Bagh sit-in.

In sequence, the pillar's closing synthesis sets the farmer protests beside the JP movement of 1974 to 1975, the Chipko movement from 1973, the Narmada Bachao Andolan from 1985, the Naxalite and Maoist movements from 1967, the women's movement from 1975, the Anna Hazare movement of 2011 and the Aam Aadmi Party formed from it in 2012, the Telangana statehood movements of 1969 and of 2001 to 2014, and the Shaheen Bagh sit-in that began on 15 December 2019. Each is treated in its own article in this pillar.

Behind the pillar stand the fourteen frameworks of the corpus. The founding settlement of the republic was read at the series' opening through [the founding three-tensions framework](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), and the three tensions named there, between the centralising state and the federal units, between majoritarian democracy and minority protection, and between state-led transformation and market order, recur in the farmer protests in their exact founding form: a Union statute on a state subject, a minority region's farmers defending a procurement settlement, and a state-directed market reform resisted by its intended beneficiaries. The Nehruvian baseline of the second pillar, the Indira-era renegotiations of the third, and the coalition quarter-century of the fourth supply the institutional inheritance the movement confronted: the mandi system, the procurement state and the support price are all artefacts of the food crisis politics of the 1960s. The Modi-era settlement, read in the fifth pillar through [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), is the governing order the movement faced, and [the post-2019 election political-architectural context](/2019/08/16/2019-election-verdict-explained/) explains the parliamentary majority whose completed legislation the movement set out to reverse. The single-term majority that made the September 2020 passage possible also made the repeal a decision one leadership could take without coalition negotiation, a symmetry the record of November 2021 displays plainly.

The remaining frameworks place the protests in their full institutional field. The Sangh framework of the sixth pillar and the Congress long arc of the seventh describe the party system within which the Akali Dal's exit from the ruling alliance, and the opposition's parliamentary conduct in September 2020, took their meaning. The regional framework of the eighth pillar explains why the movement's geography was Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh and why its idiom was the panchayat and the mahapanchayat. The caste framework of the ninth pillar, centred on the Mandal renegotiation, supplies the comparison of an earlier generation's street politics over a state settlement. The religion framework of the tenth pillar governs the Nishan Sahib episode and the separatist allegations that opponents of the movement attached to it, allegations the Morcha answered by restating its programme in the language of prices and statutes. The elections framework of the eleventh pillar frames the electoral calendar inside which the repeal was announced. The judiciary framework of the twelfth pillar, built on [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), frames the basic structure question the farm law challenges would have raised and never reached: whether agriculture's place in the State List limited the Union's trade and commerce power in the manner the petitioners argued. The foreign-policy framework of the thirteenth pillar, opened by [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/), records the protests' international echoes, from the Canadian and British parliamentary mentions to the diplomatic exchange they caused. The economic framework of the fourteenth pillar, opened by [the economic-policy-as-politics framework](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/) and extended by [the political-architectural-substantive-content for post-2019 economic architecture framework](/2021/01/03/2019-corporate-tax-cut-pli-scheme/), supplies the sharpest comparison in the corpus: the reforms of 1991, enacted by a minority government in a balance of payments crisis, were never reversed; the farm laws of 2020, enacted by a majority government at the height of its strength, were repealed within fifteen months. The difference between the two reform episodes is the movement itself, and a framework that cannot price the movement cannot explain the difference. That is the corpus's closing analytical claim, and the farmer protests are its evidence.

## Organisation as the Argument: What the Federation Built

The movement's least visible achievement was organisational, and it is the variable on which the closing adjudication turns. Indian farm politics before 2020 was fragmented by state, crop and union lineage; the federations of the 1980s, Mahendra Tikait's in the west of Uttar Pradesh, Sharad Joshi's Shetkari Sanghatana in Maharashtra, the Karnataka Rajya Raitha Sangha under M.D. Nanjundaswamy, had each spoken for a region and a crop economy, and their all-India coordinations had been episodic. The Samyukta Kisan Morcha was built differently: a standing front with a working committee, a shared two-demand charter that subordinated every constituent's wider programme, a rotation system that made its physical presence sustainable, a media operation that spoke in three languages, and a discipline code enforced at its own sites. Organisations that had spent decades apart, the communist-led All India Kisan Sabha and the Bharatiya Kisan Union factions, unions of Punjab's smallholders and of Uttar Pradesh's cane belt, sat in one committee and held one line through eleven rounds of talks, a court committee, a Republic Day disaster and a change of season. The federation survived the deaths of participants, the arrest warrants against its leaders, and the counter-mobilisation of the state's full communicative apparatus.

That survival is the mechanism the citizen-renegotiation reading names. A demand becomes a renegotiation when its carriers can impose a cost for refusal that grows rather than decays with time, and every element of the Morcha's construction served that condition: rotation defeated decay, the charter defeated division, the stage and press defeated isolation, the langar defeated the economics of endurance. The state's offers map onto the federation's durability with precision. Amendments were offered when the camps were six weeks old. Suspension was offered when they had held through the winter's start and a Republic Day parade was days away. Repeal was announced when they had held a full year, rebuilt after their worst week, and carried their campaign into two election-bound states. The sequence of concessions is unintelligible without the organisation that extracted them, which is why framings that begin from the statutes or from agricultural economics must treat the outcome as an accident of politics, and why this article's framing, which begins from the federation, treats it as the predictable yield of a form.

One further comparison belongs to the synthesis, because it disciplines the claim against the charge of regional exception. The corpus contains an earlier repeal won by the governed: the land acquisition ordinance of 2014 to 2015, re-promulgated twice by the Union government and then allowed to lapse in August 2015 after opposition in Parliament and protest in the countryside made its enactment untenable. The farm laws episode differs from that precedent in degree that approaches kind. The land ordinance died as an ordinance, never having survived a full parliamentary passage into settled operation; the farm laws were passed, assented, stayed by a court, defended for fourteen months by a government with a single-party majority, and then repealed by the Parliament that had enacted them, at the demand of a federation that had refused every lesser instrument. Where the 2015 lapse taught that an ordinance could be waited out, the 2021 repeal taught that an Act could be marched out. The movements framework survives the comparison because it predicts the difference: the 2015 opposition was parliamentary and episodic, the 2020 to 2021 movement was federated, chartered and encamped, and the depth of the institutional reversal tracks the depth of the organisation that demanded it, in this corpus's cases, with a regularity that the closing section states as the series' final finding on its fifteenth pillar.

## Against the Rival Framings

Three framings compete for the protest year. The farm-laws-protest framing, carried by the bulk of press coverage and by the encyclopaedic record, treats the episode as a dispute about three statutes: their content, the agitation against them, and their withdrawal. It is accurate at the level of the documents and silent about the mechanism. It cannot explain why amendments failed and repeal succeeded, why a court committee was refused without cost, or why the movement's programme was two demands of which one was institutional, a statute for prices, rather than a price itself. The agricultural-policy framing, carried by the specialist literature, treats the episode as a chapter in marketing reform: the long argument from the model Acts through the Shanta Kumar committee to the ordinances, with the protests as the reform's political obstruction. Sukhpal Singh's Punjab-centred work and the market scholarship of writers such as M.S. Sriram give this framing its strongest form, documenting the procurement economy's regional narrowness and the fiscal weight of the status quo. It explains the stakes with precision. It does not explain the outcome, because the reform it assesses was defeated not by a better policy argument but by a superior political organisation, and a framing that treats organisation as an externality will record the repeal as an interruption rather than as the event.

The citizen-renegotiation framing defended in this article subsumes both rivals as partial records. Against the complication that the protests operated through farm-specific conditions, the repeal's dependence on Punjab and Haryana's procurement geography, on the electoral calendar of two states, and on the unions' pre-existing federation, the article concedes the conditions in full and answers that conditions are what a renegotiation works through. The JP movement worked through the conditions of Bihar politics and a railway strike; Chipko through the conditions of a hill forest economy; the mechanism the framework names, the governed converting endurance into a revised settlement, is visible through the conditions in every case the pillar examines. The test of the framing is comparative. It predicts that movements which fix a single institutional demand, hold a disciplined federation, and sustain presence at the seat of power will extract settlements in the institution they name, and the pillar's ten cases divide on exactly those variables: Telangana named a state and received it, Shaheen Bagh named a statute and did not receive its repeal, the Narmada movement named a dam's height and resettlement terms and received a part. The farmer protests named a repeal and a statute; they received the repeal in full and the statute not at all, and the framing that registers both halves, the win and the boundary, is the one this closing article defends.

The participants' own assessments belong in the record beside the scholars'. Yogendra Yadav, writing as a participant in the Morcha's coordination, assessed the movement's achievement as the restoration of the farmer as a political subject in national argument, a figure addressed by policy rather than consulted by it, and he recorded the movement's self-imposed limits, its refusal of party form and of any demand beyond its charter, as the discipline that made the restoration possible. Union leaders' post-repeal statements divided over the December settlement: the Morcha's majority read the 9 December letter as a bankable commitment and suspended the agitation on it, while Joginder Singh Ugrahan's union, which had kept its distance from several Morcha positions through the year, recorded its reservations about accepting assurances in place of a statute. The dissent is part of the residue's explanation: the guarantee demand lacked, after December 2021, the unified carrier that had won the repeal, and the 2024 march was led by formations outside the original federation. Organisation, the variable this article has pressed throughout, thus explains the settlement's asymmetry as fully as it explains the repeal: what the whole federation demanded together, it won; what it accepted as a promise and then pursued in fragments, it did not.

The framings also differ in what they predict for the residue. The policy framing expected the repeal to restore the reform question to expert committees, and the MSP committee of July 2022 answered that expectation. The protest framing expected the episode to close with the repeal, and Delhi Chalo 2.0 refuted it. The renegotiation framing expects an unsettled demand to reappear in organised form until it is met or its carriers dissolve, and the march of February 2024, its charter headed by the unmet demand, is the expectation's confirmation in the record. A series that began with a Constituent Assembly renegotiating a colonial inheritance closes with farmers renegotiating a statute at the gates of the same capital. The distance between those two scenes is the distance the corpus set out to measure, and it is measured, in the end, in institutions moved: an assembly that wrote a constitution, and a movement that unwrote three laws.

A closing word on method belongs to the series as much as to this article. The corpus set out to read Indian politics through dated documents, named holders and adjudicated disagreements, and its final episode is the one in which that method is most severely tested, because the farmer protests are close enough to their readers to arrive pre-interpreted by every camp that contested them. The article has therefore held itself to the sequence the documents form: three Acts with texts that can be read, a voice vote whose record can be inspected, eleven rounds with dated offers, an interim order and a sealed report with a release date, a televised address whose words are on record, a one-page repeal Act, a government letter, a committee notification, and a second march with a charter. Where the record divides, over the death of a young man at ITO, over the convoy at Lakhimpur Kheri, over the number of the dead and over the flag on a fort's flagpole, the article has carried the division rather than settled it, naming each account's holder. Where the record is single, it has stated it plainly. The claim the article defends, that the protests operated as a citizen-renegotiation that reversed a completed legislative settlement in the institution that made it, is offered as the reading that accounts for the largest share of that dated record with the fewest exceptions, and it is offered, as every claim in this series has been offered, against the documents themselves, which outlast the argument and invite its correction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When did the farmer protests begin?

The protests began in Punjab in the summer of 2020, after the Union government promulgated the three farm ordinances on 5 June 2020. Punjab unions held local demonstrations through July and August, escalated them after Parliament passed the Bills in September, and sustained a rail roko across the state from late September into November. A Bharat Bandh on 25 September 2020 widened the action across Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh. The all-India phase of the movement opened with the Delhi Chalo march of 26 November 2020, which carried the protests to the borders of the capital and fixed the dates by which the movement is usually remembered.

### Q: What were the three farm laws of September 2020?

The three laws were the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act 2020, the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act 2020, and the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act 2020. All three began as ordinances on 5 June 2020, passed the Lok Sabha on 17 September and the Rajya Sabha by voice vote on 20 September, and received Presidential assent on 27 September 2020. Together they freed trade in farm produce outside the regulated mandis, created a national frame for contract farming, and removed stock limits on most foodstuffs outside declared emergencies.

### Q: What is the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce Act 2020?

The Act created a category of trade area covering every place outside the yards of a market committee constituted under state agricultural produce marketing law. Within a trade area, farmers could sell to any trader holding a permanent account number, trade was freed from state market fee, cess and levy, and electronic platforms could operate across state lines. Disputes went to conciliation and then to the Sub-Divisional Magistrate, and the Act barred civil courts from hearing them. The government presented the Act as a one nation one market measure. The unions argued that fee-free trade outside the mandis would drain the mandi system and the procurement that rested on it.

### Q: What is the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act 2020?

The amendment removed cereals, pulses, oilseeds, edible oils, onion and potatoes from routine control under the Essential Commodities Act 1955. The Union government could impose stock limits on these items only in extraordinary circumstances, defined as war, famine, extraordinary price rise or a natural calamity of grave nature, with price triggers set at a doubling for perishables and a fifty percent rise for non-perishables. The government argued that predictable deregulation would draw private investment into storage and processing. The unions argued that removing stock limits outside emergencies licensed hoarding by large buyers and weakened the public procurement and distribution system.

### Q: What was the 26 January 2021 Republic Day tractor parade?

The parade was a tractor march the Samyukta Kisan Morcha called for Republic Day 2021 along routes negotiated with Delhi Police on the capital's periphery. On the day, columns moved before the agreed hour at several points, barriers were broken, and a section of protesters entered the Red Fort, where the Nishan Sahib was hoisted on a flagpole in the complex. Clashes occurred at ITO and elsewhere, one protester, Navreet Singh, died near ITO in circumstances disputed between the police account and his family, and Delhi Police reported more than three hundred injured personnel. The Morcha condemned the violence and dissociated itself from the Red Fort entry.

### Q: When did Modi announce repeal of farm laws?

Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced the repeal in a televised address to the nation on 19 November 2021, the festival of Gurpurab marking the birth anniversary of Guru Nanak. He stated that the laws had been brought for the benefit of farmers, that the government had failed to convince a section of the farming community of their merits, and that he apologised to the country for that failure. He said the constitutional process of repeal would be completed in the winter session of Parliament, and he appealed to the protesters at the Delhi borders to return to their homes and fields.

### Q: What is the post-November 2021 farmer-protest residue?

The residue is the part of the December 2021 settlement that remained unexecuted after the repeal. Parliament repealed the three laws, and the government letter of 9 December 2021 promised a committee on the support price with union representation, withdrawal of protest cases, compensation on the Punjab pattern and protection from stubble-burning prosecution. The support price committee was constituted in July 2022 without the Morcha's nominees, and the statutory price guarantee remained unenacted. In February 2024 a second march, Delhi Chalo 2.0, carried the same demand to the Punjab and Haryana border, where its camps were cleared in March 2025.

### Q: What did the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act 2020 provide?

The Act authorised farming agreements between a farmer and a sponsor for a term from one crop season to five years. An agreement had to record a price or a pricing formula carrying a guaranteed amount, with provision for a bonus or premium above it, and it barred the sponsor from acquiring the farmer's land or raising permanent structures on it. Agreements stood outside state contract-farming regulation and outside stock limits. Disputes went to a conciliation board and the Sub-Divisional Magistrate, with appeal to the Collector, and dues could not be recovered against the farmer's land. The government called the frame price assurance; the unions called it an unequal forum.

### Q: Why did the unions demand a legal guarantee of minimum support price?

The support price was an administrative announcement without statutory force, effective mainly where public procurement operated, which meant paddy and wheat in a few states. The unions reasoned that if trade migrated out of the mandis into the fee-free trade area the new laws created, procurement volumes and the price realisation that rested on them would decline, and an announced price without legal force could be lowered or abandoned by any later government. A statutory guarantee, recommended in the Swaminathan Commission's reports, would convert the announcement into an enforceable entitlement. The demand therefore ranked with repeal in the Morcha's charter from November 2020 onward.

### Q: How many rounds of talks did the government hold with the unions?

Eleven rounds of formal talks were held between 14 October 2020 and 22 January 2021. The first rounds involved Punjab unions and officials; from 1 December 2020 Agriculture Minister Narendra Singh Tomar led the Union side with Piyush Goyal and Som Parkash. The government tabled written amendment proposals on 9 December 2020, which the unions rejected, and at the tenth round on 20 January 2021 offered to suspend the laws for twelve to eighteen months under a joint committee, which the Morcha's general body rejected the next day. The eleventh round on 22 January ended in deadlock, and no further round was convened.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court order on 12 January 2021?

A bench of Chief Justice S.A. Bobde with Justice A.S. Bopanna and Justice V. Ramasubramanian stayed the implementation of the three farm laws until further orders and constituted a four-member committee to hear the grievances of the agitating farmers and the views of the government and to make recommendations. The order was an interim arrangement in the pending challenges; it decided nothing on the constitutional validity of the Acts. The Court recorded its expectation that the stay and the committee would help resolve the impasse at the borders. The unions welcomed the stay and refused to appear before the committee.

### Q: What happened at Lakhimpur Kheri on 3 October 2021?

At Tikunia in Lakhimpur Kheri district, a convoy of vehicles associated with Union Minister of State Ajay Mishra and his son Ashish Mishra drove into a body of protesting farmers who were demonstrating against the minister's visit. Four farmers and a journalist died in the impact, and three occupants of the convoy were killed in the violence that followed. The Morcha stated the vehicles had deliberately run down peaceful protesters. The Mishra family stated the convoy had been attacked and the driver had lost control. A Special Investigation Team chargesheeted Ashish Mishra and others and described the incident as pre-planned; the trial court framed charges in December 2022.

### Q: How many deaths did the Samyukt Kisan Morcha attribute to the protest year?

The Morcha maintained a running list of those it recorded as having died during the agitation, at the camps, travelling to and from them, and in episodes including Lakhimpur Kheri. On 19 November 2021 it put the figure at nearly seven hundred, and on 4 December 2021 it transmitted a list of 702 names to the Union government in support of its compensation demand. The Union government's stated position, given in a written answer in the Lok Sabha, was that the Agriculture Ministry held no record of deaths in the agitation and that compensation therefore did not arise. The two positions were never reconciled.

### Q: What did Prime Minister Modi say in the 19 November 2021 address?

The Prime Minister said the three laws had been enacted for the benefit of farmers, particularly small farmers, and that his government had explained them through every available forum for over a year. He stated that the government had nevertheless failed to convince a section of farmers, apologised to the countrymen for that failure, and announced that the laws would be repealed through the constitutional process in the session of Parliament beginning that month. He appealed to the protesting farmers to return home, announced a committee on making the support price system more transparent and effective, and framed the repeal as a decision taken in the nation's interest on an auspicious day.

### Q: What did the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 do?

The Repeal Act was a short statute that repealed the three farm laws of 2020 in their entirety. The Bill passed the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha on 29 November 2021 without discussion, over opposition demands for a debate; the President gave assent on 30 November, and the Act was published in the Gazette on 1 December 2021. It repealed the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, and undid the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act by omitting the provision that amendment had inserted into the 1955 Act.

### Q: When did the Supreme Court committee report become public?

The committee submitted its report to the Supreme Court in a sealed cover on 19 March 2021, after Bhupinder Singh Mann's recusal had reduced its membership to three. The report remained unpublished through the repeal and into the following year, although member Anil Ghanwat publicly pressed for its release. Ghanwat made the report public on 21 March 2022. The released text recorded that most organisations consulted had supported the Acts with amendments, recommended against repeal, and proposed measures including statutory backing for the support price process and greater implementation flexibility for states. By its release the Acts it assessed had been repealed for over three months.

### Q: What did the government letter of 9 December 2021 contain?

The letter, sent by the Union Home Ministry to the Samyukta Kisan Morcha, recorded the government's decisions on the movement's pending demands. It stated that the committee on the support price would include representatives of the Morcha alongside Union and state governments and agricultural experts; that cases registered against protesters in the Union territories would be withdrawn and the states would be asked to withdraw theirs; that compensation to the families of farmers who died would follow the pattern adopted by the Punjab government; and that farmers would not be prosecuted for stubble burning under the air quality law. The Morcha announced the suspension of the agitation the same day.

### Q: What is Delhi Chalo 2.0 of February 2024?

Delhi Chalo 2.0 was a second march on the capital called from 13 February 2024 by the Samyukta Kisan Morcha (Non-Political) and the Kisan Mazdoor Morcha, formations distinct from the original Morcha, under Sarwan Singh Pandher and Jagjit Singh Dallewal. Its charter headed the demand for a statutory support price guarantee on the Swaminathan formula. Haryana sealed the Punjab border at Shambhu and Khanauri, and the columns camped there rather than entering Delhi. The young farmer Shubhkaran Singh died at Khanauri on 21 February 2024 in disputed circumstances. Dallewal fasted at Khanauri from November 2024, talks resumed in early 2025, and Punjab Police cleared the camps in March 2025.

### Q: How does the citizen-renegotiation reading differ from the agricultural-policy framing?

The agricultural-policy framing reads the episode as a chapter in marketing reform, assessing the three laws against the economics of mandis, procurement and private investment, and treating the protests as the reform's political obstruction. It explains the stakes precisely but leaves the outcome unexplained, because the reform fell to organisation rather than to argument. The citizen-renegotiation reading treats the protests as the governed revising a settlement: a federation fixed one institutional demand, refused executive substitutes for a parliamentary answer, and sustained its presence until the legislature reversed itself. The framing accounts for the amendment package's failure, the suspension offer's rejection, the committee's refusal, and the repeal's exact form, which the policy framing records without explaining.

### Q: Why did the unions refuse to appear before the Supreme Court committee?

The unions objected to the committee's composition. All four members named on 12 January 2021, Bhupinder Singh Mann, Ashok Gulati, Pramod Kumar Joshi and Anil Ghanwat, had publicly defended the reform direction of the farm laws before their appointment, and the Morcha stated that a body so composed could not hear its case impartially. The Morcha also held that its negotiation was with the government that had made the laws, not with a court-appointed panel, and that appearing would move the question from the political forum, where its strength lay, to an expert forum whose verdict it could not control. It welcomed the stay while boycotting the committee, and Bhupinder Singh Mann recused himself two days later.
