Fortune, chance, and accident do the decisive work in Romeo and Juliet, and the play's famous fate is mostly a poster hung over a machine built of mishaps. A servant who cannot read his own errand delivers the lovers to each other. A sword passes under an intervening arm and kills the wrong man. A friar is sealed inside a plague house with the one letter that could save everyone, while a servant rides fast with news that is accurate in every detail and false in its whole meaning. The heroine wakes minutes after the hero has poisoned himself in the dark beside her. None of these events is written in any star. Each is a collision: two chains of action, each intelligible on its own, meeting at a point nobody chose. The argument of this article is that contingency, not destiny, is the tragedy's operating system, that the characters call the system Fortune because their culture gave them a goddess for it, and that counting the accidents, sorting them, and testing each one for necessity produces a colder and more accurate account of the ending than the star-crossed label ever supplied.

## Fortune, Chance, and Accident in Romeo and Juliet: The Verdict in Brief

The verdict can be stated plainly before the evidence is assembled. Fate, in the strict sense of an outcome fixed in advance by an order outside human action, causes nothing that happens on stage. The Prologue announces such an order, calling the pair "star-cross'd" and their love "death-mark'd" (Prologue 6, 9), but the plot that follows declines to honour the announcement with machinery. What the plot supplies instead is a sequence of accidents: unintended, unforeseeable collisions between a feud, a secret marriage, a sleeping potion, a postal errand, a plague regulation, and a handful of minutes in a tomb. Choice loads each accident with consequence, since a city without the feud would have shrugged off a delayed letter, but choice does not manufacture the accidents themselves. Fortune is the name the sufferers give the pattern. When Romeo stands over two corpses of his own making and cries "O, I am fortune's fool!" (3.1.131), he is not reporting a metaphysical discovery. He is reaching for the largest available word for the fact that events have slipped the leash of his intentions.

That distinction, between the vocabulary of fortune and the mechanics of accident, organizes everything that follows. The governing synthesis of fate against free will, with its three-way ledger and its overdetermination verdict, belongs to [the fate and free will article](/2023/12/22/fate-and-free-will-romeo-and-juliet/), and this article compresses that verdict to a pointer: fate owns the play's language of explanation, choice owns its decisions, and neither owns its timings. What remains unowned by that synthesis, and what this article claims as its territory, is the accident class itself: the near misses catalogued as a class, the difference between an accident and a mistake and a design, the way contingency behaves as a mechanism with rules of its own, and the criticism that has tried to name it.

Three tests will be applied to every disaster in the catalogue. The first is the removal test: strike the event, and ask whether the catastrophe still happens on schedule. The second is the intention test: did any agent in the scene aim at this outcome, or did the outcome arrive sideways, against or beside every aim in the room? The third is the foreseeability test: could a reasonable person inside the play's world, with the knowledge available at the time, have predicted the collision? An event that fails intention and foreseeability, and whose removal breaks the chain, is a pure accident. An event with an aim behind it is a choice, however disastrous. An event produced by an agent's error of judgment, where the agent aimed at something else and misjudged a foreseeable risk, is a mistake. The classes matter because the play's critics, its characters, and its popular reputation keep trading one for another, calling mistakes fate and calling pure accidents blame. The ledger near the end of this article sorts the disasters by these tests. Its finding can be previewed in one sentence: the catastrophe requires at least three pure accidents in the final movement alone, each one necessary, none of them sufficient, and all of them laid across choices that made Verona the kind of city where a mishap could kill.

### What did an Elizabethan mean by calling a fall an act of Fortune?

An Elizabethan who called a fall an act of Fortune was not offering a shrug. The phrase assigned the event to a recognized power with a recognized instrument, the wheel, and a recognized habit, the raising of the low and the casting down of the high without regard to desert.

Fortune in this usage is neither fate, which fixes outcomes in advance, nor providence, which orders outcomes toward good. Fortune is the retrospective name for the vertical movement of a life, the felt experience of being lifted or dropped by something that does not answer prayers and does not keep accounts. When the play's characters say fortune, they mean that power and its wheel, and the word carries a whole philosophy of history inside it, which the sections below unpack before testing it against the plot's actual machinery.

## Fate, Fortune, Chance: Three Words That Do Not Mean the Same Thing

The confusion begins with vocabulary, because English lets fate, fortune, and chance circulate as synonyms, and lazy reading of the tragedy lets them blur into a single fog of doom. The play's own usage is more precise than its reputation. Fate appears almost exclusively in the frame and in retrospect: in the Prologue's "fatal loins" and "death-mark'd love" (Prologue 5, 9), in Friar Laurence's closing report of "a greater power than we can contradict" (5.3.153), in phrases spoken after the damage, by men accounting for a finished thing. Fortune, by contrast, is the word of people inside the machinery while it is still moving: Romeo at 3.1, mid-catastrophe, Juliet at 3.2, bargaining for a husband's return, Friar Laurence at 5.2, holding an undelivered letter. Chance, the third term, is the word the play almost never speaks at the moments chance is busiest, a point the ledger section takes up. The distribution is itself an argument. Fate is how the story is packaged; fortune is how it feels from inside; chance is what is actually happening.

Defined strictly, the three terms describe three different universes. Fate is a universe with a script: the end is settled before the beginning, and events are the script's delivery mechanism. Fortune is a universe with a wheel: outcomes rise and fall in cycles, the pattern is real but the pattern has no destination and no moral, and the only prediction available is that whoever is up will come down. Chance is a universe with collisions: independent causal chains, each lawful in itself, intersecting at points that no chain intended and no order prescribed. A fourth term haunted the period's thinking and shadows all three: providence, the doctrine that a benevolent intelligence supervises the collisions and bends them toward good ends. Comedy, as Susan Snyder's work shows, runs on providential chance, where the intersections keep rescuing people. This tragedy runs on chance stripped of the supervisor. The intersections keep killing people, and the survivors reach for fortune, and then for fate, because bare chance is the one explanation that offers nobody to bargain with, blame, or defy.

The Friar's herb speech in Act 2 states the non-fatal universe in miniature, though its close reading belongs to the cell scene's own article. "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied, / And vice sometime by action dignified" (2.3.21-22): outcomes depend on application, timing, and handling, not on essences fixed in the stars. A poison and a medicine share a plant. That is a pharmacist's cosmology, and it is the cosmology the plot obeys. Nothing in the action is intrinsically fatal; everything becomes fatal by misapplication, mistiming, and collision. The potion that should stage a reunion stages a funeral because a letter met a plague regulation on the road. The steel that should defend a friend opens the friend's body because an arm moved across the line of the thrust.

### Is fortune merely fate wearing a friendlier mask?

The short answer is no, and the difference is the hinge of the whole article. Fate explains in advance: given fate, the wise response is submission, since resistance changes nothing. The two doctrines produce different behaviour in the characters who hold them.

Fortune explains only in retrospect: given fortune, the wise response is humility at the top of the wheel and patience at the bottom, since movement is certain and direction is not. Nobody in the play submits to fortune the way a fated hero submits to an oracle. They curse fortune, bargain with fortune, beg fortune to spin once more. Juliet's great apostrophe in Act 3, examined below, is a negotiation, and one does not negotiate with fate. The mask metaphor fails because the faces underneath differ: fate has a plan, fortune has a wheel, and the wheel is what the sufferers in this play actually describe when they speak carefully.

One further distinction does quiet work throughout. An accident is not the same thing as a coincidence, though the two are constantly confused in discussions of the ending. A coincidence is the intersection itself, two chains arriving at the same point at the same time, neutral in value: a friar and a letter and a plague house. An accident is a coincidence with damage in it, an intersection whose outcome nobody wanted and somebody pays for. The play's final movement is a stack of coincidences that turn accidental one by one as the stakes they carry come due. Sorting them requires keeping the stack visible, event by event, rather than melting it into a general atmosphere of doom. Atmosphere is how fate readings survive; enumeration is how they die.

## The Wheel of Fortune: The Period's Machinery for Explaining Falls

To hear the word fortune as the first audiences heard it, start with the image that dominated European reflection on reversal for a thousand years: the goddess Fortuna turning her wheel, with kings and beggars strapped to its rim. Boethius, writing his Consolation of Philosophy in a prison cell in the sixth century while awaiting execution, gave the image its canonical voice. Fortune speaks in her own defence and admits the charge against her as her nature: turning is what she is, and the man who rode up on her wheel signed its terms when he climbed on. The Consolation's distinction mattered for every later century. Boethius separates providence, the divine mind's still overview of all events, from fate, the unfolding of those events in time, and sets fortune loose as the name for how the unfolding feels to creatures trapped inside it, who see rises and falls without the overview. An Elizabethan reader formed by this tradition, and the tradition was a schoolroom commonplace, heard three different claims in the three words, and heard fortune as the most human of them: not the truth about the universe, but the truth about being inside it without a map.

The de casibus tradition turned the wheel into a genre. Boccaccio's collection of falls of illustrious men, Englished and expanded in the Mirror for Magistrates, processed princes, commanders, and favourites through the same machinery: exaltation, a turn, a drop, a ghost complaining of the goddess. The falls were exemplary precisely because they were not fated in the astrological sense and not punished in the providential sense. They demonstrated the wheel's indifference. Frederick Kiefer's Fortune and Elizabethan Tragedy, the standard modern history of the idea on the English stage, documents how thoroughly this machinery saturated the drama the first audiences knew. Kiefer's fortune is a genuine operative power in the period's imagination, distinct from both God and the stars: a personified distributor of goods and reversals whose defining trait is inconstancy, and whose presence in a play signalled that outcomes would be governed by turn rather than by desert. On Kiefer's account, when Romeo names fortune he is not decorating his guilt with a metaphor. He is citing the reigning explanation for why the meritorious and the worthless change places without warning.

The wheel also carried a specific visual grammar that the play exploits without ever staging the emblem itself. The rim has a top, where the favoured ride, and a bottom, where the crushed pass underneath, and its motion is continuous, which means every position is temporary. Juliet's speech in Act 3 works the grammar exactly: she addresses Fortune as a person with a known character, "all men call thee fickle" (3.2.60), and then asks the fickleness to operate in her favour for once, to spin her banished husband back by the same inconstancy that took him. The speech only makes sense inside wheel logic. One does not beg fate to be fickle; fate's terror is that it never is. One begs fortune, whose terror and whose promise are the same property, that she never stays.

Against this background the Prologue's astral vocabulary stands out as the play's most misleading sentence. "Star-cross'd" promises an astrological machine, planets in adverse aspect crossing the lovers' paths, and the play then delivers no astrology at all: no horoscope is cast, no planet is consulted, no character's fortune is read from a chart. The stars in the action are decorative and defiant, images in love poetry and one cry of rebellion in Mantua, while the operative machine is the wheel's poorer cousin, the collision. Kiefer's history helps measure the bait and switch. An audience primed for fortune's wheel by a generation of de casibus drama got the wheel's effects, rise and reversal, arbitrary distribution, desert ignored, delivered through mechanisms too humble for any emblem: a guest list, a quarantine order, a servant's eyesight report from a churchyard. The goddess never appears. Her work is done by clerks, searchers, and messengers, which is the play's bleak joke about how reversal actually arrives in a governed city.

## Fortune's Fool: The Thrust That Went Under an Arm

The tragedy's first corpse is produced by the purest accident in the play, and it is worth slowing the famous duel down to its mechanics, because the mechanics are the argument. Tybalt comes hunting Romeo and finds Mercutio. Romeo, newly married to a Capulet and bound by a kinship he cannot declare, refuses the challenge and attempts to part the fighters when Mercutio takes it up. In the text as René Weis prints it in the Arden Third Series edition, the stage direction derived from the second quarto has Tybalt thrust Mercutio in under Romeo's arm. The detail is the whole scene in one piece of blocking. Romeo's body, interposed to prevent a wound, becomes the screen behind which the wound is given. His peacekeeping does not merely fail; it supplies the condition of the killing. Mercutio's own report strips the event of any grandeur: "I am hurt. / A plague o' both your houses! I am sped" (3.1.90-91). A plague on both houses is the curse of a man who cannot find a responsible party shaped like a villain, and his diagnosis by curse lands on the system, the two households whose quarrel provided the street, the challenge, and the blades.

Apply the three tests. Intention: Tybalt intended to wound Mercutio eventually, but the mortal thrust as delivered, under the arm, at the moment of intervention, was aimed at no such geometry; Mercutio intended a fencing exhibition and a humiliation of the prince of cats, not his own death; Romeo intended the prevention of exactly this. The killing fails the intention test in its decisive particular. Foreseeability: that a street fight might end in a wound was foreseeable, and the men who drew swords own that risk, which is why the ledger will not class the death as pure accident. The specific mortal outcome, though, a scratch that Mercutio himself measures with a joke, "not so deep as a well, nor so wide as a church door" (3.1.96-97), turning mortal in a world without antisepsis, was the kind of escalation nobody in the scene priced in. Removal: strike the interposition, and the duel proceeds as a formal single combat between two skilled fencers whose likely outcomes are wounds, shame, arrest, a city scandal, outcomes the comedy of the first two acts could have absorbed. Strike the duel itself and everything downstream vanishes, but the duel was a choice. The interposition was the accident, and the accident is what converted a brawl into a body.

Then Romeo names the event, and his naming is the most quoted piece of misdirection in the play. "This gentleman, the Prince's near ally, / My very friend, hath got his mortal hurt / In my behalf" (3.1.108-110) is accurate accounting, and Romeo immediately spends the accuracy on a metaphysical alibi: "O, I am fortune's fool!" (3.1.131). The line's dramatic function is double. It registers a real bewilderment, since Romeo's intentions and the outcome stand in grotesque disproportion, and disproportion between intention and outcome is the felt signature of accident. It also relocates agency. A fool of fortune is a plaything, and a plaything is not answerable. Within a dozen lines Romeo has killed Tybalt in open revenge, an act of pure choice that no wheel turned for him, and the fortune cry sits between the accident he suffered and the choice he made, blurring the border in both directions. The criticism of the line has often taken Romeo's word for it and filed the whole scene under fate's irony. The ledger files it differently: the wound under the arm is accident riding on chosen violence; the revenge killing is choice; the famous sentence is a character mistaking the first for the whole, in a play that keeps inviting its audience to make the same mistake in grander terms.

### Does Romeo blame fortune to excuse himself at 3.1?

In part, and the text lets an audience watch the excuse being assembled. Romeo's first words over Mercutio are precise about his own causal role: the hurt was got "in my behalf," and his self-reproach names his softness, his temper unmanned by love, with an exactness that shows he understands the mechanics perfectly well.

The fortune cry comes later, after Tybalt's death, at the moment when consequence arrives in the form of the Prince's party entering. It is timed like a plea. Yet the excuse is not cynical, because the disproportion Romeo feels is real: he chose peace and produced a corpse, and a vocabulary of pure choice has no shelf for that outcome. Fortune is the shelf his culture built for it. The line is best read as an honest man reaching for the wrong category under pressure, and the error is diagnostic, since the play's whole argument about accident begins with its hero unable to tell a mishap from a metaphysic.

Mercutio deserves a paragraph in any anatomy of accident, because he is the play's one clear-eyed theorist of the random, and the play kills him with it. His Queen Mab speech imagines a fairy no bigger than an agate stone driving over sleepers' noses and assigning dreams by whim, a miniature cosmology of arbitrary distribution. His wit treats the world as a lottery conducted by tiny, careless powers. Then the lottery selects him through the gap under his friend's arm, and his exit lines keep the theory to the end: the wound is a scratch, the scratch is enough, "ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man" (3.1.97-98). The pun is the sound of a mind filing a mortal accident under wordplay because no grander category is available in the seconds allowed. After Mercutio, the play's accidents stop being funny, and the criticism that treats the duel as fate's hinge rather than chance's masterpiece mistakes the announcement for the event. Nothing announced this death. It happened in a gap, in the plain space between an arm and a body, and the gap is where this tragedy lives.

## The Guest List: A Servant Who Could Not Read Starts the Tragedy

Every account of the play's causes should be required to start at Act 1 Scene 2, where the entire love story is set in motion by a literacy problem. Capulet, having settled the Paris negotiation, sends a servant out with the written list of guests for the evening's feast and the instruction to invite them. The servant cannot read. His lament is comic prose with a philosophy degree: "It is written, that the shoemaker should meddle with his yard, and the tailor with his last, the fisher with his pencil, and the painter with his nets; but I am sent to find those persons whose names are here writ, and can never find what names the writing person hath here indited" (1.2.39-44). He must find a reader. The reader he finds, by walking the same street at the same hour, is Romeo, who is sighing over Rosaline, whose name is on the list, and who accepts the servant's invitation, extended to anyone not of the house of Montague, to "come and crush a cup of wine" (1.2.81-82). Romeo's own summary of the encounter is the play's smallest fortune text: "Ay, mine own fortune in my misery" (1.2.58), spoken about his ability to read, at the exact moment reading becomes the hinge of his life.

Run the tests on this, the founding accident. Intention: the servant intended any literate stranger; Romeo intended a distraction from Rosaline; Capulet intended a full feast. The meeting of these three intentions at that corner, with that list, in that hand, was aimed at by none of them. Foreseeability: a master who hands a written errand to a man whose reading he has never tested has made an error, and the error belongs to the household's management, but the error's consequence, that the reader would be the one young Montague in Verona pining for a listed guest, lay beyond any reasonable forecast. Removal: strike the illiteracy, and the servant delivers his invitations door to door, Romeo never sees the list, never learns Rosaline will be at the feast, never enters the Capulet hall masked, and there is no play. The event passes every test for pure accident, and it is not a minor one. It is the efficient cause of the meeting, the love, the marriage, and the tomb. The Prologue had promised that the stars did it. The second scene shows a staffing failure doing it.

In the comic tradition the play inherits, this exact device, the errand gone sideways, the letter or list delivered into the wrong hands, is providence wearing a clown's costume: the mishap that secretly serves the lovers. A servant's error in a comedy puts the right document before the right eyes and the plot calls it fortune with a wink, meaning design. Shakespeare keeps the device and reverses its polarity over the length of the play. The first misdelivery serves the lovers; the last misdelivery destroys them; and the two events are the same event, a text entrusted to a carrier who cannot guarantee its arrival, separated by four acts and a change of weather. Audiences who feel the ending is fated are often feeling the rhyme between these two errands and mistaking the rhyme for a plan. A rhyme is not a plan. It is what accident looks like when a poet arranges the telling, and confusing the arrangement of the telling with an arrangement inside the told world is the founding error of the fate reading.

## Juliet Bargains with the Wheel: Fortune in Act 3 Scene 2

Juliet is the play's least fatalistic major voice, which makes her one great fortune speech the more valuable as evidence of what the word does in a character's mouth. The news has arrived in scrambled form, Tybalt dead, Romeo banished, the Nurse's lament having first let her believe Romeo himself was the corpse. Alone, she apostrophizes the goddess directly: "O Fortune, Fortune! all men call thee fickle; / If thou art fickle, what dost thou with him / That is renown'd for faith? Be fickle, Fortune, / For then, I hope, thou wilt not keep him long, / But send him back" (3.2.60-64). Every element of wheel doctrine is present. Fortune has a character, and the character is inconstancy. Inconstancy is usually the complaint against her; Juliet converts it into the petition's logic. A constant power that had taken Romeo would keep him; a fickle one might return him by the same arbitrariness that exiled him. The prayer is a bet placed on the wheel's continued turning, placed by a speaker who understands the machine she is betting on better than any other character in the play understands any machine.

Set the speech beside Romeo's fortune cry and the difference between the lovers' metaphysics comes into focus. Romeo uses fortune retrospectively, as an alibi for a finished disaster, fortune as the name of what already crushed him. Juliet uses fortune prospectively, as a counterparty, fortune as a power whose next move might be solicited. Neither usage resembles fate. Fate cannot be petitioned, and fate cannot be an alibi either, since under fate the alibi and the crime are equally scripted. Both lovers, at their moments of maximum pressure, reach past the Prologue's astrology for the wheel, and the reach is the play's quiet correction of its own poster. The characters who live the events do not experience them as a script unfolding. They experience them as a run of turns, some vicious, some kind, whose only law is that the run continues. Experience is on the wheel's side. The plot's mechanics, as the catalogue keeps showing, are on the side of bare collision, and the gap between the wheel the characters believe in and the collisions the audience watches is the space where the criticism of accident operates.

There is a further precision in Juliet's speech that the fortune literature, Kiefer's history included, helps bring out. She does not ask Fortune to be just. She does not claim Romeo deserves return, or that the banishment was a miscarriage she is appealing to a higher court. Desert has dropped out of the transaction entirely, and its absence is the wheel's signature: the goddess distributes without a ledger of merits, so the only available strategy is to ask her to keep moving. The Friar, by contrast, moralizes every turn, reading calamity as affliction sent for instruction, and the Prince closes the play by converting the whole run of turns into a judgment with pardons and punishments. Juliet's pagan clarity about the machine, it does not care, it only turns, is the closest any character comes to the play's own view, and it comes from the character with the least power to affect the machine's next position. The strong moralize accident into providence or fate; the powerless see it plain.

## The Quarantined Letter: Plague, Paper, and a Sealed Door in Act 5 Scene 2

The catastrophe's central accident deserves the fullest mechanical description the play affords, and the play, unusually, affords a complete one. Act 5 Scene 2 exists to explain a failure, and its explanation is a small masterpiece of bureaucratic causation. Friar John, dispatched to Mantua with the letter that explains the potion, the feigned death, and the plan, reports his errand step by step. He went to find a brother of the order to accompany him, as the rule of travel required. He found the brother visiting the sick in a house where plague had been identified. The searchers of the town, the parish officers charged with exactly this duty, "Suspecting that we both were in a house / Where the infectious pestilence did reign, / Seal'd up the doors, and would not let us forth, / So that my speed to Mantua there was stay'd" (5.2.8-11). He could not send the letter by another hand, could not bribe or argue his way out, and eventually returned with the letter still in his possession: "I could not send it, here it is again" (5.2.13). The scene-by-scene anatomy of this short scene, its placement after the poison purchase rather than before, and its information design belong to [the undelivered letter article](/2023/12/01/act-5-scene-2-undelivered-letter/), which owns Act 5 Scene 2 as a scene. The accident anatomy is this article's concern, and the anatomy is remarkable for containing no villainy, no negligence worth the name, and no remedy.

Apply the tests, and the quarantine scores as the cleanest pure accident in the tragedy. Intention: no agent in the sequence aimed at the outcome. Friar John aimed at Mantua. The brother aimed at the sick. The searchers aimed at Verona's public health, and by their lights succeeded. Friar Laurence aimed at Romeo's enlightenment with an urgency he states himself. The collision of these four innocent aims produced the single most lethal event in the play. Foreseeability: plague quarantine was a known feature of the world, a routine hazard of travel in the period, and a prudent sender might in principle have duplicated the letter or sent a second messenger by another road. The Friar's failure to build redundancy into the plan is the one thread a blame reading can pull, and the blame article pulls it properly. But redundancy against this specific failure, a companion errand that had to happen before departure, a house sealed on suspicion rather than certainty, a detention measured in exactly the days the plan could not spare, asks for foresight calibrated to a scenario rather than to a risk. Mistakes are misjudgments of foreseeable risks. This was a foreseeable category with an unforeseeable fit to the one week in Verona's history when a two-day delay was a death sentence, and the ledger classes it as accident with a human margin, the margin noted and priced in the verdict below.

Laurence's response to the report is the tragedy's fortune vocabulary at its most exposed: "Unhappy fortune! By my brotherhood, / The letter was not nice but full of charge, / Of dear import, and the neglecting it / May do much danger" (5.2.17-20). Watch what the cry does. The mechanism he has just heard is specific, institutional, and modern in flavour: officers, a sealed door, a detained courier. The name he gives it is a goddess. The gap between the mechanism and the name is the whole fortune phenomenon in one line. Fortune is what a mind says when the causal chain is too long, too branching, and too innocent to hold a culprit, and the pressure to say something larger becomes irresistible. The line also performs a quiet absolution: if fortune neglected the letter, the plan's architect did not. Yet the Friar immediately acts on the accident model rather than the goddess model, seizing a crowbar and hurrying to the tomb to be present at the waking, which is the correct response to a logistics failure and a pointless response to a deity. His feet believe in accident even when his mouth says fortune. The play keeps staging that split, in character after character, and the criticism that follows the mouths rather than the feet ends up writing about fate in a play whose people behave, under pressure, as if everything were still to be salvaged by speed and improvisation.

### Could any precaution inside the play's world have defeated the quarantine?

Precautions existed in the abstract: a duplicate letter sent by a second route, a verbal message entrusted to a Mantuan contact, a shorter feigned sleep. Each precaution, examined inside the play's conditions, dissolves on contact with the same contingency it was meant to defeat.

A second messenger doubles the secrecy risk of a plan whose survival depended on a single pair of hands; the Friar had already been forced to use a brother rather than a trusted servant because the matter required the order's confidentiality. A shorter sleep shrinks the margin the quarantine actually consumed while widening the risk that Juliet wakes in the vault to no rescuer, the very horror her soliloquy rehearsed. The honest finding is that the plan was a reasonable gamble against ordinary friction, and the quarantine was not ordinary friction; it was the tail risk, the low-probability event whose cost the plan had no way to insure against. Classing the outcome as the Friar's mistake requires pricing tail risks as foreseeable, a standard under which every accident becomes someone's error and the category of accident disappears altogether, taking the play's point with it.

The plague itself repays a moment's attention as the accident's engine, because plague in the 1590s was not an exotic visitation but a recurring administrative condition of urban life. Theatres closed for it; households were shut up with a mark on the door; searchers viewed corpses and certified causes; movement between towns carried suspicion as a matter of routine. Shakespeare's company worked around plague closures as a condition of employment, and the audience that first watched Friar John's report had lived the procedure he describes. That familiarity is the accident's realism and its horror. No thunderclap interrupts the plot, no oracle misleads, no witch prophesies. A municipal health measure, correctly applied by minor officials doing their jobs competently, kills the heroine as efficiently as any dagger. The play's deepest claim about contingency is folded inside that choice of mechanism: the modern world of offices, errands, seals, and schedules generates its own tragedies without consulting the heavens, and the heavens, consulted afterward in the fortune vocabulary, are a commentary track laid over a disaster the commentary cannot reach.

## Balthasar's Report: The Accident of Being Right Too Soon

The quarantine stops the true message. What the true message's absence makes possible is the second accident of the final movement, and it is subtler: a false report that is not a lie. Balthasar rides to Mantua at speed, having stood in the Capulet churchyard and watched Juliet's body carried to the family vault with full funeral ceremony. His report is the report of an honest eyewitness: "Her body sleeps in Capel's monument, / And her immortal part with angels lives. / I saw her laid low in her kindred's vault" (5.1.17-20). Every clause is justified by the evidence available to him. The body did sleep in the monument; he did see it laid low. The falsity lives entirely in the inference the evidence invites, that sleeping is death, and the inference is the one every mourner in Verona drew, including the family that staged the funeral. Balthasar's news is the plan working exactly as designed, received by exactly the wrong reader. The potion plot required the whole city to believe Juliet dead; it succeeded; and its success, arriving in Mantua without the letter that was supposed to accompany and correct it, is indistinguishable from the catastrophe it was built to prevent.

The scene article on Act 5 Scene 1 owns the Mantua scene as a scene: Romeo cheerful from a dream, the three questions, the poison purchase, the Apothecary's poverty. The accident reading adds one observation to that ownership. Romeo's fate vocabulary in the scene, the celebrated defiance of the stars at 5.1.24, is quoted and weighed in the fate synthesis, and it matters here only for its timing: Romeo reaches for the stars at the precise moment when the operative facts are a sleeping draught, a sealed door, and a servant's ride. The metaphysical gesture is a measure of how completely the mundane machinery has hidden itself. Nothing in Mantua looks like an accident scene. It looks like doom arriving by courier, and it is remembered as doom arriving by courier, and the remembering is the fate reading's primary evidence. Strip the scene to its logistics and the doom resolves into a scheduling outcome: the true letter and the false report were dispatched into the same road network, one was detained by a health regulation and one was not, and the network delivered them in the order that killed. Order, not content, is the tragic variable. Had the report arrived a day later, or the letter a day sooner, the identical documents produce a reunion, and the identical Romeo, character, stars, and all, lives to be a husband in exile awaiting a pardon the Friar was already engineering.

The Mantua sequence, stripped to its logistics, expresses a postal asymmetry. Romeo's haste in acting on the news is real, and the time-and-haste study owns that tempo and its system. But haste acting on true news is a virtue, and Romeo had no channel through which truer news could reach him, because the channel was sitting in a sealed house in Verona explaining itself to a fellow friar. The counterfactual is brutal in its smallness: the difference between the tragedy as written and a difficult comedy of exile and reconciliation is the health status of one house that a travelling friar happened to step into before departure. Contingency does not get purer than that, and any verdict on the play's causes that cannot state that sentence plainly has declined to look at the mechanism.

### What exactly does Balthasar get wrong, if everything he reports is what he saw?

Nothing, at the level of observation, and everything, at the level of fact, and the distance between those two levels is the accident. Balthasar saw a funeral and reported a death; in a city where funerals certify deaths, the report was the evidence functioning normally.

His error is the plan's error reflected in a mirror: the feigned death was engineered to be indistinguishable from a real one, and the engineering held for every observer, including the one observer whose belief was fatal. The scene isolates a species of accident that neither fate nor choice vocabularies describe well, the epistemic accident, in which no event goes wrong and only knowledge does, because true premises were assembled by a mind missing the single fact that would invert their meaning. Verona's tragedy has epistemic accidents at every level, a marriage nobody may mention, a kinship nobody may declare, a sleep nobody may explain, and the tomb is simply the place where the last of them comes due.

## Minutes in the Tomb: The Last Near Miss at Act 5 Scene 3

The final accident is the smallest and the most famous: the matter of minutes by which Romeo's death precedes Juliet's waking. Its smallness is its meaning. Fate operates in the register of eternities, decrees laid down before birth; this ending is decided in the register of the corridor and the doorway, who arrives first, who is delayed by a conversation, who stops to fight whom in a churchyard. Paris is at the tomb because his private mourning has brought him there on that night with flowers and sweet water, a devotion nobody scheduled. Romeo fights him because two armed men meet in the dark at the door of a vault each believes the other is desecrating. The Friar, hurrying with his crowbar and spade, stumbles among the graves and arrives to find the first death accomplished and the second minutes away. Juliet wakes into the one configuration of the scene that cannot save her: a living rescuer offering flight, a dead husband offering example, and a watch approaching whose noise forecloses deliberation. The tomb scene's architecture, its procession of entries and its closing inquiry, belongs to the tomb scene's own article. The accident anatomy is a queue. Every person who enters the churchyard that night enters in the wrong order or at the wrong interval, and the play's last lesson in contingency is that order and interval, not intention, distribute the deaths.

The Friar's own summary, offered minutes later to the Prince, reaches for the largest available category: "A greater power than we can contradict / Hath thwarted our intents" (5.3.153-154). It is a beautiful sentence and an evasive one. What thwarted the intents, on the evidence the audience has watched, is a list: a plague house, a sealed door, a fast servant, a slow friar, a mourning count, a vial timed to a wedding that had been moved. The greater power is a sum of smaller obstructions wearing a theological coat, and the coat is cut to fit the speaker's need, since a thwarted intent implies an intender, and an intender can be blamed, appeased, or defied, while a list can only be read. The play ends with the list being read anyway, in the Friar's long narration and the inquiry that follows, and the fathers' reconciliation built on top of it. Verona settles for the theological coat in public and the list in the record. Criticism has mostly chosen as Verona chose. The ledger below reads the list.

### Why does Friar Laurence arrive at the tomb minutes too late, and what delayed him?

His delay is a braid of the same small stuff as everything else in the final movement. He learns of the letter's failure only when Friar John returns, which is to say after the plan's timetable has already been consumed by the detention he is hearing about.

He must then wait for night, since the rescue of a supposedly dead bride from her family's vault is an errand for darkness, and the play's own clock, the forty-two hours he prescribed, sets the waking for that night whether he is ready or not. On the way he is a man of nearly sixty crossing a churchyard in the dark with tools, and the text gives him a stumble among the graves for his entrance. Behind the braid sits the structural point: the plan had exactly one courier, one rescuer, and one timetable, a chain with no slack in any link, so that every delay anywhere in the system arrived at the tomb as his lateness. Systems with no slack convert accidents into outcomes with perfect efficiency, and the Friar had built such a system while preaching, in another scene, the doctrine of going slowly. The accident reading does not mock him for it. It notes that the play distributes its causation so widely that even the catastrophe's project manager experiences the ending as something that happened to his schedule.

One quantitative observation belongs in this section, because it disciplines the fate debate more than any speech. The ending's margin is measurable, and it is measured in minutes. Romeo is still warm when Juliet wakes; the Friar says so in substance as he urges her flight, and the watch's approach is audible within the same span. A fate that operates on a margin of minutes is not fate in any sense the word can bear, since fate's whole point is that margins do not matter. What operates on margins is logistics. The audience's tears at the tomb are shed over a scheduling failure so narrow that a delayed duel, a faster horse, a slower poison, a waking five minutes earlier, any of a dozen trivial perturbations, unwrites the scene. The play engineers that narrowness deliberately and then lets its characters call the result heaven's scourge. A criticism worthy of the engineering should keep both facts in view at once: the narrowness is designed, by the playwright, and it is accident, inside the world designed. Design at the level of authorship and accident at the level of events are not rivals. The second is how the first chooses to feel.

## The InsightCrunch Accident Ledger: Every Disaster, Sorted and Tested

The catalogue can now be assembled. The InsightCrunch accident ledger sorts the tragedy's disasters by the three tests used throughout this article: whether removal breaks the chain, whether any agent intended the outcome, and whether the collision was foreseeable by the people inside it. Three classes result. Fate, as a class, is retained in the ledger for completeness and receives no entries from the staged action: not one disaster in the play is shown to issue from a decree, an oracle, a horoscope, or a supernatural intervention of any kind, and the class stands empty as the ledger's loudest finding. Choice collects the disasters that agents aimed at or knowingly risked. Pure accident collects the collisions that nobody aimed at and nobody placed inside the scene could reasonably have foreseen, whose removal rewrites the ending. A middle register, accident riding on choice, holds the events where a chosen risk met an unintended geometry, and it is the register where honest sorting gets difficult and where the interesting criticism lives.

Read as a classroom handout, the ledger doubles as a revision instrument: each row is a claim that can be checked against the scene it cites, and a seminar that re-sorts even two rows has done more real work on the play's causation than a term of gesturing at the stars. The ledger's three-way sort differs from the fate ledger's in its question. The fate synthesis asked what governs the whole; the accident ledger asks what, concretely, would have to be different for nobody to die in the tomb, and its answer is embarrassingly small and entirely unastral.

## Comic Providence Turned Malignant: Susan Snyder and the Machinery of Mishap

The strongest critical account of the accident mechanism remains Susan Snyder's, in The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies. Snyder's structural insight is that the play is built on the chassis of festive comedy: a young pair blocked by old men, a clever go-between, a secret marriage, a potion, a feigned death, a tomb that should become a bridal chamber at the moment of recognition. In comedy, that chassis is driven by a benevolent randomness the tradition called providence. Storms scatter the right people onto the right islands; letters arrive at the dramatically perfect moment; twins and tokens surface exactly when identity needs them. Chance in comedy is design felt from below, the characters' word for the author's kindness. Snyder's tragedy is the same chassis with the kindness withdrawn at the joints. The devices still fire; they fire wrong. The go-between's message is the one that fails. The feigned death is believed by precisely the audience it had to fool and deceived precisely the one man it had to reach. The recognition scene in the tomb happens, complete with explanations, letters produced, and identities established, and it happens over corpses, after the interval in which recognition could have saved anyone has closed. Accident, on Snyder's account, is what comic providence becomes when the genre turns: not a different machinery, but the same machinery with its timing poisoned.

Snyder's account explains something the fate reading never can, which is the audience's peculiar sensation at the tomb, the feeling of having watched a rescue miss by inches rather than a doom descend from centuries. Comedies train that sensation in reverse: the near miss that resolves is comedy's heartbeat, and spectators carry the trained expectation into the tomb scene, where the play cashes it as horror. The Friar does arrive. The bride does wake. The explanation does get delivered and believed. Every element of the comic resolution is present and correctly performed, and the lovers are dead anyway, because the elements arrived in the wrong order and at the wrong intervals. Order and interval are accident's variables. A fate tragedy could not produce this sensation, since under fate the elements would never assemble at all, or their assembly would feel hollow. The play's ending feels like a machine that nearly worked, and the criticism that honours the feeling is the criticism that starts from the machine.

Where Snyder's account needs supplementing is at the level of the characters' inner weather. A genre mechanism explains why the plot's joints are cut as they are; it does not explain why everyone inside the plot keeps saying fortune. The period material supplies the missing layer. The first audiences did not need the playwright to tell them what to call a reversed errand and a sealed door; their culture's wheel vocabulary rushed in to name the mechanism from inside, and the characters speak that vocabulary natively. Snyder gives the tragedy's engineering; Kiefer's history gives its phenomenology, the felt experience of living under a power that raises and drops without consulting desert. The two accounts are usually shelved as rivals, mechanism against metaphysic. They are better read as descriptions of two storeys of the same building, the plot's construction and the inhabitants' weather report, and the adjudication below proceeds on that division of labour.

## Is Fortune a Form of Fate, or Fate's Opposite? The Disagreement Adjudicated

The brief's complication can now be faced directly, because the catalogue has done its work. Is fortune a form of fate, a department of the same determinism, or is fortune fate's opposite, the doctrine that nothing is determined at all? The critics divide in a way the evidence can judge. On one side stands the period reading that Kiefer documents and that the moralizing tradition behind the sources assumed: fortune as an instrument within a governed universe, the wheel as God's or fate's executive arm, raising and dropping souls for reasons hidden from them but real. On this reading the play's accidents are only apparent; underneath them an order operates, and the Friar's greater power is the correct name for it. On the other side stands Snyder's mechanical reading: the accidents are the substance, the order is the characters' retrospective story about them, and the play's design consists precisely in showing order-talk failing to match event. René Weis, as the play's modern editor, occupies the instructive middle: his notes on the crucial passages attend to the documentary specificity of the mechanisms, the quarto stage direction that puts the thrust under the arm, the plague procedure the searchers follow, as if to insist that the text's accidents are specified like facts, not waved at like symbols. An editor's duty is to the words, and the words keep specifying machinery.

The adjudication goes to Snyder on the mechanism and to Kiefer on the vocabulary, with the complication resolved as follows. Fortune is fate's opposite in function and fate's twin in rhetoric. In function, the two doctrines contradict: fate means the ending was fixed and the path is a delivery system; fortune means nothing was fixed and the path is a sequence of turns that could have gone otherwise at every joint, which is exactly what the removal test confirms at every joint of this plot. Remove the quarantine and the script, if there were one, should still find a way to kill the lovers; scripts are resourceful. The play offers no evidence of any such resourcefulness waiting in reserve. It offers one chain, visibly fragile, visibly contingent, and the fragility is the finding. In rhetoric, though, fortune-talk and fate-talk do identical work in a sufferer's mouth: both relocate the cause of a disaster outside the speaker, both convert a list of obstructions into a single addressable power, and both therefore console. "Unhappy fortune" and "a greater power" are the same gesture at different altitudes. The play lets its characters make the gesture and lets its audience watch the list underneath it, and the criticism that repeats the gesture without reading the list has sided with the characters against the play.

So the verdict on the brief's question, how much of the catastrophe is sheer bad luck, can be stated with the ledger's numbers behind it. The feud, the haste, the secrecy, and the plan are the catastrophe's standing conditions, all chosen, all blameworthy in their several ledgers, and the blame article maintains that accounting in full. The catastrophe itself, the conversion of those conditions into two bodies in a tomb on that particular night, is bad luck's work at every decisive joint. Four pure accidents stand in the chain; break any one of the final three and the lovers live; break the first and they never meet. Sheer bad luck is therefore not a garnish on a fated or a chosen tragedy. It is the tragedy's margin, the whole difference between the world where the standing conditions produced a scandal, an exile, and a hard marriage, and the world the audience watched, where they produced a double suicide and a pair of golden statues. Margins are where plays live. By the only measure that matters, whether the ending survives the removal of the factor, fortune in the sense of blind contingency decides this tragedy's fate more completely than the stars decide anything in it, and the star-crossed lovers of the poster are, on the evidence of the plot, the luck-crossed lovers of the ledger.

### How many accidents must align in sequence before the ending becomes unavoidable?

Three in the final movement, and the count is stricter than it sounds. The quarantine alone does not kill: it creates a window in which Mantua holds a false belief. The false report alone does not kill: had the letter already arrived, Balthasar's news is a puzzle Romeo resolves by riding to the Friar.

The tomb minutes alone do not kill: they execute a sentence the first two accidents wrote. The ending becomes unavoidable only when the chain closes, accident upon accident, each one foreclosing the rescue the previous one had left open. This sequential structure is why single-cause criticism keeps failing the play. Blame the Friar and the quarantine acquits him; blame the stars and the sealed door laughs at them; blame haste and the detention was not haste but its opposite, a man prevented from moving at all. The catastrophe is an alignment, and alignments are accident's masterpiece, the one configuration in which contingency produces something that looks, from behind, exactly like design.

## The Carriers of Accident: Minor Figures, Major Consequences

A structural peculiarity of the tragedy has gone underweighted in the criticism: the decisive accidents are almost all carried by figures at the edge of the action, men with a scene or a speech or less, who neither know the stakes nor survive in memory beside the lovers. The servingman with the guest list has no name in the early texts and no idea that his illiteracy is a plot engine. Friar John has one scene, most of it a travel report, and no motive, grievance, or flaw for a blame reading to grip. Balthasar has a handful of lines across two scenes and a ride. The searchers of the town do not appear at all; they are a procedure described at second hand, a door closing offstage. The play's causation runs through a relay of minor hands, and the relay is the meaning. In a fate tragedy the hinge figures are oracles, gods, witches, agents of the order that has decreed the end. In this tragedy the hinges are staff. The universe that kills the lovers is administered, and its administrators are clerks, couriers, and tradesmen who did their jobs with ordinary competence and went home.

The pattern explains a feature of the ending that fate readings struggle with: the total absence of a dischargeable villain. The Prince's closing inquiry, with its pardons and punishments left unspecified, is a court convened over a catastrophe whose causal chain contains almost no indictable link. The searchers cannot be summoned. Friar John can only repeat his errand. Balthasar can only repeat his eyesight. The play forces its own chief magistrate to distribute blame across a community, "all are punish'd," because the accident chain has laundered individual responsibility out of the final movement entirely. The blame debate, maintained in full in [the blame article](/2022/04/08/who-is-to-blame-romeo-and-juliet-deaths/), keeps circling this drain, and the accident ledger explains why the circling never ends. Blame requires a node where a different choice was available at reasonable cost. The final movement's nodes are a plague regulation, a servant's eyes, and a clock. None of them can be cross-examined.

## When Luck Ran the Other Way: The Accidents That Served the Lovers

Any honest ledger must record the accidents that helped, because their existence is the accident reading's best defence against the charge of selective counting. Contingency in the first two acts runs steadily in the lovers' favour, with the same mechanism and the opposite sign. The guest-list accident itself is the founding instance: blind chance, aimed at nothing, delivers Romeo to the one room in Verona where his life changes. Inside the feast, recognition by voice should have ended the adventure in blood, and instead Capulet's pride intervenes, an old man's vanity about his hospitality functioning as the lovers' unearned shield. The courtship then proceeds through a series of successful transmissions that the later acts will invert one by one. The Nurse's errands, for all their delay and comedy, arrive. The message about the wedding reaches its recipient. The secret ceremony is performed without discovery, in a city where the couple are the two most watched young people of the two most hostile houses. Read without knowledge of the ending, the first half of the play is a comedy in which luck is a member of the lovers' party, and the audience's retrospective knowledge that the luck will turn is supplied by the Prologue, not by the machinery, which gives no sign of changing its nature. It does not change its nature. It only changes its results, which is what makes it luck rather than a curse.

The potion itself is the supreme instance, and the criticism has persistently misfiled it. The Friar's draught is the one machine in the play that performs exactly to specification. Juliet drinks, sleeps the prescribed span, is mourned, is entombed, and wakes on schedule, every parameter as designed. Nothing in the chain of the potion's operation misfires, improvisation included: her terror soliloquy rehearses the waking-in-the-vault horror and the design answers it by sending the Friar to be present. The catastrophe enters the potion plot from outside it, through the letter, which is to say through a separate system, the postal one, failing on an unrelated errand. Two systems, the pharmaceutical and the postal, each functioning or failing on its own terms, intersect at Juliet's body, and the intersection is the tragedy. This is contingency in its purest dramatic form, and it is worth contrasting with the fate model's requirement. Under fate, the plan should fail because it was a plan against destiny, its flaw metaphysical. Under the accident model, the plan fails because a second system had a bad week, its flaw logistical, and the second account is the one the text bothers to dramatize, in a full scene, with named officers and a sealed door. The play spends its explanatory budget on logistics. Criticism should spend its attention where the play spent its budget.

The turning of the luck can be dated, and the date is instructive. The last favourable accident is Capulet's restraint at the feast, or perhaps the wedding's successful secrecy; the first lethal one is the thrust under the arm. Between them stands Mercutio's death, which the fate synthesis treats as the balance point between chance and choice, and which the accident ledger classes as the hinge where the sign flips. After 3.1, no accident in the play helps anyone the audience cares about. The wedding day is advanced into the plan's margin. The quarantine falls in the plan's window. The report outruns the letter. Paris chooses the fatal night for his devotions. The waking misses by minutes. A statistician would call the run unremarkable over enough trials; a dramatist calls it a second half. Snyder's genre account explains the flip as the comic matrix inverting, and the explanation holds, provided it is not mistaken for a force inside the fiction. Inside the fiction there is no flip, only a run, and the characters experience the run's second half with the wheel vocabulary the first half never needed. Nobody names fortune while the luck is good. The goddess is only ever invoked over losses, which is the surest sign that she is a bookkeeping device for pain rather than a power in the world the play builds.

## Bradley's Discomfort: Accident as a Critical Scandal

The accident reading has one famous enemy, and his eminence makes the disagreement worth adjudicating rather than merely reporting. A. C. Bradley, in Shakespearean Tragedy, famously declined to admit this play to the company of the great tragedies, and among his grounds the structure of the catastrophe bulked large: the ending turns on chance, on the failure of a message, in a way that the mature tragedies, where ruin ripens from character, do not permit. For Bradley the accident was an artistic immaturity, the young playwright leaning on machinery that the older artist of Hamlet and Lear had learned to refuse. The judgment set the terms for a century: accident in the catastrophe became the thing about the play that required apology, and fate, which at least sounded like design, became the polite alternative explanation, the reading that rescued the playwright from the charge of building his climax on a missed delivery. Much of the star-crossed orthodoxy in classrooms since then is Bradley's embarrassment wearing the Prologue's clothes.

The adjudication turns on whether the accident machinery is a failure of construction or a construction of a different kind. The textual evidence supports the second. A playwright leaning carelessly on chance does not write a full scene itemizing the chance's mechanism, with the companion errand, the sick-visiting, the suspicion, the sealed doors, and the returned letter flourished as proof. He does not plant a matching misdelivery in the second scene of the play, in comic register, so that the closing one rhymes. He does not give the catastrophe a margin of minutes and then have a character remark the warmth of the corpse's vicinity, the rescuer's stumble, the watch's approach, all the instruments of near-rescue calibrated and audible. The machinery is not leaned on; it is exhibited. Weis's editorial attention to the passages tells the same story from the documentary side: the accidents are among the most precisely specified events in the text, down to a stage direction about an arm. What Bradley priced as immaturity is better priced as a young tragedian's discovery, that a catastrophe assembled from innocent collisions produces a waste and a pity distinct from the waste of character tragedies, the pity of the avoidable, which no decree and no flaw can generate. The mature Shakespeare did not unlearn the discovery. The later tragedies keep their accidents, a handkerchief dropped at the wrong moment, a sea fight's outcome, a letter's delay in a storm; they merely learned to hide the machinery inside character so thoroughly that the seams stopped showing. The verdict on Bradley is therefore respectfully reversed: the accident is not the play's apology. It is the play's invention, and its long critical afterlife as a scandal measures how completely the fate poster succeeded in covering the machine it advertised.

One more witness should be heard before the FAQs, because the play itself keeps the most honest score. Juliet, in the potion soliloquy, imagines the plan's failure modes with terrible completeness: suffocation in the vault, madness among the bones, waking before Romeo comes. She prices every risk internal to the design. The risk that destroys the design, a detained courier, is the one she cannot imagine, because it lives in a different system from every risk she rehearses. The play's finest intelligence audits the plan and misses the accident, not from folly but from the structure of foresight itself, which scans the system it knows. That is the tragedy's final word on contingency, delivered before the catastrophe begins: accident is not the risk one failed to take seriously. Accident is the risk that was standing outside the room where the risks were being listed, in a sealed house, behind a door with a mark on it.

## The Advanced Wedding: How a Choice Armed the Accidents

Between the choice class and the accident class the ledger keeps a border, and the border's most important crossing is Capulet's decision to advance the Paris wedding. The sequence deserves precision. In Act 3 Scene 4, hours after Tybalt's death, Capulet fixes the marriage for Thursday, having first mused that Wednesday is too soon. In Act 4 Scene 2, presented with Juliet's feigned repentance, he advances the ceremony to Wednesday morning out of sheer relieved delight, and the household's wedding machinery, the cooking, the music, the guests, compresses itself into a single frantic night. The deadline mechanics of the play, the forty-two hours and the diary of the five days, belong to the timeline and haste studies. The accident anatomy of the decision is different and simpler: the advancement removed the plan's slack. The Friar's schedule had been built against the Thursday date, with the letter timed to reach Mantua, the waking timed to the vault's occupancy, and a margin for ordinary friction. The Wednesday wedding consumed the margin before the quarantine consumed anything, so that a detention which the Thursday timetable might have absorbed became, against the Wednesday timetable, exactly fatal. No accident in the final movement works alone. Each works against a window that a grieving father's improvisation had already narrowed, which is why the ledger refuses to class the ending as pure accident in the aggregate even while classing its decisive joints as pure accidents one by one. Contingency kills by fitting into spaces that choices have left for it, and the fit here is machined to the day.

The decision's motive adds a second layer of the accidental, this one epistemic. Capulet advances the wedding because he reads his daughter's weeping as grief for Tybalt and prescribes marriage as its cure, telling Paris that her sorrow has made her desperate and that the match will staunch it. The weeping is for Romeo. The father's diagnosis is wrong in its object, wrong in its remedy, and delivered with total confidence by the person with the most authority to act on it, which makes the advancement an accident of knowledge wearing the costume of a choice. The ledger keeps it in the choice class because Capulet aimed at the outcome he got, a Wednesday wedding; but its placement there should carry the asterisk this paragraph supplies. Verona's catastrophe is assembled from such hybrids at every level: intentions built on misread evidence, executing flawlessly, into a world whose other systems are failing on unrelated errands. A criticism that sorts only by intention will call the ending chosen; a criticism that sorts only by outcome will call it fated; the accident reading sorts by mechanism and finds the hybrids, which are the truth of the matter and the reason the play's causation has resisted two simpler vocabularies for four centuries.

## Misadventure: The Word Hidden Inside the Prologue

The frame's own diction has been hiding the accident reading in plain sight since the first fourteen lines. The Prologue's second quatrain promises the machinery of the stars, "star-cross'd" and "death-mark'd," and then, in the same breath, describes the lovers' story as their "misadventur'd piteous overthrows" (Prologue 7). Misadventure is not a fate word. In the legal English of the period it is close to a technical term: death by misadventure was the coroner's category for a death that occurred accidentally in the course of a lawful act, distinguished precisely from felony and from natural causes, a verdict that assigned the death to chance and acquitted every hand involved. The Prologue, the play's most fate-flavoured utterance, files its own catastrophe under the coroner's accident category before the first scene begins. Audiences hear the stars because the stars are loud. The misadventure sits in the line at the volume of a clerk entering a verdict, and the whole tragedy can be read as the slow amplification of that quieter word until, by Act 5, it is the only word the evidence supports.

The distribution of the play's causal vocabulary across its speakers confirms the frame's split allegiance. Fate language concentrates in mouths that are summarizing, framing, or excusing: the Chorus before the fact, the Friar after it, the Prince over the bodies. Fortune language concentrates in mouths that are inside the event and still hoping to influence it: Romeo over Mercutio, Juliet over the banishment, Laurence over the returned letter. Chance, the accurate term, is the one nobody reaches for at the decisive moments, because chance offers the speaker nothing: no power to defy, no goddess to petition, no order to submit to, only a list of collisions and the speaker's own name somewhere in it. The play's rhetoric thus performs the very evasion its plot corrects. Every character who explains the catastrophe upgrades accident into fortune or fate; the plot, which never speaks in its own voice, keeps presenting the unupgraded evidence, sealed doors, fast servants, narrow minutes, and letting the gap between the explanations and the evidence widen until the audience is standing in it. That gap is where this article has tried to stand throughout, and the ledger is its instrument, since a ledger cannot upgrade anything. It can only sort, test, and total, and its total is the verdict the speeches keep avoiding: the lovers were not singled out by heaven. They were failed, in sequence, by a guest list, a health regulation, a postal order, and a clock, in a city whose hatred had made sure that failure would be enough.

## An Education in Near Misses: How the Play Trains Its Audience in Accident

The catastrophe's accidents land with their full force because the play has spent its first half educating the audience in the class they belong to, through a curriculum of near misses that all resolve harmlessly. Romeo is recognized at the feast, and survives the recognition. The orchard courtship proceeds within earshot of a great house, and is not overheard by anyone who matters. The Nurse's errand dawdles for hours, and the delay costs nothing but temper. The wedding is arranged, performed, and concealed inside a single day in a feuding city, and no leak springs. Even the parting at dawn, lark against nightingale, resolves in an escape down a ladder with seconds to spare, Romeo gone before the chamber fills. Each episode teaches the same lesson in the grammar of comedy: this world generates collisions constantly, and the collisions glance off. The audience learns the base rate. Collisions are frequent, survivable, and often secretly helpful, and the lesson is not a lie, since the base rate inside the fiction genuinely is benign until it is not.

The education is what separates the accident tragedy from a mere string of misfortunes, and it explains the peculiar critical history in which the ending's machinery kept being mistaken for fate. A run of benign collisions followed by a run of lethal ones, with the mechanism constant throughout, creates in retrospect the illusion of a pattern tightening, of a net that was always closing. The illusion is powerful because it is the same illusion the characters suffer from: Romeo reads his dream, the Friar reads his greater power, the Prince reads his scourge, all of them converting a change in outcomes into a change in the world's government. The audience's disciplined response, the one the ledger enforces, is to hold the mechanism constant and let the outcomes vary, which is the statistical posture and also, not coincidentally, the posture of the play's clearest thinker. Juliet audits the potion plan for failure modes inside the system she knows and accepts the residue she cannot audit. She is the only major character who behaves as if the world runs on contingency rather than on addressed powers, petitioning Fortune once, in a figure of speech, and thereafter making plans. The plans were good. The residue killed her. A tragedy that can state that sentence in its heroine's conduct has no need of stars, and the stars' persistence in its reception history is a measure of how much more comfortable an addressed power is, even a hostile one, than a residue that nobody audits and nobody sends.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why does Friar Laurence cry "Unhappy fortune!" at the moment the letter fails?

The cry is the sound of a specific mechanism being upgraded into a goddess. What Laurence has just heard is institutional and mundane: a companion errand, a house under suspicion of plague, searchers doing their duty, a door sealed, a courier detained and released too late. Nothing in the report has a face. "Unhappy fortune!" (5.2.17) gives the failure a face and an address, converting a chain of offices and accidents into a single power that can be lamented in one breath. The naming also performs quieter work for the speaker. If fortune neglected the letter, then the plan's architect, who used one courier and built no redundancy into the one errand the plan could not survive losing, stands at one remove from the failure. The article's reading does not accuse the Friar of bad faith; the disproportion he feels is real. It notes only that his mouth says goddess while his feet, seizing a crowbar and hurrying to the tomb, answer a logistics problem, and that the play repeats that split in nearly every character who names the catastrophe.

### Q: What is the Wheel of Fortune, and what would the play's first audiences have pictured when a character named Fortune?

The first audiences pictured a goddess turning a great wheel with human lives strapped to its rim: kings riding at the top, beggars crushed at the bottom, every position temporary because the turning never stops. The image came down through Boethius and the de casibus tradition of falls of great men, and it carried a precise doctrine inside the picture. Fortune distributes rises and reversals without consulting desert, so the fall of the mighty proves nothing about their sins and the rise of the lowly proves nothing about their merits. Movement is the only law. Inside that picture, naming Fortune was not a vague gesture at doom. It was a specific claim that an outcome belonged to the turning rather than to a plan, a judgment, or a star chart, and it implied a specific consolation: what the wheel took in one turn it might return in another, which is why Juliet can petition the goddess to be fickle in her favour. Fate allows no petitions. The wheel invites them, because a turning power can always turn again.

### Q: How does Boethius's Fortune differ from the fate the Prologue announces?

Boethius separates the two with care. Fate, in the Consolation of Philosophy, is the temporal unfolding of a providential order, events arriving in sequence as a fixed whole is delivered into time. Fortune is something else: the name for how that delivery feels to creatures inside the sequence who cannot see the whole, and in the goddess's own defence she claims no order at all, only rotation. The Prologue announces the fate side of the distinction, a story settled before it starts, its end spoken aloud in a sonnet. The plot keeps delivering the Fortune side: outcomes that arrive as turns, reversible in principle at every point, whose pattern is visible only backward and whose authors keep turning out to be errands, officers, and clocks. The difference matters practically. Under the Prologue's doctrine the sensible posture is submission, since margins cannot matter to a settled story. Under Boethius's Fortune the sensible posture is vigilance about margins, since everything rides on them. The characters who survive longest in the play's world, and Juliet above all, behave by the second doctrine while the first is being read aloud over their heads.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch accident ledger, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch accident ledger is this article's sorting instrument: a table of the tragedy's thirteen decisive turns, from the guest list to the waking in the tomb, each classified as fate, choice, accident riding on choice, or pure accident, and each tested by removal, so that striking the event shows whether the catastrophe survives it. The pattern it exposes is a distribution no single speech in the play ever states. The fate class stands empty; not one staged disaster issues from a decree, a horoscope, or a supernatural hand. Choices cluster in the middle of the plot, the feud's arson and the lovers' engineering, where they build the standing conditions. Pure accidents cluster at the two hinges where the story's existence is decided, the meeting and the catastrophe, and the final movement alone requires three of them in sequence. The ledger's pattern is therefore the article's verdict in tabular form: conditions are chosen, outcomes are collided into, and the difference between a scandal and a double suicide in Verona is a stack of mishaps that no one in the play ever totals up.

### Q: How does Susan Snyder's account of comic providence explain why the same mishaps turn fatal here?

Snyder's argument, in The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies, is that the play is built on comedy's chassis: blocked lovers, a go-between, a secret marriage, a potion, a feigned death, a recognition scene. In comedy that chassis is driven by a benevolent randomness, where storms, twins, tokens, and mistimed letters keep rescuing the young from the old, and the audience learns to trust the machinery. The mishaps are identical in kind in the tragedy; what changes is their polarity at the joints. The letter that should unite is the letter that fails. The feigned death is believed by the one man it had to inform. The recognition in the tomb is complete, accurate, and minutes too late to be anything but an inquest. Accident, on this reading, is comic providence with its timing poisoned, the same devices firing in the wrong order and at the wrong intervals. The account explains the audience's distinctive grief at the ending, which is not the grief of watching doom descend but the grief of watching a rescue miss, staged by a playwright who trained the expectation of rescue in the play's own first half.

### Q: Where does the accident ledger place the guest-list errand, and how does its class differ from the quarantine's?

The ledger places both in the pure accident class, and the kinship is the point: the tragedy's first and last great turns are the same event, a text entrusted to a carrier whose errand fails. The difference lies in the direction of the failure and in what each failure delivers. The servingman in Act 1 Scene 2 cannot read, so the document escapes its intended route and lands before the one pair of eyes in Verona that turns an invitation into a destiny; the failure is one of delivery outward, and it gives. The quarantine in Act 5 seals the document inside its route entirely; Friar John carries the letter faithfully and brings it back undelivered, a failure of containment, and it takes. One mishap misdirects a text toward its most consequential reader, the other prevents a text from reaching the only reader who could act on it, and between those two postal failures the entire play happens. Sorting them into one class is not a dismissal of their difference. It is the recognition that the play's causation is postal from first to last: whoever controls the movement of writings controls Verona, and no one controls it.

### Q: What makes Balthasar's report an accident rather than a lie or a betrayal?

A lie requires a speaker who knows the truth and intends deception; a betrayal requires a trust knowingly broken. Balthasar has neither knowledge nor intent to offer either charge. He stood in the churchyard, watched Juliet's body carried into the Capulet vault with full funeral ceremony, rode to Mantua at speed, and reported exactly what a competent eyewitness should report: "Her body sleeps in Capel's monument" (5.1.17). The falsity of his news lives in the inference the evidence compels, that the sleeping body is a dead one, and that inference was drawn by the whole city, including the family that staged the funeral. His report is the potion plan succeeding perfectly and arriving at the wrong address, since the plan required everyone to believe Juliet dead and neglected to exempt the one observer whose belief would be fatal. The ledger therefore classes the report as a pure accident of knowledge: no event went wrong, no agent failed a duty, and only the assembly of true premises in a mind missing the inverting fact produced the catastrophe's trigger. It is the tragedy's cleanest proof that disaster needs no villain and no liar, only a gap.

### Q: Why is Paris at the tomb on the one night Romeo returns, and what class of accident is his presence?

Paris is at the tomb because his mourning has made a ritual of the place. He comes by night with flowers and sweet water to strew the bridal bed that became a grave, a devotion the play shows as habitual rather than arranged for that evening, and nothing in his errand knows anything about Romeo, Mantua, or a waking scheduled for the same hours. Two private griefs, each intelligible and each blameless, arrive at the same door on the same night, and the meeting converts instantly into a duel because each man reads the other through the feud's categories: desecrator, villain, enemy. The ledger classes the presence as pure accident, the intersection of independent chains, while the duel that follows it belongs to choice, since Romeo warns his opponent off and Paris insists on an arrest he has no power to complete. The distinction matters to the ending's arithmetic. Paris's body is not the catastrophe, but the minutes his resistance consumed belong to the narrow margin inside which Juliet's waking missed her husband alive, so the accident of his presence taxes the ending twice, once in blood and once in time.

### Q: Does Juliet treat Fortune as a person she can bargain with in Act 3 Scene 2?

She does, and the bargaining is doctrinally exact. Hearing of Tybalt's death and Romeo's banishment, she addresses the goddess directly: "O Fortune, Fortune! all men call thee fickle; / If thou art fickle, what dost thou with him / That is renown'd for faith? Be fickle, Fortune, / For then, I hope, thou wilt not keep him long, / But send him back" (3.2.60-64). The logic is wheel logic at its most sophisticated. Fortune's defining vice, inconstancy, is converted into the petition's ground: a constant power that exiled Romeo would keep him, while a fickle one may return him by the same arbitrariness that took him. Juliet does not plead desert, accuse the goddess of injustice, or submit to a decree, the three postures that fate and providence invite. She negotiates with a turning power for another turn. Set beside Romeo's use of the same word as a retrospective alibi, her usage marks the difference between the lovers' metaphysics: he names fortune over what has finished, she bargains with it over what might still move, and the play's plot, in which everything does keep moving and nothing is settled until the tomb, is on her side of the argument.

### Q: Where does a coincidence end and an accident begin in the play's chain of events?

A coincidence is an intersection: two causal chains, each lawful on its own terms, arriving at the same place at the same time. An accident is a coincidence with damage in it, an intersection whose outcome nobody intended and somebody pays for. The distinction sounds verbal and does heavy work in the ledger, because most of the play's coincidences are harmless or helpful and only some convert. A servant needing a reader and a lovelorn Montague crossing the same street is a coincidence; it becomes the founding accident only in retrospect, when its cost is known. A friar visiting the sick while carrying a letter is a coincidence of errands; the sealed door converts it into the catastrophe's central accident. Sorting by conversion, the point at which an intersection acquires a price, isolates the decisive turns and reveals their rarity: of the hundreds of meetings in the play, a handful convert, and those few are spaced exactly where the plot's existence is at stake. The play's subject is not that coincidences happen. It is that conversion is a matter of timing and stakes, both of which the feud had already set to lethal.

### Q: Why does Friar John seek a companion before travelling, and how does that errand become the hinge of the ending?

Friar John sought a companion because the rule of his order and the customs of travel required one: he went "to find a barefoot brother out, / One of our order, to associate me" (5.2.5-6), and found the brother in a house where the sick were being visited and plague was suspected. The searchers sealed that house with both men inside it. The hinge quality of the errand lies in its complete ordinariness at every step. No haste, no negligence, no shortcut: a procedural requirement, conscientiously met, walked the letter into the one building in Verona that the authorities were about to close. The catastrophe required the intersection of a rule, a sickbed, and a suspicion, and the play specifies all three with a clerk's patience, as if daring the audience to find the fated part. The errand also exposes the plan's hidden dependency: the Friar's design outsourced its most time-sensitive step to a companion-requirement he did not control and a public-health regime he could not predict, which is why the accident reading classes the quarantine as accident with a human margin rather than as anyone's blunder.

### Q: Why are the play's decisive accidents carried by men who do not know the weight of what they carry?

Because ignorance is what makes the accidents structurally reliable. A carrier who knew the stakes would behave differently: the servingman who understood the guest list would find a Capulet reader, Friar John, told the letter held two lives, would have fought the searchers or found a smuggler's route out of the sealed house, and Balthasar, let in on the potion, would have reported a sleep instead of a death. The plot protects its coincidences by distributing knowledge so that no carrier holds enough of it to interfere, and the distribution is also the play's social observation. Verona's knowledge, like its violence, is compartmented by household, order, and rank, and the compartments are the secrecy the lovers and the Friar chose for protection. The accident chain is therefore not an external misfortune that befalls the design from outside. It is the design's own secrecy returning as fate's disguise: every safeguard that kept the marriage and the plan hidden also guaranteed that the people moving the decisive objects would move them blind. The stars take the credit in the speeches. The compartments do the work in the plot, and a reader who totals the compartments has explained the ending without remainder.

### Q: Why does the accident ledger refuse to class Mercutio's death as pure accident?

Because the death sits on chosen ground. Men drew swords in a public place, on a hot day, inside a feud whose rules everyone present understood, after warnings from Benvolio that the Capulets were abroad and the day was primed for a brawl. That a wound would result from such a scene was foreseeable in kind, and the ledger's choice component records the foreseeability honestly. What was not foreseeable, and not intended by any party, was the wound's geometry and its outcome: Tybalt's thrust passing under the arm of a peacemaker who had stepped between the fencers, a scratch by its victim's own joking measure, which a world without antisepsis converted into a death within the hour. Pure accident in the ledger's terms requires the decisive particular to fail both the intention and the foreseeability tests, and Mercutio's death passes neither cleanly: intended as a duel, unintended as a killing, foreseen as a risk, unforeseen as a screened thrust at a third body. It therefore stands in the middle register, accident riding on choice, as the ledger's hardest and most instructive row, the event where the play's two causal systems are fused in a single motion of an arm.

### Q: Does the play contain accidents that work in the lovers' favour, and when do they stop?

It contains a run of them, and the run is the first half of the play. The guest-list failure delivers Romeo to the feast. Capulet's pride shields the recognized intruder. The orchard courtship goes unheard, the Nurse's errands arrive, the secret wedding is performed and concealed in a feuding city, and the dawn parting resolves with seconds to spare. Read forward without the Prologue, the machinery of coincidence is the lovers' ally throughout, functioning exactly as comic providence functions in the tradition Snyder describes. The run stops at the duel in Act 3 Scene 1, and after Mercutio's death no accident in the play benefits anyone the audience is attached to: the wedding advances into the plan's margin, the quarantine falls inside its window, the false report outruns the true letter, Paris keeps his vigil on the fatal night, the waking misses by minutes. The ledger draws no metaphysical conclusion from the turn, and that restraint is the accident reading's discipline. A curse would imply a curser. A run is only a run, and the play's refusal to supply a power behind the change of sign is the strongest internal evidence that none is operating.

### Q: What separates the Friar's potion plan from an accident, when its success also hangs on timing?

Agency and specification. The plan is a designed instrument with named parameters: a distilled liquor, a deathlike trance of two and forty hours, a vault, a letter, a rescuer waiting at the waking. Its author chose it over alternatives, briefed its subject on its operation, and rehearsed its failure modes with her, which is the opposite of an accident's profile on every count. Where the plan touches timing, the timing is internal and calibrated, the potion performing, in the event, exactly to specification; Juliet sleeps the prescribed span and wakes on schedule in the presence of the appointed rescuer. The catastrophe enters from a system the plan did not contain, the postal one, whose failure was no part of the design's parameters and no failure of its chemistry. Sorting the plan as a choice, structured as a gamble, keeps two errors at bay. It refuses the blame shortcut that dissolves a reasonable design into its unlucky outcome, and it refuses the fate shortcut that treats the plan's collision with an unrelated system as the design's secret meaning. The plan was a good instrument destroyed by weather in a neighbouring system, and the ledger's classes exist to say exactly that.

### Q: If Fortune is fate's opposite, why do the characters reach for both words at the same moments?

Because the two words do the same job in a sufferer's mouth even though they contradict each other in a philosopher's treatise. Fate supplies a plan, fortune supplies a wheel, and either supply converts a list of collisions into a single addressed power, which is what grief wants: something to blame, defy, or bargain with. The Friar moves between the two registers within a single scene, crying fortune over the returned letter and then reporting a greater power over the tomb, because each register answers a different pressure, the letter's need for a name and the inquiry's need for a verdict. The play lets the contradiction stand in the speakers rather than resolving it in the action, and that standing contradiction is itself evidence. A universe governed by one doctrine would not need two vocabularies at its catastrophes. A universe governed by collisions produces both, since every sufferer improvises the explanation that lets him go on speaking. The ledger's job is to refuse the improvisation and keep the list, where the contradiction dissolves into one class: accident.

### Q: Once the accidents are counted, what is left of the Prologue's claim to explain the play?

Less than its fame suggests, and more than nothing, which is the honest remainder. The Prologue predicts the outcome accurately: the lovers die, the parents' strife is buried by the deaths, the two hours of traffic deliver a finished tragedy. A prediction that comes true is not automatically an explanation, and the play's action supplies the mechanism the Prologue withholds, a mechanism of errands, officers, and minutes that no astrological claim could have forecast in detail. What remains of the Prologue's claim is a statement about genre and audience knowledge rather than about causation inside the fiction: the Chorus tells the ending so the audience can watch the collisions knowing where they land, which converts suspense about the outcome into suspense about the mechanism. Readers who want the full debate about whether fate is real in the play or a rhetorical device can pursue it in [the is-fate-real article](/2030/11/08/is-fate-real-or-just-rhetoric/), which weighs the star vocabulary against the staged machinery. This article's contribution is the count: a frame that announces stars and a plot that runs on letters is a frame whose authority the plot has quietly withdrawn.

### Q: How many accidents in the final movement must happen in sequence, and what happens to the ending if any one is removed?

Three, and the chain's fragility is the accident reading's strongest exhibit. Remove the quarantine and the letter reaches Mantua, Romeo rides to the tomb as a rescuer, and the potion plot completes as designed. Keep the quarantine but reverse the postal order, letting the Friar's explanation arrive even a day before Balthasar's report, and Romeo's ride is to the Friar's cell for instructions rather than to the Apothecary for poison. Keep both accidents and remove the tomb's timing, letting Juliet wake before the draught is drunk, and the scene becomes a hurried flight from the watch rather than a double death. Each accident alone leaves a rescue route open, which is why the ending feels designed in retrospect: three independent failures closing three independent exits looks like a net. Looked at forward, as the characters live it, the three failures are unrelated events in unrelated systems, a health regulation, a postal asymmetry, a sleeping draught's timetable, and their alignment is the one configuration that contingency is supposed to produce occasionally. That it produced it on the final night is the tragedy, not a proof of design.

### Q: What does Frederick Kiefer's history of fortune add that a modern talk of bad luck misses?

Kiefer's Fortune and Elizabethan Tragedy restores the word's period weight. Modern talk of bad luck treats fortune as a shrug, a synonym for randomness with no content. For the first audiences, fortune was a doctrinally serious power with an iconography, a literature of falls, and a specific function: to explain reversals that neither merit nor providence could account for, without surrendering the universe to mere chaos. Kiefer shows dramatists and audiences treating the wheel as an operative explanation for why the deserving fall and the unworthy rise, which changes how the play's fortune speeches should be heard. When Romeo says he is fortune's fool, a modern ear hears self-pity; a period ear hears a man citing the reigning theory of reversal, a theory his audience had watched turn princes into ghosts in the Mirror for Magistrates. Kiefer's limit, in this article's adjudication, is that period belief describes the characters' weather, not the play's machinery. The wheel explains why the characters reach for the word. It does not explain the sealed door, which remains, stubbornly, a door.

### Q: How should an essay use the accident ledger to argue that the catastrophe is contingent without calling it meaningless?

Build the argument on the removal test rather than on adjectives. Open with the thesis that the catastrophe's conditions are chosen and its outcome is collided into, then work the ledger row by row, showing for each decisive accident that striking it rewrites the ending, and for each choice that keeping it was necessary but insufficient. The structure defeats both rival readings at once: the fate reading fails at the quarantine, where no star is consulted, and the pure-blame reading fails at Friar John, where no agent's choice explains the sealed door. Conclude by answering the natural objection, that contingency sounds like meaninglessness, with the ledger's second finding, which is that the accidents are meaningful in the other sense: they expose Verona's systems, its postal routes, its health regulations, its compartmented knowledge, as the true machinery of the tragedy, and a society whose offices can kill without anyone's intention is a subject worth a tragedy. An essay that ends there has argued contingency and meaning together, exactly as the play does, and it has done so without asking the stars to do any work they never perform on stage.
