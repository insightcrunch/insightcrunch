The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights, known everywhere by its initials as the NCPCR, is the institution to which India directs its outrage when a child is failed by the systems built to protect that child, and it is almost invariably described in terms the statute never gave it. It is called upon to order, to direct, to punish and to rescue, as though it were a court, a police force and a ministry compressed into one body seated in Delhi. The Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, which created it, built something quite different: a body that can summon any witness in the country, compel the production of any document, inspect any institution in which children are lodged, and inquire into any violation of a child's rights on its own motion, and that can then do exactly three things with what it finds, every one of which stops short of command. It may recommend that a government prosecute. It may recommend that a government grant interim relief. Or it may go to a constitutional court and ask the court to issue the order it cannot issue itself. Everything else the public believes about this institution is expectation laid over a design that was never drawn to carry it.

![The NCPCR and the child rights commissions of India, an institution profile of a commission that holds civil court powers of inquiry without any power to issue a binding order - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That gap between expectation and design is the subject of this profile, and it is best stated as the institution's namable claim at the outset. The child rights commissions are bodies that can compel evidence and not outcomes. Within an inquiry, their authority is real and coercive: attendance can be enforced, testimony taken on oath, records requisitioned from any court or office. At the end of the inquiry, their authority dissolves into recommendation, and the government on the receiving end is obliged to do one thing only, which is to explain itself in writing if it chooses not to comply. Almost every criticism levelled at these commissions, that they failed to act, that their findings changed nothing, that a report gathered dust, is in substance a criticism of a choice Parliament made in the statute, attributed instead to the body that merely exercised the powers the statute conferred. The distinction matters because it directs the remedy. If the problem is design, the answer lies in amendment. If the problem is that the designed machinery was never staffed, funded or even constituted in much of the country, the answer lies elsewhere, and this profile will argue that the second failure is the larger one, because a recommendatory body that exists everywhere and reports relentlessly is a different institution in practice from a recommendatory body that exists on paper in a third of the jurisdictions it was meant to cover.

## The road to a commission: from the Convention to the Act of 2005

The commissions were not an indigenous bureaucratic invention. They were the institutional residue of a treaty. India acceded to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child on 11 December 1992, and the preamble of the Act of 2005 recites that accession, along with India's participation in the 1990 World Summit for Children and its Declaration on the Survival, Protection and Development of Children, the adoption of the National Charter for Children in 2003, and the outcome document titled A World Fit for Children adopted by the United Nations General Assembly Special Session on Children in May 2002. The Convention obliged its parties to take all appropriate legislative, administrative and other measures for the implementation of the rights it recognised, and the committee that monitors the Convention pressed states to build independent national institutions for children, bodies that could watch the state's own machinery from outside it. A commission was the Indian answer to that pressure: not a department of the government that ran the schools, homes and hospitals, but a statutory outsider charged with examining how that machinery treated children.

The drafting history carries one name worth recording. Before the Act of 2005 there was a draft National Commission for Children Bill prepared in 2000 by an expert committee chaired by Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer, and the eventual statute grew out of that exercise, although it emerged five years later and in a different shape, as a statute creating two tiers of commissions and a set of Children's Courts rather than a single national body alone. The bill that became the Act was passed by Parliament in December 2005, and the Act received the assent of the President on 20 January 2006. That sequence of dates explains the feature of this statute that confuses more readers than any other, and which this series states precisely wherever it appears: the statute is called the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005, because that is the year of its passage and the year written into its short title, but it is Act 4 of 2006 in the official numbering of Indian legislation, because an Act takes its number in the Gazette sequence of the year in which it is assented to and published, and the assent fell in January 2006. Neither date is an error. The 2005 in the name and the 2006 in the number are both correct, each recording a different event in the statute's passage.

Assent did not bring the Act into force. Section 1 left commencement to a date the Central Government would appoint by notification in the Official Gazette, and the rules for the National Commission were framed during 2006. The Commission itself was constituted on 5 March 2007, more than a year after the statute that created it had been enacted. That interval between enactment and existence is the first appearance of the pattern this profile returns to repeatedly, because the same interval, stretched far longer, is the story of the state commissions. Its first Chairperson was Shantha Sinha, a professor of political science at the University of Hyderabad who had founded the Mamidipudi Venkatarangaiya Foundation and spent decades moving children out of labour and into schools in Andhra Pradesh, work for which she received the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 2003. She held the office for two consecutive terms, from 2007 to 2013, which is the maximum the statute permits, and the institution's early character, insistently field-based, sceptical of official returns, and inclined to treat the out-of-school child as the measure of every other failure, bears the mark of that tenure.

The definition on which the whole institution rests is tucked into Section 2 of the Act, and it is broader than most readers expect. The Act does not define a child by age in its own text at all. It defines child rights to include the children's rights adopted in the Convention on the Rights of the Child, adopted on 20 November 1989 and acceded to by India on 11 December 1992. The age boundary of eighteen therefore enters the commissions' jurisdiction through the Convention, which treats a child as a person below eighteen, rather than through a domestic definition clause, and the commissions' working universe is every person below that line. The wider architecture into which this body fits, the statutes that define childhood differently for different harms and the committees and boards that decide individual cases district by district, is mapped in [the guide to child protection laws in India](/2016/04/01/child-protection-laws-india-guide/), which owns the field as a whole; this profile owns the commissions themselves, their powers, and the limits written into those powers.

## Composition: seven people, six fields, and an appointment process that decides more than the powers do

Section 3 of the Act directs the Central Government, by notification, to constitute the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights. The Commission consists of a Chairperson and six Members. The Chairperson must be a person of eminence who has done outstanding work for promoting the welfare of children, a qualification framed entirely in terms of reputation and record rather than office or profession. The six Members must be appointed from amongst persons of eminence, ability, integrity, standing and experience in six named fields, and the fields are the statute's own map of what childhood involves: education; child health, care, welfare or child development; juvenile justice or care of neglected or marginalised children or children with disabilities; elimination of child labour or children in distress; child psychology or sociology; and laws relating to children. At least two of the six Members must be women. The composition is therefore deliberately multidisciplinary, and it is worth pausing on what it does not require. No seat is reserved for a judge. No seat is reserved for a civil servant, a police officer, a medical professional as such, or a representative of state governments, whose machinery the Commission exists to examine. Parliament designed a body of eminent outsiders drawn from the fields that study and serve children, and it left the state's own administrative cast outside the room.

The appointment route in Section 4 is where institutional profiles usually find the real story, and this one is no exception. The Chairperson and Members are appointed by the Central Government by notification. For the Chairperson, the Act adds a filter: the appointment is to be made on the recommendation of a three-member Selection Committee constituted by the Central Government under the chairmanship of the Minister in charge of the Ministry or Department of Women and Child Development, the Commission's own administrative ministry. The filter matters, and so does its history. As enacted, the committee was to be chaired by the Minister in charge of the Ministry of Human Resource Development; an amending Act, Act 4 of 2007, substituted the Women and Child Development ministry, with effect from 29 December 2006, before the Commission had even been constituted, tracking the movement of the child portfolio between ministries. Members, unlike the Chairperson, carry no statutory selection committee at all in the text; their appointment rests with the government of the day, guided by the eminence criteria in Section 3. The structural consequence deserves to be stated plainly, because it conditions everything said later about independence. Every person who sits on this Commission owes the appointment to the executive, the committee that selects its head is chaired by the minister whose department the Commission must monitor, and the only counterweights the statute supplies are the eminence qualifications, a fixed tenure, and the publicity of the Commission's reports. Whether those counterweights suffice is a question this profile takes up in the comparison and the debate below; the point here is architectural, that the design chose eminence and tenure as its safeguards rather than an insulated selection process.

### Is the NCPCR part of the government?

No. The Commission is a statutory body created by an Act of Parliament, not a department of the Ministry of Women and Child Development, although that ministry is its administrative ministry. The government appoints its members, funds it by grants and supplies its secretariat, but fixed tenure and reports laid before Parliament give it an institutional life of its own.

The tenure provisions of Section 5 are short and strict. The Chairperson and every Member hold office for a term of three years from the date of assuming office. No person may hold the office for more than two terms, so the maximum possible service is six years, the span Shantha Sinha completed. Age ceilings run alongside the terms: a Chairperson may not hold office after attaining sixty-five years, and a Member may not hold office after attaining sixty years, so a term can be cut short by the calendar of birth as well as by the calendar of appointment. Resignation is by writing under the member's hand addressed to the Central Government. Section 6 leaves salary, allowances and conditions of service to be prescribed by the Central Government, with a protective proviso that they may not be varied to the office-holder's disadvantage after appointment, a standard independence device borrowed from the drafting of other statutory bodies.

Removal, under Section 7, is by order of the Central Government. The Chairperson may be removed on the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, and the same provision lists the disqualifying conditions under which the Chairperson or any Member may be removed: adjudication as an insolvent, engagement in paid employment outside the duties of office during the term, refusal or incapacity to act, a declaration of unsoundness of mind by a competent court, abuse of office rendering continuance detrimental to the public interest, conviction and sentence of imprisonment for an offence which in the opinion of the Central Government involves moral turpitude, and absence without leave from three consecutive meetings of the Commission. No person may be removed without an opportunity of being heard. Two features of this scheme are analytically important. The first is that the removal power sits wholly with the executive that appoints, without the interposition of a judicial inquiry as a statutory precondition for the misbehaviour ground; the hearing requirement is the principal procedural guard. The second is quieter: Section 8 requires a casual vacancy, whether by death, resignation or otherwise, to be filled within ninety days by a fresh appointment, and the person appointed serves the remainder of the term. The statute therefore contemplates a Commission that is never meant to run short-handed for more than a quarter of a year. The record of vacancies at the national level and, far more, at the state level, is a measure of how far practice has run behind that text, and it is taken up in the discussion of the state commissions below.

The Commission's internal machinery is sketched in Sections 10 and 11. The Commission must meet regularly at its office in Delhi, and not more than three months may intervene between one meeting and the next. Decisions are by majority, with a casting vote for the person presiding in the event of a tie. The Central Government must appoint a Member-Secretary, an officer not below the rank of Joint Secretary or Additional Secretary to the Government of India, who is responsible for the proper administration of the Commission's affairs and its day-to-day management, and must make available such other officers and employees as are necessary. Salaries and administrative expenses are paid out of the grants made to the Commission under Section 27, which are sums the Central Government pays after due appropriation by Parliament, and the accounts are audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India under Section 29. Two later provisions complete the picture of a body inside the state's fiscal and policy embrace even while it examines the state. Section 33 provides that in the discharge of its functions the Commission shall be guided by such directions on questions of policy relating to national purposes as the Central Government may give it, and that if a dispute arises about whether a question is such a question of policy, the decision of the Central Government is final. Section 34 obliges the Commission to furnish returns and information about its activities as the Central Government requires. Neither provision has been the site of a famous confrontation, and directions of this kind are a standard clause in Indian statute-drafting for government-funded bodies, but an honest profile records them, because together with executive appointment, prescribed salaries and grant funding they define the formal perimeter within which the Commission's independence has to be asserted by practice rather than by text.


## The functions: a mandate drafted in the widest terms the subject allows

Section 13 of the Act lists the functions of the Commission, and it is drafted expansively, because it was written to cover a subject, the whole of childhood, rather than a sector. The functions fall into families, and the families matter more than the lettering. The first family is review. The Commission must examine and review the safeguards provided by or under any law for the time being in force for the protection of child rights and recommend measures for their effective implementation, and it must present reports to the Central Government, annually and at other intervals it deems fit, on the working of those safeguards. The second family is inquiry into violation: the Commission must inquire into violations of child rights and recommend the initiation of proceedings in such cases. The third family is the examination of conditions. The Commission must examine all factors that inhibit the enjoyment of rights of children affected by terrorism, communal violence, riots, natural disaster, domestic violence, HIV and AIDS, trafficking, maltreatment, torture and exploitation, pornography and prostitution, and recommend appropriate remedial measures. Alongside that, it must look into matters relating to children in need of special care and protection, a category the Act spells out to include children in distress, marginalised and disadvantaged children, children in conflict with law, juveniles, children without family and children of prisoners. The fourth family is knowledge and advocacy: the Commission must study treaties and other international instruments, undertake periodical review of existing policies, programmes and activities on child rights and recommend for their effective implementation in the best interest of children; undertake and promote research in the field; and spread child rights literacy among the sections of society and promote awareness of the safeguards available, through publications, the media, seminars and other means. The fifth family, inspection, is important enough to receive its own discussion below. The sixth is the complaint and suo motu jurisdiction in clause (j), which is the engine of the Commission's daily work and the hinge between its functions and its powers. A residual clause lets the Commission perform such other functions as it considers necessary for the promotion of child rights and any matter incidental to the functions listed.

Two observations about this catalogue belong in any serious profile. The first is its direction of travel. Four of the six families produce paper: reviews, reports, research, recommendations and literacy. The catalogue is the charter of a body designed to change what governments know and what they are pressed to do, not a body designed to do the things itself. The Commission does not run a school, a home, a hospital or a police station, and it cannot admit a child, discharge a child, prosecute an offender or discipline an official. The second observation is that the catalogue is jurisdiction without a subject-matter fence. The review function reaches the safeguards provided by or under any law for the protection of child rights, which is to say the whole statute book as it touches children, and the inquiry function reaches violations of child rights as the Convention defines them. Where the National Commission for Women works within the field of women and the National Commission for Scheduled Castes within a named constitutional constituency, the child rights commissions hold a brief over a stage of life that intersects every ministry, every state department and every district administration in the country. The breadth explains both the volume of the complaint load described later in this profile and the frequency with which the Commission is found pronouncing on subjects, from school fees to internet content, that a narrower body would never reach.

### Can the NCPCR take up a case without a complaint?

Yes. Clause (j) of Section 13(1) empowers the Commission to take suo motu notice of matters relating to deprivation and violation of child rights, non-implementation of laws for the protection and development of children, and non-compliance with guidelines and instructions aimed at children's welfare. A press report or a field visit can set an inquiry in motion without any complainant.

The suo motu power deserves a closer look, because it is the function through which the Commission most often enters public life. The text of clause (j) is three-limbed. The Commission may inquire into complaints and take suo motu notice of matters relating to deprivation and violation of child rights; to non-implementation of laws providing for protection and development of children; and to non-compliance with policy decisions, guidelines or instructions aimed at mitigating hardship to children, ensuring their welfare and providing relief to them. Where a matter falls outside complaint and notice alike, the Commission may take up the issues arising out of such matters with the appropriate authorities, a formulation that covers the letter, the summons for a report and the public statement, the everyday instruments of the Commission's pressure. In practice the suo motu route means that the threshold question in a widely reported incident, a death in a shelter home, a corporal punishment case filmed in a classroom, children found working in a hazardous unit, is not whether someone with standing has complained but whether the Commission chooses to notice. The choice is discretionary and unreviewable in any practical sense, which gives the office-holder's judgment an importance out of proportion to the body's formal powers, and it is one reason the identity and inclinations of successive chairpersons have shaped the institution's public profile so visibly. It also creates the expectation problem in its sharpest form: a body that visibly takes notice is assumed to have taken charge, and the statute gives it no charge to take.

The complaint jurisdiction itself is informal by design. The Act prescribes no form, no fee, no limitation period and no standing requirement for the complaints the Commission inquires into under clause (j), a silence this profile returns to in the comparison with the human rights commission, whose statute bars inquiry into events more than a year old. A complaint may come from the child, from a parent, from a teacher, from a non-governmental organisation, from a journalist, or from a stranger who witnessed an event, and the Commission's own complaint channels, which include the online portal described in the discussion of the complaint route below, are built to receive it without legal assistance. The informality is a considered feature of commission design across the Indian statute book: these bodies were built for complainants who cannot brief a lawyer, and the informality is purchased at the price of the findings, since a proceeding that dispenses with pleadings, parties and decrees cannot end in one.

## The systemic brief: what a commission sees that no court and no department can

Between the complaint work and the inspection work lies the function that most distinguishes a commission from every other body in the field, and it is the function that a court's docket and a department's hierarchy both structurally exclude. A court sees the cases brought to it, one dispute at a time, framed by the parties' pleadings; a department sees its own programme, measured by its own returns. The Commission's review function under Section 13(1)(a), its treaty and policy review under clauses (f) and (g) and its reporting duty under clause (b) position it to see the field entire: the safeguards of every law touching children, compared against the Convention the Act takes as its measure, examined in the round rather than case by case or scheme by scheme. The products of that vantage are its systemic reports, on the working of safeguards, on the condition of institutions of a class, on the situation of children in a region or a predicament, which no litigant commissioned and no department would have produced about itself, and which enter the public record with the authority of a statutory body's inquiry powers behind the facts they state.

The treaty dimension of the systemic brief closes the circle with which this profile opened. The Convention under which India reports periodically to the United Nations Committee on the Rights of the Child is the same Convention the Act writes into the definition of child rights, and the commissions are the domestic institution that the international process presupposes: the Committee's concluding observations on India's periodic reports have addressed the commissions themselves, urging the establishment of state commissions across the country and the adequacy of the institutions' independence and resources, so that the weakness this profile documents in the state tier is on the international record as a treaty-compliance question and not merely as administrative untidiness. The Commission's research and literacy functions belong to the same systemic family. A body that studies treaties, reviews policies in the best interest of children, promotes research and spreads child rights literacy through publications, media and seminars is building the climate in which its recommendations operate: officials who have been trained by the Commission's programmes answer its requisitions differently, and a public that has absorbed the vocabulary of child rights complains to it in larger numbers and with better aim. The systemic brief is slow work, and it rarely produces the photograph that the incident work produces, but it is the part of the mandate no other institution holds, and the commissions' long-run influence on the statute book, visible in the amendment of the labour law's reach and in the monitoring assignments that later Parliaments kept handing them, was earned there rather than in the complaint stream.

## The powers of inquiry: the civil court borrowed, and only for the asking of questions

Section 14 is the provision on which the Commission's coercive reputation rests, and its terms repay exact reading. While inquiring into any matter referred to in clause (j) of Section 13(1), the Commission has all the powers of a civil court trying a suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, in particular in respect of five matters: summoning and enforcing the attendance of any person and examining that person on oath; requiring the discovery and production of any document; receiving evidence on affidavits; requisitioning any public record or copy of it from any court or office; and issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses or documents. The list is the standard evidence-gathering armoury of the Indian commission model, and its limits are in the words that frame it. The powers exist while inquiring. They are powers over the process of finding out: over attendance, testimony and paper. They are not powers over the merits: the Commission cannot, by the force of this section or any other, decree a claim, award compensation as a judgment, sentence an offender, suspend an official, close an institution or stay a government decision. A civil court trying a suit holds both kinds of power, the procedural and the dispositive. The Act borrows the first kind and withholds the second, and any account of the Commission that quotes Section 14 without that distinction misleads its reader at exactly the point where accuracy matters most.

### Does an NCPCR inquiry follow the rules of a court trial?

No. An inquiry is an investigation, not a trial: there are no pleadings, no parties and no charge framed against anyone. The Commission borrows the civil court's powers only to gather evidence, through summons, oath, documents and records, and works inquisitorially, calling for reports and taking statements, before recording a finding that ends in a recommendation rather than a judgment.

The second limb of Section 14 is less quoted and worth setting beside the first. The Commission may forward any case to a Magistrate having jurisdiction to try it, and the Magistrate to whom the case is forwarded proceeds to hear the complaint against the accused as if the case had been forwarded under Section 346 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. This is a genuine procedural bridge between the Commission's inquiry and the criminal process: material gathered under commission summons can enter the court system as a complaint before a Magistrate rather than evaporating as a report. It is, however, a bridge to a prosecution that the state must then conduct and a court must decide, and it confers no power on the Commission to direct either. The Commission also conducts its work through the instruments that surround a formal inquiry: it calls for reports from district and state authorities, constitutes inquiry committees and fact-finding teams, visits the site of an incident, and records the statements of officials and affected children. Those instruments are administrative practice built on the statutory powers rather than powers enumerated in the Act, and their effectiveness depends on the cooperation of the very machinery under examination, which an authority determined to stall can supply slowly, partially or in a form that answers the letter of the requisition and none of its purpose.

The honest summary of the powers chapter is therefore double-sided. Against an individual witness or a custodian of records, the Commission is strong: attendance can be compelled, evidence taken on oath, documents requisitioned from any office in the country, and a refusal to cooperate is a defiance of powers Parliament granted in terms modelled on a court's. Against the outcome, the Commission is unarmed by the same text. The strength and the weakness are not in tension by accident; they are the two halves of a single decision, taken in 2005 as it had been taken in 1993 for the human rights commission, that fact-finding should be coercive and decision should remain with governments and courts. The reader who grasps Section 14 and Section 15 together possesses the entire institutional argument of this article, and the complaint table that follows simply lays the same argument out step by step.


## Complaint to outcome: what the Commission can compel at each step

The findable artifact for this profile is the anatomy of a single complaint, traced from the moment it is made to the furthest point the statute allows it to travel. Read the final column first if time is short: it records, step by step, the precise locations at which the Commission's authority runs out, and it is the most useful single page a complainant, a child rights worker or an aspirant can take from this article.

| Step | What happens | What the Commission can compel | What the Commission cannot do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complaint or suo motu notice | A complaint is received in any form, or the Commission notices a matter itself under Section 13(1)(j) | It can take cognizance without a formal complainant, a fee or a limitation period | It cannot take up a matter pending before a State Commission or another commission under Section 13(2) |
| Registration and scrutiny | The complaint is registered, examined and assigned; reports may be called from the district or state authority concerned | It can require the authority to report on the facts and the action taken | It cannot itself deliver the frontline response, which belongs to the police, the Child Welfare Committee and the district administration |
| Inquiry | The Commission or its team inquires, visits the site and records statements | It can summon and enforce attendance, examine on oath and requisition records under Section 14 | It cannot arrest, search, seize or prosecute |
| Inspection, where an institution is involved | A custodial home, residence or institution meant for children is inspected under Section 13(1)(i) | It can enter and inspect, and take up remedial action with the authority controlling the institution | It cannot close the institution, suspend its staff or order a child's transfer |
| Finding | The Commission records whether a violation of child rights or a contravention of law is disclosed | It can publish its finding and its reasoning | The finding binds no one and creates no enforceable obligation |
| Recommendation to prosecute | Under Section 15(i), prosecution or other action is recommended to the government or authority | It can recommend, and it can forward a case to a Magistrate under Section 14(2) | It cannot direct a prosecution, overrule a refusal or conduct the trial |
| Approach to a constitutional court | Under Section 15(ii), the Commission moves the Supreme Court or a High Court | It can ask the court for directions, orders or writs | It cannot issue the direction itself; only the court can bind |
| Recommendation of interim relief | Under Section 15(iii), interim relief to the victim or family is recommended | It can recommend the grant and record a refusal in its reports | It cannot award compensation as a decree or execute payment |
| Report and action-taken memorandum | The annual or special report goes to government under Section 16 | It can require, through the report, a public accounting of acceptance or rejection | It cannot compel acceptance of any recommendation it has made |

The table's payload is the pattern in its last column. At no step does the Commission's authority mature into an order against the violator or the defaulting authority. What accumulates instead, step by step, is a record: a requisitioned file, a sworn statement, an inspection report, a reasoned finding, a recommendation, and eventually a place in a report laid before Parliament with the government's own memorandum beside it. The Commission's theory of change is that a sufficiently authoritative record, publicly owned, moves governments that orders from an unaccountable body could not legitimately move. Whether that theory works depends on the follow-through machinery, which is the subject of the next section, and on the publicity the record receives, which depends on the Commission's own energy and the press's attention. A complainant who understands the table will use the Commission for what it is: a route to an authoritative finding and to the doors of institutions that can bind, not a substitute for those institutions. General information of this kind describes the statutory route as it stands; it is not advice on any individual case, where the choice of forum and the timing of each step deserve specific guidance.

## How a complaint travels: the route in prose

The table compresses the journey; the prose restores its texture, because a complainant's experience of this institution is made of intervals and silences that a step diagram cannot hold. A complaint begins in any of the forms the informal design permits: a letter to the Commission in Delhi, a complaint carried to a State Commission, a filing on the eBaalNidan portal, a report in a newspaper that the Commission notices on its own motion, or a matter raised during a Member's tour or a public hearing. No format is prescribed, no fee is charged, no lawyer is needed, and the complaint need not come from the child or the family; teachers, doctors, social workers, journalists and neighbours all appear in the intake of these bodies, as do children themselves. On receipt, the complaint is registered and read for the first institutional question, which is not whether the grievance is justified but whose grievance it is in law: whether it discloses a deprivation or violation of child rights, a failure to implement a law for the protection or development of children, or a non-compliance with policy decisions and guidelines, the three limbs of clause (j), and whether some other commission is already seized of the same matter, which Section 13(2) makes a bar.

Most complaints then travel the referral road. The Commission calls for a report from the authority on the ground, the district magistrate, the superintendent of police, the district child protection unit, the education department, the management of the institution, and the authority's reply becomes the spine of the file. Where the reply is evasive, contradictory or silent, or where the allegation is grave, the matter escalates: a hearing at which officials are summoned, a fact-finding team sent to the district, an inspection of the institution where the child is lodged, statements recorded on the Commission's own authority under Section 14. The complainant's part in this machinery is smaller than complainants expect. There are no pleadings to file, no evidence to lead in the forensic sense and frequently no hearing at which the complainant appears; the inquiry is inquisitorial, conducted by the Commission with the authority, and the complainant's role is to supply the initiating information and to respond when the Commission seeks more. The file closes in one of the Act's three directions, a recommendation of proceedings, a recommendation of interim relief, an approach to a court, or it closes administratively, on the authority's report of action taken, with the closure recorded in the Commission's accounts of its work. The complainant who receives word that the complaint has been closed after a report from the district has not been fobbed off with a formality invented for the occasion; that closure is the design reaching its ordinary terminus, and the time to have chosen the decisional forums in parallel, the police, the Child Welfare Committee, the court, was at the beginning. The Commission's file, meanwhile, retains its value after closure: the requisitioned reports, the recorded statements and the finding are a public-authority record of the episode, available to be placed before the court or the legislature by anyone who takes the matter further, and in the institution's own theory that afterlife is not a consolation but the point.

Two features of the route deserve the complainant's attention before the first step is taken. The first is the choice between the national and the state body. Where a functioning State Commission exists, it is the natural first forum for a complaint arising within the state: it sits closer to the district machinery, works in the state's language and administration, and its pendency will bar the National Commission from the same matter under Section 13(2). The National Commission's complaint jurisdiction is at its most valuable for matters that cross state lines, matters engaging central institutions and laws, matters in which the state machinery itself is the respondent in a way that compromises the state forum, and matters of policy and pattern rather than of a single district's default. The second feature is timing. The Act sets no limitation period, and a complaint does not die of delay as a human rights complaint does after a year; but evidence decays, children are moved, registers are rewritten and officials are transferred, and the practical limitation on a commission inquiry is set by the survival of the record rather than by any clause in the statute. A complaint made while the facts are fresh gives the Section 14 powers something to bite on; a complaint made years later asks the Commission to reconstruct, and its findings, when they come, will speak to the system more readily than to the child.

## The report, the memorandum, and the one obligation the statute does impose

If the commissions cannot command, what exactly does the statute oblige anyone to do about their recommendations? The answer is in Section 16, and it is more substantial than the toothlessness slogan suggests, though it stops well short of enforcement. The Commission must submit an annual report to the Central Government and to the State Government concerned, and it may submit special reports at any time on a matter of such urgency or importance that, in its opinion, the matter should not be deferred until the annual report. The governments must cause the annual and special reports to be laid before each House of Parliament or the State Legislature respectively, along with a memorandum of action taken or proposed to be taken on the Commission's recommendations and the reasons for non-acceptance of any recommendation, within a period of one year from the date of receipt of the report. The design is a reason-forcing device. A government may reject a recommendation, wholly or partly, but it must say so on the record, in a document laid before the legislature, and it must give its reasons. The sanction for a bad reason is political and parliamentary: questions, debate, committee scrutiny and press coverage of an official document in which the state has explained, in its own words, why a child's established grievance will not be remedied.

This is the point at which the judicial anchor of this profile belongs, and it can be stated as a proposition about the statute's own text rather than as a survey of authority. The jurisprudence on commissions of this model, developed around the human rights commission and applied across the family of recommendatory bodies, holds consistently that a recommendation is not a binding direction and does not by itself create an enforceable obligation; a court will not execute a commission's recommendation as though it were a decree, and the remedy for official inaction lies in the political process the report sets in motion or in a fresh proceeding before a court that can bind. The Act of 2005 presupposes exactly that position: a statute that requires reasons for non-acceptance necessarily contemplates that non-acceptance will occur, and a legislature that intended findings to bind would not have built an elaborate machinery for explaining why they were not followed. The commissions' own route to a binding outcome confirms the reading. Section 15 places the approach to the Supreme Court or a High Court among the steps after inquiry, which it would not need to do if the Commission's own word were law. The binding order, when one is needed, must come from a bench; the Commission's constitutional role is to be the applicant with the best record in the courtroom.

The weakness of the reason-forcing design is calendar, and an honest profile should name it. The memorandum may follow the report by up to a year, annual reports themselves are frequently laid later than the year they describe, and a special report's urgency is blunted when the accounting for it arrives on the legislature's table long after public attention has moved. None of that is a defect the commissions can cure from inside, since the laying obligation and its timetable bind governments, not the Commission. It does mean that the Commission's most reliable product is not the eventual memorandum but the contemporaneous record: the inquiry report, the inspection findings and the public statement issued while the matter is alive, which is why the effectiveness of these bodies tracks the visibility of their work more closely than the visibility of a court's or a regulator's work ever needs to.

## Inspection: the function with its hands on the institution

Clause (i) of Section 13(1) gives the Commission a function unlike the review and reporting families, because it is physical. The Commission may inspect or cause to be inspected any juvenile custodial home, or any other place of residence or institution meant for children, under the control of the Central Government or a State Government or any other authority, including an institution run by a social organisation, where children are detained or lodged for the purpose of treatment, reformation or protection, and take up remedial action with those authorities if found necessary. The reach is comprehensive: observation homes, special homes, children's homes, shelter homes, fit institutions and the whole residential apparatus in which the state and its licensees keep children, together with institutions run by voluntary organisations, are open to the Commission's inspection. The power to cause an inspection to be made extends the Commission's eyes through teams and state counterparts, without which a seven-member body in Delhi could not pretend to oversight of institutions numbered in the thousands.

### Can the NCPCR inspect a children's home or custodial institution?

Yes. Section 13(1)(i) empowers the Commission to inspect, or cause to be inspected, any custodial home or other place of residence or institution meant for children, under the control of the Central Government, a State Government or any authority, including institutions run by social organisations, and to take up remedial action with the controlling authority where inspection makes it necessary.

The institutional world into which this inspection power enters is the one built by the juvenile justice statute, with its Child Welfare Committees, Juvenile Justice Boards and registered child care institutions, and the working of that machinery is the subject of [the Juvenile Justice Act 2015 explained](/2016/05/15/juvenile-justice-act-2015-explained/), which owns the institutions themselves; the commissions stand outside that machinery as its inspectors and critics. The distinction between the two kinds of body is the most consequential in the whole field, and it is drawn again in the overlap discussion below: a Child Welfare Committee decides the case of the individual child before it and its orders bind; the Commission inspects the home in which that child is lodged and its findings do not. Inspection is nevertheless among the Commission's most effective instruments in practice, for a reason grounded in the nature of residential institutions. A closed institution's worst features survive on invisibility. An inspection converts conditions into a document: occupancy against sanctioned capacity, staff vacancies, the state of registers, the separation of categories of children, the presence or absence of the committees and inspections the law already requires. The document then travels, into the annual report, into a special report, into a court proceeding where one follows, carrying facts that an authority must answer rather than impressions it can dismiss. The limit remains the familiar one: the Commission takes up remedial action with the authority, and the authority decides. But of all the Commission's paper powers, inspection produces the paper that is hardest to ignore, because it is generated by the Commission's own eyes rather than compiled from the returns of the inspected.


## The State Commissions: where the design meets the federal fact

Chapter IV of the Act builds the second tier. Section 17 provides that a State Government may constitute a body to be known by the name of the state as its Commission for Protection of Child Rights. Every word of the contrast with Section 3 carries weight. For the National Commission, the Central Government shall, by notification, constitute the body: a duty. For a state, the government may constitute one: a permission. The Act that made the national body compulsory left its state counterparts, the bodies geographically and administratively closest to nearly every child in the country, to the discretion of the governments they would exist to examine. A State Commission, where constituted, mirrors the national composition exactly: a Chairperson who is a person of eminence with outstanding work for the welfare of children, and six Members, at least two of them women, drawn from the same six fields, appointed by the State Government by notification, with the Chairperson appointed on the recommendation of a three-member Selection Committee constituted by the state and chaired by the Minister in charge of the department dealing with children. Terms are the same three years, the same two-term ceiling and the same age limits of sixty-five and sixty, under Section 19. A Secretary, an officer not below the rank of Secretary to the State Government, runs its administration under Section 21. Its annual and special reports go to the State Government under Section 23 and are laid before the State Legislature with the same action-taken memorandum and the same obligation to state reasons for non-acceptance. Section 24 then applies the national provisions to the state bodies with the necessary substitutions: the removal and vacancy provisions, the meeting provisions, sub-section (1) of Section 13 on functions, and Sections 14 and 15 on the powers of inquiry and the steps after it. A State Commission therefore holds the identical institutional character as the National Commission, recommendatory to the core, within its state's territory.

### Should a complaint go to the State Commission or to the NCPCR?

Ordinarily to the State Commission, where a functioning one exists: it sits closer to the district machinery, and its pendency bars the National Commission from the same matter. The national body is the better forum for matters crossing state lines, engaging central institutions, raising a policy pattern, or arising where the state body is unconstituted or dormant.

The capacity problem that follows is structural, and the brief for this profile directs that it be described structurally rather than as a list of states, because the position changes as notifications issue and terms expire. The pattern, constant across the institution's life, is unevenness: states that constituted their commissions promptly and staffed them, states that constituted them years late, states in which the office of Chairperson has stood vacant for extended periods between terms, states in which Members' places have gone unfilled so that the six-field composition exists in the notification and not in the meeting room, and states in which the commission functions with a skeleton secretariat borrowed from the department it monitors. The consequences are not distributed evenly across the statute's scheme; they concentrate at its foundations. The National Commission is barred by Section 13(2) from inquiring into a matter pending before a State Commission, a bar that presumes functioning state bodies as the first forum for most complaints. The monitoring mandates described in the next section are assigned jointly to the national and state commissions, and the education and sexual offences statutes are administered in districts, not in Delhi. A design that routes the volume of a subcontinent's child rights complaints through state-level bodies, and then makes those bodies optional and leaves their staffing to the monitored governments, has its weakest joint exactly where the load is greatest. The series has documented the same institutional capacity pattern in the tribunal clusters, bodies created by statute and then left partly unconstituted for long stretches, and its recurrence here, in a field where the complainants are children and cannot organise to demand a functioning forum, gives the pattern its sharpest form. The statute is not silent about the remedy for vacancies at this tier either. Because Section 24 applies Section 8 to the state commissions, a casual vacancy in a state chair or membership is to be filled within ninety days, for the remainder of the term, by a fresh appointment through the state selection process. A state commission that sits for a year without a Chairperson is therefore not merely an administrative embarrassment; it is a body operating outside the timetable its own statute prescribes, and the gap between the ninety-day text and the multi-year reality is the cleanest single measurement of the distance between the institution as enacted and the institution as maintained.

## The monitoring mandates: three other statutes hand the commissions a second job

The Act of 2005 gave the commissions their functions; three later statutes gave them assignments inside other legal regimes, and the assignments are the reason the commissions appear in litigation and administration far outside their parent Act. The education statute came first. Section 31 of the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 charges the National Commission and the State Commissions, in addition to their functions under the Act of 2005, with examining and reviewing the safeguards for rights provided by or under the education Act, inquiring into complaints relating to a child's right to free and compulsory education, and taking the steps their parent Act provides, so that a parent complaining of a denied admission, an illegal fee or an unlawful screening test has a statutory route to the commissions as the education law's designated monitor. The sexual offences statute followed the same architecture: Section 44 of the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 designates the National Commission and the State Commissions to monitor the implementation of that Act's provisions in the manner prescribed, and the juvenile justice statute completed the set when Section 109 of the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 assigned the same commissions, in addition to their functions under the Act of 2005, to monitor the implementation of the juvenile justice Act in the manner prescribed, gave them their parent Act's inquiry powers while inquiring into offences under it, and required their work under that section to be included in the annual report. The commissions' seats inside [the Right to Education Act 2009 guide](/2015/01/01/right-to-education-act-2009-guide/) and [the POCSO Act 2012 explained](/2016/04/15/pocso-act-2012-explained/) are thus statutory, not courtesy arrangements, and each of those articles owns its own statute; the point that belongs to this profile is what the accumulation of mandates does to the institution.

It does two things. It multiplies the commissions' presence: a body that monitors the education statute reviews school safeguards and inquires into schooling complaints; a body that monitors the sexual offences statute tracks the designation of special courts, the appointment of special public prosecutors and the working of child-friendly procedures, and reports on them; a body that monitors the juvenile justice statute watches the registration and functioning of child care institutions and the committees and boards. And it compounds the design question, because monitoring is a recommendatory body's most natural work and also its most easily inflated. To monitor is to review, to collect, to compare against the statute and to report; the monitored obligations continue to bind the governments and authorities that owe them, and a monitoring commission acquires no new power to enforce the monitored statute against a defaulting school, police unit or department. The mandates therefore widen the Commission's jurisdiction without deepening its authority, and each new assignment, announced as a strengthening of child protection, increases the range of failures for which the Commission will be blamed in proportion as it increases the range of failures the Commission can document. That arithmetic, mandate growth at constant power, is the quiet driver of the criticism examined below.

One mandate within the education statute deserves separate mention because it quietly converts the state commissions into something their parent Act never made them: an appellate forum. The education Act's own grievance route begins in the locality, where a person having a grievance relating to the right of a child to free and compulsory education may make a written complaint to the local authority, which must decide the matter within a period of three months after affording a reasonable opportunity of being heard. From the local authority's decision, the statute carries an appeal to the State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, which decides the appeal. The provision is easy to pass over and wrong to pass over, because it locates a decisional function inside the recommendatory architecture: in the appellate seat the State Commission does not merely report on the education system but disposes of an individual grievance against it, and its disposition of the appeal is the terminus the statute provides. Practitioners in the education field learn to treat the state commission accordingly, as the second forum in a two-step statutory remedy, while the parent Act's chapters continue to describe a body that recommends. The coexistence of the two characters in one institution, adviser to the system in its annual report and appellate authority over the system's local decisions in the individual case, is the single most instructive complication in the commissions' design, and it shows what Parliament does when a recommendatory body earns its trust: it does not rewrite the body's charter, it hands the body decisions at the margin, one statute at a time.

## Children's Courts: the Act's forgotten third institution

The long title of the Act promises three creations, the National Commission, the State Commissions and Children's Courts for providing speedy trial of offences against children or of violation of child rights, and the third is the least remembered. Section 25 empowers a State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, to specify by notification at least one court in the state, or for each district a Court of Session, to be a Children's Court for the speedy trial of such offences. Section 26 requires the state to specify a Public Prosecutor, or appoint an advocate of not less than seven years' practice, as a Special Public Prosecutor for every Children's Court. Two provisos in Section 25 explain the institution's faint footprint: nothing in the section applies where a Court of Session is already specified as a special court, or where a special court is already constituted for such offences under any other law. The field of trying offences against children was progressively occupied by special courts under other statutes, above all the special courts of the sexual offences statute, which carry the great majority of prosecutions for offences against children, and the Children's Court of the Act of 2005 subsists in the space those designations leave. The institution is nonetheless examinable and conceptually important, because it completes the Act's theory of itself: Parliament in 2005 imagined a system in which commissions would inquire and recommend, and designated courts would try, swiftly and with specialised prosecutors, the offences the inquiries uncovered. The commissions were built, however unevenly; the dedicated trial forum largely was not, and the speedy trial the long title promised migrated into the special court architecture of later statutes. The breadth of the Children's Court's intended remit is worth a final notice, because it exceeds the remit of the special courts that displaced it. A Children's Court was to try offences against children or of violation of child rights, a formula wide enough to reach the offences of the general penal law and of every special statute at once, where the special courts of the sexual offences statute are confined to that statute's own offences. In the design of 2005, the trial of a child-related offence was to be gathered into a designated forum with a specialised prosecutor, whatever the source of the offence; in the architecture that actually developed, the forum follows the statute, and a single episode may be tried across as many courts as there are statutes engaged. The concurrence of the Chief Justice, which Section 25 requires before a state may designate its courts, remains the provision's quiet safeguard, placing the creation of the forum beyond the executive's unilateral reach.


## The formation years: an agenda set by its first occupant

Institutions are made twice, once in the statute and once in their first years, and the child rights commissions were made the second time by their founding Chairperson. Shantha Sinha came to the office in 2007 from the movement against child labour in Andhra Pradesh, and the Commission's early agenda bore the movement's signature conviction, acquired district by district in the work of the Mamidipudi Venkatarangaiya Foundation, that every child out of school was the operative unit of failure, whatever the administrative category of the child's absence. The Commission under her leadership pressed for the amendment of the child labour law so that the prohibition would reach the whole of childhood rather than stopping at fourteen, a position the law eventually moved towards by amendment in 2016; it built its field method around the district visit, the public hearing and the camp court, in which officials answered, in the presence of the affected community, for the registers and returns their departments filed in Delhi; and it took up the children of districts affected by civil strife through its Bal Bandhu initiative, which placed community-level volunteers between children and the armed and administrative machinery on all sides of those conflicts. The founding tenure also fixed the Commission's public method, which persists as its institutional style: arrive after the incident, inspect the institution, name the responsible office, issue the finding loudly, and follow the file.

The second making matters to the profile for a structural reason. A commission whose output depends on its occupant, as the discussion of independence argued above, will show the fingerprints of its first occupant longest, because the first occupant sets the precedents of assertiveness that successors either extend or retreat from. The Commission's formation years set the assertive precedent: that the body would use its suo motu power without waiting to be asked, that its Members would tour districts as field officers rather than sit as a Delhi tribunal, and that its reports would address the systemic cause rather than close with the individual apology. Successive chairpersons have run the office with visibly different emphases, as the design invites and the record of every Indian commission confirms, and the contrast between tenures is itself part of the evidence for this profile's central claim: that the statute built a megaphone and left the volume to the holder. What did not vary across tenures is the statutory perimeter, and the recurring rediscovery of the perimeter by each new set of occupants, in the form of recommendations that governments declined, interim relief that authorities did not grant and findings that changed the record without changing the result, is the institution's biography written in a single repeated paragraph.

## The record: a small secretariat against the complaint load of a subcontinent

The Commission's working life divides into the complaint stream, the monitoring stream and the public stream, and the annual reports it lays before Parliament are the record of all three; the reports publish, year by year, the complaints received, the inquiries undertaken, the inspections conducted and the recommendations made, and the figures in those reports should be read there, in their own tables, rather than in summary anywhere else, because the categories move between editions. Two durable features of the record can be stated without a figure. The first is asymmetry of scale. A Chairperson, six Members and a secretariat headed by a Member-Secretary in Delhi, replicated unevenly across the states, face a jurisdiction defined by every child in India and a complaint intake that arrives by post, by hand, through state counterparts and through the online complaint portal the Commission opened in 2015 under the name eBaalNidan, which lets a complainant file a violation report from anywhere and track its progress. The portal lowered the cost of complaining to nearly nothing, as it was meant to, and thereby increased the flow into a machinery whose inquiry capacity did not grow at the same rate; the gap between registration and inquiry is the standing administrative fact of the institution, visible in its own reports' distinction between complaints received, complaints taken up and complaints closed after reports from the authorities concerned. Much of the Commission's complaint work is in truth referral work: the complaint is forwarded to the district or state authority whose duty the matter is, a report is called for, and the file closes on the authority's response, a process that resolves many grievances and satisfies few complainants who expected the Commission itself to act.

The second durable feature is the visibility of the suo motu and inspection work relative to its volume. The episodes by which the public knows the Commission are disproportionately its interventions in reported incidents: teams dispatched to the site of a custodial death or a shelter home scandal, inspections of institutions whose conditions became a scandal, statements on corporal punishment, child labour rescues and the treatment of children in the media, and, during the pandemic years, the Commission's place in the Supreme Court's suo motu proceedings concerning children in protection homes and children who had lost one or both parents, in which data on children in need of care and protection was gathered through the Commission's Bal Swaraj portal. Those episodes are the design working as designed: an authoritative outsider arrives, inspects, records and publishes, and the machinery that can bind, the district administration, the state government, the court, is pressed into motion by the record rather than commanded by the visitor. They also teach the public the wrong lesson about the institution's ordinary powers, because the arrival is memorable and the paper trail that follows is not.

For the complainant and the child rights worker, the practical archive matters as much as the public one. A commission file accumulates precisely the material that later proceedings need and that no family should have to reconstruct from memory: the complaint, the authority's report, the statements recorded, the inspection notes and the recommendation. Keeping that material complete, ordered and private is a discipline in itself, and the working model for it is the one described in [VaultBook's account of how journalists protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), a single encrypted workspace, controlled by the person responsible for the file, in which sensitive records are assembled without scattering copies across inboxes and shared drives that leak. A child's complaint file contains a child's identity, and the care owed to it is the care owed to a source.

## Overlap: four doors, one corridor, and a bar on using two at once

A single incident in a child's life can engage several statutory bodies at once, and the map of the doors is part of this institution's honest profile. A girl assaulted in a residential school may be the subject of a criminal case under the sexual offences statute before its special court, of a care and protection proceeding before the Child Welfare Committee, of a complaint to the State Commission or the National Commission, of a complaint to the National Commission for Women where the victim is a girl within that body's remit as it is commonly exercised, of a human rights complaint where a public servant's failure is in issue, and of the school and district administration's own disciplinary machinery. These doors are not interchangeable. The court tries and can punish. The Child Welfare Committee decides the individual child's care, custody and restoration, and its orders operate on the child and the institution directly. The commissions inquire and recommend. The administration disciplines its own. The complainant who mistakes the recommendatory door for the decisional one loses time that the other proceedings do not pause for, and the statute offers less guidance on the choice than it should.

The one hard rule the Act supplies is the bar in Section 13(2): the Commission shall not inquire into any matter which is pending before a State Commission or any other Commission duly constituted under any law for the time being in force. Section 24 carries the same scheme to the state bodies through the application of Section 13(1) and the inquiry provisions. The bar prevents duplicative inquiries and inconsistent recommendations between commissions, and it disciplines the national body to leave the first examination to the state tier. Its cost is the fork it forces at the threshold: a matter lodged with a dormant state commission is pending before it, and the national body's hands are tied by the very pendency that the state body's inactivity prolongs. The bar is defensible as federal courtesy and frustrating as lived experience, and the two descriptions are both true of the same sentence. The prudent complainant's course, which child rights workers learn by experience, is to run the decisional proceedings, the police complaint, the Child Welfare Committee, the court, as the primary track, since those bodies can bind and protect, and to use the commissions in parallel where the statute allows, for the record, the inspection and the pressure, rather than as a substitute for either.

## Three bodies the Commission is not

The brief for this profile records the errors other pages make about these commissions, and the largest is conflation: the Commission is routinely described as the body that rescues children, decides their custody or runs the child helpline. It is none of them, and the distinctions are the field's working anatomy. The Child Welfare Committee is a bench of the district, constituted under the juvenile justice statute for every district, which receives the individual child in need of care and protection and decides that child's case: production, inquiry, interim custody, restoration, placement in a children's home or with a fit person. Its orders are decisions, operative on the child and on the institutions holding the child, and its proceedings are the individual case-processing heart of the system. The Commission, by contrast, never has a child before it in a proceeding that ends in an order. A parent who knocks on the Commission's door seeking custody will be sent, properly, to the Committee; a government that wants to know why the homes in a district are failing will be sent, properly, to the Commission. The Childline service on the number 1098 is different again: an emergency outreach and response service for children in distress, operated as the field's intake and rescue-coordination channel, which connects the child to the police, the Committee, the district child protection unit and medical help. The commissions receive the cases that Childline's work brings to light and examine the systems Childline works within, but the helpline is not theirs and the rescue vehicle is not theirs either. The District Child Protection Unit completes the contrast from the administrative side: a unit of the district administration itself, charged with implementing the child protection machinery in the district, maintaining the registers of institutions and coordinating the non-institutional services. It is the monitored machinery, not the monitor.

Keeping the four straight is not pedantry. Each body answers a different failure: the Committee answers the individual child's need for a decision, Childline answers the emergency's need for a response, the district unit answers the administration's need for an apparatus, and the Commission answers the system's need for an examiner that the system's own officers do not control. Complaints routed to the wrong body are not refused, the field's bodies refer and forward as a matter of daily practice, but they are delayed, and in child protection delay is measured in a child's life rather than in a file's pendency. The Commission's own complaint guidance is built on this anatomy, and the aspirant who can reproduce it, which body decides, which body rescues, which body administers and which body examines, holds the distinction on which a large share of the examination questions in this field, and a larger share of its practical mistakes, actually turn.

## The comparison the design invites: the human rights commission and the Indian default

The child rights commissions were the second great construction on a template the Indian state had already used, and the comparison is the fastest route to understanding both. The Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 had created the National Human Rights Commission a dozen years earlier, on the same architectural plan: a multi-member body of eminent persons, functions of review and inquiry, the powers of a civil court while inquiring, steps after inquiry that run to recommendation of prosecution, approach to the constitutional courts and recommendation of interim relief, and annual reports laid before the legislature with an action-taken memorandum. The treatment of [the NHRC's powers and functions](/2010/09/15/nhrc-powers-functions-explained/) owns that body's record, and the design comparison belongs here: the Act of 2005 follows the Act of 1993 so closely in its inquiry and post-inquiry chapters that the later statute reads as the earlier one's child, which is the series thesis thread of this profile in its precise form. Two rights commissions, created a decade and more apart for different constituencies, were given the same settlement between coercive fact-finding and non-binding outcomes. A settlement repeated is a policy, and the recommendatory commission is the Indian default for rights protection outside the courts, not an oversight in any single statute.

The differences between the two commissions are as instructive as the resemblances. The human rights commission was anchored, at its creation, in the judiciary: its Chairperson was originally required to have been a Chief Justice of India, a requirement widened by amendment in 2019 to include a former judge of the Supreme Court, and serving or former judges sit among its members, giving the body a judicial centre of gravity the child rights commission deliberately lacks. The child rights commission's eminence criteria point the other way, towards the field: educators, health and development specialists, child psychologists and practitioners, a composition that trades forensic authority for subject knowledge. The human rights statute also carries a limitation discipline the child rights statute omits: the human rights commission is barred from inquiring into a matter after the expiry of one year from the date of the alleged violation, while the Act of 2005 sets no clock on a complaint about a child at all, a silence consistent with the realities of its subject, since violations against children characteristically surface years late, through a teacher, a doctor, a rescue or the child's own majority. And the human rights commission's deemed membership draws the chairpersons of the other national commissions, including the child rights commission's Chairperson, into its deliberations for specified functions, a statutory acknowledgement that the commissions form a family with overlapping territory. The National Commission for Women, created by its Act of 1990, completes the family portrait on the same lines: inquiry powers borrowed from the civil court, outcomes in recommendation. A reader who has understood one of these bodies has understood the grammar of all of them, and can predict, for any new commission Parliament creates, exactly where its powers will stop.

Why the default recurs is itself worth a paragraph of analysis, because it explains why reform proposals that ignore the reasons will keep failing. The first reason is constitutional caution: India's courts guard the boundary between adjudication and administration, and a legislature handing binding power to an appointed body invites challenge on the separation of powers and on the rights of the directed parties to a hearing before a forum with appellate supervision. The second reason is administrative convenience: a recommendatory commission costs the executive nothing in obedience while yielding it a monitored, documented field and a recipient for public anger that is not a ministry. The third reason is the genuine one the defence in the debate below states: the commissions were made to notice what courts and departments cannot see, and the evidentiary and systemic work they do is work no order-issuing body in the field is positioned to do as well. The three reasons together, caution, convenience and conviction, are the load-bearing walls of the design, and any amendment that touches the recommendatory character alone, leaving the walls standing, will produce a commission with nominal powers and the same practical reach.


## Money, audit, and the formal perimeter of independence

An institution profile that stopped at the Commission's powers would leave its most persistent quiet question unexamined: how independent is a body appointed by the executive, funded by the executive and subject to the executive's policy directions? The Act's financial chapters supply the framework for the answer. The Commission lives on grants: the Central Government, after due appropriation by Parliament, pays it such sums as it thinks fit for the purposes of the Act, and the Commission spends those sums at its own discretion, the expenditure counting against the grants. The State Commissions live on the equivalent state grants under Section 28. The accounts are maintained in the form the government prescribes in consultation with the Comptroller and Auditor-General, audited by the Auditor-General, and the certified accounts and the audit report go to the government, which lays the audit report before Parliament or the state legislature. The Commission also furnishes returns and information on its activities as the Central Government requires under Section 34. Formally, then, the Commission is a statutory body within the public purse, examined by the public auditor, reporting its accounts and its activities to the government that funds it.

Two readings of that framework are both available, and the profile should give both honestly. The defence of the design runs on the ordinariness of it: almost every statutory commission in India, the human rights commission included, is funded by grants and audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General, and the financial controls are the standard machinery by which public money is accounted for, not a leash designed for this Commission in particular. The salary protection in Section 6, the prohibition on disadvantageous variation after appointment, is the standard statutory device for insulating office-holders from financial retaliation, and the audit by a constitutional authority rather than a departmental one is itself a guard against petty manipulation of the Commission's funds. On this reading, independence is protected where it needs to be, in the tenure, the removal procedure and the publication of reports, and the financial chapters are plumbing.

The critique runs on the accumulation of small dependencies. The grant is at the government's discretion in amount, the secretariat is supplied by the government, the Member-Secretary is a serving officer of government rank appointed by the government, and the Commission's establishment costs compete in the same budget as the departments the Commission examines. Section 33 adds the policy direction clause, by which the Commission is guided on questions of policy relating to national purposes, with the government the final judge of what counts as such a question, and Section 34 adds the returns obligation. None of these provisions has produced the kind of public confrontation that would make them famous, and that absence is itself part of the evidence on both sides: it may show that the formal perimeter is unobjectionable because never tested, or that it works precisely because it never needs to be tested, since a body that must ask for its staff, its funds and its premises knows, without being told, where the boundaries of the tolerable lie. The profile's own judgment on this point is deliberately narrow and examinable: the Commission's independence is not the independence of a court, it is the independence of a watchdog whose appointment, budget and secretariat are in the hands of the watched, and its actual independence in any given year is therefore a function of its leadership's assertiveness and the government's tolerance rather than a property the statute guarantees. The record bears this out in the most visible way possible, which is that the institution's public energy has plainly varied with its chairpersons, and a design whose output depends on the occupant is a design whose safeguards were under-built.

## The commissions before the courts: the applicant with the best record

The Section 15 approach to a constitutional court is the commissions' one doorway into the binding world, and its practice is the least understood part of the institution. The provision is disarmingly brief: the Commission may, on completion of an inquiry, approach the Supreme Court or the High Court concerned for such directions, orders or writs as that court may deem necessary. The Commission therefore enters the courtroom not as an adjudicator of the dispute but as a litigant, and in most instances as a litigant for the public interest, placing its inquiry record before a bench and asking the court to do what the statute will not let the Commission do itself. The posture matters for standing doctrine as much as for power. An ordinary complainant may struggle to satisfy a court that a systemic failure in the child protection machinery is that complainant's own legal injury; a statutory commission charged with monitoring the field carries its own authorisation to speak to the system. Courts, in turn, have learned to treat commission material in the register it was meant to be used in: as an expert, disinterested factual record on which judicial orders can be built, a function the Supreme Court's suo motu proceedings during the pandemic assigned to the Commission in practice, working through its inquiries and its data-gathering portals when the court needed to know what was happening to children in institutions and to children left without caregivers.

The relationship runs in both directions. Courts appoint the commissions as their eyes in the districts when a systemic case needs a fact-finder with field capacity and no adversarial posture, and commissions place before courts the records their inspections and inquiries generate when they need an order no one else will make. The children in conflict with the law and children in need of care, whose individual cases are decided by Juvenile Justice Boards and Child Welfare Committees, are the field's decision-makers for persons; the commissions are the field's decision-less conscience, and the constitutional court is the hinge between them. This is the mechanism by which the recommendatory design ceases to be toothless in the individual case without acquiring teeth of its own: the Commission's finding becomes the factual foundation of a judicial direction, and the direction binds. The limitation is the one the brief for this profile states honestly and this section repeats: the route through a constitutional court is slow, resource-intensive, rare, and chosen by the Commission for a fraction of its findings, so the practical consequence of most findings remains a recommendation to a government that may or may not act, and a report to a legislature that may or may not read it. The court doorway is real, and it is narrow.

A final proposition about the judicial environment closes this section, because aspirants and practitioners both need it and because it is stated here in its narrowest defensible form. The courts do not execute a commission's recommendation as though it were a decree, and no provision of the Act of 2005 invites them to. What the courts do, repeatedly, is receive the commission's findings as material for directions in writ jurisdiction, defer to the commission's systemic recommendations in continuing mandamus, and on occasion ask the commissions to report back to the court on compliance, which converts the commission from a recommendatory body into the court's monitoring agent for the life of the case. The distinction is precise and worth the care: a recommendation is not enforceable as a commission's own order, but it is capable of becoming the substance of a court's order, and the path between those two states runs through a bench, never through the Commission's own signature.

## The standing debate: should a commission that can compel testimony be able to compel anything else?

The proposal to give the child rights commissions binding powers recurs whenever a recommendation is publicly ignored, and the stance this profile is required to take is to state both cases at full strength before giving its verdict. The case for enforcement powers, as made from inside the institution and from the child rights movement, runs as follows. Shantha Sinha, the Commission's founding Chairperson, argued from the experience of the office that a recommendation which a government could decline at no cost to itself would, in the run of cases, be declined, and that the Commission's inability to secure even interim protection for a child it had found to be in danger made its inquiries an exercise in documentation while the documented harm continued. HAQ: Centre for Child Rights, the Delhi-based organisation co-founded by Enakshi Ganguly and Bharti Ali, which has tracked the commissions since their first years and published assessments of their functioning, has pressed the connected structural critique: that the commissions were given the widest mandate in the field and the thinnest instruments, that their budgets and secretariats are controlled by the departments they monitor, and that the action-taken memorandum, the one follow-through device the statute contains, is laid late and debated rarely, so that the accountability loop the Act designed closes on paper and not in fact. On this view, the interim relief recommendation is the scandal in miniature: a body that has inquired, found a child injured by a violation and identified the relief the child needs may only ask, and the asking may be answered by silence without consequence. A commission that can compel a witness to attend from across the country, the argument concludes, is trusted with coercion over persons; the refusal to trust it with any coercion over outcomes is a choice to keep child rights enforcement exactly as weak as the machinery that failed the child in the first place.

The case against binding powers, stated at its strongest, is constitutional rather than complacent. A commission is an appointed body of eminent persons, selected by the executive, financed by grants, subject to governmental directions on questions of policy relating to national purposes under Section 33, and answerable to no electorate. To arm such a body with power to direct schools, police forces, hospitals and district administrations, to order compensation and to enforce its orders, would be to create an executive authority without executive accountability, duplicating the functions of the magistracy and the administration inside a body designed as their critic. The constitutional order already contains institutions that issue binding orders after inquiry: the courts, whose writ jurisdiction the Commission may invoke under Section 15, and the decisional bodies of the juvenile justice statute, whose committees decide individual cases. The commission model, on this view, is not a failed court but a different instrument, whose contribution is the authoritative public record, the inspection, the systemic review and the reasoned recommendation that a legislature and a press can act on, and whose weakness in the individual case is the price of its freedom to roam across the whole field without the discipline of parties, pleadings and precedent. The defence adds a practical limb: binding powers would judicialise the commissions, wrapping their informal, fee-free, form-free complaint intake in procedure, representation and appeal, and the complainants the design most serves, children and the poor, would be the first casualties of the transformation.

The verdict this profile reaches, on the record the statute and the institution together supply, is that the debate about binding powers, though genuine, is the second question and not the first. The evidence for that ordering is in the design's unglamorous clauses. A power to bind would not constitute a single additional state commission, fill a single vacant chair, shorten a single delayed action-taken memorandum or add a single inquiry team to a secretariat in Delhi; and those four deficits, unlike the recommendatory character, are failures to operate the statute Parliament actually passed rather than consequences of its terms. The recommendatory model has demonstrably moved governments where its products were visible and followed up, through special reports, inspections whose findings could not be answered, and the court route under Section 15, which converts the Commission's record into binding orders at the point where binding orders are constitutionally meant to originate. The reform that the record supports is therefore not the conversion of the commissions into courts but the completion of the commissions as designed: state bodies constituted everywhere and kept filled within the statute's own ninety-day vacancy discipline, inquiry capacity proportioned to the complaint load the portals generate, and the Section 16 memorandum treated by legislatures as the accountability instrument it was drafted to be, laid on time and debated. Within that completed design, one targeted strengthening commands support on both sides of the argument and from this profile: the interim relief recommendation, addressed to a child in present need, is the point at which non-bindingness costs the most and the accountability objection weighs the least, and a statutory duty on the authority to respond to such a recommendation within a fixed period, with reasons recorded, would close the cruellest gap in the present scheme without disturbing its constitutional logic.

## Verdict: judge the institution against its design, then judge the design's unfinished half

The institution profile ends where it began, with the distinction between the body and its blueprint. The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and its state counterparts were designed as the child rights instance of India's default rights institution: coercive in inquiry, recommendatory in outcome, universal in jurisdiction, informal in intake, and dependent for effect on publicity, legislative follow-through and the constitutional courts. Against that design, the national body's record is a working demonstration of both the model's reach and its ceiling, a commission that has inspected, inquired, monitored three major statutes and supplied records on which courts and governments have acted, and that has never once, because it never could, ordered a single official to do a single thing. Against the same design, the unfinished half of the institution is the state tier, permissive in its creating clause and uneven in its existence, through which the statute routed the great majority of its work. The reader who needs the commissions should use them with the grain of the design: complain early and in writing, keep the file complete, run the decisional proceedings in parallel, and treat a commission's finding as what it is, the strongest paper a non-binding body in India can produce. The reader who judges them should judge the right defendant. The commission that failed to act is usually a statute that failed to empower, or a government that failed to constitute, staff and answer the body it created, and the correction of each of those failures lies in a different set of hands.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights do?

The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights examines and reviews the safeguards that every law provides for the protection of child rights, inquires into violations of those rights, studies the conditions of children affected by violence, trafficking, disaster and exploitation, inspects institutions where children are lodged, spreads child rights literacy, and reports to the Central Government on how the safeguards are working. It also monitors the implementation of the education, sexual offences and juvenile justice statutes. Its instruments are inquiry, inspection and recommendation: it can summon witnesses and compel documents, but it cannot issue binding orders, award compensation or prosecute anyone.

### Q: What powers does a child rights commission have?

While inquiring, a child rights commission has all the powers of a civil court trying a suit under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908: summoning and enforcing the attendance of any person and examining that person on oath, requiring the discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records from any court or office, and issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses or documents. It may inspect any institution where children are detained or lodged, take up matters on its own motion, and forward a case to a Magistrate for trial. Its powers govern the gathering of evidence; they do not extend to deciding the case.

### Q: Can a child rights commission order relief?

No. A child rights commission cannot order relief, compensation or any other remedy, because its findings are recommendations and not decrees. On completing an inquiry it may recommend that the government grant interim relief to the victim or the victim's family, recommend the initiation of prosecution or other action, or approach the Supreme Court or a High Court for directions. The power to order relief rests with the government that accepts a recommendation, with the Child Welfare Committee in a care and protection case, and with the courts. A complainant who needs an enforceable order must therefore run the decisional proceedings alongside any commission complaint.

### Q: How do you complain to a child rights commission?

A complaint may be made in any form: by letter to the National Commission in Delhi or to the State Commission, in person, or online through the Commission's eBaalNidan portal. No fee is charged, no format is prescribed and no lawyer is required. The complainant need not be the child or a relative; any person, including a teacher, a doctor, a social worker or a child, may bring a violation to the Commission's notice, and the Commission may also act on a newspaper report on its own motion. The complaint should identify the child, the place, the institution or authority involved and what happened, with dates and documents where available.

### Q: What does a Children's Court decide?

A Children's Court under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act tries offences against children and offences involving the violation of child rights, with the object of speedy trial. It is a court specified by the State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, either as at least one court for the state or as a Court of Session for each district, and cases before it are conducted by a Special Public Prosecutor. In practice, most prosecutions for offences against children are tried by the special courts designated under the sexual offences statute, which occupy much of the field the Children's Court was designed for.

### Q: Do all states have a child rights commission?

No. The Act obliges the Central Government to constitute the National Commission but only permits a State Government to constitute a State Commission, using the word may where the national provision uses shall. Most states have constituted commissions over time, but the position is uneven: some commissions were set up years after the Act, some have sat without a Chairperson or with Members' places unfilled for long periods, and some have functioned with skeleton staff. Where no functioning State Commission exists, complaints that belong at the state level travel to the National Commission in Delhi or go unexamined, which is the design's weakest point.

### Q: Which other laws does the child rights commission monitor?

Three statutes assign the commissions monitoring work beyond their parent Act. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 makes the National and State Commissions responsible for examining the safeguards for the education right and inquiring into complaints about free and compulsory education. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 designates both commissions to monitor the implementation of that Act's provisions. The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 assigns them to monitor the implementation of the juvenile justice system, with the same inquiry powers and a duty to include that work in their annual reports.

### Q: Why are child rights commissions criticised?

The standing criticism is that the commissions cannot enforce anything: their recommendations can be ignored, their interim relief is a request, their reports are laid late and debated rarely, and complainants who expected an order receive a recommendation. A second criticism concerns capacity, since several State Commissions have been unconstituted or chairless for long periods, leaving the statutory first forum empty. A third concerns independence, because appointments, grants and the secretariat all rest with the governments being monitored. Defenders answer that the commissions were designed as inquiry and recommendation bodies on the human rights commission model, and that the criticism is of Parliament's design rather than of the bodies exercising it.

### Q: Who appoints the Chairperson and Members of the NCPCR, and on whose recommendation?

The Central Government appoints the Chairperson and the six Members by notification. The Chairperson is appointed on the recommendation of a three-member Selection Committee constituted by the Central Government and chaired by the Minister in charge of the Ministry of Women and Child Development, the Commission's administrative ministry. The Act originally named the Minister for Human Resource Development; an amendment in 2006 substituted the Women and Child Development ministry before the Commission was constituted. The Members carry no statutory selection committee in the text and are appointed by the government from persons meeting the eminence and experience criteria in the Act, at least two of the six being women.

### Q: How long is the term of an NCPCR Chairperson or Member, and what are the age limits?

The Chairperson and every Member hold office for a term of three years from the date of assuming office, and no one may hold the office for more than two terms, so six years is the maximum service. Age ceilings cut across the terms: a Chairperson may not hold office after attaining sixty-five years of age, and a Member may not hold office after attaining sixty. A casual vacancy caused by death, resignation or removal must be filled within ninety days by a fresh appointment, and the person appointed serves only the remainder of the term. The founding Chairperson, Shantha Sinha, completed the full two terms between 2007 and 2013.

### Q: Why is the child rights commission Act called the 2005 Act when it is numbered Act 4 of 2006?

Both dates are correct, and each records a different event. Parliament passed the bill in December 2005, and the statute's short title therefore names it the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005. The President's assent, however, was given on 20 January 2006, and an Indian Act takes its number in the Gazette sequence of the year of assent and publication, which made it Act 4 of 2006. The same pattern recurs across the statute book wherever a bill passed late in one year is assented to early in the next, and the juvenile justice statute in this field carries the same pairing of a 2015 title with a 2016 number.

### Q: Who are the six Members of the NCPCR and which fields must they come from?

The six Members are appointed from amongst persons of eminence, ability, integrity, standing and experience in six fields named in the Act, one field for the spread of the body's expertise: education; child health, care, welfare or child development; juvenile justice or care of neglected or marginalised children or children with disabilities; elimination of child labour or children in distress; child psychology or sociology; and laws relating to children. At least two of the six must be women. Together with the Chairperson, who must have done outstanding work for the welfare of children, the seven form a deliberately multidisciplinary body in which no seat is reserved for a judge or a civil servant.

### Q: What is the difference between the NCPCR and a Child Welfare Committee?

The two bodies differ in the one thing that matters to a family: a Child Welfare Committee decides, and the NCPCR recommends. A Child Welfare Committee is a district-level bench under the juvenile justice statute that receives the individual child in need of care and protection and passes orders about that child's custody, restoration, placement and care, orders that operate directly on the child and the institution. The NCPCR never decides an individual child's case. It inquires into violations and the working of the system, inspects the institutions in which committees place children, and recommends action to governments. Custody questions belong to the Committee; systemic failure belongs to the Commission.

### Q: What is eBaalNidan and what can be reported through it?

eBaalNidan is the National Commission's online complaint portal, opened in 2015, through which any person may report a violation of a child's rights from anywhere in the country and then track the progress of the complaint. It receives complaints across the Commission's whole jurisdiction: abuse and exploitation, children in institutions, denial of admission or other education grievances within the Commission's monitoring mandate, child labour, trafficking, neglect and non-implementation of the laws protecting children. The portal replaced distance with a web form for complainants far from Delhi or a state capital, although the inquiry that follows a portal complaint travels the same statutory route as a complaint made by letter.

### Q: Can the government refuse to accept an NCPCR recommendation?

Yes, and the Act itself makes the refusal visible rather than preventing it. The Commission's annual and special reports must be laid before Parliament or the State Legislature together with a memorandum of the action taken or proposed on its recommendations and the reasons for non-acceptance of any recommendation, within one year of the report's receipt. A government may therefore decline to prosecute, to grant relief or to change a practice the Commission has criticised, but it must say so in a document placed before the legislature and must record its reasons. The obligation the statute enforces is the obligation to explain, and its sanction is parliamentary and public scrutiny.

### Q: Can the NCPCR approach the Supreme Court or a High Court directly?

Yes. Among the steps the Act allows the Commission on completing an inquiry is to approach the Supreme Court or the High Court concerned for such directions, orders or writs as that court may deem necessary. The Commission enters the court as an applicant, not as a court itself: it places its inquiry record before the bench and asks for the binding order it has no power to issue. This route is the design's bridge between recommendation and enforcement, and courts have also drawn on the Commission as a fact-finding and monitoring instrument in proceedings concerning children. It is powerful but slow, and the Commission uses it for a small fraction of its findings.

### Q: Is there a time limit within which a complaint must be brought to the NCPCR?

The Act prescribes no limitation period for complaints, no form and no fee. That silence is a deliberate contrast with the human rights commission's statute, which bars inquiry into an alleged violation after one year from the date of the act constituting it. The absence of a clock suits the subject, because violations against children commonly come to light years after the event, through a teacher, a doctor, a rescue or the survivor's own adulthood. Practical limits still apply: evidence decays, records are lost and officials are transferred, so a delay that the statute forgives can still defeat the inquiry the Commission is asked to conduct.

### Q: What happens if the same matter is pending before a State Commission and the NCPCR?

The National Commission is barred from inquiring into it. Section 13(2) of the Act provides that the Commission shall not inquire into any matter which is pending before a State Commission or any other commission duly constituted under law. The rule keeps the two tiers from running parallel inquiries and inconsistent recommendations on the same facts, and it makes the State Commission the first forum for matters arising within its state. Its hard edge appears where a State Commission is dormant: pendency before a body that is not sitting can still exclude the national body, which is why the constitution and staffing of the state tier matters to complainants far beyond their own states.

### Q: Who constitutes a State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, and is a state obliged to set one up?

A State Commission is constituted by the State Government of the state concerned, by notification, and no, the Act does not oblige a state to set one up. Section 17 uses the permissive word may for the states, where Section 3 obliges the Central Government to constitute the National Commission. Once constituted, a State Commission mirrors the national body: a Chairperson and six Members, at least two of them women, from the same six fields, appointed on a selection committee's recommendation for the Chairperson, with three-year terms, the same inquiry powers and the same recommendatory outcomes, and reports laid before the State Legislature with an action-taken memorandum.

### Q: How is the NCPCR funded, and to whom is it accountable?

The Commission is funded by grants from the Central Government, paid after due appropriation by Parliament in such sums as the government thinks fit, and its salaries and administrative expenses are met from those grants. Its accounts are audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India, whose report is laid before Parliament. It answers for its work through its annual and special reports to the Central Government and the State Governments concerned, which must be laid before the legislatures with memoranda of action taken, and it must furnish returns and information about its activities as the Central Government requires. Its Members are appointed by the Central Government and may be removed by it on the grounds the Act states.
