The Ethics in Government Act of 1978 is the statute on which the federal government's ethics architecture rests, and the question the statute answers is how a government polices the financial interests and future ambitions of the officials who run it. The answer was structural rather than exhortatory. Instead of directing officials to behave well, the statute built machinery. It made senior officials disclose their finances to the public. It created an office charged with administering conflict of interest rules across the executive branch. It restricted the advocacy that former officials could sell after leaving office. It constructed a prosecutor who did not answer to the department whose senior figures that prosecutor might be called to investigate. Three of those four constructions endured as permanent features of federal government. The fourth produced the statute's constitutional climax and its most instructive ending, a provision the Supreme Court sustained and Congress then allowed to die.

![The Ethics in Government Act of 1978, the post-Watergate statute that built federal ethics law on disclosure, an ethics office, post-employment bars, and the independent counsel](/assets/images/blog/blog-09.webp)

The formal identity is exact. The Ethics in Government Act of 1978 is Public Law 95-521, printed at 92 Stat. 1824 and signed on October 26, 1978, by President Jimmy Carter. The statute arrived as one instrument in a family of post-scandal reforms that included the [Federal Election Campaign Act](/2021/09/01/federal-election-campaign-act-1971/), the companion statute that rebuilt the rules governing money in federal elections, and it shared with that statute a founding judgment formed by the Watergate investigations: misconduct flourishes in unwatched spaces, and the durable remedy is to build the watching into the structure. Where the campaign finance statute watched money moving toward officeholders, the ethics statute watched the officeholders, their assets, their outside ties, and their conduct after the office had been surrendered.

A reader who masters this statute can hold four constructions apart without confusing them. The disclosure titles made the finances of senior officials a public record across all three branches of government. The ethics office gave conflict of interest administration an institutional home without giving that home a prosecutor's powers. The post-employment bars priced an official's future advocacy at the moment the official was deciding questions in office. The independent counsel title attempted the boldest separation of the four, removing a defined class of prosecutions from executive control altogether, and the fate of that title supplies the profile's central tension. The Supreme Court sustained the construction against a constitutional challenge in 1988, by a margin of seven to one. Congress allowed the provision to expire eleven years later and replaced it with a departmental regulation. The sections that follow take the four constructions in the order the statute built them, beginning with the scandal that made the statute necessary.


## The Occasion and the Enactment

The occasion for the Ethics in Government Act was an institutional failure made visible by the Watergate investigations, and the statute that followed is best read as an answer to that failure rather than as a general code of official virtue. During the administration of President Nixon, a special prosecutor named Archibald Cox held office under authority supplied by the Attorney General, and the executive removed him from that office. A prosecutor whose authority rested on executive permission occupied a position that the executive could withdraw, and the withdrawal itself proved the dependence. The arrangement had presented prosecutorial independence as a matter of executive forbearance, sustained only for as long as the executive chose to sustain it. Nothing in the structure had prevented the outcome, because the structure had lodged the protection and the threat in the same hands. The investigations therefore left behind a problem of design rather than a problem of character. The difficulty demonstrated by the removal was that individual judgment operated inside an arrangement that could be altered by the very authority the prosecutor might be called upon to examine. A response that left the arrangement untouched while exhorting better judgment within it would have missed the point the episode had established.

### How many titles did the Ethics in Government Act contain?

The act as enacted contained seven titles. Titles One through Three imposed financial disclosure on the legislative, executive, and judicial branches. Title Four created the Office of Government Ethics. Title Five set post-employment restrictions. Title Six created the special prosecutor mechanism, and Title Seven created the Senate Legal Counsel.

The premise of the statute followed directly from that diagnosis. The Ethics in Government Act proceeded on the view that disclosure and structural separation are more reliable than reliance on individual judgment. Disclosure addressed one side of the problem by moving information about official finances out of private knowledge and into a filed public record, where other institutions and members of the public could examine it without depending on an official to volunteer an account of personal interests. Structural separation addressed the other side by distributing functions among offices and branches so that a single authority would not control every step of a process that might reach that authority itself. Neither device assumed that officials lacked judgment. Both devices assumed that judgment, however sound, provided an insufficient foundation for public confidence when the arrangement allowed the person exercising the judgment to control the conditions under which the judgment was exercised. A filed report could be reviewed by an ethics office other than the filer. A prosecutorial appointment could be placed in hands other than those of the department that would otherwise direct the inquiry. A restriction on later representation could operate by rule rather than by appeal to the departing official to recognize a conflict unaided. In each instance the statute substituted a verifiable procedure for a private assurance.

Congress enacted the statute alongside other post-scandal reforms, and placement within that company matters for understanding the measure Congress produced. The act did not emerge as a solitary response to a single episode. The removal of a prosecutor who served by executive permission belonged to a broader loss of confidence in arrangements that asked institutions to police themselves through discretion alone, and the legislative response of the period moved on several fronts at once. Within that setting, the act occupied a defined share of the work, addressing money in public life through financial disclosure, conflict of interest standards through a dedicated ethics office, the movement of officials into private representation through restrictions that followed an official beyond the term of service, and prosecution itself through a mechanism for appointing a prosecutor outside the ordinary departmental chain when specified conditions were met. Grouping the measure with its companion reforms clarifies why the act reached so many subjects under one cover. The subjects shared a common premise, and Congress treated that premise as a single legislative problem even though the solutions took different institutional forms.

The formal enactment was compact in its particulars. The measure became Public Law 95-521, reported at 92 Stat. 1824, and President Jimmy Carter signed the act on October 26, 1978. A public law number and a Statutes at Large citation mark the point at which proposals about institutional design acquired the force of law and became available for administration, litigation, amendment, and repeal on the same terms as any other statute. A statute prompted by the exposure of dependence inside one administration thus became law under another administration, through the ordinary processes of passage and presidential approval, and the mechanism at its center would later be tested in the Supreme Court, sustained there, and then permitted by Congress to expire. Each later development depended on the enactment having taken the form it took. Because Congress had written the special prosecutor mechanism into a statute rather than leaving the office to departmental practice, a court could assess the arrangement against the Constitution. Because Congress had written the other titles into the same statute, later Congresses could preserve most of the edifice while allowing one title to lapse, and the history of the act became a history of selective survival rather than wholesale acceptance or rejection.

The architecture of the act as enacted carried seven titles, and a reader who follows the title structure in order encounters the logic of the whole design. Titles I, II, and III imposed financial disclosure branch by branch, with one title for the legislative branch, one title for the executive branch, and one title for the judicial branch. Title IV created the Office of Government Ethics. Title V set post-employment conflict of interest restrictions, amending 18 U.S.C. section 207. Title VI created the special prosecutor mechanism, amending Title 28, and the mechanism later carried the name independent counsel. Title VII created the Senate Legal Counsel, a minor title that receives a passing mention in a profile organized around the larger structures. The order was not accidental, and the order was not merely administrative. Congress began with disclosure, the obligation that would touch the largest number of officials and produce the public record on which much of the rest of the system depended. Congress then supplied an office to direct the executive branch side of the ethics regime, then addressed the conduct of officials after government service ended, then addressed the exceptional case in which ordinary prosecutorial arrangements were judged inadequate, and then closed with the Senate Legal Counsel title. The progression moved from the routine to the exceptional, from an annual filing obligation borne by thousands of officials to a prosecutorial appointment triggered only under specified conditions involving covered persons and alleged violations of federal criminal law.

How a reader parses a statute title structure determines what the reader expects each part to do, and the Ethics in Government Act rewards attention to that parsing. A title in a statute of this kind marks a distinct grant of duties, a distinct set of persons covered, and often a distinct institutional home, and confusion about the structure produces confusion about the substance. A reader who treats the act as a single undifferentiated ethics code will expect the Office of Government Ethics to perform every function the act mentions, including prosecution, although the act assigned prosecution under the special prosecutor title to a court appointed officer and left the ethics office to advise, regulate, and review. A reader who treats the disclosure titles as a preamble to the prosecutorial title will undervalue the filings that constitute the part of the statute most citizens encounter, although the disclosure regime operated continuously while the prosecutorial mechanism operated only when triggered. A reader who folds the post-employment title into the disclosure title will miss the difference between revealing an interest and restricting an activity, although the act treated revelation and restriction as separate instruments aimed at separate risks. Careful parsing also explains why later events affected the titles unevenly, since titles could be amended, reauthorized, or allowed to expire separately.

The disclosure titles illustrate the value of that care, because the branch by branch arrangement in Titles I, II, and III embodied a judgment about coverage. Congress did not impose financial disclosure on the executive branch alone, although the occasion for reform arose from events inside the executive branch and although the executive branch held the largest administrative apparatus for collecting and reviewing reports. Title I addressed legislative personnel, Title II addressed executive personnel, and Title III addressed judicial personnel, so that senior officials across all three branches filed public reports under the same statute. The covered filers included the President, the Vice President, Members of Congress, candidates for those offices, senior executive officials at or above a senior pay threshold, judges and justices, and senior judicial and congressional staff. The report content reached sources and amounts of income, gifts and reimbursements, the identity and approximate categorical value of assets held and liabilities owed, transactions in real property, commodities, and securities, outside positions held, and agreements and arrangements that included future employment and continuing participation in employee benefit plans, together with specified interests of a spouse and dependent children. The filing rhythm ran from entry into office within thirty days, to annual filing by May 15, to a filing on leaving office. The supervising ethics office for each filer reviewed the reports, and the reports were available to the public on request, with the statute restricting use of report information for commercial and other improper purposes. Arranged across three titles, the regime presented disclosure as a general obligation of senior public office rather than as a burden imposed on one branch by the others.

Title IV then supplied the institutional center for the executive branch portion of the regime by creating the Office of Government Ethics. As enacted, the office stood within the Office of Personnel Management, and a Director appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate headed the office for a five year term. The functions assigned to the office explain why a separate title was warranted. The office held overall direction of executive branch conflict of interest policy, issued regulations and formal advisory opinions, reviewed public financial disclosure reports of presidential nominees and senior officials for conflicts, oversaw agency ethics programs carried out by designated agency ethics officials in each agency, and provided training and education. The office advised and regulated. The office did not prosecute, and the office did not enforce the criminal conflict of interest statutes, because criminal enforcement rested with the Department of Justice and discipline rested with the employing agency. The distinction mattered to the architecture. Congress placed the day to day direction of ethics policy in a small specialized office while leaving coercive enforcement in the institutions that held enforcement authority elsewhere in the government, and the separation kept advice available to officials who sought guidance before acting without converting the advising office into a prosecutor of the officials the office advised. In 1989 the office was separated from the Office of Personnel Management and continued as a small independent agency, a later adjustment that altered the institutional address of the title without altering the basic assignment of functions the title had created in 1978.

Title V addressed a risk that neither disclosure nor an ethics office could reach on its own, because the risk arose after an official had left the office in which the relevant knowledge and responsibility had been acquired. Title V set post-employment conflict of interest restrictions, amending 18 U.S.C. section 207, and the resulting framework contained three bars of differing duration. A permanent bar provided that a former official could never represent another person before the government on a particular matter involving specific parties in which the official had participated personally and substantially while in office. A two year bar reached matters that had been pending under the official responsibility during the final year of service, even without personal participation. A one year cooling off bar provided that senior officials could not, for one year after leaving, make representational communications to or appearances before the former agency on behalf of another person seeking official action. The bars restricted representation and communication on behalf of others, and the bars did not bar employment itself, a limitation that kept the title aimed at the use of public office in later private advocacy rather than at the fact of later private work. Within the architecture, Title V extended the premise of the act across time. Disclosure governed the record an official made while serving, the ethics office governed the standards and review that surrounded service, and the post-employment title governed a defined set of activities after service had ended, so that the protections of the statute did not expire at the moment an official departed with knowledge of matters handled in office.

Title VI occupied a different register from the titles that preceded the title, because Title VI concerned criminal prosecution rather than disclosure, advice, or later representation. Title VI amended Title 28 and created the special prosecutor mechanism, later renamed the independent counsel. The design responded directly to the occasion with which the profile opened. Where the prosecutor in the earlier arrangement had served under authority the Attorney General had supplied and the executive could withdraw, the statutory mechanism placed the appointment in a judicial body after a defined preliminary process, defined the jurisdiction of the appointed counsel, gave the counsel the full investigative and prosecutorial powers of the Department of Justice within that jurisdiction, and restricted removal to the Attorney General personally and only for good cause. The arrangement therefore attempted to secure by statute an independence that departmental practice had failed to secure by permission. The attempt carried a constitutional cost that the other titles did not carry. Disclosure obligations, an advisory ethics office, and post-employment bars regulated officials without transferring prosecutorial power outside the executive branch, while Title VI did transfer the exercise of that power in covered cases to an officer appointed by judges and removable only on a restricted ground. That difference explains why Title VI generated the litigation, the repeated reauthorization debates, and the eventual lapse that dominate the later history of the act, while the other titles proceeded through amendment and administration without a comparable challenge to the place of each title in the constitutional structure.

Title VII completed the statute with the creation of the Senate Legal Counsel, and proportionality requires that the title receive brief treatment. The title formed part of the enacted architecture, and a profile that omitted the title would misstate the structure Congress enacted. The title did not, however, carry the weight of the larger titles in the operation of the act, and the title did not supply one of the organizing pillars around which the profile is built. The Senate Legal Counsel title performed a minor institutional function beside mechanisms that reached every senior official in the government or reallocated prosecutorial power in covered cases.

The seven titles resolve into four pillars, and the resolution is an act of interpretation grounded in the structure rather than a simplification imposed on the structure from outside. The first pillar is public financial disclosure, and the pillar rests on Titles I, II, and III together because the three titles performed one function across three branches. Counting the disclosure titles as three separate pillars would fragment a single regime into branch fragments and would obscure the judgment that disclosure was a general obligation of senior office. The second pillar is the Office of Government Ethics, resting on Title IV alone, because the office supplied a distinct function that no other title performed, the direction and regulation of executive branch ethics policy. The third pillar is the post-employment restrictions, resting on Title V, because the bars governed a distinct period in the life of an official and a distinct kind of conduct. The fourth pillar is the independent counsel, resting on Title VI, because the mechanism supplied a distinct answer to the prosecutorial problem that had occasioned the statute. Title VII stands outside the pillar structure as a minor title, acknowledged in the architecture and set aside in the organization. The four pillar frame therefore preserves every major function in the act while grouping the functions by the work each function performed, and the frame gives the profile a structure that follows the statute rather than rearranging the statute to suit the profile.

That framing also prepares the central arc of the profile, because the four pillars did not share a single fate after 1978. Three pillars endure. The disclosure regime continued through amendment, including the adjustment supplied by the Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge Act of 2012, and the disclosure provisions of the 1978 act were never repealed. The Office of Government Ethics continued after separation from the Office of Personnel Management in 1989 as a small independent agency. The post-employment framework continued through extension and tightening, including the changes carried by the Ethics Reform Act of 1989. The fourth pillar followed a different course. The independent counsel mechanism was sustained by the Supreme Court in Morrison v. Olson in 1988, in a decision that upheld the appointment arrangement and the good cause removal restriction against constitutional challenge, and Congress then allowed the mechanism to expire on June 30, 1999, without reauthorization. A pillar that survived judicial scrutiny therefore ended by legislative choice rather than by judicial invalidation, and a mechanism created to place prosecutorial independence on a statutory foundation was replaced on the day of expiration by departmental regulations for a special counsel operating inside the Department of Justice. An account of the act must explain not only how Congress built four structures on one premise in 1978, but also why three held a place in the law across the decades that followed while the fourth, after vindication in the Supreme Court, was left by Congress to lapse.

The statute's seven titles resolve into four operative pillars, and the table below states the whole profile in one view: what each pillar established, where it sits in the enacted structure, and what became of it. The exercise generalizes. A reader confronting any unfamiliar statute can separate the law into its operative constructions before arguing about any one of them, which is the discipline set out in [how to read a federal statute](/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/), and the ethics statute rewards the discipline more than most, because its four pillars had four different fates and cannot be described accurately in a single sentence about the act as a whole.

## The Financial Disclosure Pillar

The Ethics in Government Act of 1978 rested on a premise stated plainly in its design. Disclosure and structural separation were more reliable than reliance on individual judgment. An official could recognize a conflict of interest in some cases and fail to recognize one in others, and a system built entirely on private self assessment would leave the public with no way to test whether the recognition had happened at all. The disclosure titles answered that weakness by converting a private judgment into a public record. The statute required senior officials to place their financial lives on paper, in a standardized form, at regular intervals, and to make that paper available for inspection by anyone with a reason to look.

Disclosure operated differently from the statute's other pillars because disclosure was preventive and continuous rather than episodic. A post employment bar mattered only when an official left government. An investigation mattered only when credible information suggested a crime. Disclosure mattered every year, for every covered official, whether or not anyone suspected wrongdoing. The routine character of the obligation was the point. By making revelation ordinary, the statute lowered the cost of scrutiny and raised the cost of concealment, without requiring any enforcement official to open a case, issue a subpoena, or accuse anyone of anything. The report arrived because the calendar required it, and the public character of the report meant that scrutiny could begin without permission from the institution being scrutinized.

### Who has to file financial disclosure reports under the Ethics in Government Act?

Senior officials in all three branches file public reports. The duty covers the President, the Vice President, Members of Congress and candidates for those offices, senior executive officials, judges and justices, and senior congressional and judicial staff. Each filer reports to the supervising ethics office for the filer's own branch.

The breadth of that coverage deserves attention, because the statute drew its filing population from the top of each branch rather than from a single institution. The President and the Vice President filed. Members of Congress filed, and candidates for federal office filed as well, a choice that extended the obligation to persons seeking power rather than only to persons already exercising it. The candidacy rule closed an obvious gap. A candidate could otherwise campaign while keeping the financial interests that might shape future decisions entirely private until after an election had settled who would govern. In the executive departments and agencies, the duty reached senior officials at or above a senior pay threshold, tying the obligation to rank and responsibility rather than to job title alone. In the judicial branch, judges and justices filed.

Around the principals stood the staff. Senior congressional staff and senior judicial staff filed as well, reflecting a judgment that influence in government does not stop at the persons whose names appear on ballots or commissions. Each element of the population corresponded to a distinct route by which money could influence a decision. An elected official faced reelection and its accompanying pressures. A senior executive official exercised delegated authority over contracts, regulations, grants, and enforcement choices. A judge or justice decided cases in which a financial interest, even a modest one held through a spouse or a dependent child, could bear on the appearance and the reality of impartial judgment. A regime covering only the most visible figures would have missed the less visible hands through which much governmental work passes.

### Three Titles for Three Branches

The statute wrote the disclosure duty three times. Title I addressed legislative personnel. Title II addressed executive personnel. Title III addressed judicial personnel. A drafter seeking economy alone could have written a single title, defined the covered population once, and applied the definition to the whole government. The decision to repeat the duty branch by branch was a considered structural choice. Each branch guards its internal administration with some jealousy, and a single disclosure title administered by a single office would have raised immediate questions about which branch controlled the personal financial information of the others. An executive office collecting and reviewing the reports of Members of Congress and federal judges would have placed the executive in possession of sensitive material concerning the very officials charged with checking executive power. The three title structure dissolved the problem by keeping each branch's disclosure house in its own hands.

Review followed the same design. Reports went to the filer's supervising ethics office within the filer's own branch, and that office reviewed the filing for conflicts of interest and for completeness. The executive branch reviewed executive filers through its ethics structure. The legislative branch reviewed legislative filers through its own ethics offices. The judicial branch reviewed judicial filers through its own administrative channels. The statute thus created three parallel systems with substantially parallel content, rather than one system with one custodian. Separation of powers, in this corner of the law, took the practical form of separate forms, separate custodians, and separate reviewers, each operating inside the branch whose personnel stood under the duty.

The arrangement carried a practical advantage alongside the constitutional one. A supervising ethics office reviewing the reports of its own branch understood the work of that branch from the inside. Reviewers knew which decisions a given office made, which industries a given committee oversaw, and which categories of cases a given court heard, and that institutional knowledge sharpened the review. Routing review through the branch's own office therefore joined a constitutional rationale to a practical one. At the same time, the repetition of the duty across three titles gave the content of disclosure a uniformity that wholly decentralized drafting might have lost. The categories demanded from a senator, a senior executive official, and a federal judge were substantially the same categories, because the underlying risk was substantially the same risk. Substance was harmonized, category by category, in deference to the equal claim of the public to know the financial circumstances of senior power wherever senior power sat.

### What a Report Must Disclose

The content of the report formed the core of the pillar, and the statute specified that content in categories broad enough to capture the ordinary ways in which private finance and public duty intersect. The first category reached income. A filer reported the sources and amounts of income, identifying who had paid and how much, so that a reader could see not only that a filer had resources but where the resources originated. Source mattered as much as amount. The second and third categories reached the balance sheet. A filer identified assets held and liabilities owed, each stated in categorical values rather than exact figures, sketching holdings and obligations in a form detailed enough to reveal the direction and rough scale of every interest. A fourth category captured movement. Transactions in real property, commodities, and securities were reportable, because movement between annual portraits was itself information, and a purchase or sale by an official with advance knowledge of governmental action was precisely the kind of event the disclosure regime existed to expose.

Gifts and reimbursements formed another category, distinct from salary and investment return because gifts arrive through relationships rather than markets. A filer reported gifts received and reimbursements for travel and related expenses, identifying the source, so that hospitality and generosity directed at senior officials entered the public record. Outside positions formed a further category. A filer reported positions held outside government, as an officer, director, employee, or partner of an enterprise or organization, because a title held in a private entity could divide loyalty as effectively as a payment. Agreements and arrangements completed the forward looking portion of the report. A filer disclosed agreements concerning future employment and arrangements for continuing participation in employee benefit plans maintained by a former employer.

The final category extended the report beyond the filer's own name. Specified interests of a spouse and of dependent children entered the disclosure, within limits the statute defined. The extension recognized the economics of a household. Assets held by a spouse were not sealed away from a family's common welfare, and a rule reaching only assets titled in the filer's name would have invited evasion through the simplest of transfers. Taken together, the categories described a financial biography rather than a single financial fact. Income explained where money came from. Assets and liabilities explained where a filer stood. Transactions explained how the position had changed. Outside positions explained which institutions held a claim on the filer's time and loyalty. A reader equipped with that biography, repeated annually across a career, could follow the financial story of a public life in sequence, and an ethics reviewer could intervene where the story began to intersect with the duties of the office.

### Why Values Are Categorical Rather Than Exact

The decision to state assets and liabilities in categorical values, in ranges of approximate worth rather than in exact amounts, was a deliberate calibration. Exact figures would have produced a more precise report and a more invasive one. A reviewer asking whether an official should participate in a matter affecting a company needed to know that the official held an interest in the company and roughly how substantial that interest was. Recusal analysis turned on the existence and general magnitude of an interest, not on its value computed to the dollar. Categorical reporting therefore supplied the information the system actually used, in the form the system actually used it, while withholding a precision that would have served curiosity more readily than oversight.

The categorical approach also protected the integrity of the filing process itself. Ranges absorbed valuation uncertainty gracefully. The tradeoff carried a genuine cost. Ranges can shelter significant variation, and two holdings assigned to the same category can differ considerably in real value, so a reader receives an informed approximation rather than an audited account. The statute accepted approximation as sufficient because the pillar aimed at visibility rather than at accounting. A further consideration concerned participation. Disclosure law operates on the willingness of qualified persons to accept senior office, and a requirement perceived as a complete surrender of financial privacy would have raised the personal price of service and generated resistance capable of weakening compliance across the board. Categories lowered that friction without surrendering the essential disclosure. The ranges in the reports are not a defect or a loophole in the design.

### The Filing Rhythm From Entry to Departure

Disclosure under the statute was not a single event but a rhythm, and the timing carried much of the system's logic. The first filing came on entering office, within thirty days. The entry report captured the financial position a new official brought through the door of public power. A baseline performed quiet but essential work. Without one, an official whose wealth grew during service could attribute the growth to any source at all, and no reader could distinguish investment fortune from the yield of office. The entry filing also forced an early encounter between the new official and the ethics structure of the branch, within the first month of service, so that advice about recusal, divestiture, or restructuring could arrive before a collision between private interests and public duties rather than after one.

The second element of the rhythm was the annual filing, due by May 15 of each year. Annual repetition converted disclosure from a portrait into a sequence, and the sequence was where much of the informational value lived. A single report showed holdings. A run of reports showed behavior, acquisitions timed against governmental decisions, debts retired, outside positions gathered or shed, agreements formed as a departure approached. Regularity also disciplined the filer. An official who knew that a transaction would appear in a filing within the year faced the prospect of explanation at the moment of deciding whether to undertake the transaction at all, and that anticipatory effect formed part of the pillar's preventive design. The fixed date mattered as well.

The third element closed the account. A filer submitted a termination report on leaving office, capturing the position at departure and completing the arc that the entry report had opened. Departure was a moment of particular sensitivity, since agreements about future employment disclosed in the final filings illuminated the transition from public service to private life from the inside. The termination report also prevented a quiet evasion. Without it, an official could resign ahead of a filing date and carry the financial record of the last interval of service out of public view entirely. Entry, annual, and termination filings together meant that no stretch of a senior career in office lay outside the sequence, and the continuity of the sequence, as much as the content of any single report, constituted the disclosure the statute promised.

### Public Availability and the Limits on Use

A disclosure report locked in a drawer would have been a compliance exercise rather than a pillar of public ethics law, and the statute therefore made the reports public. Reports were available to the public on request, a formulation that placed the burden of inspection on the interested citizen while guaranteeing that the inspection could occur. Availability was the operative mechanism of the entire title structure. Every other feature of the pillar, the categories, the rhythm, the review, depended for its force on the knowledge that the finished document could be read by outsiders. Review by a supervising ethics office supplied professional scrutiny inside the branch. Public availability supplied democratic scrutiny outside it, and the two forms of scrutiny reinforced each other. Publicity was not a byproduct of the system. Publicity was the enforcement atmosphere in which the system breathed.

Availability carried restrictions, and the restrictions clarified what the publicity was for. The statute restricted the use of report information for commercial and other improper purposes. A report could be read to evaluate the fitness and conflicts of a public official. Report information could not properly be harvested as raw material for solicitation, for commercial exploitation, or for purposes unconnected with the oversight the disclosure existed to serve. The distinction rested on the same balance that produced categorical values. The public claim on the information arose from the filer's public role, and the claim extended as far as that role justified and no farther. Use of the information to hold power accountable fell squarely within the justification. Use of the information as a mailing list or a marketing resource fell outside it, and the statute said so. News organizations built routines around the annual filing season. Research staff for opposing campaigns read the reports of rival candidates as a matter of course.

### Review, Referral, and a Persistent Error

Filing and publicity did not exhaust the system, because a report could reveal a problem as readily as it could certify the absence of one, and the statute assigned the first professional response to the receiving office. The supervising ethics office for the filer's branch reviewed each report, examining the disclosed interests against the filer's duties to determine whether a conflict of interest existed or appeared to exist, and whether corrective action was required. The routine outcomes of review were advisory and administrative. A reviewer could direct attention to a holding that required recusal from a category of matters, could recommend divestiture of an asset that collided repeatedly with the work of the office, could require additional information where an entry was incomplete or unclear, and could certify the report where the review disclosed no conflict requiring action.

Review had a harder edge where a filing suggested something beyond a manageable conflict. When information in a report, or the failure to supply information the report required, suggested a violation of law or of the governing ethics rules, the posture of the receiving office shifted from advice toward referral, and the matter could move toward the authorities charged with enforcement. The reviewing office itself occupied the advisory and regulatory side of the line. Examination, guidance, and referral belonged to the office. Prosecution did not. That division of labor kept the reviewing function approachable, since a filer could seek guidance from reviewers without treating every consultation as the opening of a prosecution, while preserving a channel through which genuine violations disclosed on the face of a filing could reach the officials empowered to act on them.

One correction belongs in this section because the error concerns the survival of the disclosure pillar itself. A recurring mistake treats the financial disclosure titles as though the titles shared the fate of the independent counsel title, which expired on June 30, 1999 after Congress declined to reauthorize that mechanism. The disclosure titles were never repealed. Titles I to III continued in force through the lapse of Title VI and beyond it, and the filing rhythm described in this section proceeded without interruption while the special prosecutor provisions passed into history. The mistake is nevertheless a mistake with practical consequences, because a citizen who believed disclosure had ended in 1999 would stop looking for reports that continued to be filed, reviewed, and made public year after year. A later section of this article addresses the amendment of the disclosure titles by the Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge Act of 2012.

The disclosure pillar is the part of the Ethics in Government Act that citizens encounter in ordinary life, and the reason is embedded in everything described in this section. The independent counsel mechanism belonged to rare and dramatic episodes and then to history. Disclosure alone produced a public artifact, repeated annually, attached to named and familiar officeholders, readable by anyone willing to ask. A voter curious about the investments of a senator, a reader following a reporter's account of a cabinet official's holdings, a researcher tracing the career of a judge through a sequence of filings, each met the statute in the same place, at the point where a private financial life had been rendered, in categorical values and named sources and dated transactions, into a public record. That encounter is the pillar working as designed.

## The Office of Government Ethics

Before the Ethics in Government Act of 1978, executive branch conflict of interest administration had no single institutional home. Standards existed, but those standards lived in scattered executive orders and in agency practice, and the arrangement left a structural gap between the rules on paper and the daily work of applying those rules to particular officials in particular circumstances. A prohibition against using public office for private gain meant little if an official facing a close question had no settled place to ask that question, and if different agencies answered similar questions in different ways without a common framework to reconcile the answers. Financial interests that might conflict with assigned duties could likewise pass unexamined until a controversy forced attention, because no central office held responsibility for reviewing disclosures at the point of entry into senior service or for setting a uniform method by which agencies examined those disclosures.

Title IV of the act answered that problem by creating an office rather than by adding another prohibition. The disclosure titles imposed obligations on officials across the three branches, and the post employment title restricted representation after service, but Title IV supplied the administrative machinery for the executive branch, an office charged with direction, guidance, and review. The premise behind the design held that disclosure and structural separation were more reliable than reliance on individual judgment alone. An office gave that premise a working form. Standards could be collected, interpreted, and taught from one place, and conflicts could be identified through a regular process instead of through accident or exposure after the fact.

### An Institutional Home Inside the Personnel Office

Title IV established the Office of Government Ethics within the Office of Personnel Management. Placement inside the personnel office reflected the nature of the work as Congress defined the work in 1978. Ethics administration resembled personnel administration in its reach and in its routine character. The office would deal with standards that applied across agencies, with forms and review procedures, with training, and with the conduct expected of officials as a condition of federal service. Housing the new office inside an existing establishment also avoided a large separate bureaucracy for a function that was coordinative by design. The ethics office was not built to run agency programs directly. The design placed a small central office at the point where government wide personnel policy was made, and left daily administration of ethics programs to the agencies themselves under central direction.

That placement carried a tension the structure never fully concealed. An office responsible for the ethics policy of the entire executive branch, including review of the disclosures of presidential nominees and the most senior officials, sat inside another agency whose leadership served within the same branch whose conduct the ethics office was meant to guide. The tension did not make the office powerless, and the statutory assignment of functions gave the Director a distinct role defined by law rather than by delegation from the personnel office. Placement nevertheless meant that independence depended in part on statutory devices built into Title IV, most visibly the method of appointment and the length of the term of the Director. The office began as a center for policy and guidance embedded in the personnel system, and the advisory character that defined the office afterward was present from the start.

### Appointment, Term, and Partial Insulation

Title IV placed the office under a Director appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate. Each element of that appointment method did separate work. Presidential appointment located responsibility in the head of the branch the office served, which fit a function concerned with executive branch standards and personnel. Senate consent added a public stage to the selection, at which the nominee for Director could be examined on the administration of conflict of interest policy before taking office. Confirmation also gave the office standing that appointment by an agency head alone would not have supplied, because the Director owed the position to a process involving both political branches and producing a record of commitments and qualifications. For an office whose influence depended heavily on persuasion, clarity, and perceived neutrality, that standing mattered as a source of authority apart from any formal power.

The Director served a five year term. The length furnished a partial insulation device, and the word partial describes the device with precision. A fixed term meant that a Director did not serve from day to day at the pleasure of the personnel office or as an ordinary subordinate within a host agency, and a five year span did not align exactly with the four year cycle of a presidential term. A Director could therefore expect, in the ordinary course, to serve across a change in administration, carrying institutional memory about interpretations, disclosure practice, and agency programs from one set of senior officials to the next. At the same time, the device stopped well short of full independence. The Director remained a presidential appointee in the executive branch, chosen through the ordinary appointments process. Insulation in Title IV was a matter of degree, a deliberate buffer rather than a wall, matching an office expected to be close enough to the executive branch to be heard and distant enough to be trusted when advice proved unwelcome.

### Direction, Regulations, and Advice Officials Could Rely On

The central function assigned to the office was overall direction of executive branch conflict of interest policy. Direction meant more than occasional statements after a controversy had drawn attention to a gap in the rules. Direction meant setting the framework within which agencies built ethics programs, answered questions from employees, reviewed disclosures, and arranged training. Through that framework, a standard that might otherwise have been read differently in different departments could receive one authoritative interpretation for the branch as a whole. Uniformity protected officials as much as uniformity constrained officials, because an official who followed the common standard had a stronger basis for confidence that conduct accepted in one agency would not be condemned under a different reading in another.

Regulations formed the most formal instrument of that direction. By issuing regulations, the office could translate broad statutory prohibitions and disclosure duties into procedures, definitions, and expectations that agencies and officials could apply before a questionable act occurred. Formal advisory opinions served a related but distinct function. An advisory opinion took the standards out of the abstract and applied those standards to a described set of facts, giving the requesting official or agency a reasoned position from the office charged with direction of the policy. Reliance gave those opinions practical weight. An official who disclosed the relevant facts, received an opinion, and acted in conformity with the opinion had acted on the best available reading of the rules from the office Congress had created to supply that reading.

The advisory function also revealed the preventive logic of the Title IV design. Conflict of interest questions often turn on the character of an interest, the degree of participation in a matter, and the relationship between an official duty and an outside position or holding. Those questions often admit of resolution without punishment if the resolution comes early enough, through recusal from a matter, divestiture of an interest, resignation from an outside position, or restructuring of an assignment. An office organized around advice could prompt those resolutions as a matter of routine. Title IV therefore placed the advisory office at the front of the process, where a question could be answered while an answer could change the outcome.

### Reviewing Disclosure at the Point of Appointment

Among the most consequential functions of the office was review of public financial disclosure reports of presidential nominees and the most senior officials. The disclosure system generated reports that listed sources and amounts of income, gifts and reimbursements, assets and liabilities in categorical values, transactions, outside positions, and agreements or arrangements, together with specified interests of a spouse and dependent children. A report of that breadth served a public purpose through availability to the public, but the report also served an administrative purpose inside the government when a trained reviewer read the report against the duties the filer was about to assume. The office performed that second function for officials at the highest level, where the range of matters within an official responsibility could be widest and where an unnoticed interest could affect decisions of the greatest consequence.

Review before appointment allowed conflicts to be identified and resolved as part of the appointment process itself. A nominee whose holdings intersected with the jurisdiction of the position could address the intersection before taking office, through arrangements worked out with the guidance of the office. Review after appointment extended the same discipline into service, because annual reports and later filings gave the office a recurring basis for comparing interests against duties and for raising questions when a new interest or a changed assignment altered the analysis. The point of review was resolution rather than exposure. Identification of a possible conflict began a process of examination and adjustment, and the process succeeded when the official entered or continued in service with the conflict removed, managed through recusal, or otherwise resolved under the standards the office administered. That success left no public event to mark the occasion, which made the function easy to undervalue in accounts of the act that counted only visible sanctions.

### Oversight Through Designated Agency Ethics Officials

The office did not administer ethics programs in each agency directly. Agency ethics programs were carried out by designated agency ethics officials in each agency, and the office oversaw those programs. The division of labor reflected both scale and knowledge. The executive branch employed officials in numbers and occupations that no small central office could counsel individually, and many conflict questions turned on facts about assignments, programs, and reporting relationships that officials inside an agency were better placed to know. A designated agency ethics official stood at that point of local knowledge, receiving questions, reviewing reports within the agency, and applying branch wide standards to the work of that agency. The central office supplied the standards, the regulations, the advisory framework, and the oversight that held local programs to a common level of seriousness.

Oversight of that kind depended on methods other than command. The office could examine how agency programs functioned, press for correction where a program fell short of the framework, train the officials who ran the programs, and use the authority of central direction to keep a weak program from becoming a safe harbor for lax practice. Training and education, an assigned function in its own right, supported every other function in the design. Regulations that officials never learned had limited effect, and disclosure forms completed without an understanding of the underlying standards produced paper compliance rather than real identification of conflicts. Training for designated agency ethics officials multiplied the reach of the central office, because each official trained at the center carried the standards back into an agency and applied those standards to questions the center would never see.

### Separation and Continuation as a Small Independent Agency

In 1989 the office was separated from the Office of Personnel Management and continued as a small independent agency. The change altered the institutional setting without altering the essential character of the functions. The Director no longer operated from inside a host agency charged with personnel management for the branch, and the office acquired a formal status that matched the branch wide responsibility assigned in 1978. Separation removed the awkwardness of an ethics office for the entire executive branch sitting within another agency, and separation gave the office a distinct identity in dealings with agencies, with nominees, and with the public over the meaning of its guidance. An official seeking advice dealt after 1989 with an independent agency whose sole mission was ethics policy rather than with a component of a larger establishment whose primary mission lay elsewhere.

The scale of the agency after separation deserves emphasis because scale explained method. The office continued as a small agency, and smallness was not an accidental limitation imposed on an office designed for a larger footprint. The functions of direction, rulemaking, advisory opinions, disclosure review at the highest level, program oversight, and training could be performed by a compact staff precisely because the design relied on designated agency ethics officials throughout the government to carry the program into each workplace. Separation therefore completed the structure that Title IV had sketched. The center held independence, the agencies held administration, and the connection between center and agencies ran through standards and oversight rather than through direct operation.

### Does the Office of Government Ethics investigate and punish officials?

No. The Office of Government Ethics advises executive branch officials, issues regulations and advisory opinions, reviews certain financial disclosure reports, and oversees agency ethics programs. It does not prosecute offenses or impose discipline. Criminal enforcement rests with the Department of Justice, and discipline rests with the employing agency.

The distinction stated in that answer is the analytical core of Title IV, and misunderstanding of the distinction has persisted as a recurring error in public discussion of the office. The widespread assumption that the ethics office investigates and punishes follows a natural but mistaken line of reasoning. Title IV withheld enforcement powers deliberately. Criminal prosecution of conflict of interest offenses rested with the Department of Justice, the institution that held the prosecutorial power of the executive branch and the responsibility for bringing criminal cases in court. Discipline rested with the employing agency, the institution that held the ordinary authority of an employer over assignments, reprimands, suspension, and removal. The ethics office occupied neither role.

Separation of advice from enforcement produced practical consequences for the behavior the system encouraged. An official uncertain about a proposed course of action could approach the ethics structure for guidance without treating the approach itself as an admission that invited punishment from the office that supplied the guidance. The channel for questions remained open because the office that answered questions did not also decide prosecutions. Agencies likewise dealt with the central office as a source of standards and review rather than as an outside investigator arriving after a failure, which made routine cooperation, training, and early correction easier to sustain.

### What an Advisory Model Gains and What the Model Gives Up

The gains of the advisory model followed from prevention. Uniform standards, set centrally and applied through agency programs, reduced the variation that had characterized the period before 1978, when executive orders and agency practice carried the field without a single directing office. Preventive resolution offered a second gain, because a conflict identified through disclosure review or through a request for advice could be resolved before participation in a matter converted a manageable interest into an offense. Recusal, divestiture, and restructuring of duties cost far less, in institutional terms, than prosecution or discipline after the fact, and those remedies protected the integrity of the government decision itself rather than merely punishing the official who had compromised the decision.

The costs of the model were the mirror image of the gains. An office that advised, regulated, and reviewed, but did not enforce, held no independent sanction when advice was ignored. An official who received guidance and departed from the guidance faced consequences only if another institution acted, through prosecution by the Department of Justice in a criminal case or through discipline by the employing agency in an administrative case. Dependence on referral and on the decisions of other institutions therefore stood at the center of the design. The ethics office could identify a problem, state the standard, review the disclosure, and press an agency program toward correction, without holding the final word on punishment. Title IV built an office to make violations less likely rather than an office to punish violations after the fact.

That choice placed the Office of Government Ethics in a distinctive position among the reforms of 1978. The act trusted prosecutors with criminal enforcement, trusted agencies with discipline, trusted disclosure with public scrutiny, and trusted the ethics office with the earlier and quieter work of direction, advice, and review. The limits of the office were not defects concealed by the design. The limits were the design, stated plainly in the allocation of functions in Title IV, and an accurate account of the Ethics in Government Act has to hold both halves of that allocation together, the genuine authority to direct policy across a branch and the deliberate absence of power to prosecute or punish.

## The Revolving Door and the Post-Employment Bars

The third pillar of the Ethics in Government Act addressed a problem that disclosure alone could not reach. A financial report filed in office could reveal an interest while an official held power, but it said nothing about the value that service might acquire after the official left. Title V, amending 18 U.S.C. section 207, attacked that problem by restricting what a former official could do on behalf of private parties before the government the official had served. The expression revolving door described the movement the title governed, the passage of personnel from public office into private employment and back toward the agencies they had left, carrying knowledge, relationships, and standing acquired at public expense. Title V did not attempt to stop the movement itself. It narrowed sharply what a person who passed through the door could sell on the other side, resting on a distinction that runs through every part of section 207, between holding a private job and representing a private interest before the government. The first remained lawful. The second, within defined matters, agencies, and periods, became a federal crime.

Section 207 did not impose a single uniform ban. It imposed calibrated bars, each measured by the closeness between the official and the matter or agency involved. Personal and substantial participation in a specific matter produced a permanent bar. Responsibility for a matter without personal participation produced a bar lasting two years. Senior rank and access to a former agency produced a cooling-off period of one year. Duration was therefore a measure of proximity, and at the highest degree of proximity the separation never ended.

### What Counts as Representation Under the Statute

The operative act in section 207 was representation. In ordinary usage the word could describe almost any service performed for an employer, but the statute used the term in a narrower and more technical sense. Representation meant acting as an agent or attorney for another person before the government, presenting that other person's interests to federal officers or agencies in connection with a matter in which the United States had an interest. A former official represented another person when the official appeared before an agency, sent a communication intended for the government, or otherwise stepped forward as the channel through which a private party addressed federal power. The government was the audience, the private party was the principal, and the former official stood between them in the posture of an advocate. That posture, and not private employment in general, was what the bars withdrew for their respective periods.

That definition carried consequences in both directions. It reached conduct that never involved a formal hearing room. A telephone call to a former colleague, a letter to an agency, or a meeting arranged to press a client's position could each constitute representation if the communication sought official action on a covered matter. Informality provided no shelter, because influence in administration often travels through informal channels. The definition also left a wide field of private activity untouched. Work performed entirely inside a private organization fell outside the bars, and a former official could analyze, advise, draft, plan, and manage behind the scenes, so long as the official did not personally carry the employer's case to the government during the restricted period. The line ran between advising a client how to approach the government and approaching the government for the client.

### Can a Former Official Work for a Company That Does Business With the Government?

Yes. The post-employment bars restrict representation, not employment itself. A former official may accept a job with a private employer, earn a salary, and provide internal advice, provided the official does not represent that employer before the government on matters or before agencies that the statute places off limits for the applicable period.

That answer reflects a deliberate legislative choice. Government service was not a lifetime career for many senior appointees in 1978, who arrived from private life and expected to return to private life when their appointments ended. A statute that barred employment with any organization doing business with the government would have converted public service into a form of economic exile and deterred entry into government. Section 207 avoided those results by aiming at conduct rather than affiliation. The employer could be a contractor, a regulated company, or a law firm with a large federal practice, and the employment itself offended nothing in the statute. The offense arose only when the former official performed the specific act of representation within a barred matter, before a barred agency, or during a barred interval.

### The Permanent Bar and the Particular Matter

The first and most severe restriction in section 207 was the permanent bar, sometimes described as the side-switching bar. It provided that a former official could never represent another person before the government in connection with a particular matter involving specific parties in which the official had participated personally and substantially while in office. Each element did mechanical work. The matter had to be a particular matter, a discrete and identifiable proceeding, application, contract, claim, investigation, or transaction, rather than a broad subject area. It had to involve specific parties, identifiable persons or entities whose interests were at stake. The involvement had to be personal, meaning the official acted directly, and substantial, meaning the involvement mattered to the proceeding rather than amounting to a passing or ministerial touch. When those elements converged, the bar attached for life.

The side-switching image captured the evil the permanent bar addressed. An official who had built the government's position in a specific dispute, evaluated a specific claim, or negotiated a specific contract possessed an intimate map of the government's strengths, weaknesses, reservations, and internal deliberations concerning that matter. If the official could cross to the other side of the same matter upon leaving office, the official could deploy that map against the government that had entrusted it, and the private party would effectively purchase the government's own work product, together with the credibility of the person who had produced it. The permanent bar foreclosed that transaction absolutely. It did not ask whether the official disclosed confidences, whether the new representation succeeded, or whether the official acted from corrupt motives. The participation itself created the disability. Structure replaced case by case judgment about character, because a regime requiring proof of actual betrayal in each instance would detect only a fraction of the harm and would legitimate the rest.

The lifetime duration followed from the nature of the asset the bar protected. Knowledge of a particular matter does not depreciate on the schedule of an ordinary cooling-off period. A contract dispute, a license application, or a claim against the government can remain alive for many years, and the official's firsthand understanding of the government's position retains its value for as long as the matter remains alive. A bar of one year or two years would merely have postponed the sale of that understanding until the calendar turned, leaving the private party free to hire the official, wait out the interval, and then present the official before the government when the restriction expired. The permanent bar eliminated that waiting strategy by eliminating the terminal date. The bar also followed the matter rather than the office. It did not depend on the official's former title, rank, or agency. Wherever the particular matter traveled within the government, the former official was disabled from representing another person in it, because the disability arose from the official's personal history with the matter itself. The official's knowledge and influence over that matter had been acquired in trust for the public, and the statute treated that acquisition as permanent. It never became the official's private property, and no passage of time converted it into a salable asset.

The bar's narrowness kept it tolerable. It reached only particular matters involving specific parties, so it did not disable a former official from working across an entire industry or policy field, representing another company in a different contract, or addressing general policy questions that lacked specific parties and an identifiable matter. A lifetime prohibition defined by particular matters consumed only the proceedings the official had touched, leaving the remainder of a professional life intact.

### The Two Year Bar and Official Responsibility

The second bar extended the framework beyond matters the official had personally handled. It provided that for two years after leaving office, a former official could not represent another person before the government in a particular matter involving specific parties that the official knew or reasonably should have known was actually pending under the official's official responsibility during the final year of service. Official responsibility was defined by authority rather than by action. It meant the direct administrative or operating authority, whether intermediate or final, to approve, disapprove, or otherwise direct government action. A matter lay under an official's responsibility when the official held power over it within the chain of command, even if subordinates performed every act of investigation, drafting, and recommendation and the official never saw the file.

Official responsibility was therefore broader than personal participation in every dimension that mattered. Personal participation required the official's own hand in the matter. Official responsibility required only the official's power over the matter, exercised or not. The danger the two year bar addressed did not depend on the official's hand. An official who held authority over a pending matter could shape its environment without touching it directly, through the selection and direction of subordinates and through signals about institutional priorities. Subordinates deciding a matter under a superior's responsibility worked in the shadow of that superior's preferences, and a superior who anticipated private employment had incentives to let that shadow fall in directions a future employer would reward. The bar also addressed appearances. A private party that hired the responsible official immediately after a favorable decision presented a sequence that looked like payment for services rendered, whether or not the official had lifted a finger in the matter, and the statute treated the protection of public confidence as a reason in itself.

The two year duration reflected the intermediate character of the relationship. Knowledge acquired through responsibility alone was thinner than knowledge acquired through personal participation, lacking the detailed command of evidence, negotiation history, and internal deliberation that personal involvement conferred. Supervisory knowledge also decayed more predictably, because the matter continued to develop under new leadership after the official departed. Two years gave that decay time to operate. The knowledge element, covering matters the official knew or reasonably should have known were pending under that responsibility, prevented escape through cultivated ignorance of the office's own docket. The result was a bar wider in coverage than the permanent bar and shorter in life, matching the burden to the intimacy of the connection.

### The One Year Cooling-Off Bar for Senior Officials

The third bar operated on a different axis. The permanent and two year bars were organized around matters, asking what the official had done or supervised. The one year cooling-off bar was organized around rank and former agency, asking who the official had been and where the official had served. It provided that senior officials, for one year after leaving service, could not make representational communications to, or appearances before, their former agency on behalf of another person seeking official action. The bar did not require that the official had participated in the matter, supervised it, or known of it while in office. A wholly new matter fell within the bar if the official carried it to the former agency within the year. The trigger was the combination of seniority, recency, and destination.

That asset was access. A senior official leaving an agency carried relationships with former colleagues and subordinates, familiarity with the agency's decision processes, and the residual deference that rank commands after the rank itself has been surrendered. A communication from a former senior official arrived with a weight that the same communication from a stranger could not match. The cooling-off bar treated access of that kind as a public asset for one year, long enough for personnel to turn over, for informal authority to dissipate, and for the agency to encounter the former official as an ordinary advocate rather than as a returning superior. The period was short precisely because the asset decayed fast and because the bar was broad. Breadth and brevity were paired, as narrowness and permanence were paired in the side-switching bar.

The cooling-off bar turned on a further distinction between representational communications and other forms of post-employment activity. The bar reached communications and appearances made with intent to influence the agency on behalf of another person seeking official action. Intent to influence was the dividing element. A former senior official who contacted the former agency for social reasons, requested publicly available information, or communicated on the official's own behalf stood outside the prohibition. More consequentially, the bar left untouched the entire field of behind the scenes activity. During the cooling-off year itself, a former senior official employed by a private firm could advise the firm on strategy, draft documents that others would present, prepare colleagues for meetings with the agency, and analyze the agency's likely response, provided the official did not personally make the representational contact. Others in the firm could appear and speak. The statute regulated the voice and presence of the former official, not the flow of the official's knowledge into the employer's internal deliberations.

### A Separate Bar Tied to Trade and Treaty Negotiations

Section 207 also carried a one year bar addressed to a specialized form of service, participation in trade and treaty negotiations. A former official who had participated personally and substantially in an ongoing trade or treaty negotiation, or who had access to specified nonpublic information concerning such a negotiation, faced a one year restriction on representing, aiding, or advising another person concerning that negotiation. The provision reached beyond direct representation to encompass aid and advice, a wider set of verbs than the general bars employed. In a negotiation, the government's asset was its position, its fallback points, and the sequence in which concessions might be offered, and strategic use of that information by a former participant could damage the national position through advice inside a private organization as readily as through a formal appearance. The one year period corresponded to the life of negotiating positions, which shifted as rounds concluded and instructions were revised.

### Why the Statute Restricts Contacts Rather Than Hiring

The design choice to restrict contacts rather than hiring determined the lived experience of the statute for departing officials. A hiring ban would have been simpler to state and far harder to administer fairly. Employers have many lines of business, most unrelated to any individual official's service, and a ban keyed to the employer would have punished connections that posed no danger while inviting evasion through subsidiaries, affiliates, and consulting arrangements. A contact ban followed the former official as an individual, attached to the specific acts that created the risk of divided loyalty, and left every other act free. A representational contact was also a discrete, observable event, a letter sent, an appearance made, a communication logged, which investigators could establish without reconstructing the internal life of a private firm. Hiring, by contrast, was a lawful act that became wrongful only through speculation about what the hired official might do afterward. By criminalizing the contact and permitting the job, section 207 placed its prohibition at the exact point where private interest met public power, and preserved a flow of expertise that a hiring ban would have dammed.

### The Extension to the Legislative Branch

The framework enacted in 1978 centered on the executive branch, whose officials administered the contracts, licenses, benefits, and enforcement matters that made representational contacts valuable. The Ethics Reform Act of 1989, Public Law 101-194, extended revolving door restrictions to the legislative branch and tightened the regime. The extension acknowledged that the assets the statute regulated, access, relationships, and inside familiarity with an institution's processes, existed in Congress in comparable forms. A senior legislative aide or a Member departing for private employment carried knowledge of pending legislation, of committee processes, and of the preferences of former colleagues that private interests valued for the same reasons they valued agency access. The 1989 act brought that movement within statutory restriction and adjusted the existing framework in a tightening direction, leaving the basic architecture intact while extending it to new classes of officials whose service created the same risks in a different branch.

### The Design Logic of Pricing Future Advocacy

The closing question about Title V is why Congress chose prohibitions of this shape, when the underlying evil was a state of mind, the official tempted in office by the prospect of reward after office. The statute's answer was that motive in office is nearly impossible to police directly. An official who softened a decision in hope of private employment rarely recorded the hope, and proof that a decision rested on anticipated reward rather than on judgment was seldom available. A regime requiring proof of that motive would have punished only the careless and left the incentive untouched. Section 207 attacked the incentive instead of the motive. By disabling the former official from performing specified advocacy after departure, the statute reduced the market value of the advocacy a private employer might otherwise reward. The expected return on currying favor therefore fell at the moment the official made decisions in office, because the statute had already fixed the price of the future service the favor might purchase.

Each official serving under section 207 made choices in office inside a legal environment that had prevalued the official's post-employment usefulness and had set that value to zero across the matters and intervals of greatest sensitivity. The official gained nothing, in market terms, by tilting a particular matter toward a prospective employer, because the official could never represent that employer in that matter at all, and gained little from trading on access, because a year of silence before the former agency stood between departure and any representational use of that access. The deterrents operated prospectively and impersonally, without inquiries into gratitude, ambition, or intent in any individual case. Title V structured the exit from government so that the most valuable thing an official could carry through the door, the capacity to advocate before the government in the official's own former work, was the one thing the law required the official to leave behind.

## The Independent Counsel Mechanism and the Problem of Self Investigation

The fourth pillar of the Ethics in Government Act of 1978 differed from the other three in kind and in fate. Disclosure placed financial facts on a public record. The Office of Government Ethics organized advice and review. Post employment restrictions governed conduct after service ended. Title VI, codified before lapse at 28 U.S.C. sections 591 to 599, addressed a problem that arose while an official remained in office, when the ordinary machinery of federal criminal law pointed inward toward the officials who directed that machinery. Title VI created an officer outside the ordinary chain of command for a defined case, appointed by judges rather than by the Attorney General, exercising prosecutorial power within limits set at the moment of appointment. The design rested on credibility as much as on law. A decision to decline prosecution could be sound and remain unpersuaded in public estimation if the decision came from a subordinate of the person cleared. The statute answered by relocating the decision, for a narrow class of persons and on a defined trigger, to an officer whose selection the executive branch did not control.

### The Structural Problem Behind a Separate Prosecutor

Federal criminal enforcement ordinarily runs through the Department of Justice, headed by the Attorney General, who serves under the President and directs the work of the department. That arrangement carries a clear line of accountability in ordinary cases, where the subject of an investigation holds no authority over the investigators. The arrangement changes character when the person whose conduct is in question sits at or near the top of that hierarchy. The Attorney General might be asked to evaluate the conduct of the President who appointed the Attorney General, of the Vice President, of a cabinet officer in the same administration, or of a senior official inside the Department of Justice itself. The problem, as the statute framed the matter, was institutional and recurrent rather than an accusation against any individual officeholder.

Disclosure alone could not solve that problem. Financial reports expose interests, and ethics advice guides conduct before a violation occurs, but neither device decides what happens after information arrives suggesting that a covered official may have broken federal criminal law. At that point the question becomes who investigates, who evaluates the evidence, and who decides whether a prosecution follows. If the answer remains the Department of Justice in every case, the department acts as investigator of the government that the department serves, and every outcome carries the imprint of that relationship. Title VI separated one category of decision from that relationship. The separation was partial and procedural. The Attorney General retained the first examination of incoming information, the department retained ordinary authority over the vast run of federal cases, and the special mechanism engaged only after a threshold finding moved a matter into the hands of a court appointed counsel.

### Who counted as a covered person under the statute?

The statute covered the President and the Vice President, cabinet level officials, senior officials of the Department of Justice and specified other agencies, and senior national campaign officials, among others named in the text. Coverage followed office and responsibility rather than party, and the class defined whose alleged conduct could set the mechanism in motion.

The covered persons class performed the first act of limitation in the statute, and the limitation mattered as much as the inclusion. Title VI did not create a roving prosecutor for federal crime in general, nor did the title transfer ordinary prosecutions involving private citizens or lower level employees to a court appointed officer. The class reached officials whose proximity to executive power created the credibility difficulty that justified a separate mechanism. The President and the Vice President stood at the head of the list because the Attorney General serves in an administration that those officials lead. Cabinet level officials followed, since a cabinet officer and the Attorney General answer to the same President. Senior officials of the Department of Justice presented a distinct version of the problem, because the department would be investigating its own leadership, examining superiors who controlled assignments and tenure within the same institution. Senior national campaign officials extended the class beyond formal office, to influence that ran through the campaign organization that placed an administration in power.

### From Specific and Credible Information to a Preliminary Investigation

The mechanism began with information, qualified in two respects before any obligation attached. The Attorney General had to receive specific and credible information that a covered person may have violated federal criminal law, other than minor offenses. Specificity required more than rumor, generalized suspicion, or accusation stated without factual content. Credibility required a basis for treating the information as worthy of examination. The exclusion of minor offenses kept the extraordinary machinery from engaging over matters that the ordinary system could absorb without the structural concern that justified a separate counsel.

Receipt of qualifying information set a preliminary investigation in motion, bounded at ninety days, with one possible extension granted by the special division. A fixed period prevented an allegation against a senior official from remaining in indefinite suspension, neither cleared nor advanced, and disciplined the preliminary stage to a threshold purpose. The Attorney General examined whether reasonable grounds existed to believe that further investigation was warranted. That standard sat below a decision to charge and below a judgment about ultimate proof. The preliminary investigation asked a screening question, and the ninety day frame, subject to a single extension granted by the special division rather than by the Attorney General acting alone, kept the screening from becoming the investigation itself.

The outcome divided into two paths, treated with deliberate asymmetry. If the Attorney General found no reasonable grounds to believe that further investigation was warranted, the matter closed, and that decision was not subject to judicial review. No court could order the Attorney General to revisit the threshold judgment. The unreviewable character of a negative determination preserved executive judgment at the threshold and prevented the mechanism from becoming automatic upon the arrival of an allegation. The stage filtered matters that would close inside the executive branch from matters that, once the threshold was met, the executive branch could no longer retain.

### The Special Division and the Transfer of the Appointment Decision

Once the threshold was met, the character of the proceeding changed completely. The Attorney General was required to apply to the special division for the appointment of an independent counsel. The application was mandatory rather than discretionary. The Attorney General did not weigh whether a court appointed counsel would be wise as a matter of policy, and the department retained no option to keep a qualifying matter in house after the threshold finding had been made. That mandatory quality formed the hinge of Title VI. Before the threshold, judgment resided with the Attorney General, protected from judicial review when the judgment closed a matter. After the threshold, the statute withdrew the appointment decision from the executive branch altogether and placed selection of the prosecutor, and definition of the assignment, in judicial hands, so that the appointment decision left the executive branch entirely.

The special division was a three judge panel of the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit designated by the Chief Justice. The panel did not conduct the investigation that followed an appointment. The statute assigned the division two functions. The division appointed the counsel, selecting the individual who would hold prosecutorial authority for the matter. The division also defined the prosecutorial jurisdiction of the counsel, establishing the scope of the assignment within which the counsel would act. Appointment and jurisdiction arrived together, from the same judicial act, so that the counsel took authority from the order that created the assignment rather than from any instruction issued by the department that the counsel had been appointed to bypass. Definition of jurisdiction carried weight equal to the appointment itself, since a written judicial determination, made before the investigation proceeded, supplied the measure against which counsel actions could be understood.

### The Counsel in Office, With Powers, Protection, and a Reporting Duty

Within the jurisdiction that the special division defined, the independent counsel held the full investigative and prosecutorial powers of the Department of Justice. The grant was plenary within its field. The counsel could direct an investigation, gather evidence through the means available to federal prosecutors, evaluate the evidence assembled, and make charging decisions, without obtaining clearance from the Attorney General or from any official inside the department for steps taken within the defined assignment. That completeness mattered, because a counsel who required departmental approval for subpoenas, for witness examinations, or for presentation of a case would reproduce inside the mechanism the dependence that the mechanism existed to remove. Independence meant legal authority to act, exercised under the counsel judgment alone within the jurisdiction conferred.

Protection against removal completed the structure that appointment and jurisdiction began. An independent counsel could be removed only by the Attorney General personally, and only for good cause, stated in the statutory formulation as physical disability, mental incapacity, or any other condition that substantially impairs the performance of the counsel's duties. The Attorney General could not delegate the removal decision to a subordinate, and dissatisfaction with the direction of an investigation or disagreement with a charging judgment did not meet the statutory ground. The personal character of the authority concentrated responsibility for any removal in a single identifiable officer, at the highest level of the department. The good cause limitation confined the grounds for that authority. Together, the requirements meant that the executive branch retained a narrow and specified power over counsel tenure while surrendering the broader power of removal at will that an executive superior ordinarily holds over a subordinate prosecutor.

Independence coexisted with a reporting duty toward the legislative branch. The counsel reported to Congress at the close of an investigation. That closing report gave Congress, as the institution that created the mechanism and retained authority over continuation of the statute, an account of work performed under an appointment that the statute had made possible. The design distributed functions across the branches in a deliberate pattern. The Attorney General conducted the preliminary investigation and made the threshold application. The special division appointed the counsel and defined the jurisdiction. The counsel investigated and prosecuted within that jurisdiction. The Attorney General alone, personally and for good cause, could remove the counsel. Congress received the closing report and decided, through reauthorization, whether the mechanism would continue to exist.

### A Mechanism First Named for Prosecution and Later Renamed for Independence

The office entered the statute book under a different name from the name the office carried for most of the statutory life. As enacted in 1978, Title VI created the mechanism under the name special prosecutor. The Ethics in Government Act Amendments of 1982, Public Law 97-409, signed on January 3, 1983, renamed the office independent counsel. The change altered no part of the structural problem, and the renaming accompanied the first reauthorization of the mechanism rather than a replacement with a new institution. The same trigger, preliminary investigation, special division, and defined jurisdiction continued under the new name. Special prosecutor described a function, the prosecution of a particular matter by an officer appointed for that matter. Independent counsel described a relationship, or more precisely the absence of dependence between the officer and the executive officials within the field of the assignment.

### The Sunset Design and the Cycle of Renewal, Lapse, and Expiry

A sunset provision sets a date on which statutory authority expires unless Congress acts to continue the authority, and Title VI lived under that discipline throughout the existence of the independent counsel provisions. The 1982 amendments reauthorized the mechanism and renamed the office in the same act. From that point forward, survival depended on periodic affirmative decisions by Congress, taken against a known expiration date, rather than on inertia. Supporters of continuation had to obtain a new statute. Opponents could prevail by preventing a new statute from passing before the deadline arrived.

Congress reauthorized the provisions again in 1987 through the Independent Counsel Reauthorization Act of 1987, Public Law 100-191, signed on December 15, 1987. That act continued the mechanism and left the architecture of trigger, preliminary investigation, special division appointment, defined jurisdiction, plenary powers within jurisdiction, and removal only for good cause in place. Five years later, the cycle produced a different result. The provisions were allowed to lapse on December 15, 1992. Lapse differed from repeal, because Congress took no statute away by an affirmative vote against the mechanism in that year. The authority expired because the sunset date arrived without a reauthorization statute, and during the interval that followed, Title VI supplied no basis for a new appointment. The office ceased to be available as a matter of law, not because a court had invalidated the office, but because the statutory clock ran out.

The lapse of 1992 proved temporary. Congress reauthorized the mechanism through the Independent Counsel Reauthorization Act of 1994, Public Law 103-270, signed on June 30, 1994. The 1994 act revived the framework after an interval during which the mechanism had no legal existence, restoring appointment by the special division upon mandatory application by the Attorney General, with jurisdiction defined by the appointing panel. The revival demonstrated a feature of sunset design that a permanent statute does not display. Expiration did not settle the question permanently in either direction, and a later Congress remained free to restore the authority by a new enactment.

The final date arrived on June 30, 1999, when the independent counsel provisions expired and no reauthorization followed. The 1999 expiry closed Title VI as an operating part of the Ethics in Government Act while the disclosure titles, the Office of Government Ethics, and the post employment restrictions continued under separate authority, a separation that the four pillar structure makes visible. The fourth pillar ended by legislative inaction at a sunset deadline, as the 1992 lapse had ended an earlier statutory life, except that after 1999 no later Congress revived the mechanism. Figures across the political spectrum who had experienced the mechanism from both sides, as officials in administrations subject to the mechanism and as participants in governments that operated alongside a counsel, supported non renewal at the final expiration.

### Two Arguments Held at Equal Strength

Behind every stage of the reauthorization cycle stood two arguments about the location of prosecutorial power, each stated in strong form by the position that advanced the argument. The accountability argument held that the prosecution of senior officials cannot credibly be left to the subordinates of those officials. On that view, the defect in ordinary handling was structural, present before any individual decision was made, because a subordinate who cleared a superior acted under the shadow of appointment and removal, and a subordinate who charged a superior acted against the administration that the subordinate served. Defenders of the mechanism presented special division appointment, defined jurisdiction, plenary powers within jurisdiction, and the good cause removal standard as a measured answer to that defect, confined to a defined class and engaged only after the Attorney General threshold examination had found reasonable grounds.

The executive integrity argument held, with equal force in the position that advanced the argument, that criminal prosecution is executive power and that executive power must answer to the elected executive. On that view, the authority to investigate and prosecute federal crime belongs to the President and to officers accountable to the President, because execution of the laws is assigned to the executive branch and because democratic accountability for prosecutorial choices runs through an elected head of that branch. A prosecutor whom the President could not remove at will, appointed by judges rather than by the executive, and armed with the full powers of the Department of Justice within a jurisdiction that judges defined, weakened that line of accountability where prosecutorial discretion carried the greatest consequence. Opponents of the mechanism presented the same features that defenders praised, judicial appointment, removal only for good cause, and independence from departmental direction, as the source of the difficulty rather than as a solution, and the opposing position warned that an officer insulated from executive control could pursue an assignment with resources and duration unchecked by the discipline that accountability to a superior imposes on ordinary prosecutors. Congress answered in favor of continuation in 1982 and in 1987, allowed the authority to lapse in 1992, answered in favor of revival in 1994, and allowed the authority to expire without renewal in 1999, returning at set intervals to a question that the structure kept open.

### The Threshold of the Constitutional Challenge

The arguments about structure were arguments about the Constitution as well as arguments about policy, because the location of prosecutorial power, the appointment of officers, and the restriction of removal each raised questions that the constitutional text assigns to particular branches. Policy debate in Congress could proceed on assessments of credibility and accountability, but the mechanism operated through subpoenas, charging decisions, and coercive legal authority, and any person subjected to that authority could test the validity of the statute in court as a defense against compliance. Title VI placed an officer appointed by judges in possession of the prosecutorial powers of the Department of Justice, protected against removal except for good cause, and that concentration of contested features in a single office made a constitutional challenge to the statute itself a predictable stage in the life of the mechanism.

That stage arrived through ordinary operation rather than through an abstract request for judicial advice. Recipients of subpoenas issued by an independent counsel contested the constitutionality of the statute in resisting the subpoenas, and the challenge placed the design of Title VI before the courts as a live controversy between parties with concrete obligations at stake. The first full test thus began at the point of compulsion, where a person ordered to provide testimony or material answered that the officer issuing the order held authority under a statute that the Constitution did not permit. The section ends where the litigation begins, with a statute in full operation, a counsel in office under a judicial appointment, subpoenas issued within a defined jurisdiction, and recipients who refused the premise on which the whole structure rested, that Congress could create such an officer at all.

## Morrison v. Olson and the End of the Independent Counsel

The constitutional test of the independent counsel provisions arrived within a single investigation and turned on resistance to compulsory process. Recipients of subpoenas issued by an independent counsel contested the statute itself, arguing that the office had been created in a manner the Constitution did not permit and that compliance could not be compelled by an officer appointed and protected in the manner Title VI prescribed. The court of appeals accepted the challenge and held the statute unconstitutional. That judgment placed the entire mechanism in doubt, because a holding that the counsel was unlawfully appointed reached beyond the particular subpoenas and denied legal effect to the authority the statute conferred. The Supreme Court reversed. The decision, Morrison v. Olson, 487 U.S. 654 (1988), was decided on June 29, 1988, by a vote of 7 to 1. Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for the majority. Justice Kennedy took no part in the consideration or decision of the case, and Justice Scalia alone dissented. The posture matters to the holding. The Court did not review an abstract disagreement between the branches over ethics policy. The Court reviewed a judgment that had invalidated a statute in the course of a live enforcement dispute, and reversal restored the statute as operative law.

### What did the Supreme Court decide in Morrison v. Olson?

The Supreme Court reversed the court of appeals and upheld the independent counsel provisions. The Court held that the counsel was an inferior officer whose appointment by the special division satisfied the Appointments Clause, that the division acted within Article III, and that good cause removal preserved sufficient executive control.

The three holdings formed a single chain, and the order in which the Court addressed them carried analytical weight. Classification came first, because the validity of appointment by the special division depended on whether the counsel counted as a principal officer or as an inferior officer under the Appointments Clause. The functions of the appointing body came second, because appointment by judges raised a distinct question under Article III about the tasks federal courts may perform outside the decision of cases and controversies. Removal came third, because the tenure protection in the statute raised the broadest question of the three, whether Congress could condition the removal of a prosecutor on good cause without withdrawing from the President the control Article II requires. The majority sustained the statute at each link.

### Inferior Officer Status and the Appointments Clause

The Appointments Clause distinguishes principal officers, whom the President appoints with the advice and consent of the Senate, from inferior officers, whose appointment Congress may vest in the President alone, in the heads of departments, or in the courts of law. Title VI vested appointment of the independent counsel in the special division, a three judge panel of the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit designated by the Chief Justice. That vesting was permissible only if the counsel was an inferior officer. The majority held that the counsel belonged in the inferior category. The office was limited in the dimensions that mark subordination. The counsel exercised jurisdiction defined by the special division in the appointment order rather than jurisdiction at large over federal criminal law. The duties of the office were confined to the investigation and prosecution assigned in that order. The tenure of the office was tied to the assignment and ended with the completion of the work the appointment defined. The counsel held no policymaking authority for the executive branch and occupied no continuing place in the administration of the Department of Justice. On those features the majority treated the counsel as an officer directed and bounded in a manner principal officers are not, and the conclusion followed that Congress could vest the appointment in a court of law without offending the Appointments Clause.

The reasoning also addressed the incongruity objection pressed against interbranch appointment. The challengers treated appointment of a prosecutor by judges as a structural mismatch, on the ground that selection of an executive officer by the judicial branch blurred the separation the Constitution maintains. The majority rejected the proposition that the Appointments Clause imposes an absolute bar on appointments across branch lines. The text of the clause names the courts of law as a permissible repository of appointment power for inferior officers, and the majority read that textual permission as controlling over a general objection drawn from separation of powers principles in the abstract. The inquiry therefore turned on the character of the office rather than on the branch identity of the appointer, and because the office was inferior, appointment by the special division stood. Selection by judges placed the choice of counsel outside the chain of command that the accountability position identified as the source of the conflict the statute addressed, since prosecution of covered senior officials by officers answerable to those officials presented the dependence the statute was constructed to remove. The majority sustained that insulation by tying it to the limited character of the office, and the limitation did analytical work in the removal analysis as well.

### The Functions of the Special Division Under Article Three

The second holding concerned the special division itself. Article III confines the judicial power of the United States to the decision of cases and controversies, and the challengers argued that the tasks Title VI assigned to the panel exceeded that confinement. Under the statute the special division appointed the counsel on the application of the Attorney General, defined the prosecutorial jurisdiction in the appointment, received reports the statute required, and possessed authority connected to the completion and termination of the counsel assignment. The majority held that those functions did not violate Article III. The appointment function rested on the express provision of the Appointments Clause for inferior officers, which contemplates judicial participation in appointment without converting the appointment into an exercise of judicial power over the merits of a prosecution. The definition of jurisdiction accompanied the appointment and bounded the assignment at its creation rather than directing the conduct of the investigation that followed. The majority drew a line between the administrative and appointing tasks the statute assigned to the panel and the adjudication of the guilt or innocence of any person, a matter the statute left to the ordinary courts in proceedings the counsel brought. Because the panel decided no criminal case, directed no prosecutorial judgment within the jurisdiction it defined, and issued no order resolving the rights of a defendant, the functions remained compatible with Article III limits.

The majority paired that conclusion with attention to the boundary the statute had to respect in the opposite direction. A judicial role that extended to supervision of the investigation, to direction of charging decisions, or to review of discretion within the defined jurisdiction would have presented a different question, because direction of prosecution is executive action and supervision of that direction by judges would have entangled the branches in a manner the Appointments Clause permission does not reach. Title VI withheld that supervisory role. After appointment and definition of jurisdiction, the conduct of the investigation belonged to the counsel, the preliminary assessment that triggered an application belonged to the Attorney General, and the adjudication of charges belonged to the trial courts. The special division occupied a narrow station at the creation of the office and at the formal close of the assignment, and the majority treated that narrowness as decisive. The same limited design that supported inferior officer status also kept the judicial contribution within constitutional bounds.

### Good Cause Removal and the Sufficient Control Test

The third holding addressed removal, the provision at the center of the structural dispute. Title VI provided that an independent counsel could be removed only by the Attorney General personally and only for good cause, stated in the statute in terms of physical disability, mental incapacity, or any other condition that substantially impairs the performance of the counsel's duties. The restriction removed the counsel from at will removal by the President and from removal by any subordinate officer of the Department of Justice. The challengers argued that prosecution is an executive function and that a prosecutor insulated from presidential removal disabled the President from taking care that the laws be faithfully executed. The majority rejected a categorical rule that every officer exercising executive authority must serve at the pleasure of the President. The question the majority posed was functional rather than formal: whether the restriction impermissibly interfered with the ability of the President to perform the constitutional duty of faithful execution. The answer turned on the degree of control the executive branch retained. The Attorney General, an officer appointed by the President and removable at will, retained the removal power itself, conditioned on good cause. The executive branch retained the threshold decision whether to conduct a preliminary investigation and whether grounds existed for an application to the special division. The counsel operated within a jurisdiction defined at appointment and exercised the powers of the Department of Justice inside that jurisdiction alone. Measured against that retained control, the majority held that the good cause restriction did not impermissibly interfere with the executive and that the executive retained sufficient control to perform its constitutional duties.

The sufficient control test carried consequences beyond the dispute decided in 1988. By framing the inquiry around the degree of control retained rather than around the label attached to the function performed, the majority permitted Congress a measure of design latitude for offices of limited scope while withholding approval from insulation of officers at large. The reasoning depended at each step on the limited character established in the first holding. A prosecutor of general jurisdiction, continuing tenure, and departmental policymaking authority would have presented the removal question in a materially different form, and the counsel of Title VI possessed none of those attributes. Proponents of the accountability position read the holding as confirmation that prosecution of the most senior officials could be placed at one remove from the officials exposed to investigation without disabling the presidency. Proponents of the executive integrity position read the same holding as a departure from the premise that executive power answers through a single elected head. The second reading found its fullest expression in the lone dissent, which rejected the functional inquiry itself.

### The Lone Dissent and Purely Executive Power

Justice Scalia dissented alone, and the dissent rejected the premise on which the majority removal analysis rested. In the dissent's account, criminal prosecution is a purely executive power. The power to investigate and to prosecute violations of federal criminal law belongs to the executive branch in its entirety, and Article II vests the executive power in the President. A statute that placed a prosecutor beyond presidential removal at will therefore stripped the President of exclusive control over a function the Constitution assigns to the presidency alone. The dissent treated the degree of control the majority found sufficient as beside the point. Partial control over a purely executive function was, in that account, a constitutional defect rather than a saving feature, because the question was not how much control the President retained over the counsel but whether Congress could divide control over prosecution between the President and an officer the President could not remove. The dissent answered that division was impermissible in any degree, and presented the case as one in which the wolf arrived in the disguise of a wolf.

The dissent also addressed the institution rather than the doctrine alone. A counsel appointed outside the ordinary chain of command, armed with the full investigative and prosecutorial powers of the Department of Justice within a defined jurisdiction, and protected against removal except for good cause would operate, in the dissent's prediction, under pressures different from the pressures on a prosecutor inside the department. The institution would bend toward the political pressures of the moment in which it operated, because an officer created for a single assignment, judged in public by the outcome of that assignment, and freed from the institutional restraints of a continuing department faced incentives toward expansion of the investigation and toward prosecution that the dissent regarded as inherent in the structure. The accountability position answered that insulation from the investigated officials was the point of the structure and that dependence on those officials presented the graver defect. The dissent answered that accountability for prosecution runs through the elected President to the electorate, and that a prosecutor answerable to no elected official displaced that chain rather than repaired it. The two positions divided on a premise neither side surrendered between 1988 and the expiration of the statute in 1999: whether the central risk in prosecution of senior officials was dependence on the executive or independence from the executive.

The dissent acquired a standing in separation of powers law disproportionate to a lone vote. The opinion ranks among the most cited and debated dissents in separation of powers law. Judicial treatment gave that standing a defined boundary. In Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, decided in 2020, the Supreme Court treated Morrison as a narrow exception tied to the status of the independent counsel as an inferior officer with limited duties, rather than as a general license for removal restrictions on principal officers exercising broad authority. The 2020 treatment left the 1988 judgment in place for the office the 1988 Court had examined and withheld extension of the reasoning to officers of a different rank and scope. The boundary drawn in Seila Law matched the structure of the majority reasoning in Morrison itself. The dissent therefore entered later doctrine in two forms at once: as the fullest judicial statement of the position that prosecution admits no division of executive control, and as the losing side of a precedent the Court in 2020 confined to narrow ground.

### How did the special counsel regulation differ from the independent counsel statute?

The regulation placed the special counsel inside the Department of Justice under appointment and removal by the Attorney General, while the statute had provided for appointment by a judicial panel and removal only for good cause. The regulation therefore depended on departmental permission and could be revised by the department itself.

The statute expired on June 30, 1999, without reauthorization. Congress had renewed the mechanism in earlier cycles, in 1982 and in 1987, had allowed a lapse in 1992, and had restored the provisions in 1994, so expiration in 1999 was a deliberate choice against a record of prior renewals. Support for non-renewal came from figures across the political spectrum who had experienced the mechanism from both sides, from the side of the officials who administered the statute and from the side of the officials exposed to investigation under it. The sequence produced the namable claim at the center of the profile: a constitutional victory was followed by legislative repudiation. The Supreme Court had sustained every challenged feature of the design in 1988, and the elected branches declined in 1999 to continue the design the Court had sustained. Constitutional permissibility and legislative judgment parted on the same institution eleven years apart.

On June 30, 1999, the day of expiration, Attorney General Janet Reno issued departmental regulations codified at 28 C.F.R. Part 600, published at 64 Fed. Reg. 37038. The regulations authorized the Attorney General, or the acting Attorney General where the Attorney General was recused, to appoint a special counsel, ordinarily from outside the government, and to define the jurisdiction of the counsel in the appointing order. The special counsel operated within the Department of Justice, was subject to departmental regulations, and was removable by the Attorney General for misconduct, dereliction of duty, incapacity, conflict of interest, or other good cause stated in the regulation. Every structural feature that had defined the statutory office changed character in the regulatory office. Appointment moved from a judicial panel to the head of the department. Tenure protection moved from a statute Congress alone could amend to a regulation the department could revise or rescind without an act of Congress. The office moved from a position outside the department, exercising departmental powers under a judicially defined jurisdiction, to a position inside the department under the authority of the Attorney General.

The counter-reading of the replacement deserves statement at full strength, because the equivalence of the two offices was contested from the date of issuance. On that reading, the regulation is not the equivalent of the statute. The regulation resolves the constitutional objection identified in the Morrison dissent precisely by restoring the dependence the statute existed to remove. A counsel appointed by the Attorney General, housed inside the executive branch, subject to departmental regulation, and removable by the Attorney General holds independence by permission rather than by law. The regulations were drafted to structure appointment, jurisdiction, and removal in terms that signal restraint on departmental interference. The legal character of the arrangement differs from the statutory arrangement regardless of practice under it, because the restraint rests on a text the issuing department retains power to rewrite. Defenders of the regulatory model answered from the executive integrity position that the arrangement restored the constitutional chain: prosecution remained executive action, answerable through the Attorney General to the President and through the President to the electorate, with flexibility for appointment in a particular matter without a standing statutory office. Defenders of the statutory model answered from the accountability position that flexibility purchased at the price of dependence reproduced the conflict the 1978 design had addressed.

### The STOCK Act Amendment of the Disclosure Title

The counsel pillar ended in 1999. The disclosure pillar followed a different course. The Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge Act of 2012, known as the STOCK Act, Public Law 112-105, signed April 4, 2012, amended the disclosure title of the Ethics in Government Act. The amendment affirmed that insider trading law reached Members of Congress and other covered officials under the 1978 framework, required periodic transaction reports of securities transactions above a threshold within forty five days of the transaction, and required public electronic availability of disclosure reports for the most senior filers. In 2013 Congress narrowed the electronic posting requirement for most filers below the most senior ranks, in Public Law 113-22, leaving the core disclosure obligations and the transaction reporting requirement in place while adjusting the scope of electronic publication. The disclosure provisions of the 1978 act were never repealed, and the assumption that the disclosure title lapsed with the independent counsel title in 1999 misstates the record. The Office of Government Ethics continued to administer executive branch disclosure under Title IV, and the filing rhythm of entry reports, annual reports by May 15, and termination reports continued under the amended title.

The asymmetry is the closing observation of the profile. One statute, Public Law 95-521, signed October 26, 1978, produced a disclosure regime that Congress amended and modernized in 2012 and adjusted in 2013, and post-employment restrictions in 18 U.S.C. section 207 that later statutes extended and lengthened. The same statute produced an independent counsel mechanism that the Supreme Court upheld in 1988 by a vote of 7 to 1 and that Congress allowed to expire in 1999 without replacement by statute. The disclosure pillar endured because disclosure imposed obligations on officials without transferring executive power between the branches. The counsel pillar transferred appointment and removal of a prosecutor away from the President, survived judicial review on the narrow ground of a limited inferior office, and failed to survive the legislative judgment of the officials who had operated under it from both sides. Constitutional survival and political survival proved to be separate tests, and the four pillars of the 1978 act divided on exactly that line.


## What the Statute Teaches About Building Watchers

Read as a whole, the Ethics in Government Act of 1978 is a study in the difference between preventive and reactive integrity machinery. The three enduring pillars work before any offense occurs. Disclosure forces the conflict into the open at the moment an official takes office and at regular intervals afterward, so that journalists, colleagues, opposing campaigns and ordinary citizens can see the interest while the official is exercising power. The ethics office resolves conflicts at the level of advice, recusal and divestiture, converting most problems into administrative adjustments rather than prosecutions. The post-employment bars operate on incentives rather than detection, removing the market value of a favorable decision by denying the decision maker the right to advocate the matter afterward. None of the three requires proof of a crime, and none depends on the character of the official being watched. That indifference to character was the statute's founding wager, formed in the aftermath of investigations that had shown how thoroughly an administration could fail when integrity rested on the judgment of the individuals inside it.

The fourth pillar worked on the opposite logic, and its history states the profile's namable claim in full. The independent counsel was reactive machinery, built for the moment after credible information suggested that a senior official had committed a crime and the ordinary prosecutor labored under a disabling conflict. Congress sustained the design through repeated reauthorizations, the Supreme Court sustained it against the Appointments Clause, Article III and separation of powers in Morrison v. Olson, and the constitutional question was therefore settled in the statute's favor by as authoritative a route as the legal system offers. Congress then let the provision expire on June 30, 1999, declining to renew a mechanism that the judiciary had upheld, with support for non-renewal arriving from figures across the political spectrum who had experienced the mechanism from both the prosecuting and the investigated side of it. A constitutional victory followed by legislative repudiation is a rare sequence in the life of a statute, and the lone dissent in the case acquired an afterlife that majority opinions seldom achieve, becoming a standard reference point in separation of powers argument and receiving, in the 2020 Seila Law decision, a narrowing treatment that confined Morrison to the inferior officer setting in which it arose. The majority won the case. The dissent increasingly framed the doctrine's later development, and the statute at the center of both disappeared in the interval.

The replacement arrangement sharpens rather than softens the lesson. A special counsel appointed under the regulations at 28 C.F.R. Part 600 holds office inside the Department of Justice, receives jurisdiction from the Attorney General's appointing order, answers to departmental regulation, and can be removed by the Attorney General under the regulation's stated grounds. Defenders of the regulatory model describe a prosecutor who is independent in practice while remaining accountable within the executive branch, which answers the constitutional objection that the Morrison dissent pressed. The counter-reading answers that independence held by permission is a different institution from independence held by law, because the appointing authority that grants the protection can revise the instrument granting it, without assembling a majority of both houses of Congress and presenting a bill to a President. Both readings agree on the underlying facts of the two mechanisms. They disagree about which feature matters more, the accountability the regulation restores or the insulation the statute removed, and that disagreement is the form in which the argument the statute began in 1978 continues inside the law that replaced it.

The disclosure habit the statute normalized extends beyond the officeholders it first covered, and a reader tracing the wider transparency architecture of the same era will find the pattern repeated. The [Lobbying Disclosure Act](/2021/11/01/lobbying-disclosure-act-1995-guide/) applies the publicity logic to the advocates who approach the government from outside, requiring registration and reporting from those paid to influence it, while the [Freedom of Information Act](/2021/10/01/freedom-of-information-act-1966-guide/) supplies the general instrument by which citizens obtain the records that disclosure regimes and ordinary administration generate. Together the three statutes describe a single governing instinct applied to three different subjects: money in elections, influence over officials, and the finances of the officials themselves. For a route through the full series, the [US legislation study guide](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/) sets the study path, and a reader assembling the primary materials for this statute, the enacted text at 92 Stat. 1824, the Morrison majority and dissent, the 1999 regulations and the STOCK Act amendments, can [keep filings, case notes, and statutory timelines organized free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html). The statute's three surviving pillars reward that assembly, because each operates in the open and leaves a public record to study. The fourth pillar rewards it for the opposite reason. Its record closed in 1999, and the closed record is the evidence.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Ethics in Government Act require officials to disclose?

The act required senior officials in all three branches to file public reports disclosing the sources and amounts of their income, the identity and approximate value of assets they held and liabilities they owed, transactions in real property, commodities and securities, gifts and reimbursements received, outside positions held, and agreements or arrangements such as future employment understandings and continuing participation in employee benefit plans. Specified financial interests of a spouse and dependent children were included, because a conflict routed through a household member defeats the purpose of disclosure as readily as one held directly. Values were reported in categories rather than exact figures, a design that exposed the existence and rough scale of an interest without converting the filing into a complete statement of personal wealth.

### Q: What is the revolving door restriction in the Ethics in Government Act?

The revolving door restriction is the set of post-employment bars the act wrote into 18 U.S.C. section 207, limiting the advocacy a former official may sell after leaving government. The strictest bar is permanent: an official may never represent another person before the government in a particular matter involving specific parties in which the official participated personally and substantially while in office. A second bar runs for two years and covers matters that lay under the official's official responsibility during the final year of service. A third bar imposes a one year cooling-off period during which senior officials may not make representational contacts with their former agency on behalf of another person. The bars restrict representation, not employment itself.

### Q: Did the Ethics in Government Act create the independent counsel?

Yes. Title VI of the act created the office, under the original name of special prosecutor, as a prosecutor appointed outside the ordinary chain of command of the Department of Justice for cases involving covered senior officials. The Ethics in Government Act Amendments of 1982 changed the title to independent counsel, and the mechanism operated under that name through its reauthorizations. Appointments were made by a three judge panel known as the special division, acting on the application of the Attorney General, and the counsel held the full investigative and prosecutorial authority of the Department of Justice within the jurisdiction the panel defined. The provision expired on June 30, 1999, when Congress declined to reauthorize it, and no statute has recreated the office since that date.

### Q: What did Morrison v. Olson decide about the Ethics in Government Act?

In Morrison v. Olson, 487 U.S. 654 (1988), the Supreme Court upheld the independent counsel provisions of the act against constitutional challenge, by a vote of seven to one. The Court held that an independent counsel is an inferior officer, so the Constitution permitted Congress to vest the counsel's appointment in the special division of judges rather than in the President. The Court further held that the special division's functions did not violate Article III, and that limiting the Attorney General's power to remove a counsel to removal for good cause did not impermissibly interfere with the President's executive authority, because the executive branch retained sufficient control over the counsel to perform its constitutional duties. Justice Scalia dissented alone, arguing that criminal prosecution is a purely executive power that the statute had unconstitutionally taken from the President.

### Q: Why did the independent counsel provision lapse in the Ethics in Government Act?

The provision lapsed because it was written with a sunset and Congress, after two decades of experience with the mechanism, chose not to renew it before the authority expired on June 30, 1999. The choice crossed the political spectrum: figures who had served in administrations investigated under the statute and figures who had supported its use against opposing administrations converged on the judgment that the mechanism's costs, in open ended jurisdiction, duration and political pressure on prosecutors, exceeded its accountability gains. The lapse was therefore a legislative judgment about a working institution rather than a response to judicial invalidation, since the Supreme Court had upheld the provision eleven years earlier in Morrison v. Olson. Earlier cycles had followed the same pattern of expiry and revival, with a lapse in 1992 reversed by reauthorization in 1994, but the 1999 expiry was never reversed.

### Q: What replaced the independent counsel from the Ethics in Government Act?

The replacement was a departmental regulation rather than a statute. On June 30, 1999, the day the independent counsel provisions expired, Attorney General Janet Reno issued regulations codified at 28 C.F.R. Part 600, under which the Attorney General may appoint a special counsel to conduct an investigation or prosecution that would present a conflict of interest for the Department of Justice under ordinary procedures. The Attorney General defines the special counsel's jurisdiction in the appointing order, the counsel operates within the department and under its regulations, and the Attorney General may remove the counsel for misconduct, dereliction of duty, incapacity, conflict of interest or other good cause. Because a regulation can be revised or rescinded by the department that issued it, the replacement carries none of the statutory permanence or judicial appointment that defined the predecessor office.

### Q: What office did the Ethics in Government Act create?

The act created the Office of Government Ethics, established by Title IV within the Office of Personnel Management and headed by a Director appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate for a five year term. The office directs executive branch policy on conflicts of interest, issues the regulations and formal advisory opinions that govern official conduct, reviews the public financial disclosure reports of presidential nominees and the most senior officials, and oversees the agency ethics programs administered day to day by designated agency ethics officials. In 1989 the office was separated from the personnel office and continued as a small independent agency. Its authority is advisory and regulatory rather than prosecutorial, a division of labor examined in the profile's treatment of the office.

### Q: Did the STOCK Act amend the Ethics in Government Act?

Yes. The Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge Act of 2012, Public Law 112-105, signed on April 4, 2012, amended the disclosure provisions of the Ethics in Government Act rather than creating a separate disclosure code. The STOCK Act affirmed that the insider trading prohibitions reach Members of Congress and other covered officials, required covered filers to submit periodic transaction reports of securities transactions within forty five days of the transaction, and required electronic public availability of disclosure reports for the most senior filers. A 2013 statute, Public Law 113-22, narrowed the electronic posting requirement for most filers below the most senior ranks. The amendments confirm that the 1978 disclosure framework remained the operative foundation, since Congress modernized the filing duties inside the original statute instead of replacing them.

### Q: How often do officials file financial disclosure reports under the Ethics in Government Act?

Covered officials file on a three part rhythm set by the statute. A new entrant files an initial report within thirty days of assuming a covered position, so the filer's interests are on the record before the exercise of power begins. The filer then reports annually, with the annual report due by May 15 covering the preceding calendar year. A termination report follows departure from the covered position, closing the record for the period of service. Candidates for the presidency, the vice presidency and Congress file on a schedule tied to candidacy. The rhythm matters because disclosure detects conflicts through comparison over time: a transaction or a new outside position appears against the baseline of the entry report, and the annual sequence makes gradual changes visible to the reviewing ethics office and to any member of the public who inspects the filings.

### Q: What is the difference between an independent counsel and a special counsel?

The two titles belong to different legal instruments. An independent counsel was a creature of statute: appointed by a three judge panel on the application of the Attorney General under the Ethics in Government Act, vested by law with the prosecutorial powers of the Department of Justice within a jurisdiction the panel defined, and removable only by the Attorney General and only for good cause. That office ceased to exist when the statute expired on June 30, 1999. A special counsel is a creature of regulation: appointed by the Attorney General under 28 C.F.R. Part 600, given jurisdiction by the Attorney General's own order, housed within the Department of Justice, and removable by the Attorney General under the regulation's stated grounds. The statutory office derived its independence from law that the executive could not alter. The regulatory office holds its independence at the pleasure of the department that created it.

### Q: Who appointed an independent counsel under the Ethics in Government Act?

Appointment belonged to the special division, a panel of three judges of the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit designated by the Chief Justice for that function. The process began inside the executive branch: the Attorney General, on receiving specific and credible information that a covered person may have violated federal criminal law, conducted a preliminary investigation, and if reasonable grounds for further investigation existed, the Attorney General was required to apply to the special division. The panel then selected the counsel and defined the counsel's prosecutorial jurisdiction in its appointment order. The design deliberately divided the appointment between branches, so that the decision to trigger the mechanism rested with the Attorney General while the choice of the prosecutor and the boundaries of the assignment rested with judges who held no stake in the administration under examination.

### Q: Could an independent counsel be removed under the Ethics in Government Act?

Yes, but only through a narrow channel that the Supreme Court examined closely in Morrison v. Olson. The statute permitted removal by the Attorney General personally, and only for good cause, a standard the statute illustrated by physical disability, mental incapacity or any other condition substantially impairing the counsel's performance. The President could not remove a counsel directly, no subordinate official could act in the Attorney General's place, and dissatisfaction with the counsel's investigative direction was not itself a statutory ground. The majority in Morrison treated that restricted channel as the feature that preserved sufficient executive control, since the Attorney General retained a genuine, if bounded, power to act against a counsel who was not performing competently. The dissent answered that any channel short of removal at will transferred executive power away from the President, which is why the removal clause sat at the center of the constitutional dispute.

### Q: Does the Ethics in Government Act ban former officials from working for private companies?

No. The post-employment bars regulate representation, not employment. A former official may take a private position, including a position with a company that does extensive business with the government, and may advise that employer behind the scenes, subject to the bars. What the official may not do is step forward as the company's representative before the government on matters the statute reserves: never, on a particular matter involving specific parties in which the official participated personally and substantially; for two years, on matters that were under the official's responsibility; and, for senior officials, not before their former agency at all during the one year cooling-off period. The distinction reflects the statute's target. The corruption risk lies in the former official's access and influence being presented to the government on a client's behalf, and that presentational act is what the bars withdraw.

### Q: How long did the Attorney General have to conduct a preliminary investigation under the Ethics in Government Act?

The statute bounded the preliminary investigation at ninety days. Within that window the Attorney General weighed whether specific and credible information about a covered person warranted a full investigation, using the limited tools the statute allowed at that stage. If the inquiry could not be completed in the period, the Attorney General could ask the special division for one extension, which the statute capped at sixty additional days. At the end of the process the Attorney General faced a fork with asymmetric review. A determination that no reasonable grounds existed closed the matter and was not subject to judicial review, while a determination that grounds existed obliged the Attorney General to apply to the special division for an appointment. The clock therefore structured the gatekeeping function: long enough for a genuine screening inquiry, short enough that the screening itself could not become an indefinite substitute for the appointment decision.

### Q: What does the Office of Government Ethics do with a financial disclosure report after it is filed?

The office's review depends on the filer's rank. Reports of presidential nominees and the most senior executive officials go to the Office of Government Ethics itself, where reviewers examine the filing for conflicts of interest, comparing reported assets, income sources and outside arrangements against the duties of the position. Where a conflict appears, the standard remedies are administrative: recusal from particular matters, divestiture of an asset, resignation from an outside position, or a written ethics agreement recording the steps the filer will take. Reports of other executive branch filers are reviewed by the designated agency ethics official in the filer's own agency, under standards the office sets. The office does not prosecute false filings; a filing that suggests a knowing violation is referred into channels where the Department of Justice and the employing agency hold the enforcement authority. Review is thus preventive in design, aimed at arranging an official's affairs so that the conflict never has to be punished.

### Q: Which officials were covered persons under the independent counsel provisions of the Ethics in Government Act?

The statute defined a class of senior figures whose alleged offenses the ordinary department could not credibly investigate itself. The class included the President and the Vice President, cabinet level officers, the senior leadership of the Department of Justice including the Attorney General and the Deputy and Associate Attorneys General, the Director and Deputy Director of Central Intelligence, the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, and specified senior officials of the President's election campaign organization, among other senior positions the statute enumerated. Coverage also extended for one year after a covered person left office, so that resignation could not defeat the mechanism. The logic of the class was positional rather than personal: it gathered the officials who stood at or near the apex of the executive hierarchy, where the Attorney General's subordinate relationship to the subjects of an investigation was most acute and where an appointment outside the department carried its strongest justification.

### Q: How did the Ethics Reform Act of 1989 change the Ethics in Government Act?

The Ethics Reform Act of 1989, Public Law 101-194, was the most substantial revision of the ethics statute after its enactment, and it worked mainly by widening the framework's reach. The 1978 post-employment bars had been built for the executive branch; the 1989 act extended revolving door restrictions to the legislative branch, bringing Members of Congress and senior congressional staff within a cooling-off structure of their own. The act also tightened the surrounding regime, limiting outside earned income for higher salaried officials across the branches, restricting honoraria, expanding financial disclosure in specified respects, and placing new limits on gifts and privately funded travel. The pattern of the revision is instructive: a decade after enactment, Congress treated the 1978 architecture as the settled foundation and amended it outward, applying its premises to a branch the original statute had covered for disclosure but not for post-employment advocacy.

### Q: Where were the independent counsel provisions of the Ethics in Government Act codified?

The provisions sat in Title 28 of the United States Code, the judiciary title, at sections 591 to 599, because Title VI of the 1978 act operated by amending Title 28 and lodging the mechanism alongside the provisions governing the Department of Justice and the courts. Section 591 defined the covered persons and the Attorney General's duties on receiving information; the sections that followed carried the preliminary investigation, the application to the special division, the counsel's powers and duties, and the removal standard. The placement expressed the mechanism's hybrid design, since the office depended on both the Attorney General and a judicial panel. After the provisions expired on June 30, 1999, the sections remained in the code as the record of a lapsed authority, while the operative authority for outside prosecutors moved into the Code of Federal Regulations at 28 C.F.R. Part 600, a change of address that mirrors the change from statute to regulation.

### Q: Why was the independent counsel originally called a special prosecutor?

The office created in 1978 carried the name special prosecutor, the title inherited from the ad hoc prosecutors appointed during the Watergate period under the Attorney General's own authority. The Ethics in Government Act Amendments of 1982, Public Law 97-409, signed on January 3, 1983, substituted the title independent counsel, and the office operated under the second name through the reauthorizations of 1987 and 1994 until the provisions expired in 1999. The renaming changed no power, duty or procedure; the appointment mechanism, the jurisdiction defining role of the special division and the good cause removal standard were identical under either label. The dual naming explains a persistent source problem for researchers, since accounts of the office's first years speak of special prosecutors while the constitutional litigation and the later reauthorizations speak of independent counsels, although a single statutory office is meant in both vocabularies.

### Q: How can a student remember the structure of the Ethics in Government Act?

The reliable mnemonic is the four pillar frame used throughout this profile: disclosure, office, door, counsel. Disclosure points to the public financial reports required by the first three titles, one title for each branch. Office points to the Office of Government Ethics created by Title IV, the advisory institution that administers the system. Door points to the revolving door bars of Title V, the permanent, two year and one year restrictions on post-employment advocacy. Counsel points to the independent counsel of Title VI, the pillar that was litigated in Morrison v. Olson and allowed to expire in 1999. A student who can state what each pillar did, which titles carried it, and which three pillars survived can reconstruct the statute on an examination without memorizing the seven titles in sequence, and can then attach the details: the 1978 enactment date, the 1988 decision, the 1999 lapse and the 2012 STOCK Act amendments to the disclosure pillar.
