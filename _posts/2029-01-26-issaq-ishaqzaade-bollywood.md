Two Hindi films, released fourteen months apart in the same northern Indian state, set out to retell the same tragedy, and they divide the play between them almost as if by negotiation. Habib Faisal's *Ishaqzaade* (2012) takes the feud of religion and electoral politics. Manish Tiwary's *Issaq* (2013) takes the feud of clan, caste pride and land. One film became a commercial hit, launched a leading man, and carried its heroine to a National Film Award special mention. The other became a documented box office disaster and a byword among reviewers for adaptation gone wrong. The verdict of this comparison is plain, and the evidence for it is structural rather than sentimental: *Ishaqzaade* is a genuine adaptation of *Romeo and Juliet*, because it understands that the play's feud is not background colour but a working system with its own economy, its own honours and its own appetite, while *Issaq* borrows the plot's furniture, renames the families, and loses the system in transit. Issaq keeps more of the play's incidents. Ishaqzaade keeps more of the play.

![Issaq and Ishaqzaade compared as Bollywood adaptations of the Verona feud - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The comparison matters beyond two filmographies because the play itself refuses to explain its feud. An adapter of *Romeo and Juliet* cannot copy the cause of the hatred, since the text supplies none; the cause must be invented, and the invention is the adaptation. What a filmmaker chooses as the reason two households kill each other is the truest statement the film makes about the society it addresses. Faisal chose an election fought across a Hindu and Muslim divide in a fictional Uttar Pradesh town. Tiwary chose a war between two sand-mining clans on the ghats of Banaras, complicated by a Naxal insurgency that neither family controls. Both choices are intelligent on paper. Only one survives contact with the play's design, and the distance between the two outcomes is the most instructive lesson the Indian screen offers about what this tragedy is actually made of.

One refinement of the frame should be registered at the outset, because it organises everything that follows. The three fractures usually listed for these films, caste, religion and politics, are not shared evenly between them, and the unevenness is the comparison's first finding. *Ishaqzaade* takes religion and politics, fusing them into a single electoral machine in which a Hindu house and a Muslim house contest one seat. *Issaq* takes clan and caste pride, the internal hierarchy of one religious world, and grounds them in land. Neither film attempts all three fractures at once, and the one that came closer to the full Indian stack of divisions is the one that chose fewer of them and fused those completely. A feud, like a government, fails by diffusion. The play's Verona holds its hatred at the level of pure form, two houses and no reasons, and the adapter's task is to find the smallest set of local reasons that can generate the same form. Faisal's set has two elements in fusion. Tiwary's set has three elements in parallel, clan, contract and insurgency, and parallel fractures, unlike fused ones, can each be settled separately, which is why his film keeps almost settling and its lovers keep almost escaping.

## One State, Two Productions, Opposite Fortunes

The documented production records describe near-perfect symmetry and near-perfect divergence. *Ishaqzaade* was written and directed by Habib Faisal, produced by Aditya Chopra under the Yash Raj Films banner, and released on 11 May 2012. Its story is credited to Aditya Chopra and Habib Faisal, its music to Amit Trivedi with lyrics chiefly by Kausar Munir, and its photography to Hemant Chaturvedi. It starred Arjun Kapoor in his debut as Parma Chauhan and Parineeti Chopra, in her first leading role after a supporting debut the previous year, as Zoya Qureshi, with Gauahar Khan as the brothel keeper Chand Bibi. Filming began on 15 October 2011 and took place largely in and around Lucknow and Hardoi. The reported budget was 16 crore rupees, and the worldwide gross reached 61.3 crore rupees, with a domestic net of roughly 45.4 crore: a clear commercial success for a film carried by two near-newcomers, and one that broke the first-week record for a Hindi film without an established star cast.

*Issaq* was directed by Manish Tiwary, written by Padmaja Thakore-Tiwary, Manish Tiwary and Pawan Sony, produced by Dhaval Gada and Shailesh R. Singh for Pen Studios, and released on 26 July 2013. It starred Prateik Babbar as Rahul Mishra and Amyra Dastur, in her debut, as Bachchi Kashyap, supported by a large character ensemble that included Ravi Kishan, Makarand Deshpande, Neena Gupta, Rajeshwari Sachdev, Sudhir Pandey and Prashant Narayanan. Its songs were composed principally by Sachin-Jigar, with contributions from Sachin Gupta and Krsna. Box Office India records a budget of 25 crore rupees against a gross of about 5.4 crore, and files the film under the trade's most brutal verdict category, disaster. The more expensive film recovered barely a fifth of its reported cost. The cheaper film returned close to four times its budget. The symmetry extends to the casting pattern, since each production paired a connected male newcomer, a son of the industry in Kapoor's case and the son of two celebrated actors in Babbar's, with a debuting or near-debuting heroine, and to the geography, since both stories unfold in Uttar Pradesh within a few hundred kilometres of each other. Everything else diverges.

### How large is the gap between the two films' commercial records?

The gap is the difference between a hit and a disaster on comparable material. *Ishaqzaade*, made for a reported 16 crore rupees, grossed 61.3 crore worldwide and held for weeks on the strength of word of mouth. *Issaq*, made for a reported 25 crore rupees, grossed roughly 5.4 crore and was classified a disaster by Box Office India.

One production multiplied its cost nearly fourfold in gross receipts; the other failed to recover even a quarter of its cost, despite opening a year later on the same story, in the same state, with an explicit Shakespeare banner in its publicity.

The critical records diverge in the same direction, though with one loud dissent that any honest comparison must register. *Ishaqzaade* drew mixed to warm notices: Taran Adarsh of Bollywood Hungama awarded three and a half stars and called it "a volatile and intense story with ample doses of fanatical romance," Rachit Gupta of Filmfare awarded three stars and called it a "fantastic Indian adaptation of Romeo and Juliet," and Anupama Chopra concluded that "*Ishaqzaade* does provide half a good time," a sentence whose grudging arithmetic still concedes the half. Against those stand Rajeev Masand's two and a half stars and a charge that the film resorts to "an unforgivably unoriginal climax," and Trisha Gupta's Firstpost critique of its ending and its thematic execution. *Issaq* drew something closer to a consensus of rejection. Shubhra Gupta of The Indian Express found it "without a singular voice of its own" and judged that "it drowns in its own noise," awarding one and a half stars. The Times of India held that the film "lacks vibe, soul or depth needed for a classic love story" and that "Tiwary misses every target." Piyasree Dasgupta of Firstpost asked "what is a greater tragedy, *Romeo and Juliet* or what *Issaq* made of that classic love story." Raja Sen of Rediff headlined his notice "an insultingly bad film." The single prominent dissent came from Subhash K. Jha, who praised a "desi Varanasi must-see adaptation" that "would certainly have made Shakespeare smile" and called the film "one of the most innovative adaptations of Romeo & Juliet in any language." That dissent is examined on its merits in the critics section below, because a verdict that ignores the strongest contrary review is a verdict half argued.

Between the two releases sits a third film that neither production could ignore, and which this comparison touches only at the length the record requires. Sanjay Leela Bhansali's *Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela*, released in November 2013, four months after *Issaq*, carried the same play to a Gujarati clan war and to the year's commercial heights, a trajectory traced in full in [the companion study of Bhansali's Gujarati version](/2029/01/19/ram-leela-bhansali-adaptation/). The sequence matters for one reason only: by the end of 2013, the Indian market had delivered three verdicts on the same adaptation problem within nineteen months, and the ranking of the three by both commerce and criticism is the ranking this article's structural argument predicts. The film that rebuilt the feud as a functioning system prospered. The film that rebuilt it as décor did not.

The two titles encode the two methods before a frame is seen. *Ishaqzaade* is a coinage, built from *ishq*, love, and the Persian-derived suffix *zaade*, born of: the love-born, those begotten by love rather than by their houses. The film's international festival title sharpened the coinage into a thesis, "Born to Hate... Destined to Love," assigning birth to the feud and destiny to the love, an inversion of the play's Prologue, in which birth assigns the feud and the stars assign the rest. *Issaq*, by contrast, is a spelling: the same root word for love, *ishq*, respelled with a double s for distinctiveness and left to carry the film's whole claim. One title argues; the other brands. The marketing repeated the division. *Ishaqzaade* was sold by its banner as a launch and a love story against a political backdrop, its trailer released on 15 March 2012 to a campaign that foregrounded the newcomers and the town. *Issaq* was sold explicitly on the Shakespeare connection, its listings and publicity announcing a contemporary adaptation of the play set in the Uttar Pradesh heartland, its promotions extending to Banarasi paan stalls in Mumbai malls. The film that advertised the source least insistently is the film that rebuilt it. The film that advertised the source in its first sentence is the film that rented it. Adaptation studies rarely receive so clean a controlled comparison, two productions of the same play in the same state within fourteen months, differing chiefly in their theory of what adaptation is. The sections that follow are an attempt to state that difference in full.

## The Play's Empty Centre: A Feud Without a Cause

Every adaptation of this play begins at the same void. The Chorus opens the tragedy with a summary that names everything except the one thing a realist narrative needs: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue, lines 1 to 4). The grudge is ancient, which is to say its origin is placed beyond memory and therefore beyond inquiry. No scene in the play recovers the cause. No character states it. When the Prince intervenes after the first street brawl, he condemns the violence in terms that reach for origins and find only abstractions, "bred of an airy word" in an old man's quarrel (1.1). Jill L. Levenson, editor of the Oxford Shakespeare edition of the play, treats the hatred in her introduction as a given of the Veronese world, a condition the drama inherits rather than explains, and the editorial tradition behind her edition records the same blank across the early texts: the quartos and the Folio all begin after the cause has been forgotten. The full anatomy of that blank, and of the criticism built upon it, belongs to [the series study of why the Capulets and Montagues hate](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/); what matters here is the consequence for filmmakers. The feud in the play is pure structure: two houses "both alike in dignity," perfectly symmetrical, divided by a distinction without a stated difference.

### What cause does the play itself give for the Verona feud?

None, and the absence is deliberate design rather than missing information. The Prologue calls the hatred an "ancient grudge" and moves on within four lines to its consequences. The text holds that silence from the first scene to the last.

No character in five acts names the insult, injury, debt or rivalry that started it, and the Prince's own account traces the brawls to "an airy word." Shakespeare inherited source stories in which the feud is equally unexplained and chose to preserve the blank. An adapter therefore cannot transcribe the feud's cause from the text. The cause has to be supplied from the adapter's own society, which is why the invented cause is the most revealing decision in any screen version.

That design has a corollary that both films test. Because the cause is empty, the feud in the play operates as a machine for converting private feeling into public death. Mercutio, who belongs to neither house, dies of it anyway, and dies naming the mechanism: "A plague o' both your houses! I am sped" (3.1). Juliet formulates the machine's logic from inside the other house: "My only love sprung from my only hate" (1.5). The Friar's scheme treats the machine as reversible, since a marriage between the houses might convert its energy rather than merely defy it: "For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3). And the closing tableau measures the machine's cost with bureaucratic precision, the Prince informing the fathers that heaven has found "means to kill your joys with love" (5.3), before old Capulet offers the handshake of exhausted enemies: "O brother Montague, give me thy hand" (5.3). An adaptation succeeds, by the standard the play itself sets, when its invented feud can perform these four operations: absorb bystanders, poison love at its source, tempt a mediator into engineering peace through marriage, and stop only when the heirs are spent. Measured against those operations, the two Uttar Pradesh films are not two versions of one achievement. They are a pass and a failure.

One scene in the play functions as a control experiment for both films, and it is worth isolating before the film sections begin. At the Capulet feast, Tybalt recognises Romeo's voice and moves to expel him by force, and old Capulet forbids the violence in his own hall: the intruder "shall be endur'd" (1.5), because the feast's honour and the city's peace outweigh the feud's claim at the patriarch's own table. The moment defines the play's patriarchy at its most governable: the feud is real, but its senior custodians ration it, and the tragedy requires the rationing to fail gradually, through Tybalt's disobedient resentment and the street fight of 3.1, rather than all at once. *Issaq* reproduces the beat with notable fidelity: at the Kashyap Holi celebration, Teeta discovers the infiltrated Rahul and moves to kill him, and Kashyap restrains his brother-in-law publicly, humiliating him with the assertion that the house and its orders are Kashyap's own. The correspondence extends to the consequence, since the humiliated enforcer's resentment fuels the killing of Murari that follows. *Ishaqzaade* inverts the beat at its climax of the first movement: when the feud's claim reaches Surya Chauhan's own hall, in the person of a daughter-in-law under his roof, the patriarch does not ration the violence but performs it, shooting Parvati as she shields the girl. The inversion is the film's whole politics in one gesture. Verona's patriarchs are failing governors of a hatred older than themselves. Almore's senior patriarch is the hatred's chief executive, and governance is the first casualty the film records. An adapter who reproduces the feast beat has read the play. An adapter who inverts it has understood what the beat was measuring.

Poonam Trivedi, the leading scholar of Shakespeare in Indian cinemas and co-editor with Paromita Chakravarti of the Routledge volume *Shakespeare and Indian Cinemas: Local Habitations*, has argued across her work, beginning with the essay "'Filmi' Shakespeare" in Literature/Film Quarterly, that Indian popular cinema does not adapt Shakespeare in the reverent, fidelity-counting sense at all: it absorbs the plays into its own melodramatic and musical idioms, producing "filmi" transformations whose value lies in the local habitation they build for the borrowed story. The standard Trivedi's work implies is exacting in a way fidelity criticism is not. A filmi transformation earns its Shakespeare when the local habitation is fully built, when the borrowed plot's pressures arise from the new world's own rules rather than from the screenwriter's memory of the source. By that standard, the question for Banaras and for Almore is not which film keeps more scenes. It is which invented world generates the tragedy out of its own materials. The sections that follow apply that test film by film.

## Ishaqzaade: The Feud as Election in Almore

Faisal's foundational decision is to give the ancient grudge a renewable resource. The Chauhans and the Qureshis of Almore, a fictional town in Uttar Pradesh, are rival political families contesting the same legislative seat, and their hatred is refuelled on a fixed schedule by the electoral cycle itself. This is the single most intelligent invention in either film, because it solves the play's central adaptation problem in a locally exact way. Verona's feud persists without a cause; Almore's persists because it is useful. Every election converts inherited enmity into mobilised voters, armed retinues and polarised neighbourhoods, so no living character needs to remember why the families hate: the hatred pays. Surya Chauhan, the patriarch played by Anil Rastogi, and Aftab Qureshi, played by Ratan Singh, face each other as candidates and as heads of houses in the same gesture, exactly as the play's old Capulet is at once father, host and feud principal. Where the play gives its patriarchs no function beyond the feud, Faisal gives his a function that explains the feud's survival. The grudge has a payroll.

The religious divide is fused to the political one rather than substituted for it, and the fusion is the film's diagnosis. The Chauhans are Hindu and the Qureshis Muslim, and the campaign draws its energy from communal polarisation, so that a vote for the family is also a vote against the other faith's candidate. The play's symmetry, two households "both alike in dignity," survives intact: both families are equally armed, equally cynical, equally ready to stage violence for advantage, and the film's early reels show the young of both houses trading ritual abuse as children, an opening that compresses the Prologue's "ancient grudge" into a single playground scene. Zoya, the Qureshi daughter, is introduced as a practising Muslim who prays five times a day and who trades her gold earrings for a revolver, a characterisation that refuses the passive Juliet of lazy tradition. She wants her father's career. Parma, the Chauhan grandson, is introduced as a wastrel enforcer desperate for his grandfather's respect, a Romeo whose idleness is a family embarrassment rather than a poetic condition. Both portraits do what the play does: they make the lovers legible first as products of their houses, so that the love reads as treason against the system that manufactured them.

### Why does Parma marry Zoya the first time?

The first marriage is a fraud, and its fraudulence is the film's darkest invention. The wedding is staged as an act of war, planned by the bridegroom against the bride's house, and its target is a family name rather than a woman's heart.

Parma, humiliated by Zoya in their running feud and hungry to earn his grandfather's approval, devises a revenge: he courts her, converts to Islam and takes the name Parvez, submits to a marriage ceremony, and consummates the union in an abandoned railway coach, then reveals that the ceremony was staged and the marriage a weapon. The object is not seduction but dishonour, the public destruction of the Qureshi daughter's reputation and, through her, of her father's campaign. The play's secret wedding, arranged by the Friar as an instrument of peace, is inverted into a secret wedding arranged by the bridegroom as an instrument of war.

The inversion deserves dwelling on, because it is here that the film either earns or forfeits the play. In Verona, the marriage is the one act in the tragedy performed in good faith by a mediator who believes alliance can dissolve hatred, and its secrecy is a concession to the feud's power. In Almore, the marriage is performed in bad faith by a combatant who believes alliance is a fiction usable for humiliation, and its secrecy is the weapon itself. Faisal is not decorating the source. He is restaging its central device inside a society where a daughter's dishonour is a transferable political asset, and the restaging exposes something the play leaves latent: the Friar's plan and Parma's plot are the same manoeuvre viewed from opposite moral poles, the conversion of a young woman's marriage into a settlement between houses. The film then does the harder thing. Parvati, Parma's widowed mother, played by Natasha Rastogi, learns what her son has done and insists that the marriage be honoured in fact, that Zoya be taken into the house as a wife. Verona has no equivalent figure of force: Lady Montague dies offstage of grief, and the play's mothers are permitted no intervention in the feud's conduct. Parvati's insistence is the film's addition to the design, and the feud answers it with the play's own logic. When Zoya is brought to the Chauhan house and the family moves to kill her, Parvati shields the girl and Surya Chauhan shoots his own daughter-in-law dead. The patriarch kills within his own line to preserve the line's honour, the exact transaction the play's ending generalises when heaven kills the fathers' "joys with love."

The second half converts the fraud into its opposite by the play's own machinery of flight and sanctuary. Parma, turned against his house by his mother's death, flees with Zoya to the brothel of Chand Bibi, who shelters the couple. Chand Bibi occupies the structural position of the Friar, the outsider of compromised standing whose premises offer the lovers their only neutral ground, and the film is honest about the substitution: where the Friar's cell represents an institution above the feud, Chand Bibi's house represents an institution beneath the town's notice, and in Almore only the beneath-notice world is neutral. The couple marry again, in earnest and in proper form, the film thereby running the play's wedding device twice, once as weapon and once as sacrament, so that the audience can weigh the two ceremonies against each other. Zoya then makes the error the feud has designed for her, returning to her father's house at Eid in the hope that festivity and a fait accompli might reconcile the families. Her father receives her with a threat against her life. The hope the Friar invested in the marriage, that alliance might turn rancour to love, is tested by the daughter of one house in person, and the house rejects it.

### Why do both families hunt the lovers together in the last act?

Because the marriage, once genuine, threatens both houses in the same way at the same time. A Chauhan and Qureshi union that voters can see gives the lie to the polarisation on which both campaigns depend, and it produces an heir in whom the two constituencies merge.

The film therefore arrives at the play's buried premise by political reasoning: the houses that could never cooperate in peace cooperate instantly in murder, since the lovers' existence is the one interest the rivals share. The feud's final form is an alliance, the two families pooling gunmen to restore the division their power requires. Verona's fathers shake hands over the bodies only when the heirs are gone. Almore's fathers join hands before the killing, to make sure the heirs do not survive. The variation is bleaker than the source and entirely earned by the film's premise.

The ending is the restaging on which the whole comparison turns, and it must be described exactly. Pursued by the combined forces of both families, Parma and Zoya are cornered on the rooftop of a college, short of ammunition, on Eid. The families do not shoot them. With escape impossible, the lovers turn their remaining bullets on each other at Zoya's urging, so that, in the logic the film has built, love and not hatred fires the last shots; they die in each other's arms, smiling, while the gunmen withdraw and the two families depart satisfied that the matter is settled. A closing on-screen message then states the film's thesis without metaphor, addressing the killing of lovers who cross caste and religious lines in India. The play's double suicide, Romeo's poison and Juliet's dagger in the tomb, is a private catastrophe produced by failed messages and mistimed waking. The film's double shooting is a public execution converted, at the final second, into a mutual act the executioners are denied. Where Shakespeare's lovers die because the feud's world interrupts their plan, Faisal's lovers die because the feud's world leaves them exactly one decision, and they spend it on each other. Critics who called the climax unoriginal were measuring its outline, two lovers dead by their own hands, against the source. Measured by function, the outline is the only element retained. Everything the ending means has been rebuilt.

Three further elements complete the film's claim to be an adaptation rather than an exploitation of the title. The first is the treatment of honour killing as a practice rather than a plot spice: the film's families regard the lovers' deaths as administrative, a restoration of order, and the closing message names the real-world category to which the fiction belongs, a framing contemporary reviewers registered with surprise, the TIFF notice of the film remarking plainly that the reviewer had not expected a Yash Raj production to turn into a film about honour killing. The second is Zoya's retained agency at the point of death: the final decision is hers, proposed by her and accepted by Parma, so the Juliet figure authors the ending that the feud intended to author, an answer to the play's Juliet, who likewise seizes the dagger and the decision when the men around her have finished improvising. The third is the soundtrack's structural use: Amit Trivedi's songs, above all "Pareshaan," in Shalmali Kholgade's Filmfare-winning playback performance, carry the interior states that a Hindi film cannot leave to dialogue, and the music was received as integral rather than interruptive, a point on which reviewers as divided as Adarsh and Masand agree.

Two scenes carry the film's courtship, and both are inventions with no counterpart in the play, which is the point. The first is the slap: when Parma, early in their acquaintance, levels a gun at Zoya in the feud's ordinary currency of threat, she answers by striking him, the first blow of the film struck by the person the economy least permits to strike. Festival reviewers singled the moment out as the hinge of both lives, and its structural work is exact: it establishes that the love, when it comes, will be between two people who have already met as combatants on equal terms, so that the fraudulent marriage reads as a combatant's treachery rather than a rake's routine. The second is the consummation in the abandoned railway coach after the fraudulent ceremony, a scene the notices treated with unusual respect, the Times of India calling it sensitively shot even within a review that faulted the second half's collapse. The scene's placement is the film's boldest structural wager: the physical union precedes the revelation of fraud by minutes, so the audience is made to hold the marriage as real at the very moment the film declares it counterfeit, and Zoya's subsequent arc, from the gun she turns toward Parma to the rooftop she shares with him, is the long correction of that single scene's double exposure. Verona's wedding night is reported, veiled and lyrical. Almore's is shown, weaponised and then slowly, at the cost of a mother's life, made honest.

The director's preparation for this material is part of the documented record and explains the film's command of its milieu. Faisal arrived at *Ishaqzaade* as the screenwriter of *Band Baaja Baaraat* and the director of *Do Dooni Chaar*, works rooted in the textures of ordinary north Indian life, and reviewers who praised the film's small-town verisimilitude, Shomini Sen among them, commending the depiction of small-town life and its cultural nuances, were identifying a specialism rather than a lucky location scout. The feud in Almore is observed at the level of its daily transactions, the fuel shed burned because its owner supplies the rival clan, the dance troupe contested like a constituency, the child abuse exchanged like a greeting, and that granularity is what allows the film's large inventions, the fraudulent marriage, the joint manhunt, to register as developments of a system rather than as a screenwriter's escalations. Tiwary's Banaras is observed at the level of its vistas. The distinction between a system and a vista is the distinction between the two films, and it is visible in every department from the feud's economics to the heroines' firearms.

## Issaq: The Feud as Land War in Banaras

Tiwary's *Issaq* announces its method in its cast list, which maps the play's dramatis personae onto Banaras with unusual completeness. Rahul Mishra is the Romeo figure and Bachchi Kashyap the Juliet. Her maternal uncle Teeta Singh, played by Ravi Kishan, carries the Tybalt function; the holy man Baba, played by Makarand Deshpande, carries the Friar's; the maid Amma, played by Neena Gupta, carries the Nurse's; Preetam, the police officer chosen as Bachchi's bridegroom, carries Paris's; Rahul's friend Murari, played by Amit Sial, carries Mercutio's; the foreigner Roza carries Rosaline's. The Mishras and the Kashyaps are rival land and sand-mining clans, the "sand mafia" of the Ganges riverbank, fighting over contracts and riverbed in a city where illegal sand extraction is documented as both an economy and a source of violence. Kashyap, played by Sudhir Pandey, rules one house with his second wife Paro, played by Rajeshwari Sachdev, as its Lady Capulet; Manohar Mishra, played by Sandeep Bose, heads the other. Into this two-house design the film inserts a third armed force that the play has no place for: a Naxal insurgency under a leader played by Prashant Narayanan, hired, courted and deployed by the very parties the insurgency exists to fight. The film was promoted explicitly as a Romeo and Juliet adaptation, in festival listings and in its own publicity carried through television serial placements and Banarasi paan stalls in Mumbai malls, so the fidelity question cannot be evaded as a category error. *Issaq* asked to be judged as the play transplanted, and the reviews judged it so.

The feud the film invents is, on paper, the more concrete of the two. Sand is money. A riverbed contract is a measurable asset, a murdered contractor a measurable loss, and a feud over extraction rights has the documentary solidity that Almore's electoral hatred achieves by a different route. The Banaras setting reinforces the claim: the ghats, the Holi festivities at which Rahul infiltrates the Kashyap celebration and first reaches Bachchi, the city's compression of sanctity and violence into the same lanes. Jha's defence of the film rests substantially on this surface, on frames of bangles and coloured water and rooftop falls, and the surface is genuinely the film's strongest asset; cinematographer Vishal Sinha's Banaras is photographed with an affection the script does not match. The difficulty is that a feud over an asset behaves differently from the play's feud, and the film never notices the difference. Assets can be divided, bought out, surrendered or compensated; the play's grudge cannot, because it has no object. When a story's hatred has a price, every scene of refusal to settle reads as a business decision awaiting a better offer, and the tragedy's necessity leaks away. Almore's hatred also has an object, votes, but votes in a polarised election cannot be shared without dissolving the identities that contest them; the object renews the hatred by being pursued. Sand merely depletes.

### What does the Naxal thread do to the play's design?

It breaks the symmetry on which the tragedy depends. The play's Verona contains exactly two houses and a neutral authority, the Prince, whose failure to govern is part of the design. Every additional armed party dilutes the concentration of blame on which the tragedy depends.

*Issaq* adds an insurgent army that both clans attempt to use: the Naxal leader is courted as an instrument, most nakedly when Preetam, the approved bridegroom, seeks the insurgents' help to eliminate Rahul and clear his own path to Bachchi. A third force with its own politics converts a two-house tragedy into a factional war film, in which the lovers' deaths become one outcome among several armed agendas. Reviewers registered the intrusion as incoherence, the Koimoi notice calling the communist angle forced and pointless, because the film assigns the insurgency no stake in the lovers and no consequence in the feud's resolution. The Prince's structural place, meanwhile, is handed to police officers who are themselves combatants and suitors, so no figure in the film stands where Escalus stands, outside the hatred and answerable for the city.

The plot's middle follows the play's sequence with a fidelity that makes the film's failure more instructive. Teeta discovers Rahul at the Kashyap Holi celebration and is publicly restrained by Kashyap, who humiliates his brother-in-law rather than permit bloodshed in his own house, a recognisable Capulet-and-Tybalt beat. Teeta later kills Murari, the Mercutio figure, after a confrontation over Rahul's meetings with Bachchi, and Rahul kills Teeta in retaliation, the 3.1 engine firing on schedule. Kashyap then accelerates Bachchi's marriage to Preetam, the Paris arrangement, and Rahul becomes a fugitive from the police for Teeta's killing, the banishment beat restated as a manhunt. A film that tracks the source this closely cannot plead loose inspiration when its ending is examined, and the ending is where the comparison with Almore becomes decisive. *Issaq* retains the play's terminal machinery, the feigned death and the double suicide built on mistaken belief, down to the potion whose working Jha praised as an untampered Shakespearean ending. Rahul, believing Bachchi lost to him, takes his own life; Bachchi, waking from her staged death, follows him. The most faithful ending in either film belongs to the film that fails, which is the single most useful fact a student of adaptation can take from the pair. Fidelity to the machinery is worthless when the machinery's fuel has been changed. In Verona, the tomb catastrophe works because the feud has made honest communication impossible, so a sleeping girl and a uninformed lover are the system's natural products. In Banaras, the same catastrophe arrives as a plot requirement the film has not re-earned, because its lovers operate in a world of mobile phones, police networks and hired insurgents, where the information failure must be imposed rather than grown.

The film's second half, examined against the play's second half, shows the same correspondence without the same conversion. Trade reviewers who sat through the film professionally recorded the pattern without malice: Film Information's notice, the industry's own paper of record, found the romance unengaging, the lovers unsympathetic and the dialogue commonplace, conceding only that the climax holds a shocking twist that cannot salvage a film boring in long stretches before it. The concession is diagnostic. In the play, the tomb sequence shocks because every element in it has been prepared by a system the audience understands, the feud's communications blockade, the Friar's pharmacology, the watch's timing. In *Issaq*, the twist shocks because the system has not been built, and surprise substitutes for necessity. The manhunt structure of the second half, Rahul fleeing the police for Teeta's killing while the wedding to Preetam is accelerated and the bridegroom subcontracts the insurgents, generates motion in place of the play's tightening: banishment in Verona isolates Romeo from information, which is the tragedy's fuel, while the manhunt in Banaras isolates Rahul from capture, which is merely the plot's postponement. One structure converges on the tomb. The other circulates until the tomb is announced.

### Why do the reviewers who admire Issaq's images still reject the film?

Because the failure is located precisely where images cannot help, in the writing of the lovers. The notices that celebrate Banaras return, again and again, to everything except Rahul and Bachchi, and the pattern holds from the trade press to the single rave.

The Times of India found "incoherent narrative, unsketched characters" and dialogue at times embarrassing; the Indian Express found no singular voice beneath the noise; Koimoi's reviewer judged the lead actor dismally miscast and the heroine's character too sketchily written to carry sympathy; Firstpost folded the whole into its question of which was the greater tragedy, the play or the treatment. Even the film's admirers concede the ground by praising everything except the centre: Jha's rave celebrates colour and audacity while the romance it defends is asserted rather than demonstrated, and the notices that singled out acting praised the supporting cast, Ravi Kishan's Teeta and Vineet Kumar Singh's Benvolio figure Bihata among them, rather than the lovers at the centre. A Romeo and Juliet adaptation whose lovers do not compel belief has lost the one asset no setting can replace, since the play's entire feud machinery exists to be weighed against the weight of the love it crushes. In *Issaq* the feud is heavy and the love is light, and the scales never tremble.

## The Minor Machinery: Rosaline, the Nurse and the Missing Prince

The minor figures are where the two methods show most plainly, because minor figures cannot be carried by star performance and survive only as design. Rosaline, the play's unseen first love, exists in *Issaq* as Roza, a foreign hippie played by Evelyn Sharma, with whom Rahul is involved before Bachchi and through whom he gains entry to the Kashyap Holi celebration. The correspondence is exact and the function is improved on paper, since the play's Rosaline never appears and can therefore motivate nothing on screen; Roza motivates the plot's central infiltration. Yet the improvement stays inert, because the film treats the device as logistics. In the play, Rosaline's function is diagnostic: Romeo's fluent despair over her exposes a young man in love with the posture of love, so that the Juliet meeting can be measured as a change of kind. *Ishaqzaade* omits Rosaline entirely and performs the same diagnosis by other means, opening on Parma as a house enforcer whose energies are wholly feud-shaped, so that Zoya's irruption into them registers as the first unbought feeling of his life. One film keeps the character and loses the function. The other loses the character and keeps the function. The pattern by this point in the comparison will surprise no reader.

Mercutio completes the pattern, and his case is the most delicate because he is the play's licensed truth-teller, the one figure whose wit is permitted to puncture both houses and whose death turns the comedy. *Issaq* retains him as Murari, Rahul's friend, and retains his structural death: Teeta kills him in the quarrel over Rahul's meetings with Bachchi, and Rahul's retaliatory killing of Teeta follows as the play prescribes. The retention is again exact and again inert, because Murari is given loyalty without licence; the notices do not record a single line of his that survived the film, and a Mercutio whose wit leaves no trace cannot mark the hinge his death is meant to close. *Ishaqzaade* abolishes the role and distributes the licence: Zoya carries the puncturing wit in the film's first movement, her abuse and her slap doing Mercutio's work of saying what the feud forbids, and Chand Bibi carries his worldly knowledge in the second, the brothel keeper's clear-eyed account of what the town will and will not forgive. The hinge function is preserved by the distribution, since the film's turn from comedy to tragedy is executed not by a friend's death but by the revelation of the fraudulent marriage, the moment the wit stops. A role can be a person or a function. The play, read closely, insists it is a function wearing a person, and the two films' Mercutios are the smallest complete proof of which production read it closely.

The Nurse divides the same way. *Issaq* retains her as Amma, the maid played by Neena Gupta, a casting of real authority spent on a figure the script leaves decorative; the play's Nurse is Juliet's confidante, messenger and betrayer in sequence, the channel through which the secret marriage is supplied and then abandoned, and a film that keeps her name and loses her errands has kept the least of her. *Ishaqzaade* distributes her functions across the Qureshi household and Parvati: the brothers carry the surveillance, the mother the domestic interior, and Parvati the advocacy, with the betrayal function transferred entire to Parma himself, whose fraudulent marriage makes the bridegroom his own heroine's betrayer, a redistribution that darkens the design exactly where the play darkens it, in the discovery that the person closest to the bride has served the feud's interests with her trust.

### Where is the Prince in either film?

Neither film supplies an Escalus, and the shared absence is the pair's most consequential deviation from the play. In Verona the Prince stands outside the feud as the city's failed governor, pronouncing sentences the feud absorbs and closing the tragedy with an audit of his own negligence.

*Ishaqzaade* replaces the office with the electoral process itself: the state appears as the machinery both families are trying to capture, so no authority stands outside the contest, and the lovers' deaths are audited by no one but the closing caption. *Issaq* staffs the state with combatants, police officers who are also suitors and hunters, completing a world in which governance has been wholly absorbed into the war it exists to police.

Above both designs stands the Chorus, the play's own framing voice, and here the films' choices summarise their theories one final time. *Ishaqzaade* translates the Prologue into action, the childhood abuse scene doing the Chorus's work of announcing an inherited war and two marked houses before the lovers are grown. *Issaq* translates the Prologue into marketing, the star-crossed formula carried in the film's publicity synopses while the film itself begins inside the feud's operations without a frame. A chorus is a promise about how deaths will be interpreted. The film that staged its chorus interprets its deaths. The film that outsourced its chorus to the publicity leaves its deaths to the river.

## Two Juliets: The Gun and the Ghat

The sharpest single contrast between the films is carried by their heroines, because the Juliet figure is where an adaptation declares whether the feud's economy has been understood. Zoya Qureshi enters *Ishaqzaade* as a participant in her house's power: a daughter who prays, campaigns in her imagination, and buys a revolver with her gold earrings because the men of Almore carry weapons and she intends the same standing. The film's first movement grants her the full equipment of the feud, and its second movement shows the feud confiscating that equipment the moment her desire becomes inconvenient to it. Her trajectory is therefore an argument, not an arc of decoration: the house that let its daughter carry a gun for its honour will combine with its enemy to kill her for her marriage. Parineeti Chopra's performance was received as the film's centre by reviewers who agreed on little else, and the National Film Award special mention at the 60th National Film Awards ratified the consensus. The performance's distinction is that it keeps both registers alive at once, the enforcer and the bride, so that the fraudulent marriage lands as a violation of a person the audience has watched exercise power, not of an ingénue the plot has kept in cotton.

Bachchi Kashyap enters *Issaq* under the opposite dispensation. The documented record describes Kashyap's eighteen-year-old daughter as pretty and innocent, a girl who wishes to fall in love before marriage but whose inner life the film leaves, in the word the reviews returned to, unsketched. Amyra Dastur's debut performance was treated more gently than the writing around it, Koimoi finding her fine in a role too thinly drawn to give her a personality to work with, and the structural point stands independent of the actress: this Juliet is acted upon by every force in the film, courted by Rahul, bargained over by her father and uncle, promised to Preetam, mourned and avenged, while her one decisive act is reserved for the final reel's imitation of the play's dagger. Shakespeare's Juliet is the tragedy's most decisive intelligence, the character who proposes the marriage, drinks the potion and sets the terms of every scene she inhabits after the balcony. An adaptation may darken her, arm her or politicise her, as *Ishaqzaade* does, but it cannot thin her without thinning the tragedy, since the play's feud machinery exists to crush a will the audience has been made to respect.

### Why does Zoya's revolver matter to the adaptation's argument?

The revolver establishes that Zoya begins the film inside the feud's economy rather than outside it. She buys the weapon with her own jewellery, against the conventions of her house, to claim the armed standing her brothers enjoy. The purchase is the film's first full character statement.

When the same economy later marks her for death, the confiscation is complete: the gun that signified her enlistment cannot protect her, because the feud arms its young only for service, never for self-defence against the feud itself. The prop converts the play's abstract honour code into a single visible transaction.

The Romeos divide along the same line. Parma is written as a moral problem the film insists on solving the hard way: he begins as the author of the story's worst act, the fraudulent marriage, and the performance by the debuting Arjun Kapoor was received as convincing precisely because it does not soften the character's early cruelty into charm. Rahul, by contrast, is written as a temperament, battle-hardened, pleasure-seeking and fearless in the notices' own vocabulary, and a temperament cannot repent, scheme or choose in the ways the tragedy's second half requires of its Romeo. The play's Romeo kills Tybalt in a spasm and spends the remainder of the action inside the consequences, exiled, bargaining with an apothecary, racing a letter that never arrives. Rahul kills Teeta on schedule and spends the remainder of his film inside chases, which are consequences of geography rather than of character. One film's lovers are people the feud destroys. The other film's lovers are positions the feud occupies.

The *Ishaqzaade* column is a map of a society: rows name functions rather than counterparts, and where the play's personnel cannot be found, as with Paris and a single Tybalt, the absence is itself an analytical decision, since a political feud distributes its violence through parties and families rather than through one champion. The grid also exposes the one row where the supposedly freer film is the more conservative: the marriage. Faisal keeps the play's wedding at the centre of his design and builds his tragedy out of its two performances, honest and fraudulent. Tiwary, for all his surface fidelity, treats the wedding as one incident among many and reserves his fidelity for the tomb. The play's own priorities are Faisal's. In the text, the wedding occupies the structural centre and generates everything after it; the tomb is its consequence.

The reconciliation test divides the mediators one final way. In the play, the Friar's hypothesis, that a marriage can convert the feud, is never put to the fathers directly; it is destroyed in transit, by plague and misdirection, so the tragedy can preserve its irony that peace was possible and merely unlucky. *Ishaqzaade* refuses the irony and runs the experiment in person: Zoya carries the accomplished marriage home to her father at Eid, the calendar's appointed season of embrace, and the hypothesis is answered at the door with a threat against her life. The film's politics are completed by that refusal, since a feud maintained by electoral profit cannot be dissolved by a family's private happiness, and the daughter's embassy proves it at the cost of the lovers' last refuge. *Issaq* never runs the experiment at all. No character in Banaras proposes the marriage as a settlement, tests it before either patriarch, or imagines the feud as convertible; Baba counsels against the affair, the families escalate by contract logic, and the lovers' union remains a private fact in a film whose feud was never offered the chance to reject it. A tragedy of mediation requires the mediation to be attempted. One film attempts it and shows the door closing. The other leaves the door offstage, and its lovers die, in the end, of the attempt nobody made.

## Two Towns: An Invented Almore and a Photographed Banaras

Setting performs opposite work in the two films, and the opposition explains much of their difference in reputation. Almore does not exist. Faisal invented his town, shot it in and around Lucknow and Hardoi, and the invention is a discipline: a fictional polity can be given exactly the institutions the tragedy needs, two party machines, one seat, a brothel beyond the parties' reach, a college rooftop for the last stand, and nothing extraneous. Because Almore is invented, its sociology can be argued rather than merely displayed; the town is a model of a system, built to the tolerances of the feud it must sustain. Reviewers who praised the film's believable world were praising carpentry. The world convinces because every institution in it has been assigned a function in the tragedy's machinery, exactly as Verona's square, orchard, cell and tomb are functions before they are places.

Banaras exists, overwhelmingly, and the film's relation to it is devotional rather than analytical. The ghats, the river, the Holi crowds, the lanes and the bangle markets arrive on screen with the authority of the real, and the camera's love for them is the film's most documented quality. But a real city cannot be assigned functions; it brings its own meanings, its sanctity, its antiquity, its place in the audience's religious imagination, and those meanings compete with the feud rather than serving it. The result, visible across the reviews, is a film whose setting is praised in the vocabulary of tourism and whose story is condemned in the vocabulary of dramaturgy, as if the two judgements concerned different objects. They concern one object seen clearly: a location is not a world. Verona is not a documentary portrait of an Italian city but a machine for producing a tragedy, and Almore succeeds because it was built as one. Banaras fails, in this film, because it was borrowed as one.

### Which town does the play's Verona more closely resemble?

Almore, and by design rather than by geography. Shakespeare's Verona is itself an invention, an Italianate stage city assembled from source narrative and theatrical convenience, containing exactly the spaces the action requires and no surveyed reality. Faisal's fictional Almore repeats that method, a town whose institutions are the tragedy's moving parts.

Tiwary's Banaras is a documented city photographed in its own right, and its reality resists the assignment of functions; the ghats mean more than the feud can use, and the surplus meaning floods the story it was meant to carry.

The same discipline separates the films' handling of the sacred. In Almore, religion is worn by the characters as identity and deployed by the campaigns as fuel: Zoya's prayers are private discipline, her family's Islam a constituency, the Chauhans' Hinduism a rival constituency, and Eid the calendar's irony, the festival on which the daughter chooses reconciliation and the lovers choose death. The sacred in the film is therefore always doing political work, which is the film's thesis about its society stated as mise-en-scène. In Banaras, the sacred is the setting's perfume: processions, rituals and the river supply an atmosphere of fatedness that the film leans on whenever its causality thins, so that the lovers' end can be received as the city's eternal rhythm rather than as the product of named men making named decisions. The play permits fate its speeches, the Prologue's star-crossing among them, but it assigns the deaths to a chain of human acts, a feud, a banishment, a quarantined friar and an undelivered letter. A film that lets a holy city absorb responsibility for its lovers has misplaced the play's causality in its most atmospheric asset.

## The Mediators: Baba, Chand Bibi and a Dying Mother

No figure tests an adapter's understanding like the Friar, because the Friar is the play's theory of mediation made flesh: an authority outside the feud who believes the feud can be dissolved by its own rules, by marriage, sacrament and patience, and whose failure defines the tragedy's politics. The two films divide his office three ways, and the division is a map of their arguments. *Issaq* keeps a Friar in name and costume: Baba, the holy man played by Makarand Deshpande, counsels Rahul, and the notices record his function accurately by recording its limits, since the character advises against the affair and thereafter recedes into the film's gallery of wasted eminences, the phrase more than one reviewer applied to Deshpande and to Prashant Narayanan's Naxal leader alike. A Friar who warns and withdraws is a Friar stripped of the play's essential attribute, the engineering ambition that devises the wedding and the potion and thereby builds the tragedy's second half. Baba inherits the costume of mediation without its machinery, and the film's plot must consequently be driven by chases and contracts, the feud's own machinery, with the lovers' side of the story generating no counter-design at all.

*Ishaqzaade* abolishes the Friar and rebuilds his function from two figures the play does not contain. Chand Bibi, the brothel keeper, supplies the sanctuary: her house is the one address in Almore where the feud's writ runs weakly, because the town's respectability has agreed not to see it, and the lovers' refuge there restates the Friar's cell in the sociology of a small Indian city, where neutrality is found not above the social order but in its blind spot. Parvati, Parma's mother, supplies the moral initiative the Friar personifies: she is the one character in either film who attempts the Friar's actual project, the conversion of the marriage from an act of war into a settlement, insisting that her son honour the union and bringing the bride into the house under her own protection. The feud's answer, her murder by her own father-in-law's order and hand, is the film's thesis in a single act: mediation in Almore is not defeated by accident, as the Friar is defeated by a quarantined letter-carrier, but executed by the system's head for attempting the conversion. The play's mediator fails by mischance and survives to explain the bodies. The film's mediator succeeds morally and is killed for it, leaving the lovers to mediate their own ending on the rooftop. That is a darker political argument than the play's, and it is arrived at by taking the play's machinery seriously enough to ask what the machinery would meet in Almore.

Sharma's postcolonial frame, cited in the critics section that follows for its premise, sharpens the contrast once more: the Indian adaptations that reward study are those in which the borrowed tragedy's pressures are re-derived from Indian institutions. A holy man retained as décor is an institution quoted. A brothel and a mother rebuilt as the tragedy's mediating offices are institutions analysed.

## The Critics at Odds: Climaxes, Forgiveness and One Loud Rave

The honest account of these films requires named critics and an adjudicated disagreement, and the reception record supplies two. The first concerns *Ishaqzaade*'s climax. Rajeev Masand, awarding two and a half stars, praised the believable world, the leads' chemistry, the soundtrack by Amit Trivedi and Hemant Chaturvedi's cinematography, and then condemned the resolution as "an unforgivably unoriginal climax," for the lovers and for their families alike. Taran Adarsh, awarding three and a half, praised the same film's intensity and mainstream force. Trisha Gupta, in Firstpost, acknowledged the sharply drawn characters and Chopra's performance while criticising the ending and the film's thematic execution, a critique developed in the feminist reading of the film's second half, in which Zoya is cheated, bound, abused and then required by the plot to forgive. The MxMIndia survey of the reviews summarised that objection in its bluntest form, describing a second half "soaked in sloppy sexism" whose heroine is repeatedly tortured and yet decreed to forgive. Upperstall's notice reached the coldest verdict of all, holding that the film does not work and that Chopra's performance exposes, by contrast, the weakness of everything around her.

The disagreement adjudicated: Masand is wrong about the climax and Gupta is right about the forgiveness, and the two findings together locate the film's actual fault line. The climax is not unoriginal in any sense that matters. Its outline, lovers dead by their own hands, is the source's outline, retained by every faithful version including *Issaq*; its function, a mutual shooting chosen over surrender to a joint family execution squad, appears in no earlier version of the story, because no earlier version builds a world in which the families cooperate in the killing and the lovers' last freedom is the choice of each other's bullets over the families'. Originality of mechanism is a critic's metric; originality of meaning is the adaptation's, and by that metric the rooftop is the film's most original sequence. Gupta's objection lands on different ground and draws blood. The film does rush Zoya's passage from victim of the fraudulent marriage to partner in the genuine one, compressing into Parvati's deathbed insistence and a brothel sanctuary a moral conversion that the play distributes across Romeo's exile, repentance and return. The forgiveness is the film's weakest joint, and it matters that the weakness is a compression fault rather than a conception fault: the materials for the conversion exist in the film, in Parma's protection of Zoya against his own house before any reconciliation, but they are allotted less screen time than the chases that surround them. The verdict on the film's reception is therefore double. Its defenders underrated its ending. Its critics correctly identified its wound and mislabelled its location.

The second disagreement concerns *Issaq*, and it is not a split but a solo. Against Shubhra Gupta's one and a half stars, the Times of India's two stars, Koimoi's two stars and Rediff's headline verdict stood Subhash K. Jha's rave, which praised the film's visual fertility, welcomed the absence of a balcony scene, noted with approval that the apothecary's potion and the Shakespearean ending survive intact, and ranked the result among the most innovative Romeo and Juliet adaptations in any language. Jha is a serious critic of popular Hindi cinema and his notice is the strongest available statement of the film's case, which is precisely why it fails so instructively. Every virtue his review names is real and every one is located outside the film's centre: the colour of the bangle market, the audacity of the setting, the supporting performances, the fidelity of the final machinery. A defence of a Romeo and Juliet film that must be conducted entirely in terms of décor, daring and an ending's outline has conceded the only question the adaptation poses, whether two invented lovers in an invented world generate the tragedy's necessity from that world's own rules. On that question the field's consensus stands, and Jha's dissent is best preserved as the limiting case of setting-led criticism: Banaras deserved a better film, and the review's eloquence measures the size of the disappointment it declines to record.

The near-unanimity of the two-halves judgement in *Ishaqzaade*'s reviews deserves a structural explanation, since it recurs in notices that agree on nothing else. Anupama Chopra's "half a good time" is the famous formulation, but the Times of India's Madhureeta Mukherjee reached the same geometry by another route, finding a film that "starts with a bang-bang, but ends up firing blanks," and Koimoi's three and a half stars were explicitly rested on a strong first half and engaging performances. The play supplies the geometry's source. *Romeo and Juliet* is itself built as two unequal movements hinged at Mercutio's death in 3.1, a wit-driven comedy of love and street theatre that converts, in a single scene, into the tragedy the Prologue promised; adapters inherit the hinge whether they know it or not. Faisal's first half is his comedy, the children's abuse, the slap, the campaign carnival, the fraudulent courtship played at the tempo of a romance, and his hinge is the revelation of the fraud, after which the film's register hardens into flight and siege. The reviews that fault the second half are registering the conversion's cost: a comedy's lovers must be re-founded as tragedy's fugitives, and the re-founding, as the adjudication above concedes, is compressed. What the two-halves critics did not add is that the play accepts the same cost and pays it in the same currency, Romeo's banishment and the compressed machinery of Act 5 being the least loved stretch of a text whose first half contains the balcony. A hinge creaks in every faithful version. The question is whether anything stands on the far side of it, and in Almore the far side holds the joint manhunt and the rooftop, the film's two most original constructions. The blanks charge fails against the rooftop. No critic who reached it called the film's final ten minutes blank; they called them unoriginal, which is a different and answerable charge.

The third critical voice belongs to scholarship rather than to the review pages, and it settles the frame in which both disagreements sit. Paulomi Sharma's essay on reimagining the play in a postcolonial nation, published in Literature/Film Quarterly, examines how Hindi cinema moves the tragedy onto the fault lines of the Indian state, language, territory and communal power among them. Neither of the two films examined here is her subject, and her argument is cited for its premise rather than its objects: in the Indian adaptations that matter, the feud's invented cause is a political diagnosis, and the films are read most profitably as arguments about the nation that produced them. By that premise, the difference between the two Uttar Pradesh films sharpens. Almore's diagnosis is exact: a democracy in which communal division is an electoral technology will manufacture its Veronas on schedule. Banaras's diagnosis dissolves into atmosphere, a sand war and an insurgency photographed beautifully and argued loosely, until the social fractures the brief for this article names, caste, faith and political power, blur into generic lawlessness. The play's feud is a specific machine. Only one of these films specifies it.

## Honour, Guns and the Education of Sons

What the two films share, and what makes them a pair rather than a coincidence, is their agreement about where the feud lives: in an economy of masculine honour in which a daughter's conduct is family property and a son's violence is family investment. The play builds the same economy in miniature. Verona's young men are recruited into a hatred none of them authored, drilled in a code where a bitten thumb obliges a drawn sword, and the series examination of [masculinity and violence in the play](/2024/02/16/masculinity-and-violence-analysis/) traces how that code converts boys into the feud's instruments before it converts them into its casualties. Almore reproduces the mechanism with documentary fidelity. Parma's worth in his grandfather's house is measured by his usefulness as an enforcer, and his fraudulent marriage is conceived in the code's own terms, as a strike against the rival house's honour through its daughter's body. The childhood scene that opens the film, in which the young Parma and Zoya trade ritual abuse about each other's families, is the code's nursery: the hatred is taught as language before it is practised as violence. Zoya's revolver, bought with her earrings, is her attempt to enlist in the same economy on equal terms, and the film's bleakness lies in showing that the economy has a place for an armed daughter only until her sexuality becomes a liability, at which point the same families that armed their sons combine to disarm her permanently.

The weapons deserve a sentence of their own, because both films arm their worlds with the country-made pistol and the play arms its world with the rapier, and the correspondence is functional rather than decorative. Verona's sword is an honour instrument: carried by gentlemen, drawn on points of reputation, and lethal in the hands of young men trained to regard a quarrel as a debt. The pistol in Almore and Banaras occupies the identical social position, a cheap, ubiquitous sidearm whose possession certifies standing in the feud's economy and whose use is governed by the same arithmetic of insult and answer. Reviewers of both films remarked on the sheer density of firearms in frame, the gold-plated guns of the Banaras clans in the Times of India's inventory, the hundred pistols of Almore's campaign in the trade notices, and the density is the argument made visible: in a society where every retainer is armed, a private love between two houses is not a secret but an armed incident awaiting its hour. The play's Prince bans street brawling on pain of death and is ignored within a day. Neither film bothers with the ban, because neither film's state retains the standing to issue it, and that small shared omission is the pair's most quietly unanimous judgement on the world they depict.

*Issaq*'s Banaras runs the same economy at a lower temperature of analysis. Its young men are gunmen by inheritance, its Teeta a Tybalt whose honour requires a killing over a sand contract and a niece's suitor in the same gesture, and its approved bridegroom a police officer willing to subcontract a murder to insurgents, a Paris who has absorbed the feud's methods so completely that he outsources them. The materials of the play's honour world are all present. What is absent is the pressure that makes the economy legible as a system: Banaras's violence serves contracts, and contracts keep offering the exits that honour, in the play and in Almore, exists to close. An honour feud cannot be settled by compensation, because the debt is denominated in reputation. A sand feud can always be settled by a sufficiently large payment, and a story that knows this, even unconsciously, cannot keep its lovers' deaths from reading as a failure of negotiation rather than a necessity of the world.

The honour-killing frame, which *Ishaqzaade* names in its closing message and organises in its final act, deserves a precise statement because loose talk about it corrupts both films' records. The film does not depict a khap panchayat, a caste council or a village tribunal; Almore's violence is familial and political, executed by the two houses' own gunmen for reasons the film specifies as electoral and reputational together. Its claim, made in the on-screen text, is statistical and social rather than institutional: lovers who cross caste and religious boundaries in India are killed for the crossing, and the film files its fiction under that documented reality. Critics divided over the gesture, some reading the closing text as an honourable statement of intent and others as an unearned certificate of seriousness pasted over a melodrama. The adjudication offered here is that the text is earned by the film's final act and only by that act. A story in which two families cooperate to murder a married couple for the political inconvenience of their union has demonstrated, inside its fiction, the mechanism its closing sentence describes. The sentence is a caption to evidence already presented. In *Issaq*, by contrast, no equivalent frame is attempted, and the lovers' deaths are filed by the film under fate, the play's most portable and least examined category. Fate is what a feud calls itself when a story declines to specify the machine.

## Songs as Structure: Two Scores, Two Arguments

A Hindi film's songs are not decoration on the drama but part of its load-bearing structure, carrying the interiority that the form's dialogue rarely pauses to state, and the two scores divide as cleanly as everything else in this comparison. *Ishaqzaade*'s music, composed by Amit Trivedi in his first collaboration with the Yash Raj banner, with lyrics by Kausar Munir and a background score by Ranjit Barot, was received across the divided reviews as a unifying achievement: Masand, in a two-and-a-half-star notice, singled out the "excellent soundtrack" as a principal benefit, and the soundtrack's centre, "Pareshaan," carried Shalmali Kholgade to the Filmfare Award for Best Female Playback Singer and the film's lovers through the courtship's turning point. The songs perform the play's lyric function, the sonnet-work of the balcony and orchard scenes, in the only register mainstream Hindi cinema possesses for sustained first-person feeling: they are where Parma and Zoya are permitted to be lovers rather than combatants, and the feud's noise falls silent around them exactly as Verona falls silent around the orchard wall. The title song and "Jhallah Wallah," choreographed by Rekha and Chinni Prakash, perform the opposite function, staging the town's public world of campaign and swagger, so that the score maps the film's whole design, private lyric against public noise, in musical form.

*Issaq*'s score, by Sachin-Jigar with contributions from Sachin Gupta and Krsna and a background score by Prashant Pillai, contains the one element even the hostile reviews exempted from condemnation: the Times of India, in a notice that found fault everywhere else, allowed "one good melody in the whole ditty (Issaq tera)," Mohit Chauhan's title song. The concession defines the score's problem by its shape. A single admired melody inside a rejected film is the musical equivalent of the film's whole method, correspondence without system: the parts that correspond to the play's lyric needs exist, but they are not organised into the alternation of private and public registers that makes a Hindi film's songs think. Where Trivedi's score argues the film's thesis in another medium, the Banaras score decorates a thesis the film has not finished forming, and the reviews' consistent isolation of "Issaq Tera" as a survivor measures the distance between a soundtrack and an accompaniment.

## Fate and the Closing Captions: What Each Film Blames

The play opens by assigning its catastrophe to the stars and then spends five acts reassigning it to human beings, a double bookkeeping every adapter inherits. The two films settle the account in opposite directions, and their closing gestures disclose the settlement. *Ishaqzaade* opens with its Chorus substitute, the two children trading inherited abuse in a lane, an image that locates the tragedy's origin in education rather than astronomy: the lovers are star-crossed only in the sense that their houses crossed them first, in childhood, in language. The film then earns, across its final act, the right to its closing on-screen message, which names the social practice, the killing of lovers who cross caste and religious lines, to which the fiction belongs. Blame in this film is assigned with an address: to the families, to the electoral economy that pays them, and to the honour code that prices a daughter below a constituency. The message was received by some reviewers as a jarring certificate of seriousness, and that reaction is recorded in the critics section above; the adjudication stands, that a caption following a demonstrated mechanism is an argument's conclusion, not its substitute.

*Issaq* settles the account with fate, and its publicity settled it earlier: the film's own synopsis language presented the lovers as star-crossed and the love as doomed in advance, the vocabulary of the Prologue adopted as a marketing category. Inside the film, the Banaras atmosphere does the same work, wrapping the deaths in the city's aura of the eternal so that the chain of human decisions, a contract war, an uncle's killing, a bridegroom's subcontracted murder plot, a holy man's untested plan, dissolves into the river's rhythm. The critical consequence was exact: a tragedy that blames fate cannot be argued with, only admired or endured, and the reviews accordingly split between those who admired the atmosphere and those who endured the film. The play's own bookkeeping is the standard against which both settlements are measured. Shakespeare's Prince closes the account in the presence of the fathers, with heaven's scourge named as love itself and the city's governors, himself included, winking at the feud too long; blame lands on named desks. Almore's closing caption imitates that distribution of responsibility in a modern idiom. Banaras's dissolve into fate abandons it. The difference is the difference between the two films entire.

## Verdict: The Adaptation and the Setting

The pair therefore resolves into a lesson with a wide application across [the wider Bollywood tradition of retelling this play](/2029/02/02/bollywood-romeo-and-juliet-tradition/), a tradition in which the Verona story has been naturalised more often, and more variously, than in any other national cinema. *Ishaqzaade* is the adaptation: a film that understood the feud as a system requiring fuel, found the fuel in electoral communalism, inverted the play's central device into a fraudulent marriage and then redeemed the device by performing it in earnest, redistributed the Friar's function to a brothel keeper and a dying mother, abolished Paris and the single Tybalt because party violence has no champions, and restaged the double suicide as a mutual shooting that denies two cooperating families their execution. *Issaq* is the setting: a film that transported the play's cast list, street plan and terminal machinery to a photographed Banaras, gave its feud an asset instead of an appetite, admitted a third army the design has no room for, and discovered at the box office and in the reviews that correspondence is not causation. Researchers assembling a comparative dossier on the two productions, their notices, budgets and award records will find the raw materials scattered across trade papers, festival reports and review archives, and a private research collection such as [a structured personal vault for adaptation studies](https://vaultbook.net/) is the practical instrument for keeping the pair's parallel records in one ordered place.

The ranking is not close, and the fourteen months between the releases make it cleaner. Faisal's film came first, cost less, earned more, was honoured by the national awards system through its heroine, and remains the version of the story that Indian criticism argues about, which is the surest sign of an adaptation that has entered the culture's working memory. Tiwary's film came second, with the advantage of the first film's example and the play's title in its publicity, and it remains the version criticism uses as a warning. For the student of the play, the pair's joint value is that they isolate the variable. Two productions, one state, one story, one pair of debut-structured casts, one explicit source: the records differ because the theories differ, and the theories can be stated in a sentence each. *Ishaqzaade* holds that an adaptation must rebuild the feud's engine from local fuel and let the plot be whatever the engine drives. *Issaq* holds that an adaptation must preserve the plot's stations and let the setting supply the meaning. The play, which supplies an engine without fuel and stations without explanations, rewards the first theory and punishes the second with an exactness that looks, across these two records, almost experimental. The play survives both, as it survives everything, but its Indian afterlife is healthiest where a filmmaker trusts the design enough to rebuild it rather than revere it. Verona's grudge had no cause because Shakespeare knew the cause was never the point. Almore found the point. Banaras, for all its colour, kept looking at the river.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is Ishaqzaade a Romeo and Juliet adaptation?

Yes, in structure and argument, though it advertises the debt less loudly than its Banaras counterpart. Habib Faisal's 2012 film restages the play's complete design in the fictional Uttar Pradesh town of Almore: two symmetrical houses, the Hindu Chauhans and the Muslim Qureshis, sustain an inherited hatred; the young of each house fall in love across the divide; a secret marriage meant to settle the feud instead accelerates it; sanctuary, flight and a double death follow. Filmfare's reviewer Rachit Gupta named the relationship outright, calling the film a "fantastic Indian adaptation of Romeo and Juliet," and the adaptation is genuine rather than nominal because the film rebuilds the feud's cause, an electoral rivalry fuelled by communal division, instead of borrowing the plot's incidents alone. Where the film departs from the play, in its fraudulent first marriage, its absent Paris and its rooftop ending, the departures are arguments about the source rather than evasions of it, which is the mark of adaptation rather than imitation.

### Q: Is Issaq a Romeo and Juliet adaptation?

Yes, explicitly and by its own publicity. Manish Tiwary's 2013 film was promoted in its listings and synopses as a contemporary adaptation of the play, transposed to Banaras, and its cast list maps the source's personnel with unusual completeness: Rahul Mishra for Romeo, Bachchi Kashyap for Juliet, her uncle Teeta Singh for Tybalt, the holy man Baba for Friar Laurence, the maid Amma for the Nurse, the bridegroom Preetam for Paris, the friend Murari for Mercutio and the foreigner Roza for Rosaline. The film keeps the play's street-fight engine, its restrained feast confrontation, its accelerated arranged marriage and its terminal machinery of feigned death and double suicide, down to the potion. The critical consensus, examined in the body of this article, is that the correspondence of incidents did not produce a correspondence of effect, because the feud's invented cause, a war over sand-mining contracts between the Mishra and Kashyap clans, cannot generate the necessity the play's unexplained grudge generates by design.

### Q: Which film came first, Issaq or Ishaqzaade?

*Ishaqzaade* came first, by fourteen months. Habib Faisal's film was released on 11 May 2012, after a production launch in October 2011 and filming in and around Lucknow and Hardoi. Manish Tiwary's *Issaq* followed on 26 July 2013. The sequence matters to the comparison because the later film had the earlier film's example available, a commercially successful, locally grounded Romeo and Juliet set in the same state, and because *Issaq* nevertheless adopted the opposite method, fidelity to the plot's stations over reconstruction of the feud's system. A third version, Sanjay Leela Bhansali's *Goliyon Ki Raasleela Ram-Leela*, followed in November 2013, completing a nineteen-month span in which the Indian market tested three theories of adapting the same play. The commercial and critical ranking of the three, with *Ishaqzaade* and *Ram-Leela* prospering and *Issaq* failing, tracks the structural argument this article makes: the films that rebuilt the feud's engine outperformed the film that preserved its itinerary.

### Q: Who plays the lovers in Ishaqzaade?

Arjun Kapoor plays Parma Chauhan and Parineeti Chopra plays Zoya Qureshi. The film was Kapoor's acting debut, developed by producer Aditya Chopra under the Yash Raj Films banner as his launch after an earlier planned debut vehicle was shelved, and it was Chopra's first leading role following her supporting debut in *Ladies vs Ricky Bahl* the previous year. Parma is the wastrel grandson and enforcer of the Chauhan patriarch, desperate for the old man's respect; Zoya is the only daughter of the Qureshi house, a practising Muslim who prays five times daily, trades her gold earrings for a revolver and nurses an ambition to enter politics like her father. The casting of two near-newcomers was a commercial wager that the box office vindicated, and Chopra's performance became the film's critical centre, praised across notices that disagreed about almost everything else in the production.

### Q: Who plays the lovers in Issaq?

Prateik Babbar plays Rahul Mishra and Amyra Dastur plays Bachchi Kashyap. Babbar, the son of actors Raj Babbar and Smita Patil, carried the Romeo role as the pleasure-seeking heir of the Mishra sand clan; Dastur made her screen debut as the eighteen-year-old Kashyap daughter whose marriage to the police officer Preetam is arranged by her father and uncle. The supporting cast maps the play's household: Ravi Kishan as the uncle Teeta Singh, Makarand Deshpande as the holy man Baba, Neena Gupta as the maid Amma, Rajeshwari Sachdev as the stepmother Paro, Sudhir Pandey as Kashyap and Prashant Narayanan as the Naxal leader. The reviews divided sharply over the central casting, with several notices judging Babbar miscast and Dastur's role too thinly written to carry the sympathy the tragedy requires, while the supporting performances, Kishan's above all, drew the acting praise the film received.

### Q: How does Ishaqzaade end?

The lovers die by each other's hands on a college rooftop, cornered by the combined forces of both families. Parma and Zoya, married in earnest after the fraudulent first marriage and Parvati's death, flee first to Chand Bibi's brothel and then toward Jaipur; Zoya's attempt to reconcile with her father at Eid fails, and the two houses, Chauhan and Qureshi together, hunt the couple as a joint threat to both political legacies. Trapped on the rooftop and nearly out of ammunition, Zoya proposes that they shoot each other rather than be killed by their families, so that love and not hatred fires the last shots; they die in each other's arms, smiling, while the families depart satisfied. An on-screen message closes the film by naming the real-world practice the fiction has dramatised, the killing of lovers who cross caste and religious boundaries in India. The ending restages the play's double suicide as a mutual act chosen over execution.

### Q: How does Issaq end?

The film keeps the play's terminal machinery: feigned death, mistaken belief and a double suicide. After Teeta's killing of Murari and Rahul's retaliatory killing of Teeta, Rahul is a fugitive from the police, Bachchi's marriage to the police officer Preetam is accelerated, and Preetam seeks the Naxal leader's help to eliminate his rival. The lovers' final separation is engineered through the play's own device of an apparent death, the potion whose preservation the critic Subhash K. Jha praised as fidelity to the Shakespearean ending. Rahul, believing Bachchi dead and lost to him, takes his own life; Bachchi, waking from her staged death, discovers his body and follows him. The ending is thus the most faithful in incident in either film, a fact the comparison in this article treats as diagnostic: machinery retained without the system that powered it produces surprise rather than necessity, and the trade press's verdict was that a shocking climax could not salvage the hours preceding it.

### Q: What are the two families in Ishaqzaade fighting over?

The Chauhans and the Qureshis are fighting over political power in Almore, embodied in a single legislative seat both houses contest, and the fight is fused with a Hindu and Muslim communal divide. Surya Chauhan and Aftab Qureshi head rival political families whose campaigns draw energy from polarising the town's two faiths, so the inherited hatred is renewed on the electoral cycle's schedule rather than left to memory. The feud's economy is visible in its transactions: patronage, armed retinues, contested dancers and campaign violence, each house measuring itself against the other at every public occasion. This invention is the film's central adaptation decision, since the play supplies no cause for its feud at all. By giving the grudge a renewable resource, Faisal explains what Shakespeare leaves blank, why the hatred survives its own costs, and he sets the trap of the final act: a marriage merging the two houses threatens the polarisation on which both campaigns depend, so the rivals cooperate in hunting the lovers.

### Q: What are the families in Issaq fighting over?

The Mishras and the Kashyaps are fighting over land and riverbed, specifically control of the sand-mining trade along the Ganges at Banaras. The two clans run rival sand mafias, competing for contracts and extraction rights in an illegal economy documented in the region, and their war is prosecuted with gunmen, ambushes and hired force. A third power complicates the feud: a Naxal insurgency under its own leader, which both camps attempt to use against the other and which the approved bridegroom Preetam enlists to murder Rahul. The feud's cause is therefore economic and divisible in a way the play's cause is not, a difference this article treats as the film's structural flaw. Sand can be shared, surrendered or bought out, so every refusal to settle reads as a negotiation awaiting a better price, and the tragedy leaks necessity. The Banaras setting, its ghats and festivals, supplies the film's celebrated atmosphere without altering that arithmetic.

### Q: What award did Parineeti Chopra win for Ishaqzaade?

Parineeti Chopra received a Special Mention at the 60th National Film Awards for her performance as Zoya Qureshi. The National Film Awards are India's state honours for cinema, and the special mention placed her performance in the year's official record alongside the award's principal acting prizes. The performance also brought her a nomination for the Filmfare Award for Best Actress, while her co-star Arjun Kapoor was nominated for Best Male Debut at the same ceremony, and the film's music collected the production's principal popular honours, including the Filmfare Award for Best Female Playback Singer for Shalmali Kholgade's "Pareshaan." The National Award mention matters disproportionately to this comparison because it ratifies, at the level of the state's own jury, the critical consensus that the film's centre held: reviewers across the spectrum, from Taran Adarsh's three and a half stars to Upperstall's rejection of the film entire, singled out Chopra's Zoya as the production's achievement.

### Q: Was Ishaqzaade a box office hit?

Yes, decisively for a film carried by newcomers. Produced for a reported budget of 16 crore rupees, the film opened strongly in May 2012, with first-day occupancy reported between 70 and 90 percent, earned about 25.7 crore nett in its first week, a record at the time for a Hindi film without an established star cast, and closed with a domestic net of roughly 45.4 crore rupees and a worldwide gross of 61.3 crore. Trade and critical accounts concur in classing it a commercial success, and its run was driven by word of mouth beyond its opening, the second and third weekends holding at levels that certified the film had found its audience rather than merely rented one. The performance is the commercial half of this article's verdict: the adaptation that rebuilt the play's feud as an electoral and communal system was rewarded by the market the system described, while the more expensive Banaras production of the following year collapsed.

### Q: Was Issaq a box office success?

No; the documented record is a failure of the first order. Box Office India records a production budget of 25 crore rupees against a gross of approximately 5.4 crore, and classifies the film in the trade's disaster category. The film thus recovered barely a fifth of its reported cost despite advantages its predecessor lacked: an explicit Shakespeare banner in its publicity, a celebrated Banaras setting, an established supporting cast and a release window one year after a hit version of the same play had demonstrated the material's market. The commercial verdict and the critical verdict coincide, which is rarer than either alone and is the empirical spine of this article's comparison. *Issaq* was not a good film that audiences missed, nor a popular film that reviewers misunderstood, with one prominent critical dissent recorded and examined in the body of the article. On both ledgers open to judgement, the plot-fidelity method of adaptation failed in full view.

### Q: Who is the Friar Laurence figure in Issaq?

The Friar's office is carried by Baba, a holy man played by Makarand Deshpande. Baba is Rahul's counsellor and guide, the figure outside the two clans to whom the Romeo of Banaras carries his entanglement, and the character inherits the Friar's two signature instruments, the moral authority to bless or forbid the union and the pharmacology of apparent death with which the film's ending is built. The reviews record the performance chiefly as an opportunity underused: Deshpande appears in the notices' lists of distinguished actors given too little of consequence to do, alongside Prashant Narayanan's Naxal leader. The structural point, developed in this article's mediators section, is that Baba warns and recedes where the play's Friar schemes and constructs; he counsels against the affair rather than engineering the marriage, so the lovers' side of the story generates no counter-design against the feud, and the plot's second half is driven by the feud's machinery of contracts, chases and subcontracted killing instead.

### Q: Who shelters the lovers in Ishaqzaade?

Chand Bibi, the brothel keeper played by Gauahar Khan, shelters Parma and Zoya when both houses turn against them. Her establishment is the film's equivalent of the Friar's cell, the one address in Almore where the lovers can exist outside the feud's jurisdiction, and the substitution is analytical: Verona's sanctuary is an institution above the feud, the church, while Almore's is an institution beneath the town's notice, a house that respectability has agreed not to see, and therefore the only neutral ground a polarised town contains. The film pairs her function with Parvati's, Parma's mother, who performs the Friar's moral office by insisting the fraudulent marriage be honoured and bringing Zoya into the Chauhan house under her protection, an intervention the feud answers by killing her. Between the brothel keeper who hides the lovers and the mother who legitimises them, the film rebuilds the Friar's single office out of two figures the play does not contain, both drawn from the margins of the world the feud governs.

### Q: What was Parma's plan behind the first marriage in Ishaqzaade?

The first marriage is a calculated act of dishonour, devised by Parma to revenge a humiliation and to win his grandfather's approval. Having been slapped and shamed by Zoya in their running conflict, and desperate to prove his worth to Surya Chauhan, Parma courts her, converts to Islam and takes the name Parvez, submits to a marriage ceremony and consummates the union in an abandoned railway coach, then reveals that the ceremony was staged. The object is the public ruin of the Qureshi daughter's reputation and, through her, of her father's campaign: in the honour economy the film depicts, a daughter's disgrace is a transferable political asset. The device inverts the play's central mechanism, since the secret wedding in Verona is arranged in good faith by the Friar as an instrument of peace, while Almore's is arranged in bad faith by the bridegroom as an instrument of war. Parma's mother Parvati forces the film's turn by insisting the marriage be honoured in fact.

### Q: What role do the Naxalites play in Issaq?

The Naxalites form a third armed force, an insurgent army under a leader played by Prashant Narayanan, which both sand clans attempt to use as an instrument against the other. The insurgents raid and counter the clans' operations in the riverbed war, and their most consequential enlistment is by Preetam, the approved bridegroom, who seeks the Naxal leader's help to kill Rahul and clear his own path to Bachchi, despite the insurgency's enmity with the Kashyap interest he serves. The play has no equivalent force, since Verona contains two houses and a prince, and the addition was the element reviewers most consistently faulted as incoherent: the Koimoi notice called the communist angle forced and pointless, a judgement this article's analysis supports on structural grounds. A third army with its own politics converts a two-house tragedy into a factional war, dilutes the feud's symmetry and gives the lovers' deaths rival explanations the design cannot arbitrate.

### Q: What did critics say about Issaq?

The notices formed a near-consensus of rejection with one prominent dissent. Shubhra Gupta of The Indian Express awarded one and a half stars, finding the film "without a singular voice of its own" and judging that "it drowns in its own noise." The Times of India held that it "lacks vibe, soul or depth needed for a classic love story" and that its director misses every target. Piyasree Dasgupta of Firstpost asked "what is a greater tragedy, *Romeo and Juliet* or what *Issaq* made of that classic love story"; Sarit Ray of the Hindustan Times placed it among the most nonsensical of Shakespeare adaptations; Raja Sen of Rediff headlined his review "an insultingly bad film." The dissent came from Subhash K. Jha, who praised the film's visuals and audacity and ranked it among the most innovative adaptations of the play in any language. This article adjudicates for the consensus, on the ground that the rave's virtues are all located outside the film's centre.

### Q: What did critics say about Ishaqzaade's ending?

The ending divided the reviewers along a line this article examines at length. Rajeev Masand, in a two-and-a-half-star notice that praised the film's world, chemistry, music and cinematography, condemned the resolution as "an unforgivably unoriginal climax" for lovers and families alike. Trisha Gupta in Firstpost criticised the ending and the film's thematic execution, within a broader feminist objection to the second half's treatment of Zoya, who is cheated, bound and abused before the plot requires her forgiveness. Against those judgements, the Dainik Bhaskar notice praised the climax for breaking the happily-ever-after convention, and Taran Adarsh's three-and-a-half-star review endorsed the film's intensity entire. The adjudication offered in this article is that the unoriginal charge mistakes outline for function: two lovers dead by their own hands is the source's outline, retained everywhere, while a mutual shooting chosen over a joint family execution appears in no earlier version. The forgiveness objection, by contrast, identifies the film's genuine fault, a compression in Zoya's conversion.

### Q: Which film is the better adaptation of the play, Issaq or Ishaqzaade?

*Ishaqzaade*, by the standard the play itself sets, and the margin is structural rather than preferential. Faisal's film rebuilds the feud as a functioning system, an electoral and communal machine with a payroll, an honour economy and a renewal schedule, inverts the secret marriage into a weapon and then redeems the device in earnest, redistributes the Friar's office to a brothel keeper and a dying mother, and restages the double suicide as a mutual shooting that denies two cooperating families their execution. Tiwary's film preserves the play's personnel, street plan and terminal machinery with greater fidelity of incident and loses the system in transit, giving its feud a divisible asset, admitting a third army and letting fate absorb responsibilities the play assigns to named men. The records ratify the analysis: the cheaper film returned nearly four times its budget and carried its heroine to a National Film Award special mention; the dearer film was classified a disaster. The InsightCrunch Bollywood comparison in this article sets the two records side by side.

### Q: What message appears at the end of Ishaqzaade?

The film closes with an on-screen message addressing the killing of lovers who marry or love across caste and religious boundaries in India, stating the human cost of the practice the fiction has dramatised. The text follows the lovers' deaths on the college rooftop and the families' satisfied departure, and it converts the story's private catastrophe into a public indictment: Parma and Zoya are presented not as singular victims of one town's politics but as instances of a documented pattern in which families and communities murder couples for the crossing. Critics divided over the gesture, some receiving it as an honourable statement of intent and others as a certificate of seriousness the melodrama had not earned. The position argued in this article is that the final act earns the caption: a story in which two rival houses cooperate to murder a married couple for the political inconvenience of their union has demonstrated, inside its fiction, the precise mechanism the closing sentence names.
