The Food Stamp Act of 1964 answers an operative question of institutional design: by what legal mechanism can the federal government raise the food purchasing power of low income households through ordinary grocery stores, at federal expense, under state administration, without converting food assistance into a general cash grant? Each operative element of the statute responds to a part of that question. Coupons restricted spending to food. A purchase charge required a household contribution scaled to ordinary food expenditure. State agencies certified households under standards approved in Washington. Federal appropriations, capped by fiscal year, limited the value of coupons issued beyond the charges collected from households. The sections that follow trace the statute from a Depression era surplus device to a permanent program, and the record assembled across those sections supports a single organizing claim about the source of durability.

![Food Stamp Act of 1964 purchase requirement and the road to SNAP - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-15.webp)

Administration supplies the first part of the answer, because the statute divided responsibility between a federal government that financed benefits and set governing rules and state agencies that certified households and issued coupons through local offices. A companion treatment of [the legal structure of SNAP administration](/2019/12/01/snap-administration-law/) examines that division of labor in detail. The second part of the answer concerns legislative location. The program entered law as an instrument for moving agricultural abundance into consumption, and placement inside farm legislation joined nutrition spending to commodity policy inside a single recurring bill. The tension between those two halves of the farm bill receives separate treatment in [an analysis of commodity programs against nutrition programs in the farm bill](/2021/02/15/farm-bill-commodity-vs-nutrition/). Permanence, purchase, national standards, electronic delivery, and renaming each altered program design, and none of those alterations moved the jurisdictional address fixed at enactment.

### What did permanence mean under the statute?

Permanence meant standing statutory authority without a sunset date for the program itself, paired with capped appropriations for defined fiscal years and operation only in political subdivisions covered by approved state plans. Authority endured as law, while scale, geography, and household terms depended on appropriations, state requests, and federal approval.

### Why does the statute sit inside agriculture law?

The statute sits inside agriculture law because food assistance entered federal law as a method for moving surplus farm production into consumption through retail trade. Codification under Title Seven, administration by the Department of Agriculture, and jurisdiction in the agriculture committees followed that origin and persisted through every later revision of program design.

## Origins

### Hunger Beside Plenty

The food stamp idea took shape inside a contradiction that defined federal farm policy during the Great Depression. On one side stood agricultural abundance that markets could not absorb at prices capable of sustaining farm income. On the other side stood households with too little money to buy adequate diets. Federal officials described the problem as hunger in the midst of surplus, and the phrase captured more than rhetoric. Production adjustment under the Agricultural Adjustment Administration sought to raise farm returns by limiting output or removing commodities from commercial channels, while relief agencies confronted destitution among unemployed workers and their families. A policy that destroyed food or paid farmers to leave land idle while families went hungry invited public anger, and the anger registered in Washington during 1933 after reports of crops plowed under and livestock slaughtered in an effort to create scarcity. The search for an alternative produced an institutional line that ran directly to food stamps: a federal corporation charged with buying surplus commodities and moving them, outside ordinary commerce, to people on relief.

The first step came in October 1933 with the creation of the Federal Surplus Relief Corporation. The corporation received surplus farm products and passed them to state and local relief organizations for distribution to destitute families. The Department of Agriculture's official history records that the arrangement served two masters from the outset. Farmers gained an outlet for commodities that would otherwise have depressed prices, and relief households gained food that household budgets could not purchase. The mechanism, however, had limits built into the design. Families received whatever commodities the government had bought, in quantities governed by the pattern of farm surplus rather than by household need or preference. Distribution took place through relief channels, and the act of collecting commodities marked recipients as relief clients in a visible way. The food arrived as a donation tied to a particular surplus, not as purchasing power that a family could exercise in a store alongside other shoppers.

On November 18, 1935, a charter amendment changed the name of the Federal Surplus Relief Corporation to the Federal Surplus Commodities Corporation, and the corporation's administration came within the Department of Agriculture in 1937. A second legal foundation arrived with Section 32 of the act of August 24, 1935, which devoted a share of customs receipts to purposes that included encouraging domestic consumption of farm products by diverting surpluses from normal channels of trade. Section 32 money gave the Secretary of Agriculture a flexible fund for surplus removal, and the fund later supplied the legal and financial basis for both the first food stamp experiment and the pilot program of the early 1960s. By the late 1930s, the Department of Agriculture possessed three assets that a stamp plan required: experience in buying and moving surplus food at national scale, a statutory fund detached from annual relief appropriations, and a network of state and local agencies accustomed to handling federal commodities.

### Milo Perkins and the Bridge Across the Gorge

Secretary of Agriculture Henry A. Wallace presided over the department that confronted the surplus problem, and Milo Perkins, president of the Federal Surplus Commodities Corporation, became the first administrator of the food stamp experiment that emerged from that confrontation. Perkins framed the task in an image that Department of Agriculture histories preserved: farm surpluses stood on one cliff of a gorge and undernourished city people stood on the other cliff with hands outstretched, and the practical problem consisted of building a bridge across the chasm. The metaphor mattered because the bridge Perkins proposed differed from direct distribution in a decisive respect. Rather than expanding the commissary model, under which the government selected the food and handed the food to relief households, the stamp plan inserted purchasing power into ordinary retail trade. Grocers would sell the food, banks would redeem the stamps, and the federal government would subsidize only the bonus portion of the purchase. Farmers, retailers, and relief households would meet inside the normal food economy instead of outside the normal food economy.

The design also answered an agricultural objection that had shadowed surplus donation. Federal officials worried that free distribution of commodities could displace commercial sales and thereby defeat the purpose of surplus removal, because a family that received free flour might spend less at a grocery store. A stamp plan tied the federal bonus to a cash purchase, so federal money added to food spending rather than substituting for food spending. The household committed money that approximated normal food expenditure, and the government supplied additional stamps without charge. Total food demand rose, grocers gained customers, and surplus commodities moved through stores at retail rather than through warehouses at relief depots.

### The Two Stamp Design in Operation

The first Food Stamp Program began on May 16, 1939, in Rochester, New York. The Department of Agriculture's official history identifies Mabel McFiggan of Rochester as the first recipient and Joseph Mutolo as the retailer whose store redeemed the first stamps. The choice of a single city for the opening allowed federal administrators to test accounting, retailer participation, and stamp redemption before extending the plan to other communities. Participation rested on relief status. A person receiving public assistance or work relief could buy orange stamps in an amount equal to normal food expenditure for the household. For every dollar of orange stamps purchased, the participant received fifty cents of blue stamps without charge. Orange stamps bought any food a store sold. Blue stamps bought only foods that the Department of Agriculture had designated as surplus. In arithmetic that administrators repeated in explanations of the plan, a household that spent ten dollars received fifteen dollars of food purchasing power, provided that the five bonus dollars went toward the surplus list.

The surplus list changed with market conditions, and Department of Agriculture summaries of the first program recorded items such as butter, cheese, eggs, apples, fresh pears, pork, potatoes, fresh snap beans, and whole wheat flour among the foods designated at the opening. During 1941, many states made fresh vegetables eligible for blue stamp purchase during the summer while canned and frozen produce remained outside the bonus category. The list embodied the dual purpose in tangible form. Blue stamps channeled demand toward commodities in oversupply, and the restriction protected the farm objective against a simple cash grant that families might have spent on any item, food or otherwise. Orange stamps, by contrast, preserved household choice across the full range of groceries and preserved the grocer's ordinary role.

Administration followed the federal pattern of the period. The Federal Surplus Commodities Corporation set the surplus designations and supplied the stamps, state and local relief agencies certified the households, and retail grocers accepted the stamps and redeemed the stamps through commercial banks and corporation offices. The corporation itself passed into a consolidation during 1940, when surplus functions were combined in the Surplus Marketing Administration, and the corporation was dissolved on February 23, 1942, with functions passing to the Agricultural Marketing Administration. The stamp plan survived the reorganizations because the plan depended on Section 32 financing and on state certification machinery rather than on the survival of a single corporate shell.

### Scale, Limits, and the Wartime Ending

Expansion after the Rochester opening carried the plan across a large part of the country. The Food and Nutrition Service history of the program records that the first program reached approximately twenty million people at one time or another in nearly half of the counties in the United States, that peak participation reached four million people, and that total cost over the life of the program reached 262 million dollars. A Congressional Research Service history, using monthly terms, placed peak service at some four million persons a month in almost half the counties and total cost at about 260 million dollars, figures consistent with the departmental account. Four million participants represented roughly three percent of the national population at the peak, a scale large enough to demonstrate administrative feasibility in rural counties and industrial cities alike.

The same record exposed constraints that later designers studied. Eligibility followed relief rolls, so households with low wages but no relief status stood outside the plan even when diets were inadequate. The purchase requirement demanded ready cash from households whose defining characteristic was a shortage of cash, and the requirement set a floor below which the poorest families could not climb to reach the bonus. The blue stamp restriction subordinated nutrition to surplus conditions, because a food qualified for the bonus by reason of oversupply rather than by reason of dietary need. The ending came from the disappearance of the conditions that had created the program. War mobilization after 1941 absorbed unemployed labor into military service and defense production, and the unemployment rate fell to 1.9 percent by 1943. Wartime demand consumed food supplies that peacetime markets had failed to absorb, and fewer commodities qualified as surplus in any meaningful sense. The program ended in the spring of 1943, and the Department of Agriculture's official explanation, preserved in the Food and Nutrition Service history, stated that the conditions that brought the program into being, unmarketable food surpluses and widespread unemployment, no longer existed.

### The Long Interval of Proposals

The eighteen years between the spring of 1943 and the pilot program of 1961 contained no operating food stamp program, and the interval was filled instead with studies, reports, and bills. The Food and Nutrition Service history names Senators George Aiken, Robert La Follette, Hubert Humphrey, Estes Kefauver, and Stuart Symington among the members of Congress associated with attempts to enact a food stamp program during those years, a list that crossed party and region. The bipartisan character of the sponsorship reflected the dual inheritance of the 1939 experiment. Farm state members valued a program that expanded food demand, and urban members valued a program that raised the diets of low income constituents, and a stamp plan promised both effects in a single vote. Representative Leonor K. Sullivan of Missouri became the most persistent House advocate. From 1954 onward, Sullivan pressed food stamp legislation without interruption, and the Food and Nutrition Service history credits Sullivan with unceasing effort across the remainder of the decade. Persistence produced a limited statutory result before it produced a program. Legislation enacted on September 21, 1959, authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to operate a food stamp system through January 31, 1962, under authority associated with Sullivan and Humphrey.

The 1959 authorization met executive resistance. President Dwight D. Eisenhower held the authority during the final period of the Eisenhower administration, and Secretary of Agriculture Ezra Taft Benson chose not to use the authority to establish a program. A pilot authorized by Congress therefore remained dormant, and the episode clarified a structural point that shaped the next stage. A stamp program run from Section 32 funds and administrative discretion could begin without a large new appropriation, but a stamp program could also fail to begin for the same reason, at the discretion of officials who doubted the concept. Advocates who wanted permanence needed either a president willing to act or a statute that commanded action and supplied a durable framework.

### A Promise Made in the Coalfields

West Virginia converted that abstract recurrence into a presidential commitment. A decade of decline in coal mining, the economic driver of the southern part of the state, left communities in McDowell County and neighboring coalfields with chronic unemployment that the prosperity of the 1950s had not relieved. Mechanization reduced the number of miners required for a given tonnage, mines closed or cut shifts, and families that had depended on a miner's wages subsisted on relief commodities of limited variety. Senators Jennings Randolph and Robert C. Byrd of West Virginia pressed for expanded federal food provision from 1959 onward, and Byrd devoted a Senate speech on January 23, 1959, to legislation increasing funds for emergency food supply, describing citizens living in penury through no fault of their own.

Candidate John F. Kennedy encountered the same conditions during the 1960 presidential campaign, when the West Virginia primary forced sustained attention to the distress of the state. Kennedy campaigned among unemployed miners, promised federal action on food assistance if elected, and treated the promise as a commitment owed to a specific place rather than as a general pledge. The Robert C. Byrd Center for Congressional History and Education records that Kennedy promised during the 1960 campaign in West Virginia to take steps toward a federal food assistance program.

### Executive Action and the Pilot Design

President Kennedy signed Executive Order 10914 on January 21, 1961, the day after inauguration, and the order stood first in the sequence of executive orders of the Kennedy presidency. The Department of Agriculture's commemorative history of the Food Stamp Act of 1964 describes the order as the first executive order of the presidency and as fulfillment of the campaign promise made in West Virginia. Executive Order 10914 bore the title Providing for an Expanded Program of Food Distribution to Needy Families, and the text directed the Secretary of Agriculture to take immediate steps to expand and improve food distribution across the United States, using funds and existing statutory authority that included Section 32 of the act of August 24, 1935. The order rested on findings that almost seven million persons received some form of public assistance, that four and a half million persons were unemployed, that many needy persons received no food distribution at all, and that the variety of foods available through distribution was limited and nutritionally inadequate. The order therefore framed the problem in nutritional and coverage terms rather than in surplus terms alone, even though the order also noted declining farm income and the desirability of stronger farm prices.

On February 2, 1961, Kennedy announced the initiation of food stamp pilot programs as the instrument for carrying the expansion into a new form. The distinction between the January order and the February announcement reflected administrative reality. Direct commodity distribution could expand at once under existing machinery, whereas a stamp pilot required areas to be selected, state agreements to be arranged, retailers to be enrolled, and stamp issuance procedures to be designed. Isabelle Kelley, a member of the small team that designed the new program, became the first director of the pilot, and Department of Agriculture histories identify Kelley as the first woman in the department to head an action program. The pilot retained the purchase requirement from the 1939 design, so a household bought stamps representing an allotment related to food needs and received a bonus in additional stamps financed by the federal government. The pilot abandoned the central restriction of the earlier design. No separate class of stamps existed for surplus foods, and bonus stamps bought the same range of foods as purchased stamps. Department spokesmen emphasized increased consumption of perishables, a category that direct commodity distribution handled poorly and that household choice through retail stores handled well.

The first transaction under the pilot took place on May 29, 1961, in McDowell County, West Virginia. Alderson Muncy and Chloe Muncy of Paynesville, in McDowell County, received the first food stamps under the pilot, purchasing 95 dollars in stamps for a household that the Food and Nutrition Service history describes as a fifteen person household. An unemployed miner's family with thirteen children at home represented precisely the population that commodity distribution had reached inadequately and that wage income in the coalfields failed to support. Eight pilot areas operated at the start, distributed across rural counties and urban communities that department officials selected for high unemployment and low income, and a Congressional Research Service history records that the original eight projects served about 140,000 persons a month at a federal cost of 13.1 million dollars a year in fiscal year 1962.

### Expansion and the Evidence for Permanence

Secretary of Agriculture Orville Freeman expanded the pilot gradually under Section 32 authority, and growth supplied the evidence on which a request for permanence could rest. By January 1964, the pilot programs had expanded from eight areas to forty three areas in twenty two states, with 380,000 participants, according to the Food and Nutrition Service history. The same history specifies the composition of the forty three areas as forty counties together with Detroit, St. Louis, and Pittsburgh, a mixture that tested the stamp mechanism in Appalachian coal counties, in southern rural communities, and in large industrial cities with established welfare administrations. A Congressional Research Service history placed service at some 370,000 persons a month at an annual federal cost of 30.5 million dollars in fiscal year 1964, a monthly figure close to the departmental participation total and a cost figure that allowed Congress to judge the budgetary consequence of permanence with unusual precision. The pilot record answered the administrative question that had followed the stamp idea since 1943, whether a peacetime government could run a stamp program as a nutrition measure rather than as a surplus disposal measure, and the record answered the administrative question affirmatively in places as different as McDowell County and Detroit.

Expansion also exposed the limits of executive improvisation. A program financed from Section 32 and operated at administrative discretion depended on the priorities of the incumbent administration, as the unused 1959 authorization under Secretary Benson had demonstrated from the opposite direction. Participating areas entered by selection rather than by entitlement, so a county with comparable poverty outside the pilot list received no stamps at all. Eligibility standards and allotment practices rested on administrative decisions that Congress had not enacted, and appropriations discipline sat uneasily beside a growing caseload that administrators expanded area by area. Members of Congress who supported the pilot, Sullivan foremost among the House sponsors, pressed for a statute that would define the program in law, assign costs between the federal government and the states, and place expansion under legislative authority. Secretary Freeman submitted proposed legislation to establish a permanent food stamp program on April 17, 1963, and the submission converted the position of the administration into a bill that committees could examine while the pilot continued to grow.

### Toward a Permanent Statute

The assassination of President Kennedy on November 22, 1963, transferred the presidency to Lyndon B. Johnson, and Johnson committed the administration to an antipoverty program that treated food assistance as one instrument among several. On January 31, 1964, Johnson requested that Congress pass legislation making the food stamp program permanent. The request completed a progression that had begun twenty five years earlier in Rochester. A device invented to move surplus commodities through relief households had become, through the interval of proposals and the discipline of the pilot, a candidate for permanent law justified by nutrition, by farm demand, and by the documented persistence of hunger in specific American places. The Food and Nutrition Service history identifies the practical purpose of the legislation that followed as bringing the pilot program under congressional control and enacting the governing rules into law, alongside the stated purposes of strengthening the agricultural economy and improving nutrition among low income households. Permanence, on that record, was less a sudden invention of 1964 than the statutory settlement of an experiment that administrators had tested from 1939 to 1943, that legislators had pursued across the 1940s and 1950s, and that a president had revived, redesigned, and expanded between January 1961 and January 1964.

## The Statute as Enacted

The Food Stamp Act of 1964 entered the statute books as Public Law 88-525, an enactment of the 88th Congress recorded at 78 Statutes at Large 703 and codified in Title 7 of the United States Code. Congress.gov recorded the measure as House Bill 10222, introduced in the House of Representatives on March 3, 1964, and recorded the final action as the date on which the bill became Public Law 88-525, August 31, 1964. President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the act on that date. 

The long title stated the enterprise in a single sentence, and the sentence carried the dual character that shaped every operative provision. The act described itself as legislation to strengthen the agricultural economy, to help achieve a fuller and more effective use of food abundances, to provide for improved levels of nutrition among low income households through a cooperative federal and state program of food assistance to be operated through normal channels of trade, and for other purposes. The reference to the agricultural economy placed the program within agricultural legislation and the jurisdiction of the agriculture committees. The phrase cooperative federal and state program announced the administrative design. 

Section 2, headed as a declaration of policy, expanded the long title into findings. Congress declared that the abundance of food in the nation should be used cooperatively by the states, the federal government, and local governmental units to the maximum extent practicable to safeguard health and well being and to raise levels of nutrition among low income households. Congress found that increased use of food in establishing and maintaining adequate national levels of nutrition would tend to distribute agricultural abundances in a beneficial manner, strengthen the agricultural economy, and produce more orderly marketing and distribution of food. 

## The Path Through Congress

The immediate sequence began before the introduction of House Bill 10222. According to the Food and Nutrition Service history of the program, Secretary of Agriculture Orville Freeman submitted proposed legislation for a permanent food stamp program on April 17, 1963. President Johnson, on January 31, 1964, requested that Congress pass legislation making the program permanent. The bill that Congress enacted was introduced by Representative Leonor Sullivan of Missouri. Congress.gov listed Sullivan as sponsor and traced consideration through the House Committee on Agriculture and the Senate Committee on Agriculture and Forestry. The House passed the bill on April 8, 1964. The Senate passed the bill with an amendment on June 30, 1964. The House agreed to the Senate amendment on August 11, 1964, and the President approved the act on August 31, 1964. 

The House vote supplied the clearest measure of division. Legislative histories of the act recorded House passage by a vote of 229 to 189 on April 8, 1964, a margin of forty votes. Congress.gov recorded Senate passage on June 30, 1964, and House agreement to the Senate amendment on August 11, 1964, without a comparable roll call tally in the action record for those steps, so the Senate stage is best described qualitatively as passage with amendment followed by House concurrence rather than by a numerical margin. 

## Two Champions Across Two Decades

Representative Leonor Sullivan brought to the bill a record of advocacy that long predated the Johnson administration. Sullivan represented a St. Louis district, first won election in 1952 after service as an aide to her husband, Representative John B. Sullivan, and made a food stamp plan a signature cause early in her House career. In an oral history preserved by the House, Sullivan described a food stamp plan as one of her favored proposals and explained the idea as a method for distributing surplus food held in warehouses to needy people throughout the country in order to give those households a better and more nourishing diet. The Food and Nutrition Service history credited the enacted bill to Sullivan as the member who introduced the measure that Congress eventually passed. 

Senator George Aiken of Vermont supplied the Senate counterpart to that long advocacy, from within the Republican Party and from within the agriculture establishment of the Senate. Aiken entered the Senate in 1941 after service as governor of Vermont, built a reputation as a defender of small farmers and rural programs, and sponsored a national food allotment proposal in the middle nineteen forties that historians of food policy treated as a forerunner of the food stamp program. 

## Architecture of the Enacted Text

The enacted act ran to sixteen sections. Early sections declared policy, defined terms, and established the program. The Secretary of Agriculture stood at the center of the federal side of the design. Section 4 authorized the Secretary to formulate and administer a food stamp program, to issue coupons redeemable at face value through the facilities of the Treasury, and to issue regulations consistent with the act. The program therefore remained an agricultural program in administration as well as in committee jurisdiction, housed in the department that ran farm policy, even though the state agencies designated to operate the program were the agencies responsible for federally aided public assistance in each state.

Definitions in Section 3 fixed boundaries that later sections enforced. Food meant food or food products for human consumption, with express exclusions for alcoholic beverages, for tobacco, for foods identified on the package as imported, and for imported meat and meat products. A coupon meant a coupon, stamp, or certificate issued under the act, and a coupon allotment meant the total value of coupons issued to a household in a month or other period. A household meant a group of related or unrelated individuals who lived as one economic unit, shared common cooking facilities, and customarily purchased food in common, and the term also covered a single individual who lived alone with cooking facilities. Residents of an institution or a boarding house fell outside the household definition, an exclusion that removed congregate settings from a program designed around household purchase and home preparation. State meant the fifty states and the District of Columbia, a definition that left territories outside the statutory map drawn in 1964. State agency meant the agency of state government responsible for the federally aided public assistance programs, a choice that tied certification to welfare administration machinery in the states.

Section 4 also drew a line between the new program and the older method of feeding assistance. In an area where a food stamp program operated, distribution of federally owned foods to households under other legal authority was barred, except during emergency situations caused by a national or other disaster as determined by the Secretary. At the same time, Section 4 made operation depend on state initiative. The Secretary was authorized to act at the request of an appropriate state agency, and eligible households were to receive allotments within a state that had sought the program. Nothing in the section compelled a state to request a program or converted the authorization into a nationwide entitlement operating everywhere on a single effective date. Permanence described the authority, not the geography, and geography expanded only as states submitted plans and as the Secretary approved participation for particular political subdivisions.

## Purchase, Value, and the Federal Purse

The economic core of the act sat in Section 7, which governed the value of the coupon allotment and the charges made to households. A state agency was authorized to issue allotments in an amount that would give a certified household an opportunity more nearly to obtain a low cost nutritionally adequate diet. The household paid a charge equal to a portion of the face value of the allotment, determined as the equivalent of normal expenditures for food by that household. The difference between the charge and the face value constituted the federal benefit, and Section 7 provided that the excess value would not count as income or resources for any purpose under federal or state law, including laws on taxation, welfare, and public assistance. The purchase requirement preserved a feature carried from the pilot programs launched under President Kennedy in 1961, which had retained a purchase obligation while dropping the earlier Depression era device of special stamps reserved for surplus foods. 

Money moved through a dedicated federal account. Charges collected from households were deposited promptly, under regulations, in a separate account maintained in the Treasury and were available without limitation to fiscal years for redemption of coupons. Appropriations followed a capped schedule in Section 16. Congress authorized appropriations of not more than 75 million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1965, not more than 100 million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1966, and not more than 200 million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1967, with later fiscal years left to sums that Congress might authorize afterward. The portion of an appropriation needed to pay the value of allotments above the charges paid by households was transferred into the separate Treasury account. The value of coupons issued above the value charged to households had to remain within the appropriated portion transferred to the account, and if the Secretary found that requirements of participating states would exceed the limitation, the Secretary was directed to instruct state agencies to reduce coupon issuance to the extent necessary for compliance. 

Administration costs followed a separate allocation in Section 15. Each state was responsible for financing, from funds available to the state or its political subdivisions, the costs of administrative responsibilities assigned to the state, including certification of households, acceptance and storage and protection of coupons after delivery to receiving points in the state, issuance of coupons to eligible households, and control and accounting for coupons. A federal contribution softened that rule in one defined circumstance. The Secretary was authorized to cooperate with state agencies in certifying households that received no public assistance, and federal payments covered fifty percent of specified certification costs for those households, calculated from direct salary costs of interviewing and investigating personnel and immediate supervisors, travel and related costs of field investigation, and an added allowance capped at twenty five percent of the salary and travel amounts. 

## State Administration and Delegated Eligibility

Administration in Section 10 completed the cooperative design. The state agency in each participating state assumed responsibility for certification of applicant households and for issuance of coupons, with authority, subject to state law, to delegate coupon issuance to another agency of state government. Each state seeking participation submitted a plan of operation for approval by the Secretary. The plan specified the manner of operation within the state, the political subdivisions in which the state desired to conduct the program, and the effective dates of participation for each subdivision. The plan also stated the specific standards for determining eligibility of applicant households, provided for certification in accordance with the general procedures and personnel standards used for applicants under federally aided public assistance programs, supplied safeguards restricting use and disclosure of information obtained from applicant households, and provided for reports required by the Secretary. In approving subdivisions, the Secretary was directed to provide for equitable and orderly expansion among the states in accordance with relative need and readiness to meet requested effective dates. 

Eligibility delegation lived in Section 5, and Section 5 was deliberately open textured. Participation was limited to households whose income was determined to be a substantial limiting factor in attaining a nutritionally adequate diet. Within that limitation, each state agency established standards for determining eligibility of applicant households. State standards had to include maximum income limitations consistent with the income standards used by that state agency in administering federally aided public assistance programs, and state standards also had to place a limitation on resources allowed to eligible households. The standards of each state were subject to approval by the Secretary. Congress therefore set a purpose, a consistency rule, and a federal approval check, and Congress left the operative numbers to the states. Two households with identical incomes and resources could face different eligibility outcomes in different states under the 1964 text, because maximum income levels followed state assistance standards rather than a single national schedule written into the act. 

Federal oversight accompanied the discretion. Section 10 provided that, if the Secretary determined that a state agency had failed to comply substantially with the act, with federal regulations, or with the state plan of operation, the Secretary would inform the agency of the failure and allow a reasonable period for correction. After that period expired, the Secretary was directed to stop further issuance of coupons in the subdivisions where the failure had occurred until satisfactory corrective action had been taken. The Secretary also stood over the retail side of the program. Regulations under Section 8 governed applications by retail food stores and wholesale food concerns for approval to accept and redeem coupons, with consideration given to the nature and extent of the food business, the expected volume of coupon business, and the business integrity and reputation of the applicant. Section 9 provided for redemption through approved wholesale concerns or through banks, with cooperation of the Treasury Department. 

Two protections for households appeared in the administration section. Certification was to occur without discrimination by reason of race, religious creed, national origin, or political beliefs. Participating states and political subdivisions were barred from decreasing welfare grants or similar aid to any person as a consequence of participation in food stamp benefits, a protection that prevented a state from treating the federal food benefit as a reason to cut cash assistance and thereby absorb the gain that Congress intended for household food budgets. A separate direction in Section 10 instructed that all practicable efforts be made to encourage participants to use increased purchasing power for staple foods most needed in diets, with particular encouragement for foods in abundant or surplus supply, and enlisted voluntary cooperation of agencies carrying out consumer information and education programs. 

## Coverage, Exclusion, and the Limits of Permanence

Coverage followed from the interaction of household status, income and resource standards, purchase capacity, and geography. A covered unit was a household as defined in Section 3, living outside an institution or boarding house, sharing cooking facilities and common food purchase or living alone with facilities for home preparation. The household had to live in a political subdivision included in an approved state plan during a period when the program operated there, apply and submit to certification by the state agency, meet the state maximum income limitation and resource limitation approved by the Secretary, and pay the charge set as the equivalent of normal food expenditures. After certification, the household received coupons usable only in approved retail food stores and only for food as defined by the act, at prices prevailing in those stores, with the act expressly withholding authority for the Secretary to specify prices charged by wholesale concerns or retail stores. The 1964 text contained no work registration condition of the kind that Congress added through amendments enacted in 1971.

Exclusions were equally structural. Alcoholic beverages and tobacco could not be purchased with coupons, because Section 3 removed those items from the definition of food. Imported foods identified on the package and imported meat and meat products fell under the same definitional exclusion, a protection for domestic production embedded in a nutrition statute. Households with income above the state maximum or resources above the state limit were excluded even when food costs pressed hard on a budget, because eligibility turned on state standards rather than on an individualized finding beyond the general test that income substantially limited attainment of an adequate diet. Residents of institutions and boarding houses were excluded by definition, regardless of income. Households outside participating subdivisions were excluded by geography, even in a state with an approved plan that covered other subdivisions, and households in states that had submitted no plan were excluded by state choice. The purchase requirement produced an exclusion of practical force, because a household without cash equal to normal food expenditures could not obtain the allotment on the terms written in Section 7, so the poorest households, including households with no regular cash income, faced a barrier at the point of entry. 

Enforcement provisions guarded the boundaries of the program. Section 14 made unauthorized use, transfer, acquisition, or possession of coupons a felony when the coupons were valued at one hundred dollars or more, punishable by a fine of not more than ten thousand dollars or imprisonment for not more than five years or both, and a misdemeanor when the value fell below one hundred dollars, punishable by a fine of not more than five thousand dollars or imprisonment for not more than one year or both. Section 6 required that coupons be simple in design, carry only words or illustrations needed to explain purpose and denomination, and bear the name of no public official. 

The relationship between the act and the pilot programs that began in 1961 can be stated with precision. President John F. Kennedy announced initiation of pilot programs on February 2, 1961, after an executive order that called for expanded food distribution and after a campaign commitment made in West Virginia. The Food and Nutrition Service history recorded that the pilots retained the purchase requirement, discarded the concept of special stamps for surplus foods, and placed emphasis on increasing consumption of perishables. The first transaction took place on May 29, 1961, when Alderson Muncy and Chloe Muncy of Paynesville, West Virginia, bought ninety five dollars in food stamps for a household of fifteen persons. By January 1964 the pilots had expanded from eight areas to forty three areas, described in the department history as forty counties plus Detroit, St. Louis, and Pittsburgh, across twenty two states, with 380,000 participants. Public Law 88-525 converted the experiment into a permanent program authorized by statute, subjected operation to congressional appropriations and state plans, codified eligibility delegation and retailer approval and enforcement in legislative text, and removed any dependence on the lifespan of a pilot announcement. 

Permanence under the act was therefore real and limited at the same time. The authorization had no sunset date for the program itself, and the signing statement of President Johnson treated permanence as the central achievement. The funding authorization, by contrast, specified ceilings for three fiscal years and referred later years to future congressional authorization, and the Secretary possessed an express duty to order reductions when state requirements exceeded the appropriated limit. The operating map had no mandate for universal coverage, and the expansion principle in Section 10 spoke of equitable and orderly growth according to need and readiness rather than simultaneous national availability. Eligibility had no single national income schedule, and the resource limit had no figure in the statute. Purchase remained a condition of benefit, and the commodity distribution bar applied only where a food stamp program operated, which left the older system in place in areas outside the new map. The act of August 31, 1964, settled the question whether a food stamp program would exist as a standing instrument of federal policy, and the act left scale, place, and household level terms to appropriations, state plans, and state standards approved in Washington.

## The Purchase Requirement and Its Abolition

For the first fifteen years of the permanent program, a household did not simply receive food stamps. A household bought them. That design, inherited from the pilot programs of the early nineteen sixties and written into the Food Stamp Act of 1964, determined how much cash a family had to assemble before help arrived, when in the month help could be obtained, and which eligible households never appeared in the participation counts at all. Abolition changed the program in structure rather than in stated purpose. The purpose of raising levels of nutrition among low income households remained, yet the cash gate between eligibility on paper and assistance in hand was removed.

The reform arrived in stages. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973 first made the program genuinely national by requiring operation in every political subdivision of every state. The Food Stamp Act of 1977, enacted as Title XIII of the Food and Agriculture Act of 1977, then eliminated the charge for coupons, established uniform national standards of eligibility, capped household net income at the poverty line, and placed the value of the allotment on the cost of the thrifty food plan. The rewritten statute took effect on October 1, 1977, yet elimination of the purchase requirement was required of state agencies on or before January 1, 1979, after a phased regulatory transition. The participation record in the historical series of the Food and Nutrition Service of the United States Department of Agriculture shows why that final date matters, because the sharpest movement followed removal of the cash charge rather than the date of enactment alone.

### How the Purchase Worked in Practice

The mechanics rested on a distinction between the value of an allotment and the price charged for it. The coupon allotment was the total face value of coupons a household was authorized to receive in a month. The purchase requirement was the amount of cash the household had to pay to obtain that allotment. The difference between the two amounts was the bonus, and the bonus alone represented the net federal benefit added to the purchasing power the household was expected to supply from its own resources. A household with higher counted income paid more for an allotment of a given size and received a smaller bonus. A household with lower counted income paid less and received a larger bonus.

The price was scaled to income and was intended to approximate what the household would ordinarily have spent on food without assistance. Under the Food Stamp Act of 1964, a participating household paid an amount commensurate with its normal expenditures for food and received coupons representing an opportunity to obtain a low cost nutritionally adequate diet. Certification established household size, income, and resources, and the issuance table translated those facts into a purchase price and an allotment value for the month. The household then paid the charge at an issuing office, a bank, or another authorized issuance point, and received coupons for eligible food at authorized retail stores.

Amendments enacted in 1971 standardized that arrangement without removing its core. Federal law provided that allotments be sufficient to purchase a nutritionally adequate diet, established uniform national standards of eligibility, and limited the purchase requirement to no more than 30 percent of household income. Variable purchase arrangements also allowed eligible households to buy less than a full allotment, a concession that softened the lump sum problem while preserving the principle that a household had to pay something before the bonus could be released. The cap at 30 percent gave the transaction a predictable relationship to income, yet predictability on a schedule and affordability in a particular week were different questions for a household whose rent, fuel, and medical costs fell due before food money could be set aside.

Payment was a separate administrative event from eligibility. A household could be certified as eligible and fail to obtain coupons in a given month because the cash was unavailable on the day required. Participation in program statistics generally followed issuance rather than certification alone, so the purchase step filtered the caseload in a way that eligibility rules alone did not describe. Once purchased, coupons circulated much as cash within authorized stores, subject to program rules on eligible items. The resemblance to cash ended at the point of acquisition. Cash had to be surrendered first, in a single transaction, for a benefit whose value was fixed by a table rather than by the shopper.

### The Allotment and the Food Plans Behind It

The size of the allotment came from a federal food plan rather than from a household estimate of need. During the years governed by the Food Stamp Act of 1964, maximum allotments rested on the economy food plan, a minimal plan developed by the Department of Agriculture in 1961 as a nutritionally adequate diet for short term or emergency use and priced below the low cost food plan. The plan expressed a judgment about the cheapest basket of foods that could meet nutritional standards for a household of a given size, with adjustments for economies of scale as household size increased. The bonus closed part or all of the distance between ordinary spending and the plan cost for households with little income.

That foundation changed before the purchase requirement ended. In 1975 the thrifty food plan replaced the economy food plan as the basis for maximum allotments, presenting a new set of market baskets at the same minimal cost level and drawing on updated dietary recommendations, food composition data, food habits, and price information. The Food Stamp Act of 1977 then wrote the thrifty food plan into the statute itself, defining it as the diet required to feed a family of four persons, consisting of a man and a woman twenty to fifty four years of age, a child six to eight years of age, and a child nine to eleven years of age, determined in accordance with calculations of the Secretary of Agriculture. The cost of that diet became the basis for uniform allotments for all households regardless of actual composition, subject to household size adjustments and to geographic adjustments for Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Virgin Islands of the United States.

Under the formula in the Food Stamp Act of 1977, the value of the allotment issued to an eligible household equaled the cost of the thrifty food plan for that household size reduced by an amount equal to 30 percent of household income, rounded to the nearest dollar, with a minimum allotment of ten dollars per month for households of one and two persons. The formula preserved an expectation that a household would devote a share of its own income to food, yet it removed the need to convert that expectation into an advance cash payment. The expected contribution became an arithmetic deduction from the maximum benefit rather than a price collected at an issuance window. That difference defined the practical meaning of abolition.

### Why the Requirement Excluded the Poorest Households

The central defect of the purchase requirement was a liquidity problem. Assistance was conditioned on cash on hand, and the households with the least cash faced the greatest difficulty meeting the condition. A family whose income had been consumed by rent, a utility payment, a repair needed to reach work, or a medical expense could qualify under the income and resource tests and lack the money to buy the allotment in the month of need. Because the charge was collected as a lump sum for the month rather than spread across each shopping trip, the barrier rose with household size at the same moment that need rose with household size. A larger family received a larger allotment and a larger bonus opportunity, yet a larger family also had to assemble a larger payment before any of that value could be used.

Timing compounded the problem. Wages for low paid work could be weekly, biweekly, irregular, or seasonal, public assistance payments followed separate schedules, and income from casual labor could arrive after an issuance deadline had passed. A household that expected income later in the month could be food insecure early in the month and able to make the purchase only after the period of greatest scarcity had passed. Borrowing to make the purchase carried costs and risks, and a household that borrowed against a future payment surrendered part of the bonus to the cost of obtaining it.

Stigma operated alongside cash. The purchase transaction marked the participant in public, at an issuance office and again at a store where coupons distinguished the shopper from customers paying in currency. For elderly applicants, for workers near eligibility limits, and for families in small communities where issuance personnel and store clerks were known personally, that visibility could deter an application or a purchase even where eligibility was clear. Reform debate treated the purchase requirement as a barrier to participation, and the rallying phrase for the reform campaign of 1977 was the elimination of that requirement, shortened in congressional and administrative discussion to EPR.

Administrative discretion added a further layer of exclusion. Under the original structure of the Food Stamp Act of 1964, states developed eligibility standards within a federal framework, and local administration governed much of the certification and issuance experience. Variation in office practice, documentation demands, issuance locations, hours, and treatment of borderline income or household composition could produce different outcomes for households in similar circumstances in different counties. The purchase requirement magnified that burden because every administrative step preceded a cash transaction, and assembled purchase money could lose its practical value if certification or issuance slipped beyond the point at which the money had to be committed to another necessity. The system therefore served best those eligible households with enough stability to plan a monthly cash outlay and absorb delays, and least reliably those households whose poverty was deepest, whose income was least regular, and whose need was most immediate. The bonus was largest for the poorest households on paper, yet the poorest households faced the highest practical hurdle relative to available cash.

### Nationwide Operation Before Abolition

Geography imposed its own barrier during the first decade of the permanent program. The Food Stamp Act of 1964 depended on state and local decisions to operate the program, and coverage spread unevenly as states and counties adopted food stamps in place of commodity distribution. A household could meet every substantive test and live in a political subdivision where no food stamp program operated.

The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, enacted on August 10, 1973, supplied that mandate. The statute required states to expand the program to every political subdivision before July 1, 1974, and the Food and Nutrition Service recorded that the program began operating nationwide on July 1, 1974, with full implementation in Puerto Rico following on November 1, 1974. The same legislation expanded eligibility for narcotics addicts and alcoholics living under supervision in treatment and rehabilitation programs, provided for semiannual allotment adjustments, addressed Supplemental Security Income cash out arrangements, authorized bimonthly issuance, broadened the statutory definition of income, and required temporary eligibility standards for disasters. Seeds and plants producing food also became eligible purchases.

Nationwide operation changed the meaning of the participation counts. According to the Food and Nutrition Service historical participation and cost series, average monthly participation rose from 12,862,000 persons in fiscal year 1974 to 17,064,000 persons in fiscal year 1975, reflecting completion of nationwide availability together with economic conditions and food price inflation during a recession period. Participation reached 18,549,000 persons in fiscal year 1976 in the same series, before easing to 17,077,000 persons in fiscal year 1977 and 16,001,000 persons in fiscal year 1978. By the middle of the nineteen seventies the program had already become a mass national program under the purchase system, and the caseload moved with employment and prices as well as with program rules. The question after 1974 was no longer whether a poor household could find a program. The question was whether a poor household that had found a program could afford to use it.

### The Reform Enacted in the Farm Bill

The Food and Agriculture Act of 1977, Public Law 95-113, was signed on September 29, 1977. Title XIII of that omnibus statute was designated the Food Stamp Act of 1977 and rewrote the Food Stamp Act of 1964. Section 1301 of Public Law 95-113 stated an effective date of October 1, 1977 for the amended act. The statute paired access reforms with integrity provisions, including fraud disqualifications and financial incentives related to low error rates.

Elimination of the purchase requirement stood at the center of the access provisions. The statute eliminated charges for coupon allotments and directed that a household be issued the net benefit produced by the allotment formula rather than be required to pay for the maximum allotment and receive the bonus as the difference. In mechanical terms, the federal government stopped selling the full allotment and began issuing only the amount by which the thrifty food plan cost exceeded the expected household contribution of 30 percent of counted income. The household contribution survived as a calculation. The household payment disappeared as a transaction.

Uniform national eligibility standards accompanied the change. The Food Stamp Act of 1977 directed the Secretary of Agriculture to establish uniform national standards of eligibility, with specified variances, and precluded a state from establishing additional standards of its own as preconditions for participation. The statute limited participation to households whose income and financial resources were determined to be a substantially limiting factor in permitting the household to obtain a nutritionally adequate diet, set the nonfarm income poverty guidelines prescribed by the Office of Management and Budget as the basic standards of eligibility, and specified a procedure for annual updating of the poverty guidelines. In operational terms, the net income ceiling was the poverty line.

Other provisions widened the practical reach of the new structure. The act removed any requirement that household members be related, permitted tribal organizations to be designated as state agencies with respect to Indian communities in specified circumstances, and changed the stated purpose from obtaining a nutritionally adequate diet to obtaining a more nutritious diet. Access procedures included outreach obligations, a thirty day processing standard, and expedited service for households in urgent need. The statute also directed the Secretary of Agriculture to report to Congress six months after implementation of the elimination of the charge for allotments, and annually thereafter, on the effect of the elimination on participation and cost. Congress therefore treated the participation response as a measurable test of the theory behind EPR.

### A Phased Effective Date and the Moment of Elimination

Confusion about the timing of abolition arises from the difference between the effective date of the rewritten statute and the implementation date of its best known provision. Section 1301 of Public Law 95-113 made the Food Stamp Act of 1977 effective on October 1, 1977. Section 1303 provided that provisions of the Food Stamp Act of 1964 relevant to regulations governing the program remained in effect until those regulations were revoked, superseded, amended, or modified by regulations issued under the new act, with implementation to proceed as expeditiously as possible consistent with effective administration. A separate utility related rule was published in late 1977 with an effective date of January 1, 1978, and that date forms part of the phased transition, yet January 1, 1978 was not the date on which the purchase requirement ended for households across the program.

Proposed regulations covering the interrelated changes, including elimination of the purchase requirement, were published on May 2, 1978. Final regulations followed on October 17, 1978, and codified an implementation schedule requiring state agencies to eliminate the purchase requirement for all households on or before January 1, 1979. A state agency designated the month of elimination, and designation of a month other than January 1979 required prior approval of the Food and Nutrition Service, with approval available for an earlier month where the state demonstrated that an accounting procedure for the new issuance system would be in place. Federal participation analyses prepared for the Food and Nutrition Service likewise placed the effective date of the elimination at January 1, 1979 in legislative chronologies of the program. Fiscal year 1978, ending on September 30, 1978, therefore offers a pre elimination baseline in the annual series. Fiscal year 1979 contains three months under the transition and nine months beginning with the January deadline, while January 1979 itself provides the cleanest single month marker for the change.

### The Measured Participation Response

The Food and Nutrition Service short history of the program reported that when elimination of the purchase requirement took effect in January 1979, participation increased by 1,500,000 persons in one month. That figure captures the response among households eligible under the substantive tests yet unable or unwilling to make the monthly purchase. The variable that changed at the deadline was the demand for cash before issuance.

Annual averages move more slowly than a single month because a fiscal year blends months before and after a change. According to the Food and Nutrition Service Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program participation and costs series, average monthly participation was 16,001,000 persons in fiscal year 1978, rose to 17,653,000 persons in fiscal year 1979, and reached 21,082,000 persons in fiscal year 1980. The rise from fiscal year 1978 to fiscal year 1979 was about 1,652,000 persons in average monthly terms, even though fiscal year 1979 included the final quarter of 1978 before the January implementation deadline. The rise from fiscal year 1979 to fiscal year 1980 was larger, yet that second increase coincided with deteriorating economic conditions at the end of the nineteen seventies and cannot be assigned to elimination alone without qualification.

Comparison with the middle of the decade sharpens the interpretation. In the same Food and Nutrition Service series, average monthly participation had reached 18,549,000 persons in fiscal year 1976, fallen to 17,077,000 persons in fiscal year 1977, and fallen again to 16,001,000 persons in fiscal year 1978. Fiscal year 1979 therefore represented a reversal of a two year decline as well as an absolute increase, and fiscal year 1980 surpassed the fiscal year 1976 level by more than 2,500,000 persons. The pattern supports a substantial participation effect from removal of the cash charge, amplified after January 1979 by worsening labor market conditions. The January 1979 one month jump belongs most directly to EPR, because the deadline was a program rule change with a fixed implementation date. The continued growth through fiscal year 1980 belongs to EPR and to the economy together.

Elimination also changed the relationship between participation and benefit value. Under the purchase system, the bonus was the benefit, yet the face value of coupons overstated the federal addition to food purchasing power because part of the book had been paid for by the household. After elimination, the issued allotment was the net benefit itself. Eligible nonparticipants entered the issued caseload, households that had bought partial allotments could receive the full net amount produced by the formula, and households that had missed purchases in scattered months could participate with greater continuity. From 1964 to the implementation deadline of 1979, federal law promised to raise nutrition among low income households through a device that required the poorest households to produce cash in advance of help. The Food Stamp Act of 1977 answered that contradiction by nationalizing eligibility standards, capping net income by reference to the poverty line, resting allotments on the thrifty food plan, and issuing the net benefit without a sale.

## Jurisdiction and the farm bill coalition

### An assistance program housed in the agriculture department

The placement of federal food assistance inside the Department of Agriculture, and inside the jurisdiction of the agriculture committees of Congress, appears anomalous only when food assistance is classified first as welfare. The administrative record points in the opposite direction. Food assistance entered federal law as an instrument of farm policy, designed to move surplus agricultural production into consumption, and the institutional arrangements built for that purpose in the late nineteen thirties persisted through every later expansion. The Food Stamp Act of 1964 inherited a department, a set of committees, a body of statutory law codified under Title Seven of the United States Code, which covers agriculture, and a retail delivery system, without changing that institutional address.

The first food stamp program began on May 16, 1939, in Rochester, New York, under Secretary of Agriculture Henry A. Wallace and program administrator Milo Perkins. Households on relief purchased orange stamps in an amount equal to ordinary food spending, and for each dollar of orange stamps the household received fifty cents in blue stamps without charge. Orange stamps purchased any food, while blue stamps purchased only foods that the Department of Agriculture had designated as surplus. The operating agency was the Federal Surplus Commodities Corporation, funding rested on Section Thirty Two authority to encourage domestic consumption by diverting surpluses from ordinary channels of trade, and the stated problem joined unmarketable surpluses on farms and undernourished households in cities in a single frame. Peak participation reached about four million people, and the program ended in the spring of 1943 after wartime demand absorbed the surpluses and unemployment fell away. A program conceived as a bridge between a farm surplus and an urban grocery basket belonged, by design, to the officials who managed commodity purchases and surplus disposal, because the function, as defined at creation, was commodity movement with a relief benefit attached.

### Why the agriculture committees held the pen

Committee jurisdiction in Congress follows the subject as Congress has defined the subject, and food legislation followed food. When Representative Leonor K. Sullivan of Missouri, whose district covered St. Louis, introduced the bill that became the Food Stamp Act of 1964 in the House of Representatives on March 9, 1964, after pressing food stamp legislation from the middle nineteen fifties onward, consideration ran through the House Committee on Agriculture and the Senate Committee on Agriculture and Forestry. The long title of the enacted statute announced the dual purpose in agricultural language, declaring an act to strengthen the agricultural economy and to achieve a fuller use of food abundances, alongside improved nutrition among economically needy households through a cooperative federal and state program operated through normal channels of trade. Administration matched the drafting. The Department of Agriculture, through the agency that became the Food and Nutrition Service, set eligibility and benefit rules, authorized retail food stores, and supplied the full federal cost of benefits, while state agencies conducted eligibility determinations under federal standards with administrative costs shared between the two levels of government.

The contrast with cash welfare clarifies the boundary. Cash assistance to families developed under the Social Security Act, moved through the House Committee on Ways and Means and the Senate Committee on Finance, and after the welfare law of August 1996 passed through the Department of Health and Human Services as the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant. Food assistance never crossed to that side of the committee map, even in 1996, and the reasons visible in the record are practical as much as ideological. Benefit levels rested on the cost of a food market basket constructed by Department of Agriculture economists, the Thrifty Food Plan, and redemption through private grocery retail gave the program a commercial constituency in every congressional district. Above all, agricultural legislators had acquired a direct electoral interest in the program, because food assistance raised demand for farm output, and that interest defended the jurisdictional assignment against every later proposal to move the program to a welfare department or a welfare committee.

### The farm bill as the recurring legislative vehicle

The vehicle that carried food assistance after 1973 was the farm bill, a periodic omnibus measure through which Congress rewrote farm and food policy for a fixed term, most often five years. Farm bills assembled titles covering commodity support, conservation, trade, nutrition, credit, rural development, research, forestry, energy, horticulture, and crop insurance inside a single enactment, and the omnibus form served a purpose that separate bills could not match, because each title brought a distinct bloc of members into the coalition required for passage. The rhythm of the vehicle rested on permanent law. The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949 remained on the statute books as permanent law for commodity programs, and each farm bill suspended or replaced the permanent provisions for the term of the bill. Expiration therefore carried a threat that disciplined bargaining, since a lapse threatened reversion to parity based support formulas drawn from an earlier era, a prospect analysts described at the end of 2012 as a dairy cliff before Congress answered with a one year extension folded into the American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012, Public Law 112-240. Food assistance, whose authorizing statute set time limited authorizations renewed in farm bills, acquired the same legislative calendar by attachment, so that nutrition policy returned to the floor on the schedule of commodity politics rather than on a schedule set by poverty policy alone.

### The urban and rural exchange in practice

The exchange at the center of the coalition joined two groups of legislators whose districts had little in common except need of each other. Members from farm districts formed a shrinking minority of the House of Representatives as population shifted toward metropolitan areas, and commodity programs directed at a declining farm population required votes from members whose constituents produced no crops. Members from urban districts, in turn, sought a federal food assistance commitment that rural conservatives, southern Democrats in particular during the nineteen sixties, resisted as welfare. The logroll resolved both problems at once, with urban members supplying votes for commodity programs and rural members supplying votes, or acquiescence, for nutrition assistance.

The founding episode came in April 1964. President Lyndon B. Johnson sought enactment of a permanent food stamp program, carried forward from pilot programs that President John F. Kennedy had launched in 1961, and also sought wheat and cotton legislation wanted by southern cotton Democrats and plains wheat members. House leadership scheduled the two measures together on April 8, 1964, the food stamp bill passing the House first by 229 votes to 189, with the wheat and cotton bill following in the same sequence of votes. Northeastern University historian Christopher Bosso, in accounts of the episode, summarized the presidential message to rural Democrats as a direct condition, under which failure to pass food stamps would cost the cotton and wheat programs. Friction continued before formal combination, as urban food stamp supporters warned during the extension fights around 1970 that farm legislation would lose urban votes while the agriculture committees fought the nutrition program. The Nixon administration in 1970 preferred to treat food stamps alongside welfare and opposed combination, while the Senate resisted combination from the opposite calculation, judging Senate food stamp support strong enough to stand alone, and separate reauthorization through 1973 settled that round.

Formal combination arrived with the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, the first omnibus farm bill to carry food stamps and commodity programs in a single measure. Bosso described the decision of the House Agriculture Committee in 1973 as an explicit bid for the votes of urban and suburban Democrats, and the conference with the Senate settled food stamp questions substantially on Senate terms after a House deal unraveled on the floor. The partnership of Senator George McGovern of South Dakota, a Democrat, and Senator Bob Dole of Kansas, a Republican, then gave the coalition a durable bipartisan form. McGovern chaired the Senate Select Committee on Nutrition and Human Needs from its creation on July 30, 1968, after the CBS documentary Hunger in America had aired in May of that year, and Dole served as the leading Republican partner in the committee work on hunger. The Food Stamp Act of 1977, enacted as part of the Food and Agriculture Act of 1977, eliminated the purchase requirement and set national eligibility standards, and at the close of 1977 the select committee folded into the agriculture committee structure as a nutrition subcommittee while the Senate committee took the name Committee on Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry, a name that recorded the jurisdictional settlement in the title of a farm committee.

Later cycles renewed the exchange on larger budgets. The Agri-Pulse account of farm bill bargaining published on March 5, 2017, traced the defensive strength of the coalition to a test in March 1995, when Speaker Newt Gingrich pressed the Contract with America pledge to convert food stamps into state block grants and farm district Republicans, joined by members of the House Agriculture Committee, resisted removal of the program from federal control. The committee under Chairman Pat Roberts of Kansas marked up a reform that tightened eligibility while keeping the program federal, and the welfare law of August 1996 followed that division, block granting cash assistance while food stamps retained federal rules and agriculture committee jurisdiction.

### The arithmetic behind the bargain

Budget arithmetic transformed the terms of the exchange, because the nutrition side of the combined bill grew into the overwhelming majority of farm bill spending while the commodity side retained the name, the committees, and the public identity of the legislation. At enactment of the Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, the Congressional Budget Office estimated in a letter dated January 28, 2014, to House Agriculture Committee Chairman Frank Lucas of Oklahoma that direct spending under the act would total nine hundred fifty six billion dollars over fiscal years 2014 to 2023. The Economic Research Service of the Department of Agriculture, publishing the Congressional Budget Office projections on March 12, 2014, allocated eighty percent of outlays under the act to nutrition programs, eight percent to crop insurance programs, six percent to conservation programs, five percent to commodity programs, and one percent to all other programs. The Congressional Research Service, in the report titled What Is the Farm Bill, stated the same distribution in summary form, reporting that the nutrition title accounted for eighty percent of the forecasted spending of the law, with the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program supplying the vast majority of nutrition title spending, and a Congressional Research Service primer used the slightly softer formulation of nearly eighty percent.

The figure carried political weight in both directions, and each side of the separation debate quoted the same percentage for opposite purposes. Representative Marlin Stutzman of Indiana, demanding a split on the House floor on June 19, 2013, objected that eighty percent of the spending in the pending bill went toward food stamps. Defenders of combination answered from the same arithmetic that the commodity, conservation, and crop insurance titles together accounted for roughly one fifth of projected spending and commanded a far narrower base of district level support. The distribution also explained why agriculture committee leaders from farm states, including Chairman Lucas and Senate Chairwoman Stabenow in the 2013 and 2014 cycle, spent the largest share of bargaining effort on nutrition provisions.

### The campaign for separation

Proposals to sever nutrition assistance from the farm bill recurred across five decades, and the stated reasons shifted between administrative logic, which held that a means tested benefit belonged with other means tested benefits under the Department of Health and Human Services and the welfare committees, and coalition politics, which held that combination protected both farm subsidies and nutrition spending from separate majorities that might cut either program standing alone. The Heritage Foundation made the combined argument in the summer of 2016, proposing in a reform blueprint that the farm bill be split into two bills, that food stamp administration transfer to the Department of Health and Human Services, and that the Food and Nutrition Service be eliminated. The Republican Party platform adopted in 2016 likewise called placement of food stamps under the Department of Agriculture a mistake. A June 2018 reorganization proposal from the Office of Management and Budget under Director Mick Mulvaney, which would have moved the program to the Department of Health and Human Services, likewise produced no enacted transfer.

The fullest test of separation came in 2013, and the sequence exposed the weakness of the separated form. The House Agriculture Committee reported a combined bill, the Federal Agriculture Reform and Risk Management Act, designated as H.R. 1947, which proposed a reduction of twenty billion five hundred million dollars in nutrition spending over ten years. Floor adoption of an amendment by Representative Steve Southerland of Florida, allowing states to apply work requirements to nutrition program participants, cost the bill Democratic support, and sixty two Republicans voted against the bill from the opposite direction, judging the nutrition reduction too small. The House defeated H.R. 1947 on June 20, 2013, by 195 votes to 234. Representative Stutzman then pressed for a split, and Chairman Lucas answered in June 2013, in remarks reported by the NAFB News Service, that splitting the bill was unacceptable because splitting the bill meant not having a bill.

House leadership then chose separation as a procedural device, and on July 11, 2013, the House passed a revised farm bill, H.R. 2642, by 216 votes to 208, with no Democrats in favor. The bill contained no nutrition title and proposed repeal of the permanent law governing commodity programs, a provision that would have given the farm titles a permanence the nutrition title lacked. Senator Debbie Stabenow of Michigan, chairwoman of the Senate Committee on Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry, answered on July 12, 2013, that the House product was not a real farm bill, and the Senate position rested on the comprehensive bill, S. 954, passed by sixty six votes to twenty seven with a nutrition title reducing nutrition spending by four billion one hundred million dollars over ten years. More than five hundred farm, food, and conservation organizations signed a letter to Speaker John Boehner of Ohio opposing the split. The House then passed a separate nutrition bill, the Nutrition Reform and Work Opportunity Act, designated as H.R. 3102, on September 19, 2013, by 217 votes to 210. The nutrition bill proposed reductions of about forty billion dollars over ten years, authorized the program for three years rather than the five year term of the farm bill, drew all two hundred seventeen affirmative votes from Republicans, and faced a veto threat from the administration of President Barack Obama.

Recombination followed within days, and recombination supplied the verdict on the experiment. On September 28, 2013, the House adopted House Resolution 361, which combined the texts of H.R. 2642 and H.R. 3102 into a single bill under the H.R. 2642 designation for purposes of conference with the Senate. Conferees reported an agreement as the Agricultural Act of 2014, House Report 113-333, on January 27, 2014. The House approved the conference agreement on January 29, 2014, by 251 votes to 166, the Senate approved the agreement on February 4, 2014, by sixty eight votes to thirty two, and President Obama signed the Agricultural Act of 2014 into law as Public Law 113-79 on February 7, 2014. The enacted law restored the combined structure as Title Four inside a twelve title farm bill, reauthorized the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program through fiscal year 2018, and produced, in the Congressional Research Service summary of budget issues, a projected reduction of sixteen billion six hundred million dollars over fiscal years 2014 to 2023 against the Congressional Budget Office baseline, far removed from the forty billion dollar reduction in the separated House nutrition bill. Separation had functioned as a route into conference rather than as a governing structure, since neither House product could complete the legislative path alone, the farm only bill lacking Democratic support and a Senate counterpart, and the nutrition only bill lacking a Senate path.

### A coalition renewed rather than made permanent

The pattern repeated in milder form in the 2018 cycle, when the House passed a farm bill on June 21, 2018, by 213 votes to 211, carrying expanded work requirements for nutrition program participants, while the Senate passed a bipartisan bill on June 28, 2018, by eighty six votes to eleven without those requirements. Farmdoc Daily at the University of Illinois reported in December 2018 that the House conceded the contested nutrition provisions in conference and accepted the Senate approach, with no net change in nutrition title spending over ten years. The conference agreement passed the Senate by eighty seven votes to thirteen and the House by three hundred sixty nine votes to forty seven, and the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 became law on December 20, 2018, as Public Law 115-334, with nutrition assistance again carried as a title of an omnibus farm bill administered by the Department of Agriculture.

Durability of that kind differs from permanence in a precise statutory sense. No statute merged farm programs and nutrition programs into a single permanent program, and the union existed only inside successive farm bills, each enacted for a fixed term, each requiring fresh assembly of the same exchange. Commodity programs rested on permanent law from 1938 and 1949, so expiration threatened farm interests with an intolerable reversion and guaranteed a legislative vehicle on a recurring schedule. The nutrition title rested on time limited authorizations, renewed through fiscal year 2018 in the Agricultural Act of 2014 and through fiscal year 2023 in the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, so nutrition assistance depended on the vehicle arriving and on the coalition holding at each arrival. Separation advocates, including Representative Stutzman in 2013 and the Heritage Foundation in 2016, treated that dependence as evidence of an artificial alliance sustained by legislative packaging. National Farmers Union President Roger Johnson, in comments reported in 2016, treated the same dependence as evidence of a working correspondence between food production and food access, and added that a broad bill attracted more support than a narrow bill. The record between 1973 and December 20, 2018, supports the narrower institutional conclusion, since every attempt to legislate the two halves apart either failed outright, as the block grant campaign of 1995 and 1996 failed, or collapsed back into combination before enactment, as the House experiment of 2013 collapsed, while every completed farm bill carried both halves between the same covers.

Committee jurisdiction survived for the same reason the coalition survived, because each defended the other. Transfer of nutrition assistance to the Department of Health and Human Services or to a welfare committee would have stripped the agriculture committees of the title that supplied eighty percent of farm bill spending under the projections published in 2014 and left farm legislation to pass on the strength of districts with direct farm interests, a base already narrowed by twentieth century demographic change. Retention of the nutrition title kept urban members invested in legislation whose other titles concerned commodities, conservation, and crop insurance. The arrangement that began as surplus disposal in 1939 therefore persisted into the farm bill enacted on December 20, 2018, not because any Congress had settled the classification of food assistance for good, and not because the program had acquired permanent law of the kind that protected the commodity titles, but because legislators in each cycle calculated that the exchange of urban votes for rural programs and rural votes for urban nutrition assistance remained the only arithmetic under which either half of the bill reliably became law.

## Modernization and Renaming

### Administration Through State Agencies and Federal Oversight

The program was never administered from Washington alone. Its structure divided labor between a federal government that set the governing rules, financed the benefits, and supervised performance, and state agencies that dealt directly with households, retailers, and local offices. That division survived every later revision, and modernization between the nineteen seventies and the first decade of the twenty first century largely meant refining the machinery inside a framework that remained federal and state at once. Eligibility standards, benefit formulas, and the definition of a household came from federal law and regulation. Application, interview, verification, certification, issuance, and recertification took place through state agencies and local offices that acted for them, often at county level. A household encountered the program through a state decision, even though the money for the allotment came from the federal treasury and the rules behind the decision were national in origin.

Federal oversight rested with the Department of Agriculture and, within the department, with the Food and Nutrition Service. The service was established on August 8, 1969, as a separate agency of the department, consolidating federal responsibility for the domestic food assistance programs as the food stamp effort expanded toward national coverage. From that base, the service issued regulations and guidance, approved state plans of operation, reviewed state performance, authorized and monitored the retail stores and wholesale concerns permitted to accept benefits, and conducted the federal half of the measurement system that tracked payment accuracy. The agency also carried responsibility for program integrity work, including investigation of benefit trafficking, in which benefits were exchanged for cash or ineligible items, and action against stores that violated program rules, ranging from monetary penalties to permanent disqualification. Households faced disqualification periods, claims for repayment of overpayments, and prosecution in serious cases of fraud, while retailers faced loss of authorization, a sanction that gave federal rules practical force at the point of sale.

The financial bargain reinforced the division of responsibility. The federal government paid the full cost of benefits, so that a state decision to certify a household did not draw on state funds for the allotment itself. Administrative costs were shared, with the federal government bearing about one half of approved state administrative expense under the standard matching arrangement. That sharing gave states a material stake in how the program was run without giving states control over benefit levels, and it gave the federal government a reason to prescribe procedures in detail, because federal money followed state paperwork. State flexibility grew in areas such as certification periods, reporting, and outreach, and later farm legislation widened the menu of state options. Flexibility operated inside federal guardrails, however. A state could simplify an optional procedure, but a state could not rewrite the gross and net income tests, alter the national benefit formula at will, or disregard federal timeliness standards for applications and expedited service.

Administration also developed a routine rhythm. Households applied, were interviewed, and supplied verification of identity, residence, income, and deductible expenses. The state agency computed a monthly allotment by applying deductions to gross income and comparing the result with the maximum allotment for the household size, a maximum tied to the cost of the Thrifty Food Plan. Certification lasted for a defined period, and households reported changes and returned for recertification as periods expired. Each stage generated records and an opportunity for error, which explains why accuracy measurement became a central preoccupation and why modernization concentrated on systems that recorded cases and delivered benefits.

### Quality Control and the Measurement of Error

The quality control system gave the program a formal method for asking whether benefits had been paid to the right households in the right amounts. The Food Stamp Act associated with the comprehensive revision of the nineteen seventies first established the system in statute, and the system matured into a two tier process of state review followed by federal re-review. Each month, every state agency selected a sample of participating households and examined the case record to determine whether the household had been eligible and whether the allotment had been computed correctly. A review could find an overpayment, in which the household received more than the law allowed, or an underpayment, in which the household received less. Errors could arise from agency action, such as a misapplied deduction, or from household circumstances, such as income that had changed or had been reported incorrectly. Sample findings produced a state payment error rate, expressed as the share of benefit dollars issued in error, combining overpayments and underpayments against total issuance.

Federal reviewers then drew a subsample of the cases that states had reviewed and examined those cases again. The re-review validated or corrected state findings and supplied the basis for a national payment error rate, the figure most often used to describe accuracy for the program as a whole. The distinction between error and fraud mattered throughout the system. A payment error rate measured incorrect payments regardless of intent, and most errors reflected mistakes, delayed reporting, or complexity rather than deliberate deception. Fraud was pursued through separate investigative and disqualification channels. States were also required to correct identified errors, restore benefits lost through underpayment, and establish claims for overpayment recovery, so measurement fed back into individual cases as well as into aggregate statistics.

Error rates carried fiscal consequences, and the design of those consequences changed as administrators debated how to encourage accuracy without punishing states for the difficulty of the task. States with error rates above the national average faced fiscal sanctions or liabilities tied to a portion of the excess error, while states with low rates became eligible for bonuses and enhanced funding for reinvestment in program operations. The Farm Security and Rural Investment Act, enacted in 2002, revised the sanction structure effective October 1 of that year, narrowing the circumstances in which a liability would be imposed and tying exposure to error rates that exceeded a threshold set above the national average for consecutive years under a stated standard of statistical confidence. Performance bonuses for payment accuracy, application timeliness, and participation among eligible people took a larger place in the system during the same period. Accuracy thus remained a shared obligation, measured by a common method and published state by state.

The measurement system also shaped behavior in less formal ways. State administrators redesigned training around the errors that samples exposed, and federal guidance targeted recurring sources of error, such as treatment of earnings and shelter costs. National payment error rates fell from double digit levels in earlier periods to low single digit levels, with a rate of 3.81 percent reported for fiscal year 2010. The decline reflected clearer policy, longer certification periods for stable households, simplified reporting, and electronic systems that reduced mistakes associated with paper handling.

### Welfare Reform and the Redesign of Eligibility and Delivery

The largest statutory intervention between the comprehensive revision of the nineteen seventies and the renaming of 2008 came through welfare legislation rather than through a farm bill. The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 was enacted as Public Law 104-193 and was signed by President William J. Clinton on August 22, 1996. The act replaced Aid to Families with Dependent Children with Temporary Assistance for Needy Families, imposed time limits and work requirements across cash assistance, and rewrote significant parts of food stamp law in the same measure. Food stamp changes formed one of the principal sources of projected federal savings in the act. For the food stamp program, the act tightened eligibility, adjusted benefit computation in ways that restrained growth in allotments, expanded state administrative authority in selected areas, strengthened penalties for fraud and trafficking, and set a deadline for transforming the method by which benefits reached households.

Eligibility restrictions for legal immigrants were among the most consequential provisions, and a companion treatment in the series addresses the subject in full. In outline, the 1996 act made most legal immigrants ineligible for food stamps until citizenship, subject to exceptions that included refugees and asylees within a limited period after arrival, certain veterans and active duty service members and their families, and immigrants with a substantial record of qualifying work. The restrictions marked a sharp departure from the treatment that had applied since the program became national, under which legal immigrants had generally been eligible on the same financial terms as citizens. Congress revisited the policy in stages, restoring eligibility to defined groups at the end of the nineteen nineties and extending broader restorations in the farm bill of 2002, under which many legal immigrants regained eligibility after five years of residence and immigrant children and certain disabled immigrants regained eligibility without regard to date of entry. The sequence left a durable mark on administration, because agencies had to apply different rules according to immigration status and date of arrival within a single body of law.

A second set of restrictions applied to able bodied adults without dependents, a category the act singled out for a time limit tied to work. Adults from age eighteen to age forty nine who were not living with a child, were not pregnant, and were not otherwise exempt became limited to three months of benefits in a thirty six month period unless the adult worked or participated in a qualifying work program for at least twenty hours per week, or took part in a workfare assignment. The provision introduced a true time limit into a program that had previously conditioned eligibility on financial need and on general work registration rules rather than on exhaustion of a fixed number of months. Exemptions protected adults medically certified as unable to work the required hours and adults caring for an incapacitated household member, among others, and the statute allowed waivers for areas with high unemployment or an insufficient number of jobs, together with a limited pool of discretionary exemptions. Administration required states to track months of receipt across a rolling three year window.

Other provisions of the 1996 act operated more quietly. The act revised elements of benefit computation, including treatment of the standard deduction and the excess shelter deduction, in ways intended to slow the growth of allotments, and gave states added flexibility over certain certification choices. The eligibility contractions drew the greatest public attention, but the delivery mandate proved to be the provision with the most complete operational effect, because the mandate applied to every state and altered the daily experience of every participating household.

### Electronic Benefit Transfer and the End of Paper Coupons

Electronic benefit transfer replaced a paper instrument with an account. Under the older system, the federal government printed coupons, shipped coupon books under tight security, and distributed allotments that recipients carried to stores and spent in a manner close to cash. Retailers deposited redeemed coupons through the banking system, and the coupons were verified, sorted, and eventually destroyed. The process involved printing, transport, storage, counting, and reconciliation at every step, and coupons carried the risks of any bearer instrument, including theft, loss, counterfeiting, and sale at a discount in illicit markets. Electronic benefit transfer removed the instrument from circulation. The state agency credited the monthly allotment to an electronic account, and the household spent the balance with a plastic card and a personal identification number at a retailer terminal, with the transaction debiting the account and settling payment to the retailer through electronic networks. The card resembled a commercial debit card.

Experimentation preceded the mandate by more than a decade. A demonstration in Reading, Pennsylvania, beginning in 1984, tested electronic delivery, and other pilots followed. Maryland became the first state to operate electronic benefit transfer statewide, completing rollout in 1993, and early adopting states supplied evidence on retailer readiness, recipient acceptance, transaction costs, and error reduction. Adoption remained partial before 1996, because conversion required investment in systems, contracts with processors, terminal deployment among retailers, and training. Cost neutrality expectations in early policy, requiring a showing that an electronic system would not cost more than the coupon system it replaced, also slowed conversion. The 1996 act changed the posture from permission to command.

The mandate in the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act provided that, not later than October 1, 2002, each state agency was to implement an electronic benefit transfer system under which benefits were issued from and stored in a central databank, unless the Secretary of Agriculture granted a waiver to a state agency facing unusual barriers to implementation. The Food and Nutrition Service implemented the requirement through regulation and worked with states on procurement and schedules as the deadline approached. Separate legislation, the Electronic Benefit Transfer Interoperability and Portability Act of 2000, directed that systems be interoperable and benefits portable among all states by the same date, under a national standard, without imposing the cost of interoperability on participating retail or wholesale concerns. A small number of states received waivers extending schedules beyond the deadline. In June 2004, electronic benefit transfer was implemented nationwide, and from that point all benefits were issued electronically.

The legal end of paper coupons followed several years after the operational end of paper issuance. Because coupons issued before conversion lacked an expiration date, the instruments remained obligations that retailers were required to accept and the federal government was required to honor. Section 4115 of the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 closed that remainder. The provision prohibited state agencies from issuing paper coupons as of enactment, June 18, 2008, confirming in statute a practice that electronic conversion had already established. The same provision deobligated paper coupons as legal tender one year after enactment, so that coupons could no longer be redeemed after June 17, 2009, and electronic benefit transfer cards stood as the sole method of delivery as of June 18, 2009. Paper issuance therefore ended in operational terms in 2004, was barred by statute in 2008, and ceased to function as legal tender in 2009.

### Renaming Under the Farm Bill Enacted Over a Veto

The renaming arrived through the farm bill of 2008, in a legislative sequence with an unusual procedural defect at its center. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 first passed Congress as H.R. 2419, and President George W. Bush vetoed the measure. Congress overrode the veto, and the bill became Public Law 110-234 on May 22, 2008. The enrolled version presented to the President, however, had omitted the trade title, so the enacted law contained fourteen of the fifteen titles of the agreed bill. To cure the omission, Congress passed the complete text again as H.R. 6124. President Bush vetoed that bill as well, on June 18, 2008, and on the same day the House of Representatives overrode the veto by a vote of 317 to 109 and the Senate overrode by a vote of 80 to 14. H.R. 6124 accordingly became Public Law 110-246 on June 18, 2008, replacing the incomplete enactment.

Sections 4001 and 4002 of Title IV performed the renaming. Section 4001 amended the short title of the governing statute, striking the Food Stamp Act of 1977 and inserting the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008, and amended the statute throughout by striking the food stamp program and inserting the supplemental nutrition assistance program. Section 4002 supplied conforming amendments, so that headings, definitions, and cross references matched the new names. Guidance from the Food and Nutrition Service stated the effective date plainly. The renaming, together with the other food stamp provisions of the title apart from the coupon prohibition that took effect on enactment, took effect on October 1, 2008, the first day of fiscal year 2009. State agencies were permitted to retain state specific program names, while the federal name and statute carried the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and its acronym, SNAP.

The choice of name expressed a deliberate change in presentation. Food stamps described an instrument, and the instrument had disappeared from ordinary use four years before the renaming took effect. Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program described a function, supplementing the food purchasing power of a household so that the household could obtain a more nutritious diet. Federal officials presented the new name as a means of reducing stigma, recognizing electronic delivery, and directing attention toward nutrition rather than toward a coupon. The renaming did not alter eligibility or benefit computation by itself, and households experienced continuity of accounts, cards, and certification. The Food and Nutrition Act of 2008 was the same body of law under a new short title, traceable through the Food Stamp Act of 1977 to the original Food Stamp Act of 1964, and administration proceeded under the renamed statute without interruption.

### Benefit and Eligibility Modernization in the Renamed Program

Title IV paired the new names with benefit and eligibility adjustments designed to arrest erosion in the value of assistance and to reduce penalties on work and saving. The provisions took effect on October 1, 2008, unless a provision stated a different date, and the Congressional Budget Office projected that the nutrition title would add several billion dollars in program costs over the five year and ten year periods following enactment, with the greater part attributable to the food stamp changes. The standard deduction illustrated the approach. For households of one to three persons, the deduction had been fixed in nominal terms and had lost value as prices rose, while larger households had received a deduction adjusted for inflation. Section 4102 raised the standard deduction for smaller households from 134 dollars to 144 dollars and provided for annual adjustment thereafter. Section 4107 replaced the flat ten dollar minimum benefit for one and two person households with an amount set at eight percent of the maximum allotment for a household of one, which produced a minimum of fourteen dollars at implementation and connected the minimum to annual adjustment of maximum allotments.

Treatment of dependent care and of resources carried the modernization into the circumstances of working households and savers. Section 4103 eliminated the cap on the deduction for dependent care expenses, which had limited the deduction to 200 dollars per month for a dependent under age two and 175 dollars per month for an older dependent, and allowed the full amount of out of pocket dependent care costs to be deducted in computing net income. Section 4104 indexed the asset limits, adjusting the limits of 2,000 dollars for most households and 3,000 dollars for households with an elderly or disabled member for inflation, rounded down to the nearest 250 dollars, beginning on October 1, 2008. Section 4105 excluded tax preferred education accounts and tax preferred retirement accounts from countable resources, covering accounts described in the cited sections of the Internal Revenue Code, including individual retirement arrangements and employer sponsored plans, together with funds in a Federal Thrift Savings Plan account. A household could therefore hold savings for education or retirement of the kinds described in the statute without those balances defeating eligibility.

Other provisions of Title IV adjusted administration and access in smaller ways. Section 4101 excluded certain military combat related payments from income, protecting benefits of households with a member receiving such pay. The title also adjusted employment and training and nutrition education authorities, transitional benefits for households leaving cash assistance, and state use of application and reporting technologies. Guidance from the Food and Nutrition Service translated the changes into state procedures around October 1, 2008, with hold harmless treatment for a period after mandatory implementation dates. By the close of the period covered in this section, the transformation had acquired a new statutory name, a benefit structure adjusted for inflation where fixed figures had eroded value, and a delivery system in which paper coupons survived only as museum pieces.

## Measured Scale, Contested Readings, and Honest Limits

### The Scale at Peak and in Contraction

The measured scale of the program created by the Food Stamp Act of 1964 admits of little dispute, because the underlying series came from the administering agency itself, the Food and Nutrition Service, and reached publication through the Economic Research Service. In the report on fiscal 2018 issued in April 2019, Victor Oliveira of the Economic Research Service recorded that average monthly participation had reached a historical high of 47.6 million persons in fiscal 2013 and that federal spending for the program had reached a historical high of 79.9 billion dollars in the same fiscal year. The Food and Nutrition Service explanatory notes for fiscal 2015 supplied the texture behind the peak, reporting average participation of 47.64 million persons in fiscal 2013, an average benefit of 133.08 dollars per person per month in fiscal 2013, and participation equal to 15.1 percent of the resident population in fiscal 2013.

The contraction after the peak was equally well documented. Oliveira reported for the Economic Research Service that average monthly participation in fiscal 2018 stood at 40.3 million persons, a decline of 4 percent from fiscal 2017, and that federal spending for the program in fiscal 2018 totaled 65.0 billion dollars, about 5 percent less than in fiscal 2017 and 19 percent less than the historical high recorded for fiscal 2013. The same report recorded that the program accounted for 68 percent of all federal food and nutrition assistance spending in fiscal 2018. Patrick Canning and Rosanna Mentzer Morrison of the Economic Research Service, writing in July 2019, reported that the program issued 60.9 billion dollars in benefits during fiscal 2018, with the balance of federal spending carrying administration and related costs. Total federal cost and benefits issued are different measures, and arguments that move between the two without naming the measure borrow strength that the series does not supply. The trajectory also carried a built in ambiguity. The rise to 47.6 million persons in fiscal 2013 and the fall to 40.3 million persons in fiscal 2018 demonstrated, on one reading, an entitlement expanding under economic distress and contracting as conditions improved, and, on another reading, how far participation had moved from the levels of the early two thousands. The series settled the magnitude of both movements, and nothing about which deserved emphasis.

### Payment Accuracy and the Break in the Series

The payment error rate was the second great quantitative battleground, and the record contains a break that honest presentation cannot smooth over. The quality control system, under which state agencies reviewed samples of cases and the Food and Nutrition Service aggregated the results into a national rate, produced a long decline in the rate that Dorothy Rosenbaum of the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities documented from Department of Agriculture data. Rosenbaum reported in July 2014 that the combined payment error rate for fiscal 2013 had fallen to 3.20 percent, composed of an overpayment rate of 2.61 percent and an underpayment rate of 0.60 percent, the lowest overpayment rate recorded since the Department of Agriculture began the measurement system in 1981. Rosenbaum reported in July 2015 that the combined rate for fiscal 2014 stood at 3.66 percent, composed of an overpayment rate of 2.96 percent and an underpayment rate of 0.69 percent, and that less than 1 percent of benefits in fiscal 2014 went to households ineligible for the program at all. Jessica Shahin, associate administrator of the Food and Nutrition Service, supplied essential context in testimony before the House Committee on Agriculture on July 6, 2016, stating that most errors stemmed from unintentional mistakes by the state agency or the household rather than fraud, and that 62 percent of errors in the cases examined were state agency errors while 38 percent were client errors.

The later record qualified that achievement in ways that changed what the earlier figures could be taken to prove. The Office of Inspector General of the Department of Agriculture issued a report in September 2015 on quality control in eight states, and the Congressional Research Service, in its report on errors and fraud, summarized the inspector general as having determined that all eight agencies examined had deliberately weakened the integrity of the quality control process with the aid of hired consultants. The same Congressional Research Service account recorded the response of the Food and Nutrition Service, which contended that the inspector general had relied on unconfirmed information, misunderstandings of program policy, and insufficient statistical analysis. The dispute ceased to be internal in April 2017, when the Department of Justice announced settlements with Wisconsin and Virginia over the doctoring of quality control data to lower reported error rates, each state agreeing to pay about 7 million dollars, as Agri Pulse reported from the Department of Justice announcements in April 2017. Agri Pulse further reported in April 2017 that an internal Food and Nutrition Service investigation, described in department documents obtained by the publication, had found 42 of the 53 administering agencies using biased practices to measure error rates. Bonuses and penalties attached to the reported rates gave that measurement system incentive pressure during the years when the published rates reached record lows.

The Department of Agriculture then interrupted the series itself. A Food and Nutrition Service memorandum dated June 30, 2017 stated that the department would not release a national payment error rate for fiscal 2016. When the department resumed publication in June 2018, the Food and Nutrition Service reported a national payment error rate of 6.30 percent for fiscal 2017, and the department stated that no national rate had been published for fiscal 2015 or fiscal 2016 because of concern about statistical biases in the system. The department attributed the apparent increase from 3.66 percent in fiscal 2014 to 6.30 percent in fiscal 2017 to improved measurement rather than to an actual increase in improper payments, and the Food and Nutrition Service subsequently reported a rate of 6.80 percent for fiscal 2018. The rates before and after the interruption were produced under different levels of scrutiny of the state reviews behind them, so a simple line drawn from fiscal 2014 to fiscal 2017 misrepresents both ends of the comparison. Equally, the payment error rate never purported to measure fraud, as Shahin stated in the July 2016 testimony, and the trafficking estimates discussed below measured intentional misconduct that the error rate did not capture.

### The Food Security Argument at Full Strength

The strongest form of the argument for the program rested on food security, built from research designs that confronted the central difficulty of the subject directly. Households that entered the program were, by the logic of eligibility and application, in greater need than households that stayed out, so simple comparisons were expected to show participants worse off even if benefits helped. Caroline Ratcliffe and Signe Mary McKernan of the Urban Institute addressed that selection problem in a report issued by the Economic Research Service in April 2010, using the 1996, 2001, and 2004 panels of the Survey of Income and Program Participation and instrumental variable methods that exploited variation in state program rules. Ratcliffe and McKernan found that receipt of benefits reduced the likelihood of a household being food insecure by roughly 30 percent and reduced the likelihood of a household being very food insecure by roughly 20 percent. The journal version, published by Ratcliffe, McKernan, and Zhang in 2011, estimated a reduction of 16.2 percentage points in the likelihood of food insecurity, consistent with the finding of Mark Nord and Golla of the Economic Research Service in 2009 that the likelihood of very low food security fell by roughly one third when households began receiving benefits.

A second line of evidence followed households through time rather than comparing households at a moment. Mabli and colleagues at Mathematica Policy Research conducted the food security survey of participants for the Food and Nutrition Service, a study the Department of Agriculture described on release in 2013 as the largest such survey undertaken to that date, drawing on 9,811 households across 30 states. Mathematica found that participation for about six months was associated with a significant decrease in food insecurity, more pronounced in households with children, as the Department of Agriculture reported in 2013. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, summarizing the Mathematica findings, reported that food insecurity among children fell by roughly a third after six months of receipt. Mark Nord and Mark Prell of the Economic Research Service examined the increase in maximum benefits enacted in 2009, in a report issued in 2011, and estimated that from late 2008 to late 2009 the prevalence of food insecurity among low income households fell by 2.2 percentage points and the prevalence of very low food security fell by 2.0 percentage points, while benefits received by the typical participating household rose by about 16 percent and food expenditures by the typical low income household rose by 5.4 percent. Food insecurity did not decline among households with incomes just above the eligibility range, a comparison that strengthened the attribution of the improvement to the benefit increase rather than to general economic movement.

Presented at full strength, the food security argument had a specific and limited structure. The benefit arrived monthly, through ordinary retail channels, in a form restricted to food, and the record assembled by the Urban Institute researchers, by Mathematica, and by Economic Research Service economists associated that delivery with reductions in measured food insecurity of roughly one fifth to roughly one third, depending on the outcome and the design. The argument did not depend on the program eliminating food insecurity, and Ratcliffe and McKernan proceeded from a baseline in which 39 percent of near poor households had been food insecure in 2008. The claim, precisely stated, was comparative.

### The Program Integrity Argument at Full Strength

The strongest form of the integrity argument began with arithmetic rather than anecdote. In a program whose federal spending reached 79.9 billion dollars in fiscal 2013, according to the Economic Research Service report on fiscal 2018, a diversion rate of one percent represented roughly 800 million dollars in a single year. Trafficking, the exchange of benefits for cash at a discount, was the most directly measured form of intentional diversion, and the trafficking studies gave the argument its central figures. Richard Mantovani and Hoke Wilson, in the trafficking study issued by the Food and Nutrition Service in 2011, estimated that trafficking diverted about 330 million dollars annually between 2006 and 2008, or about 1.0 percent of benefits during that period. Mantovani, Williams, and Pflieger, in the trafficking study issued by the Food and Nutrition Service in August 2013, estimated that trafficking diverted an estimated 858 million dollars annually between 2009 and 2011, or about 1.3 percent of benefits during that period, and that approximately 10.5 percent of authorized stores engaged in trafficking during the 2009 to 2011 period. The Department of Agriculture framed the longer trajectory as a decline from about 4 percent of benefits in the early nineteen nineties to about 1 percent, in department statements in 2013. The two studies nearest the peak years, however, showed the rate and the dollar amount rising between the 2006 to 2008 period and the 2009 to 2011 period, driven in substantial part by program growth, as the August 2013 study stated. An integrity argument built on the official studies could therefore concede the long decline and press the nearer record, in which about one benefit dollar in seventy five was exchanged for cash.

The second pillar of the integrity argument concerned eligibility breadth rather than misconduct. The Congressional Research Service, in its report on categorical eligibility updated through 2018, described how broad based categorical eligibility allowed states to confer eligibility through receipt of a benefit funded by the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families block grant, which in many states took the form of a brochure or a hotline referral. The Congressional Research Service reported that, as of February 2018, 42 jurisdictions operated broad based categorical eligibility, that no state set a gross income limit above 200 percent of the federal poverty guidelines under the policy, and that in all but five of the jurisdictions operating the policy there was no asset test for eligibility. Under the regular federal rules described by the Congressional Research Service for fiscal 2018, by contrast, a household faced a liquid asset limit of 2,250 dollars, or 3,500 dollars for a household with an elderly or disabled member. That contrast was the substance of the complaint, and the institutional record gave the complaint an official venue. The House Committee on Agriculture, under Chairman K. Michael Conaway, issued findings in December 2016 after 16 hearings held over two years, and Agri Pulse reported on December 7, 2016 that the committee had concluded that states needed to enforce work requirements more effectively, that more could be done to cut down on errors and fraud, and that the program needed clear goals and metrics.

The third pillar joined the first two, because the inspector general findings of September 2015, the Department of Justice settlements announced in April 2017, and the internal finding of biased practices in 42 of 53 agencies together supported the contention that headline defenses of the program rested on measurements the administering system had incentives to depress. The Congressional Research Service, in its reporting on errors and fraud, drew the distinction that made the contention precise, noting that the payment error rate measured the accuracy of eligibility and benefit determinations while trafficking measured a major form of fraud that the error rate did not include. At full strength, the integrity argument did not require a claim that most benefits were misspent. The official record supplied a narrower and harder claim, built from the trafficking estimate for 2009 to 2011, an eligibility route in 42 jurisdictions as of February 2018 that could rest on a brochure and proceed without an asset test, and a measurement system whose reported record accuracy was under federal investigation within two years of the fiscal 2014 rate.

### What the Statute Did Not Do

The Act of 1964 and the legislation that succeeded it never created a general income guarantee. Benefits arrived as food purchasing power, redeemable for eligible food for home consumption through authorized retailers, and the Food and Nutrition Service rules on eligible items excluded categories of spending that a cash grant would have covered, including alcohol, tobacco, hot prepared food, and nonfood household goods. A household could not convert the allotment into rent, utility payments, medical care, or transportation through any lawful channel, and trafficking was defined as an offense precisely because the benefit was tied to food expenditure by design. The allotment rested on the cost of the Thrifty Food Plan and fell as countable net income rose, so the program supplemented a household food budget on the assumption that the household devoted part of its own income to food, rather than replacing household food spending or supporting the household budget as a whole. The average benefit at the fiscal 2013 peak, 133.08 dollars per person per month as measured by the Food and Nutrition Service, described a supplement measured against a food plan, not an income floor measured against total need.

Categorical exclusions persisted alongside the income and asset tests, and the Congressional Research Service catalogued them in its primer on eligibility and benefits issued in February 2016. The primer listed categorical denials applying to strikers, to many noncitizens, to many postsecondary students, to people living in institutional settings, and to many persons with drug related felony convictions, subject in the last case to state choices that the 1996 legislation had left open. The primer also described the time limit added in 1996, under which able bodied adults without dependents were limited to three months of benefits in any 36 month period unless the adult worked at least half time or participated in a work or training program. Nor did legal entitlement translate into universal receipt among the eligible. The Food and Nutrition Service, in its explanatory notes for the fiscal 2019 budget, reported figures from its participation rate series showing that in fiscal 2015, 83 percent of all persons eligible for the program participated. Administration also remained divided, with federal law setting the rules and federal funds paying for benefits while state agencies determined eligibility case by case, a division the quality control record showed to be a permanent source of dispute. A program can be an entitlement in law and the largest food assistance program in the federal budget, and remain a restricted, conditional, and partially claimed benefit. The record through fiscal 2018 supported every element of that description.

### Committee Jurisdiction and Political Durability

The closing assessment of the evidence returns to the claim that organizes the article as a whole. Committee jurisdiction, not program design, explains political durability. The design features documented across the article changed repeatedly across the decades, from the purchase requirement and its abolition to the shift from paper coupons to electronic issuance, from the eligibility contractions of the nineteen nineties to the restorations that followed, and from the measurement regime that produced the fiscal 2014 rate of 3.66 percent to the revised regime that produced the fiscal 2017 rate of 6.30 percent. A design based explanation would predict survival through durable agreement on particular features, and the record shows such agreement dissolving and reforming with each reauthorization cycle. The jurisdictional fact, by contrast, held constant. Authorization sat in the agriculture committees of the two chambers and moved inside farm legislation, where nutrition spending formed the largest share of the bill and farm programs supplied the coalition that carried the bill. The House Committee on Agriculture spent two years and 16 hearings examining the program before December 2016 and concluded, in the findings reported by Agri Pulse on December 7, 2016, that errors, fraud, and work enforcement required further attention, framing the outcome as improvement of a program the committee expected to continue administering. Criticism, even at full strength and even from the committee holding jurisdiction, was processed as oversight within a continuing authorization rather than as a case for termination.

The measured record supports that reading because the gravest documented challenges produced administrative correction rather than existential threat. The trafficking estimate of 858 million dollars annually for the 2009 to 2011 period, from the Food and Nutrition Service study of August 2013, produced enforcement initiatives and retailer oversight. The inspector general report of September 2015 produced a measurement overhaul and a two year interruption in the national rate. Settlements by Wisconsin and Virginia of about 7 million dollars each, announced by the Department of Justice in April 2017, penalized state conduct without disturbing the entitlement structure. Peak spending of 79.9 billion dollars in fiscal 2013 gave way to 65.0 billion dollars in fiscal 2018 through caseload decline and incremental rule adjustment rather than through repeal or block granting. Each episode was absorbed by the arrangement that placed the program inside farm legislation under agriculture committee jurisdiction, where farm and nutrition constituencies had recurring reasons to pass the same bill. Design explains what the program did, how benefits were calculated, who was excluded, and where the measurement systems failed. Jurisdiction explains why a program carrying that contested record, at that measured scale, with those documented limits, remained in place at the vantage of September 2019, facing a further cycle of amendment rather than a vote on its existence.

## A Nutrition Program Inside an Agriculture Statute

The record traced across the six sections converges on a namable claim: a nutrition program housed in an agriculture statute endured because committee jurisdiction, not program design, explains political durability. Design changed at every stage. A purchase charge gave way to issuance of a net benefit. State eligibility standards gave way to uniform national standards. Paper coupons gave way to electronic accounts. A name built on an instrument gave way to a name built on a function. The jurisdictional fact held constant through each change, with authorization in the agriculture committees and movement inside farm legislation. A broader account of [the history and structure of the farm bill](/2020/11/01/farm-bill-history-and-structure/) places that vehicle in full legislative context.

### What best explains the political durability of the program?

Committee jurisdiction best explains durability, because each farm bill reassembled the exchange of urban votes for nutrition assistance and rural votes for farm programs. Benefit formulas, eligibility rules, and delivery instruments changed repeatedly without dissolving that exchange, and attempts to legislate the two halves apart collapsed back into combination before enactment.

Adjacent statutes sharpen the contrast. Cash assistance followed a different committee path and a different administrative home, and the largest single alteration of food stamp eligibility arrived inside welfare legislation, a sequence examined in [the guide to the welfare reform legislation](/2019/08/01/welfare-reform-act-1996-guide/). Even under that legislation, food assistance retained federal rules, federal benefit financing, and agriculture committee jurisdiction, while cash assistance moved to a block grant under a health and human services department. A neighboring nutrition statute followed the agriculture route as well, and [the guide to the Child Nutrition Act](/2019/09/15/child-nutrition-act-1966-guide/) traces a parallel placement of child feeding inside agricultural law. Placement, rather than any single benefit formula, supplied the recurring coalition of urban and rural votes under which farm and nutrition titles passed together.

### How did benefit delivery change across the life of the statute?

Delivery changed from a cash purchase of paper coupons at an issuance point to an electronic account accessed by card and personal identification number at a retail terminal. The federal benefit after each change supplemented household food spending through authorized stores, and the restricted character of the benefit survived every change in instrument.

Scholarly and administrative use of a record of that length depends on source discipline, because statutory text, committee reports, agency histories, and measurement series answer different questions and fail in different ways. Researchers assembling such a record can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) as a single organized file. The statute that emerged from the process documented in the preceding sections stands as a restricted food benefit, financed federally, administered by states, measured imperfectly, contested on food security and integrity grounds at full strength on both sides, and sustained across five decades of amendment by the jurisdictional arrangement fixed when surplus disposal first joined farm policy to household nutrition.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Food Stamp Act of 1964 establish?

The Food Stamp Act of 1964, enacted as Public Law 88-525 and signed on August 31, 1964, established a permanent cooperative federal and state program of food assistance operated through normal channels of trade. The statute authorized the Secretary of Agriculture to formulate and administer the program, issue coupons redeemable at face value, and approve state plans of operation. Stated purposes joined strengthening the agricultural economy and achieving fuller use of food abundances to raising levels of nutrition among low income households. Permanence meant standing authority without a sunset date, while appropriations remained capped by fiscal year and operation extended only to political subdivisions covered by approved state plans.

### Q: What was the purchase requirement in the Food Stamp Act?

The purchase requirement was the amount of cash a certified household had to pay to obtain a monthly coupon allotment. The charge approximated the amount the household would ordinarily have spent on food without assistance, and the difference between the charge and the face value of the allotment formed the bonus, the net federal benefit. A household with higher counted income paid more and received a smaller bonus, while a household with lower income paid less and received a larger bonus. Amendments enacted in 1971 capped the charge at 30 percent of household income. Certification alone conferred no coupons, because payment stood as a separate transaction between eligibility and issuance.

### Q: When did the Food Stamp Act purchase requirement end?

The purchase requirement ended effective January 1, 1979. The Food Stamp Act of 1977, enacted as Title XIII of the Food and Agriculture Act of 1977, eliminated charges for coupon allotments, and the rewritten statute took effect on October 1, 1977. Elimination itself followed a phased regulatory transition. Proposed regulations appeared on May 2, 1978, and final regulations followed on October 17, 1978, codifying an implementation schedule that required state agencies to eliminate the purchase requirement for all households on or before January 1, 1979. A state could designate an earlier month with federal approval where accounting procedures for the new issuance system stood ready. Participation rose by 1,500,000 persons in January 1979.

### Q: Why is the Food Stamp Act in the farm bill?

The Food Stamp Act sits in the farm bill because food assistance entered federal law as an instrument of farm policy for moving surplus agricultural production into consumption. Administration by the Department of Agriculture, codification under Title Seven, and jurisdiction in the agriculture committees followed that origin. Formal combination arrived with the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, the first omnibus farm bill to carry food stamps and commodity programs in a single measure. Combination served coalition arithmetic, since urban members supplied votes for commodity programs and rural members supplied votes or acquiescence for nutrition assistance, and each recurring farm bill renewed that exchange between districts with little else in common.

### Q: When was the Food Stamp Act renamed?

The renaming took effect on October 1, 2008, under the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008, Public Law 110-246. Sections 4001 and 4002 of Title IV amended the short title of the governing statute, striking the Food Stamp Act of 1977 and inserting the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008, and replaced references to the food stamp program with the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program throughout the statute. Guidance from the Food and Nutrition Service confirmed October 1, 2008, the first day of fiscal year 2009, as the effective date for the renaming and for the other food stamp provisions of the title, apart from the coupon prohibition effective on enactment.

### Q: Did the Food Stamp Act start as a pilot?

The permanent program began as a pilot. President John F. Kennedy signed Executive Order 10914 on January 21, 1961, directing expanded food distribution, and announced food stamp pilot programs on February 2, 1961. The first pilot transaction took place on May 29, 1961, in McDowell County, West Virginia, where Alderson Muncy and Chloe Muncy purchased 95 dollars in stamps for a household of fifteen persons. Eight pilot areas operated at the start. By January 1964, the pilots had expanded to forty three areas in twenty two states, with 380,000 participants. The pilot retained the purchase requirement and abandoned separate stamps for surplus foods.

### Q: When did the Food Stamp Act become nationwide?

The program became nationwide on July 1, 1974, under the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, enacted on August 10, 1973. The statute required states to expand the program to every political subdivision before July 1, 1974, replacing the uneven geography of the first decade, under which coverage depended on state and local decisions to operate the program in place of commodity distribution. The Food and Nutrition Service recorded nationwide operation from July 1, 1974, with full implementation in Puerto Rico following on November 1, 1974. Average monthly participation rose from 12,862,000 persons in fiscal year 1974 to 17,064,000 persons in fiscal year 1975.

### Q: What replaced paper coupons under the Food Stamp Act?

Electronic benefit transfer cards replaced paper coupons. Under electronic benefit transfer, the state agency credits the monthly allotment to an electronic account, and the household spends the balance with a plastic card and a personal identification number at a retailer terminal. Conversion proceeded under a federal mandate with a deadline of October 1, 2002, and electronic issuance operated nationwide from June 2004. Paper coupons lacked an expiration date, so a statutory close followed later. Section 4115 of the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 prohibited paper coupon issuance as of enactment on June 18, 2008, and coupons ceased to be legal tender after June 17, 2009.

### Q: Where is the Food Stamp Act codified in the United States Code?

The Food Stamp Act was codified in Title 7 of the United States Code, the title devoted to agriculture. Placement in Title 7 recorded the agricultural character of the enterprise in the structure of federal law itself, alongside commodity, conservation, and farm credit statutes administered by the Department of Agriculture. Successor legislation preserved the placement. The Food Stamp Act of 1977 rewrote the original act within the same title, and the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008 carried the renamed statute forward under a new short title. Codification in an agriculture title aligned the statute with the committees holding jurisdiction and with the department charged with administration from enactment onward.

### Q: What is the Thrifty Food Plan in the Food Stamp Act?

The Thrifty Food Plan is the federal food plan that supplies the cost basis for maximum allotments. The Food Stamp Act of 1977 wrote the plan into the statute, defining the plan as the diet required to feed a family of four persons, consisting of a man and a woman twenty to fifty four years of age, a child six to eight years of age, and a child nine to eleven years of age, determined in accordance with calculations of the Secretary of Agriculture. The plan had replaced the economy food plan as the allotment basis in 1975, drawing on updated dietary recommendations, food composition data, food habits, and price information.

### Q: How is the benefit amount calculated under the Food Stamp Act?

Benefit calculation begins with the maximum allotment for the household size, set by the cost of the Thrifty Food Plan, with adjustments for household size and for specified geographic areas. Countable net income then reduces the maximum. Under the formula established by the Food Stamp Act of 1977, the allotment equaled the cost of the Thrifty Food Plan for the household size reduced by 30 percent of household net income, rounded to the nearest dollar, with a minimum allotment for small households. Deductions for items such as shelter and dependent care expenses enter the computation of net income before the percentage reduction applies, so identical gross incomes can produce different allotments.

### Q: What was the first food stamp program before the Food Stamp Act?

The first food stamp program operated from 1939 to 1943 as a surplus disposal and relief measure. The program began on May 16, 1939, in Rochester, New York, under Secretary of Agriculture Henry A. Wallace and administrator Milo Perkins of the Federal Surplus Commodities Corporation. Households on relief purchased orange stamps equal to ordinary food spending and received fifty cents of blue stamps without charge for each dollar purchased, with blue stamps restricted to foods designated as surplus. Peak participation reached about four million people, and total cost reached 262 million dollars. Wartime demand absorbed surpluses and reduced unemployment, and the program ended in the spring of 1943.

### Q: What roles did Sullivan and Aiken play in the Food Stamp Act?

Representative Leonor Sullivan of Missouri supplied the House championship of the statute across two decades of advocacy. Sullivan pressed food stamp legislation from the middle nineteen fifties onward and introduced the bill that became the Food Stamp Act of 1964, recorded as House Bill 10222 and considered through the House Committee on Agriculture. Senator George Aiken of Vermont supplied the Senate counterpart from within the Republican Party and the agriculture establishment. Aiken sponsored a national food allotment proposal in the middle nineteen forties that historians of food policy treated as a forerunner of the food stamp program, and Senate consideration ran through the Committee on Agriculture and Forestry.

### Q: What appropriations caps did the original Food Stamp Act set?

Section 16 of the Food Stamp Act of 1964 authorized appropriations of not more than 75 million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1965, not more than 100 million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1966, and not more than 200 million dollars for the fiscal year ending June 30, 1967. Appropriations for later fiscal years depended on sums Congress might authorize afterward. Charges collected from households entered a separate Treasury account available for redemption of coupons. Where the Secretary of Agriculture found that requirements of participating states would exceed the appropriated limitation, the Secretary held a duty to instruct state agencies to reduce coupon issuance for compliance.

### Q: What deadline did federal law set for electronic benefit transfer?

The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 required each state agency to implement an electronic benefit transfer system not later than October 1, 2002, unless the Secretary of Agriculture granted a waiver to a state agency facing unusual barriers to implementation. The Electronic Benefit Transfer Interoperability and Portability Act of 2000 directed that systems be interoperable and benefits portable among all states by the same date, under a national standard. A small number of states received waivers extending schedules beyond the deadline. Electronic benefit transfer operated nationwide in June 2004, and from that point all benefits were issued electronically rather than as paper coupons.

### Q: How are error rates and trafficking measured under the Food Stamp Act?

Error measurement rests on the quality control system, under which state agencies review samples of cases and the Food and Nutrition Service re-reviews a subsample and aggregates findings into a national payment error rate expressed as the share of benefit dollars issued in error, combining overpayments and underpayments. The rate measures accuracy regardless of intent and excludes fraud. Trafficking, the exchange of benefits for cash, receives separate measurement through dedicated studies. The Food and Nutrition Service study issued in August 2013 estimated trafficking at 858 million dollars annually between 2009 and 2011, about 1.3 percent of benefits. A break in the error series followed measurement disputes, with no national rate published for fiscal 2015 or fiscal 2016.

### Q: Why did the coalition behind the Food Stamp Act survive separation attempts?

The coalition survived because separation deprived each half of the farm bill of votes the other half supplied. The fullest test came in 2013, when the House passed a farm bill without a nutrition title and a separate nutrition bill without a Senate path, after defeat of a combined bill. Recombination followed within days, and the Agricultural Act of 2014 restored the combined structure with nutrition as Title Four of a twelve title farm bill. Farm, food, and conservation organizations opposed separation in large numbers. Every completed farm bill in the record carried farm and nutrition titles between the same covers, while attempts to legislate the halves apart failed or collapsed into combination.

### Q: How did eligibility standards change from state control to national control?

Under the Food Stamp Act of 1964, each state agency established eligibility standards within a federal framework, including maximum income limitations consistent with the income standards used for federally aided public assistance in that state and a limitation on resources, subject to approval by the Secretary of Agriculture. Identical households could face different outcomes in different states. Amendments enacted in 1971 introduced uniform standards and a purchase cap. The Food Stamp Act of 1977 completed the shift, directing uniform national standards of eligibility, precluding additional state standards as preconditions for participation, and setting the poverty guidelines as the basic standard, with net income capped at the poverty line.

### Q: Why was the Food Stamp Act given a new name?

The new name described function rather than instrument. Food stamps named a paper coupon, and electronic delivery had removed that instrument from ordinary use in 2004, four years before the renaming took effect in 2008. Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program described the activity of supplementing the food purchasing power of a household toward a more nutritious diet. Federal officials presented the name as a means of reducing stigma, recognizing electronic delivery, and directing attention toward nutrition. The renaming altered no eligibility rule or benefit formula by itself, and households experienced continuity of accounts, cards, and certification under the renamed statute, the Food and Nutrition Act of 2008.

### Q: What role do the agriculture committees play for the Food Stamp Act?

The agriculture committees of the two chambers hold authorizing jurisdiction over the statute and draft the nutrition title of each farm bill. Consideration of the Food Stamp Act of 1964 ran through the House Committee on Agriculture and the Senate Committee on Agriculture and Forestry, and successor committees retained the assignment through every reauthorization. Jurisdiction carries oversight as well as drafting authority. The House Committee on Agriculture conducted sixteen hearings over two years before issuing findings in December 2016 on errors, fraud, and work enforcement. Retention of the nutrition title also preserves committee influence over the largest share of farm bill spending, projected at eighty percent under the Agricultural Act of 2014.
