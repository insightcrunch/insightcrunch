A committee markup is the working session at which a congressional committee takes a measure apart and puts it back together, and the committee system that surrounds that session decides which measures receive one. Nearly every bill and resolution introduced in Congress is referred to a committee, and nearly every referred measure receives nothing further: no hearing, no markup, no vote, and no explanation. The measures that matter pass through a single scheduled event, the markup, at which members consider a prepared text, offer amendments, record votes, and decide whether the measure advances to the chamber. Between referral and that event stands one decision, taken by one officeholder, against which the rules of either chamber provide almost no recourse. The chair of the committee decides whether a markup is scheduled at all, and a measure the chair declines to schedule has, for practical purposes, been disposed of as completely as a measure voted down on the floor, except that no vote, no record, and no stated reason mark its end.

The standing committee system that gives the chair that power took its durable shape in the Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946, which consolidated dozens of overlapping panels into a smaller number of standing committees, assigned each committee a written jurisdiction, and made the committee the chamber's instrument for examining legislation in depth. The committee stage sits inside the longer route traced in [the full account of how a bill becomes law in practice](/2022/02/01/how-a-bill-becomes-law-in-practice/), and within that route the committee stage carries a distinctive weight. Hearings gather testimony, floor debate argues merits, and conference committees reconcile texts, but the markup is where the operative language of a bill is usually fixed, and the committee report that follows the markup is the document later interpreters, from agencies to courts, consult first when the enacted words require explanation. A reader who understands the markup, the gate that controls whether it happens, and the report it produces holds the key to the committee system, and a reader who also grasps how much significant legislation routes around the committee entirely holds the key to the system's limits.

## The System the Reorganization Act Built

The committee system that governed legislation in the middle of the twentieth century was not designed in a single moment. It accumulated. Across the nineteenth century and into the early twentieth century, committees multiplied as new subjects pressed for attention, as chambers created panels to handle particular streams of business, and as existing panels divided or persisted alongside their successors. A committee markup, the meeting at which members amend and vote on a measure, presupposes that a measure has a recognizable home, a chair with authority to schedule it, and members with enough concentration of assignment to master its subject. Committees overlapped in jurisdiction, duplicated one another in subject, and competed for the same legislation without a written map that settled, in advance and in public language, which panel owned which portion of the law. Gatekeeping, the power to advance a measure or to leave it unattended, rested in that environment less on a coherent division of labor than on accumulation, habit, and the position of individual chairs. The reform gave the committee system a defined shape, reduced its sprawl to a manageable number of standing panels, and wrote down the jurisdictions on which every later referral, hearing, and committee markup would depend.

### Why did Congress consolidate its committees?

Congress consolidated its committees because the pre-1946 system had grown unworkable: dozens of overlapping panels blurred accountability, consumed members in scattered assignments, and left legislation without a clear jurisdictional home. Consolidation created fewer committees, written jurisdictions, and a committee system capable of sustained oversight and orderly committee markup.

The sprawl had a logic of its own, and that logic explains why the sprawl endured for so long. A new committee created a new chairmanship, and a chairmanship carried perquisites that mattered inside the institution. Many committees were moribund, kept alive for the perquisites of their chairs. A moribund committee imposed costs that were easy to miss from outside the chamber. The committee occupied a jurisdictional claim, so a measure that touched the claim could be sent to a panel with no working capacity to consider it, or rival panels could assert competing claims that consumed time before any substantive question received attention. Each additional panel, active or dormant, also added to the assignments, meetings, and obligations that competed for a finite supply of attention. Abolition therefore required senior members to surrender positions that the institution had treated as rewards, and required the chamber to admit that a portion of its formal structure existed to serve the standing of officeholders rather than the consideration of legislation.

The burden on members made the same point from another direction. Before the Act, senators could hold up to ten committee assignments. Ten assignments did not mean ten fields mastered. Assignment at that scale meant divided preparation, conflicting meeting schedules, and a shallow acquaintance with subjects that demanded sustained attention. A senator spread across ten panels could attend fragments of many proceedings and take responsibility for few. Specialization is the central justification for a committee system. A chamber of generalists divides labor so that smaller groups can develop knowledge, examine witnesses and agencies over repeated encounters, and bring a considered measure to the floor. When assignments multiply beyond the capacity of members to perform that work, specialization becomes nominal. Consolidation addressed the roster and the schedule together, by reducing the number of standing committees to a figure that allowed assignments to carry real expectations of preparation, attendance, and accountability within a defined subject.

Overlapping jurisdiction compounded both problems. Subjects in public law do not arrive in sealed compartments, and panels created at different times for related purposes naturally reached into neighboring ground. Without defined jurisdictions in writing, overlap had no authoritative boundary against which a referral could be tested. Two committees could claim the same measure on plausible grounds, and neither claim could be resolved by pointing to a text that the chamber had adopted for that purpose. Jurisdictional ambiguity therefore affected more than administrative tidiness. Ambiguity affected which members and which chair exercised gatekeeping power over a proposal, which body of precedent and expertise would shape the text, and which interests and agencies would receive the closest hearing during consideration. The same bill text, sent to different committees, could emerge with different amendments, different emphases, and a different coalition prepared to defend the result on the floor.

The institutional answer began with study rather than with a floor vote improvised under pressure. The Joint Committee on the Organization of Congress, known as the La Follette-Monroney committee, supplied the vehicle through which the two chambers examined their own organization together. A joint inquiry mattered because the problem crossed chamber lines, even though the House and the Senate maintained separate committee structures and separate rules. Each chamber had accumulated panels, overlapping claims, and assignment burdens, and a reform confined to a single chamber would have left the legislative process passing measures between systems built on different assumptions. The joint committee format also gave the inquiry a standing distinct from the standing committees whose futures were at issue, panels poorly placed to design consolidation impartially since abolition threatened the chairs whose positions were at stake.

The Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946 converted that inquiry into law. The Act, Public Law 79-601, was signed on August 2, 1946 by President Truman, and the committee provisions took effect on January 2, 1947, with the Eightieth Congress. Congress did not treat committee reorganization as a housekeeping resolution to be adjusted informally from session to session. Congress enacted a statute, carried through the full legislative process and signed by the President, that restructured the internal division of labor through which all later statutes would pass. The written jurisdictions established under the Act became the lineage of the jurisdiction lists that the House carried in Rule X and the Senate carried in Rule XXV. The Act supplied the decisive break with the older sprawl, and the chamber rules carried the resulting map forward into the daily business of referral and committee markup.

The consolidation numbers state the scale of the break with unusual clarity. In the House, standing committees fell from 48 to 19. In the Senate, standing committees fell from 33 to 15. The Congressional Research Service and the standard counts associated with Riddick preserve those figures as the accepted measure of the reform, and reduction alone was not the purpose. A chamber could abolish panels and merely transfer confusion to the survivors if the survivors inherited undefined or conflicting territory. The significance of the move from forty-eight to nineteen, and from thirty-three to fifteen, lay in the pairing of reduction with definition. Fewer committees meant that each surviving committee held a broader and more consequential jurisdiction, that each chairmanship corresponded to a working field of legislation rather than to a preserved title, and that each member served on panels whose claims could be stated, compared, and enforced against a written standard. With nineteen House committees and fifteen Senate committees, a measure that received no consideration had a more identifiable custodian.

The Act defined each committee jurisdiction in writing, and that act of definition was the load-bearing feature of the entire structure. The written text tells the presiding officer and the parliamentarian where a measure belongs on referral. The written text tells members where to seek assignment if a subject draws their interest, and tells a chair whether a proposal falls inside the panel authority to examine. The written text tells agencies and witnesses which committee conducts oversight of a program, so that questions about administration have a destination and a committee record can accumulate around a stable subject. Most importantly, the written text converts jurisdiction from a claim asserted by a chair into a rule adopted by the chamber. A claim can be pressed with variable force depending on the influence of the claimant. A rule can be read, cited, and applied by officers charged with neutrality, and a disputed application can be argued against language that both sides accept as authoritative. Referral mechanics lean on the text because referral is an act of matching measure language to jurisdiction language. Oversight leans on the text because a committee asserting supervision over a program must ground the assertion in assigned authority. Committee markup leans on the text because the power to amend and report a measure follows from the referral that the jurisdiction made defensible.

Professional staffing gave the written map a workforce capable of using it. The Act authorized four professional staff members per standing committee. Four reads as a modest figure in isolation, but the authorization established a principle that altered the capacity of committees to perform the work that consolidation assigned to them. A committee with a broader jurisdiction and an expectation of continuous oversight required assistance that did not depend entirely on the personal staff of the chair or on information supplied by the executive agencies under examination. Professional staff could maintain files across sessions, prepare for hearings, compare statutory language, and support members during a committee markup in which amendments turned on precise wording. Staff continuity also served institutional memory, preserving knowledge of prior compromises, prior agency assurances, and prior statutory failures within the jurisdiction. The authorization therefore complemented the jurisdictional settlement. Definition told a committee what belonged to the committee. Staffing gave the committee a means, independent in part from the executive branch, to act on that understanding with drafted language and assembled evidence.

The Act also expanded the Legislative Reference Service, the forerunner of the Congressional Research Service. A legislature that consolidates committees and concentrates jurisdiction raises the cost of uninformed action, because each surviving committee speaks for a larger field and each reported measure carries the authority of a more focused examination. An expanded reference service gave members and committees a source of research that belonged to the legislative branch itself. Committee staff served the particular panel and its pending business, with knowledge organized around a defined jurisdiction. The Legislative Reference Service served Congress more broadly, supplying research that a single committee, pressed by a hearing schedule or a markup deadline, could not always generate internally. Congress reduced the number of panels, defined the territory of the panels that remained, and expanded the research capacity through which members could govern that territory without relying solely on the agencies and interests that appeared before the committees as witnesses and petitioners.

One title of the Act pointed beyond internal organization toward the regulation of those who sought to influence legislation from outside the chamber. The Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act formed Title III of the 1946 Act, establishing the predecessor regime for lobbying disclosure that a separate article in the series treats in full.

The consequences of the written jurisdictional map reached far beyond the internal convenience of the two chambers, because the map decided where subjects lived. Legislation follows jurisdiction, and jurisdiction shapes legislation in ways that begin before any hearing is called. The committee that receives a measure controls the first sustained examination of the subject, selects the witnesses who frame the problem, and prepares the text that reaches the floor. A subject assigned to one committee acquires the vocabulary, the priorities, and the neighboring statutes of the field that committee governs. The same subject, assigned elsewhere, would be framed against a different body of law and defended by a different coalition. The jurisdictional map therefore operated as a quiet determinant of policy design. Floor majorities retained the final vote, but the committee of referral set the starting text, the evidentiary record, and the range of amendments that appeared natural within the subject as the committee understood it. Referral is classification, and classification in a legislature is a form of power.

Nutrition assistance supplies the first worked example of a subject living where the map placed it. Legislation on nutrition assistance is written by the agriculture committees, and the nutrition programs sit inside farm bills. That placement can appear anomalous when nutrition is viewed only as a matter of household income or public health. The placement becomes intelligible once jurisdiction is treated as the governing fact. The agriculture committees held the assigned territory that encompassed the programs, and the farm bill became the vehicle through which nutrition assistance was written, renewed, and amended. Placement inside the farm bill then shaped the politics and the drafting of the programs. Nutrition provisions moved on a vehicle whose other titles concerned the agricultural economy, so the coalition that carried the vehicle had to accommodate both sets of interests within a single measure. Amendments were considered in the committee room of the agriculture committees, under the procedures and precedents of those panels, and a committee markup on a farm bill could join questions of farm support and nutrition support in one text that neither subject would have generated alone. The example demonstrates why jurisdiction cannot be dismissed as filing. Filing decides the room, the room decides the first draft, and the first draft frames every later choice.

The Montgomery GI Bill supplies the second example, in which a benefit associated with military service reached enactment through a defense vehicle rather than through a freestanding measure in the field suggested by the benefit alone. The Montgomery GI Bill for service members was enacted as Title VII of the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, Public Law 98-525. The Congressional Research Service veterans education primers record that placement, and the placement illustrates the same jurisdictional principle that the nutrition example illustrates, with a different configuration of committees and vehicles. Education benefits for service members touch service and the obligations that follow military duty, and the measure that carried the benefit was a defense authorization, a recurring vehicle built to move the business of the armed forces through Congress on a regular cycle. Attachment to such a vehicle gave the benefit a legislative path with its own momentum and its own place in a larger text. A freestanding veterans bill would have presented the same subject through a different jurisdictional door, with different timing and different companions inside the measure. The enacted route fixed the benefit in law as Title VII of a defense authorization, and that formal location preserved, in the statute itself, the imprint of the jurisdictional map that had routed the proposal.

Read together, the two examples clarify what the 1946 settlement changed and what the settlement left to daily practice. In each example, the identity of the writing committee and the identity of the carrying vehicle followed from assigned jurisdiction, and the consequences were substantive rather than ceremonial. Nutrition assistance acquired the legislative rhythm of the farm bill. A service member education benefit acquired the legislative form of a title inside a defense authorization. Neither result can be explained by the merits of the underlying subject considered in isolation, because merit alone does not select a committee. Jurisdiction selects the committee, and the committee system that the 1946 Act built made that selection governable by written rules rather than by the unstructured claims of a sprawling panel structure. The examples also expose the limits of any map, however carefully drawn. Nutrition assistance genuinely concerns agriculture, income support, and health at the same time. Education benefits for service members genuinely concern defense manpower, veterans policy, and education at the same time. A jurisdiction line drawn through such subjects will always leave a portion of the subject on the far side of the line, in territory assigned to another committee with a legitimate claim to examine that portion.

That remainder is the point on which the section closes, because consolidation could settle the number of committees and could write their jurisdictions, but consolidation could not settle the underlying fact that subjects overlap. Overlap under the 1946 system was not a defect introduced by careless drafting of the jurisdiction lists. Overlap arose by design, in the sense that a finite set of committees, each holding a broad field, will inevitably meet neighboring committees at boundaries where real subjects cross. Agriculture meets nutrition and health. Defense meets veterans and education. A map with no overlaps would require subjects that never cross, and legislation confronts subjects that cross constantly. The achievement of 1946 was therefore more precise than the elimination of conflict. Congress replaced an accumulation of duplicative and moribund panels with a defined set of committees, replaced unwritten claims with written jurisdictions, and supplied staff and research support for the new structure. The boundary disputes that remained were disputes inside a system, arguable against a text, rather than contests among an indefinite number of panels with indefinite claims. Managing those disputes fell to the mechanics of referral, to the officers and precedents that match measures to jurisdictions when a measure touches more than one field. The next section takes up those mechanics, where the written map meets the individual bill and gatekeeping acquires a procedure.

## Referral: Where a Measure Is Sent and Who Sends It

Referral is the first consequential assignment a measure receives after introduction, and in the committee system the assignment often matters as much as the text that receives it. Before any hearing is scheduled, before any committee markup is contemplated, and before any amendment is drafted for consideration in committee, a measure must be placed somewhere, and the placement determines which members examine the proposal, which staff develop expertise in its details, and which chair acquires authority over its progress. A measure sent to one committee enters one institutional world, with one set of precedents, priorities, and competing demands on the calendar. The same measure sent to another committee enters a different world, even though the words on the page are identical. Referral therefore functions as a decision about jurisdiction in the formal sense and as a decision about gatekeeping in the practical sense, because a committee that never receives a measure cannot report it, and a chair who never receives a measure cannot schedule it, amend it, or hold it. The textbook sequence of introduction, referral, hearing, markup, and report begins with a step that appears administrative, and the appearance misleads. Referral is announced without debate in ordinary cases, yet the routine character of the announcement conceals the weight of a choice that allocates the measure and, with the measure, the chamber's first judgment on it.

In the House of Representatives, the formal authority for referral rests with the Speaker under Rule XII, clause 2, which provides that the Speaker refers measures to committee. The rule places the decision in the office charged with presiding over the chamber, organizing its business, and giving effect to its rules. Referral is not assigned to the member who introduced the measure, to the committees that claim the measure, or to the chamber acting by vote in each individual case. The House instead centralized the function in the Speaker, subject to the jurisdiction listings of Rule X and to accumulated precedent, so that a single office bears responsibility for matching measures to committees in a consistent manner across thousands of introductions in a Congress. The rule avoids a contest over destination by treating referral as an act of the presiding authority, governed by text and precedent rather than by bargaining on the floor, and the institution accepts the assignment as the starting point for committee work unless a correction is made through procedures the House recognizes.

The Speaker does not, in ordinary practice, read each introduced measure and personally weigh its provisions against the jurisdiction of every standing committee. That day to day work falls to the House Parliamentarian, who makes referral decisions as the Speaker's nonpartisan agent. The arrangement joins formal authority to expert practice. The Speaker retains the authority that Rule XII, clause 2 confers, and the Parliamentarian supplies the detailed knowledge of rule text, precedent, and prior referrals that a sound decision requires. The nonpartisan character of the role is central to its acceptance. The Parliamentarian serves the House as an institution rather than a party or a faction, and referral recommendations grounded in that service carry a legitimacy that a partisan allocation would struggle to sustain. The Parliamentarian's involvement also explains why referral decisions display continuity across changes in party control. Speakers change, majorities change, and committee chairs change, but the office that advises on referral persists, and the body of precedent the office applies persists with it, so each new introduction is matched against the destinations assigned to comparable measures in earlier Congresses.

The text against which each measure is matched is Rule X, clause 1, which lists the jurisdiction of each standing committee in the House. Those listings define, in written form, the subject matter assigned to each panel, and the lineage of the listings runs back to the Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946, which defined committee jurisdiction in writing as part of a broader consolidation of the committee structure. A referral decision therefore begins with reading. The provisions of a measure are examined, the subjects they touch are identified, and the jurisdictional listings are consulted to determine which committee's written assignment encompasses those subjects. The difficult cases arise when a measure reaches across subjects, when a provision falls near a boundary between two listings, or when the drafting of a measure emphasizes one subject while its operative effects fall within another. In such cases precedent supplements the text, because the listings cannot anticipate every combination of provisions that sponsors may assemble. The consequences of the written listings extend beyond referral mechanics. Because nutrition programs sit inside farm bills, nutrition assistance legislation is written by the agriculture committees, and the assignment shapes the coalition that considers the legislation and the vehicle that carries it. Because the Montgomery GI Bill for service members was enacted as Title VII of the Department of Defense Authorization Act, 1985, a defense authorization measure, an education benefit for veterans moved through a defense channel rather than as a freestanding veterans bill. Placement governs authorship, and authorship governs content in ways that a later floor vote cannot easily undo.

The Senate follows a parallel route that reaches a comparable destination through a different presiding figure. Under Senate Rule XVII, referral in the Senate runs through the presiding officer, acting on parliamentarian advice, and measures are referred to the committees whose jurisdiction, as set out in the Senate's own listings under Rule XXV, encompasses their subject matter. The parallel matters for any measure that must pass both chambers, because a proposal may face a straightforward referral in one chamber and a contested jurisdictional question in the other, and the committees that shape the House and Senate versions of a measure may differ even when the introduced texts are identical. A presiding officer, advised by a nonpartisan parliamentarian, applies written jurisdiction and accumulated precedent to assign a measure to a committee, and the assignment opens or closes the path to hearing, markup, and report. In both chambers, referral rests on formal authority in a presiding office, expert nonpartisan advice in a parliamentarian, and a written jurisdictional code interpreted through precedent. For measures at jurisdictional boundaries, those supports frame a decision with genuine distributive consequences, because two committees may each present a plausible claim, and only the referral determines which claim the institution honors first.

Precedent in referral accumulates through several channels, and the channels explain both the stability of the practice and the occasional surprises it produces. The most familiar channel is the referral history of similar measures. When a measure resembles an earlier proposal in subject and structure, the destination of the earlier proposal guides the destination of the later one, and a line of comparable referrals hardens into an expectation that sponsors, committees, and staff treat as settled. A second channel is statutory change. When Congress enacts legislation that creates a program, moves a function between agencies, or redraws the boundary between two policy domains, the statute alters the landscape to which the jurisdiction listings apply, and referrals after the enactment reflect the new landscape. A committee may gain a stream of referrals because a statute placed a growing program within its listing, or lose a stream because a function migrated elsewhere, and the precedent that develops after the change supersedes expectations formed before it. A third channel is the correction, or the absence of a correction, after an initial assignment, because the institution's willingness to let an uncorrected decision stand gives even an imperfect referral a lasting effect on the jurisdictional map.

The starkest illustration of precedent hardening into jurisdiction comes from the treatment of error. The Congressional Research Service, in its manual on House committee markups, states the rule plainly: an erroneous referral of a public bill that is never corrected confers jurisdiction. The proposition deserves emphasis because it reverses the assumption that a mistake in referral remains a nullity until corrected. Under the stated rule, the referral itself, once made and left uncorrected, becomes the source of the receiving committee's authority over the measure, and the committee proceeds on the strength of the assignment the House allowed to stand. The precedent also reaches beyond the single measure, because a referral left uncorrected becomes part of the history that the Parliamentarian consults when a comparable measure appears, and a single error, repeated through deference to precedent, can shift the practical boundary between two committees. The rule therefore places a burden of vigilance on every participant with an interest in a measure's destination. A chair who believes a measure belongs elsewhere, a sponsor who expected a different assignment, and a committee that believes a measure was wrongly withheld each bear responsibility for seeking correction promptly, because silence converts the initial decision into conferring jurisdiction. Error, in referral practice, is not self correcting. The institution corrects an erroneous referral when a participant raises the error and the House acts, and the institution ratifies an erroneous referral, in effect, when no participant does so.

### Can a bill be sent to more than one committee?

A measure may be referred to more than one House committee through split referral or sequential referral, and the Speaker designates a primary committee and may impose time limits. In the Senate such multiple referral occurs only by joint leader motion or unanimous consent and remains rare in practice.

Multiple referral developed as the House's answer to a problem that the written jurisdiction listings created even as the listings solved an older problem of chaos in committee assignments. Committees with defined jurisdictions brought order, but legislation refused to respect the definitions. A measure addressing energy production might also address taxation, environmental protection, public lands, and interstate commerce, and each subject fell within a different committee's listing. Under a regime of single referral, referral to one committee alone left subjects within other committees' jurisdiction to be legislated on by a panel without the corresponding assignment, breeding resentment and jurisdictional protest. Negotiation among the chairs of the claiming committees offered another path, but negotiation made allocation depend on the bargaining strength and personal relations of individual chairs, produced outcomes that varied from measure to measure, and consumed the time of the members charged with managing substantive committee work. Multiple referral resolved the overlap by procedure rather than by negotiation among chairs. Instead of forcing a choice between committees or a bargain among them, the procedure allowed the institution to honor more than one jurisdictional claim in an ordered fashion, with the Speaker setting the terms on which each committee considered the measure.

The authorization for the practice in the House came with the Committee Reform Amendments of 1974, adopted as H.Res. 988 in the 93rd Congress and effective on January 3, 1975, at the opening of the 94th Congress. The amendments empowered the Speaker to refer a measure to more than one committee, and the Speaker could impose time limits on sequential referrals, so that recognition of multiple claims did not become a license for indefinite delay. Three forms comprised the original design, and each answered a different pattern of overlap. Joint referral sent a measure to two or more committees at the same time, so that each committee considered the measure at the same time within its own jurisdiction, a form suited to proposals whose parts were intertwined enough that separating them would distort the whole. Split referral divided a measure by designated portions, assigning particular provisions or sections to different committees, so that each committee considered the portion falling within its listing while no committee received the entirety of a text that reached beyond its assignment. Sequential referral sent a measure to one committee first and then to another committee after the first committee completed its consideration, so that the second committee examined a text already shaped by the first committee's markup, a form suited to cases in which the order of consideration mattered and one committee's judgment formed the necessary foundation for another's.

The rules governing multiple referral changed in 1995, when the 104th Congress revised the House rules as part of a broader reorganization of the committee system. Joint referrals were abolished, ending the form under which two or more committees held a measure at the same time without a designated order of primacy. In place of joint referral, the revised rules required the Speaker, when multiply referring a measure, to designate a committee of primary jurisdiction, so that one committee bore principal responsibility for the measure even when other committees received it in split or sequential form. Split and sequential referrals were retained, and time limits remained imposable, preserving the guard against delay that the 1974 design had attached to sequential consideration. The 1995 change expressed a judgment about accountability. Designation of a primary committee restored a single point of responsibility, and the primary committee's chair became the member answerable for the measure's progress while other committees contributed within the terms the Speaker set. A further adjustment followed in 2003, when the 108th Congress authorized the Speaker to refer a measure without designating a primary committee under exceptional circumstances. The exception preserved the 1995 default while restoring flexibility for cases in which designation would misrepresent the distribution of jurisdiction, and the requirement that circumstances be exceptional marked the exception as a departure from the rule rather than a replacement of it.

The Senate's practice stands in marked contrast. Multiple referral in the Senate occurs only by joint motion of the majority and minority leaders or by unanimous consent, and the practice is rare. No presiding officer in the Senate holds a standing authority comparable to the Speaker's authority to multiply refer as a matter of routine discretion under defined forms. Instead, a multiple referral requires an affirmative act of accommodation between the two party leaders or the absence of objection from every senator, thresholds that reserve the device for measures on which a broad consensus supports shared consideration. The rarity follows from the thresholds. A joint motion demands agreement between leaders who often disagree about the handling of major measures, and unanimous consent exposes the proposal to a single objection, so most measures follow the ordinary path of referral to a single committee under Rule XVII. Where the House built a procedural system with forms, designations, and time limits, the Senate kept an exceptional device gated by leadership agreement or universal acquiescence.

Referral decides which chair acquires the gate. A committee chair controls whether a referred measure receives a hearing, whether a markup is scheduled, what text serves as the vehicle for amendment, and whether a motion to report is ever put to the committee, and no rule of either chamber requires a committee to hold a hearing or a markup on a measure referred to it. The powers of the chair therefore attach at the moment of referral, because assignment of a measure to a committee is simultaneously assignment of the measure to the discretion of that committee's chair. A sponsor whose measure lands before a sympathetic chair gains a path, a sponsor whose measure lands before an opposed chair confronts a closed gate, and a sponsor whose measure is divided or sequenced among committees confronts several gates in an order the Speaker has set. Multiple referral modifies the geometry of the gate without removing it, because designation of a primary committee identifies the principal gatekeeper, a time limit constrains the gatekeeper's ability to hold a measure indefinitely, and sequential referral arranges the gates so that passage through one becomes the condition of reaching the next. Even the erroneous referral rule expresses the same linkage, since an uncorrected error confers jurisdiction and with jurisdiction confers the gate. The referral decision and the gatekeeping decision are thus linked from the first day of a measure's life in committee, and sponsors who attend to drafting, coalition building, and floor strategy while neglecting referral neglect the assignment on which the committee markup, the committee report, and the measure's survival all depend.

## Inside the Markup: Where the Operative Text Is Set

A committee markup is the business session at which a committee works on the text of a measure referred to it, considers amendments, and decides whether to order the measure reported to the full chamber. The committee markup sits after the hearing stage of the process, and the placement matters. Hearings gather testimony, build a record of argument and evidence, and expose the points on which members, witnesses, and affected interests disagree. A markup performs a different kind of work. The markup converts argument into language. Decisions taken at a markup determine the words that the chamber later debates, the words that enter a statute if the measure becomes law, and the words against which agencies and courts later measure conduct. For that reason, the markup deserves treatment as a distinct institution rather than as a routine meeting attached to a hearing schedule. The Congressional Research Service, in the manual prepared on House committee markups, treats the session as the point at which committee consideration becomes formal action on a text.

The timing of a markup belongs to the chair. No House or Senate rule requires a committee to hold a hearing or a markup on a referred measure, and no rule compels a chair to schedule either proceeding. That absence of compulsion gives the markup a double character. When a chair schedules a markup, the scheduling decision signals that a measure has survived referral and has acquired enough support, enough preparation, or enough urgency within the committee to justify formal textual work. When a chair declines to schedule a markup, the measure ordinarily remains in committee without a vote, without a report, and without a route to the floor through the ordinary committee process. Gatekeeping and drafting therefore meet in a single room. The same authority that keeps a measure off the agenda also controls the session at which a favored measure receives its operative wording, and an account of markups that separates the drafting function from the gatekeeping function misses the reason the session carries so much weight in the committee system.

A compressed view of the sequence appears in **The markup pathway table**, which traces a measure from referral through the vote to report and onward to floor scheduling.

| Stage | What happens | Who controls it | Document it produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| Referral | Measure sent to committee of jurisdiction | Speaker on parliamentarian advice | Referral record |
| Chairman's mark | Draft text placed before committee for amendment | Chair, through committee staff | Chairman's mark |
| Amendment | Members offer, debate, and vote on changes | Committee members under committee rules | Recorded amendment votes |
| Vote to report | Committee orders measure reported to chamber | Majority of committee present | Motion to report record |
| Committee report | Formal explanation and vote record filed | Committee majority, with minority views | Committee report |
| Floor scheduling | Reported measure awaits chamber action | Chamber leadership and rules process | Calendar placement |

The most consequential misunderstanding about a markup concerns the text on the table. A common account imagines members seated around a printed copy of the bill as introduced, moving through the sponsor's original language line by line and adjusting a word or a clause at a time. The Congressional Research Service account of House markups describes a different starting point. The text before the committee is usually the chairman's mark, a draft prepared under the chair's direction, or an amendment in the nature of a substitute offered at the markup, rather than the bill as introduced. That single fact corrects the most common misconception about the process. Introduction records a sponsor's opening proposal. A markup records the committee's negotiated proposal, and the distance between the two texts measures the work that committee consideration performed between referral and reporting. A reader who compares a final statute against the introduced bill alone misses the stage at which the operative language was actually chosen.

An amendment in the nature of a substitute sharpens the point. A substitute of that kind replaces the entire text after the enacting clause with a new text, so the committee's deliberation proceeds on the substitute as the base document. Amendments offered during the session then amend the substitute, and votes on those amendments settle the contents of the substitute before the committee decides whether to report the measure as amended. The device sounds technical, and the technical quality conceals a political reality. By the time a substitute reaches the table, its language already reflects decisions about scope, cost, coverage, enforcement, and compromise that the introduced bill may have left open or resolved in a manner the committee majority rejected. The markup therefore rarely begins from a blank page or from an untouched sponsor's draft. The markup begins from a prepared text that embodies a settlement, and the public session tests whether that settlement holds when other members of the committee offer alternatives in the open.

### What text does a committee actually amend?

A chairman's mark is a draft measure prepared under the chair's direction and placed before the committee as the text for amendment at a committee markup. The draft ordinarily reflects staff negotiation and administration or agency input gathered before the session, rather than the wording of the bill as introduced.

The drafting of a chairman's mark concentrates a great deal of institutional labor into a document that carries only the chair's name. Committee staff working under the chair's direction prepare the draft, and preparation involves far more than transcription. Staff negotiation precedes the session, often over an extended period of exchanges among the offices and interests engaged with the measure, and administration or agency input enters the draft as well. A provision in the mark may reflect a compromise between competing policy positions, a technical correction supplied by an agency charged with administering the program, a cost concern raised during staff discussions, or a judgment by the chair about the language most likely to command a majority of the committee. The mark gathers those judgments into one coherent text. Without a single base text, a committee of dozens of members would face the introduced bill, a stack of individual amendments, and no orderly sequence for reconciling them. The mark supplies the order. It gives every member the same starting language, the same section structure, and the same set of choices to accept, reject, or attempt to amend.

The negotiation embodied in a chairman's mark also explains why marks attract scrutiny disproportionate to their formal status. A mark has no legal force when the session opens. The mark binds no member, decides no question, and amends no statute. Influence flows from position rather than from formal authority, because the base text enjoys the advantage that attaches to any starting proposal in a collective decision. An amendment must displace language already written, must attract a majority in the room, and must survive the argument that the prepared text represents a considered balance among competing demands. Language left out of the mark faces the opposite burden, since a member seeking inclusion must draft the addition, defend the addition in debate, and win a vote. Observers who read only the introduced bill and the reported bill therefore overlook the intermediate document that structured the choice between them. The mark is the hinge document of the committee stage, invisible in the statute book and decisive in the process that produced the statute.

The sequence of a markup follows a settled pattern in both chambers, with variations governed by committee rules and by the practice of each panel. The chair opens the session, states the business before the committee, and places the measure under consideration. The committee then takes up the text for amendment. In the House practice described by the Congressional Research Service, the bill is ordinarily read for amendment by section, which means the reading clerk or the chair proceeds through the measure section by section and amendments are in order for each portion as the reading reaches that portion. Reading by section imposes a discipline on the session. A member holding an amendment to a later section waits until the reading arrives there, debate stays tied to the language immediately before the committee, and the committee resolves questions in the order the statute itself presents them. The method suits a measure whose sections build on one another, since definitions settled early govern the meaning of operative provisions considered afterward, and an early vote can foreclose arguments that a later amendment assumed remained open.

Committee practice also permits a different arrangement under which the measure is opened to amendment at any point. Under that arrangement, obtained by unanimous consent or permitted by committee practice, a member may offer an amendment to any section without waiting for a sequential reading to reach the relevant passage. Opening the measure at any point trades order for flexibility. A committee facing a small number of contested provisions can move directly to the disputes that matter, package related changes that touch distant sections, and avoid prolonged readings of language no member intends to challenge. The flexibility carries a cost in coherence, because an amendment adopted early may alter assumptions embedded in sections debated later, and members must track consequences across the whole text rather than within a single section at a time. The choice between the two arrangements therefore reflects a judgment about the measure and the committee. A settled text with scattered disputes fits the open arrangement, while a long or intricate measure benefits from the enforced sequence of a section by section reading.

Amendments give the markup its substance. A member offers an amendment to the base text, debate proceeds under the rules of the committee, and the committee votes on whether the proposed change enters the text. Debate in committee differs from debate on the floor in scale and in function. The room is smaller, the participants hold the committee's accumulated familiarity with the subject, and argument can engage drafting detail that floor debate, conducted under severe time limits before the whole chamber, rarely sustains. A debate over a definition, a deadline, an authorization figure, or an enforcement mechanism may consume substantial time precisely because the participants understand that the chosen words govern program administration after enactment. Votes on amendments are recorded, and the record matters beyond the room. The vote records from a markup, including votes on amendments and on the motion to report, became part of the committee report under the reforms adopted for the 104th Congress, so a member's position on a contested amendment entered the formal explanation that accompanied the measure to the chamber.

Recorded amendment votes also discipline the negotiation that produced the base text. A chairman's mark drafted in the expectation of unrecorded acquiescence might tilt further toward the preferences of the drafters, while a mark drafted in the expectation of recorded votes must anticipate the public positions of members whose support the reporting majority requires. Members, in turn, use amendment votes to establish positions for the chamber, for constituents, and for the later interpretive record. The markup thus generates two products at once. The amended text states the committee's recommendation, and the sequence of recorded votes maps the choices the committee confronted on the route to that recommendation.

Quorum law governs the validity of the committee's formal action, and the law draws a sharp line between ordinary business and the act of reporting. Under House Rule XI, clause 2(h), a majority of the committee constitutes a quorum for reporting a measure. Committees may set lower quorums, at not less than one third of the committee, for other business, and the contrast between the two thresholds expresses a deliberate institutional judgment. A committee may hear testimony, conduct discussion, and manage routine matters with a fraction of the membership present, because those activities inform the committee without committing the chamber. Reporting a measure commits the committee's name and recommendation to the chamber, and the House rules require the presence of a majority before that commitment can occur. The threshold also protects absent members and the minority against a small faction using a thinly attended session to send a measure to the floor in the committee's name. Presence, rather than preference alone, becomes a condition of legitimate gatekeeping at the moment the gate opens.

The history of the reporting quorum in the House includes a practice that reformers abolished in 1995. Rolling quorums allowed a committee to assemble the necessary presence cumulatively rather than at a single moment, a device that weakened the connection between deliberation and decision by permitting a vote to stand on attendance gathered across a session. The rules package for the 104th Congress, H.Res. 6, adopted on January 4, 1995, eliminated rolling quorums and preserved the majority requirement for reporting. The same reform preserved a point of order against a measure reported without a majority present, which converts the quorum rule from an internal expectation into an enforceable condition that a member may invoke on the floor. Enforcement through a point of order gives the rule practical teeth. A committee that reports a measure without the required presence exposes the measure itself to challenge, so the chair and the committee staff treat the count of members present at the reporting vote as a matter of legal consequence rather than of courtesy or custom.

Senate practice imposes the reporting quorum in a formulation that emphasizes physical presence. Senate Rule XXVI, paragraph 7(a)(1), provides that no measure shall be reported unless a majority of the committee was physically present. The word physically does work in the sentence. Presence in the Senate formulation means bodily attendance at the proceeding, a standard that answers the same concern that produced the House prohibition on rolling quorums. A majority assembled in the room hears the final debate, confronts the final text, and casts the votes that send the measure forward. The two chambers arrived at the standard through different rule texts, one framed as the constitution of a quorum for reporting and the other framed as a prohibition on reporting without a physically present majority, and the convergence reveals a shared premise. A committee speaks for a chamber of hundreds only through procedures that guarantee the committee itself acted as a collective body at the decisive moment. Proxy arrangements, accumulated attendance, and informal assent may serve other purposes in committee administration, and the rules draw the boundary at reporting because reporting is the act that moves a measure from deliberation toward enactment.

The decisive moment arrives with the vote to order the measure reported. Amendment votes settle the contents of the text, and the motion to report settles the committee's judgment on the text as a whole. A member may support several amendments, oppose others, and separately face an independent question at the end of the session, namely whether the amended measure deserves the committee's recommendation to the chamber. The vote on ordering the measure reported is the committee's formal act, the point at which deliberation becomes an official recommendation carrying the committee's name. Favorable action sends the measure forward to the report stage and toward floor scheduling. Unfavorable action, or the absence of a majority for reporting, leaves the measure in committee despite the hours of amendment debate that preceded the vote. The distinction explains why vote counts at a markup require careful reading. A lopsided tally on an amendment signals agreement on one provision, while the tally on the motion to report measures the committee's collective endorsement, and conflating the two tallies produces a distorted picture of the support a measure carries onto the floor.

Dissent inside the committee receives a formal channel rather than a merely rhetorical one. House Rule XI, clause 2(l), entitles a member who gives notice during the markup to file supplemental, minority, or dissenting views for inclusion in the committee report. The entitlement carries a specific time guarantee. A member who gives the required notice receives not less than two additional calendar days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays unless the House is in session, to file the views. The exclusion of weekends and holidays from the count protects the substance of the right, because a filing period measured in ordinary calendar days could expire across a weekend before a member and staff could draft a considered statement. House Rule XIII, clause 3(a), governs the inclusion of those views in the report, and the views are printed in the report alongside the majority's explanation. A reader of the report therefore encounters the committee's recommendation and the documented disagreement in a single document, bound together by rule rather than assembled later from scattered debate statements.

The views mechanism performs several functions within the markup process. A dissenting member gains a guaranteed place in the official record without needing to prevail in any vote, and the guarantee lowers the cost of disagreement by assuring that a losing argument at the markup can reach the chamber in a durable written form. Majority members gain as well, because the prospect of a written dissent appended to the report encourages the majority to draft its own explanation with care and to answer objections that the dissent will certainly raise. Floor debate benefits from the exchange, since members outside the committee can read the majority's section by section reasoning and the minority's critique before deciding a position. Later interpreters benefit too, because the printed views document the alternatives and objections that the committee record contains, even though the views speak for the filing members rather than for the committee as a body. The two day entitlement, modest in appearance, thus underwrites a meaningful share of the report's value as a complete record of the committee's deliberation rather than a bare announcement of the result.

Taken together, the elements of the markup explain why the session occupies the center of the committee system. The chair controls whether a markup occurs and supplies, through the chairman's mark, the text from which deliberation begins. Members test that text through amendments debated under committee rules and decided by recorded votes. Quorum law in both chambers reserves the act of reporting to a majority actually present, and the 1995 House reforms armed that requirement with a point of order and ended the rolling quorum practice that had diluted the standard. The vote to order the measure reported converts the amended text into the committee's formal recommendation, and the views entitlement under House Rule XI, clause 2(l), preserves disagreement inside the same report that announces the recommendation. A measure that emerges from a markup carries more than language. The measure carries a documented history of choices, a recorded measure of support, and a structured account of dissent, and those accompaniments distinguish a reported bill from a leadership draft or an unreported proposal at every later stage of the legislative process.

## The Committee Report and Its Second Life in Interpretation

The committee report is the document that carries a measure out of the committee room and into the chamber, and its first function is explanation. A committee markup ends with a decision to order a measure reported, but that decision alone tells the rest of the House or the Senate very little about what the committee did, why it acted, or how the text it approved would operate if enacted. The report supplies those answers in a single formal record. It presents the measure as the committee approved it, states the committee's reasons for approving it, and explains the measure provision by provision, so that a member who never sat in the markup can vote with an understanding of what the committee concluded and on what basis the conclusion rested. Because the report speaks for the committee as a body, rather than for any individual member, it carries an institutional authority that no single speech, statement, or exchange from the markup can match, and that authority explains both the care the chamber rules take over the report's contents and the weight later readers assign to the report after enactment.

House Rule XIII, clause 3 prescribes the contents of a House committee report in detail, and the prescribed contents map closely onto the questions a voting member, and later a reader of the enacted law, would ask. A report includes the text of the measure as reported, so that the document before the chamber is the committee's text and not the different text that may have been introduced months earlier. A report includes an explanation of the measure and a section-by-section analysis, in which the committee states the measure's purpose and need and then works through its provisions in order. A report includes the record of the votes cast in the markup, the Congressional Budget Office cost estimate where that estimate was timely submitted, oversight findings and performance-goal statements, and the comparative print required by the Ramseyer rule. Each element answers a distinct question. The text answers what the committee approved. The explanation answers why the committee approved it. The vote record answers how the committee divided. The cost estimate answers what the measure would cost. The oversight and performance statements answer how the committee connected the measure to its continuing responsibilities. The comparative print answers how the measure would change law that already existed.

The explanation and the section-by-section analysis form the heart of the report, because those portions translate the committee's internal deliberations into a public account that the chamber can rely on. An introduced bill arrives at a committee as a proposal, often drafted far from the committee and often revised substantially before reporting. The explanation records the committee's own understanding of the problem the measure addressed, the need the committee found for legislative action, and the effect the committee expected the measure to have. The section-by-section analysis then follows the structure of the reported text, describing in sequence what each title, section, or paragraph does. That order matters. A reader can place a sentence of explanation beside the provision it explains, compare the committee's description with the operative words, and see whether the description matches the text or reaches beyond it. Where the committee narrowed a provision during markup, broadened an exception, or rejected an amendment that would have taken the measure in a different direction, the analysis gives the chamber a disciplined place to record the choice in relation to the exact language chosen. The analysis therefore serves the vote in the chamber first, by reducing the risk that members approve language whose operation they have misunderstood, and it serves interpretation afterward, by preserving the explanation closest in time and in authorship to the text that became law.

The vote record entered House reports through the reforms of the 104th Congress, and House Rule XIII, clause 3 carries the requirement that reports record the votes cast in committee. Since the rules changes adopted in 1995, committee reports have been required to record the votes cast for and against amendments and the motion to report, with the names of the members casting those votes. The requirement changed the report from a purely explanatory document into a record of accountability as well. A member reading a report after 1995 could see not only that an amendment failed but also which members supported it and which members opposed it, and could see the division on the final decision to send the measure to the chamber. It also disciplines the explanation itself, because a committee majority writing a report that prints the minority's recorded strength on a contested amendment writes with the knowledge that readers can test the explanation against the division the report discloses. Supplemental, minority, and dissenting views, filed under the procedures of House Rule XI, clause 2(l) and included in the report under House Rule XIII, clause 3(a), extend that accountability further, giving members who gave notice during the markup a guaranteed place in the same document to state a contrary reading of the same text.

The cost estimate occupies a defined place in the report, and House Rule XIII, clause 3(c), operating under section 402 of the Congressional Budget Act, requires a committee report to include the estimate of the Congressional Budget Office where that estimate was timely submitted. The qualification matters, because it ties the requirement to the practical sequence of reporting rather than to an abstract demand that every report carry an estimate regardless of timing. Where the Budget Office submitted its estimate in time, the report carries an independent fiscal assessment alongside the committee's own explanation, prepared by an office that serves the Congress as a whole rather than the committee that reported the measure. The juxtaposition has value beyond the figures themselves, because a member can judge whether the committee's account of the measure's reach matches the fiscal footprint the Budget Office projected. For later readers, the estimate preserved in the report fixes the fiscal understanding under which the chamber acted, a record that would be far harder to reconstruct if estimates circulated separately from the document members had before them at the vote.

Oversight findings and performance-goal statements complete the prescribed contents, and their presence reflects a conception of the report as more than a vehicle for a single bill. The committee system rests on continuing jurisdiction, and a committee that reports legislation within its jurisdiction also carries responsibility for overseeing the laws and programs already inside that jurisdiction. The oversight statement connects the reported measure to that continuing work, recording the findings the committee drew from its oversight and the bearing those findings had on the legislation. The performance-goal statement performs a related function directed forward, stating goals against which the measure's results could later be measured. Together, the two statements resist a narrow reading of the report as advocacy for passage alone. The report instead presents the measure as one act within a continuing supervisory relationship between a committee and a field of federal activity, and it gives later oversight, conducted by the same committee or by a successor committee, a stated benchmark from the reporting committee itself.

### What does the Ramseyer rule require?

The Ramseyer rule requires a House committee report on a bill or joint resolution that would repeal or amend existing law to reprint the affected portion of that law and show, through struck text and italics, exactly how the statute would read if the measure were enacted.

The Ramseyer rule, carried in House Rule XIII, clause 3(e), was first established in 1929 and took its name from Representative Christian W. Ramseyer of Iowa. The rule addressed a recurring defect in legislative practice. Bills that amend existing law commonly do so by instruction, directing that a word be struck, a phrase be inserted, or a subsection be replaced, without printing the statute that the instruction alters. A member voting on such a bill from its text alone would have to reconstruct the underlying law from a separate volume, perform the amendment mentally, and then judge the result, a process that invited error precisely where precision mattered most. The Ramseyer rule replaced reconstruction with display. When a reported bill or joint resolution would repeal or amend existing law, the report must reprint the affected portion of that existing law and show, by typographical devices, how the law would read if the measure were enacted. The committee therefore reports not only its instructions for changing the law but also a picture of the law after the change, printed inside the same document that explains the measure to the chamber.

The typographical devices are simple, and their simplicity is the source of their usefulness. Text that the measure would strike from existing law appears as struck text, visibly cancelled yet legible, so that a reader can see exactly what would disappear. Text that the measure would insert appears in italics, set apart from the surrounding language that the measure would leave untouched, so that a reader can see exactly what would arrive. The unchanged remainder of the statute stands in ordinary type around those marks. A reader using the comparative print works through the affected portion in order, noting each deletion and each insertion in place, and finishes with a complete sense of the amended provision as a continuous piece of law rather than as a set of disconnected editing directions. The print also exposes choices that the bill's own language can conceal. An insertion that reads innocuously as a freestanding amendment may alter the reach of a sentence when placed inside it, and a deletion may remove a qualification on which neighboring language depended. By forcing the amended statute into print as a whole, the Ramseyer print lets members, staff, and later readers test the amendment in its actual setting before the chamber commits to it.

The print measures against the committee text when the committee reports amendments or a substitute, and that detail keeps the comparison honest to the decision the chamber is asked to make. Committees frequently report a measure in a form quite different from the introduced bill, whether through amendments adopted section by section or through an amendment in the nature of a substitute that replaces the introduced text altogether. A comparative print drawn against the introduced text would answer a question the chamber is no longer being asked, comparing existing law with a proposal the committee has already set aside. The rule instead anchors the print to the text the committee actually reported, so the struck text and italics display the distance between law in force and the law as the committee proposes to leave it. The discipline extends to the reading of the report as a whole. The section-by-section analysis explains the reported text, the vote record reflects votes on the reported text, and the Ramseyer print compares the reported text with existing law, so every element of the report converges on the same version of the measure, the version on which the chamber will vote.

The Senate maintains a counterpart to the Ramseyer rule in the Cordon rule, Senate Rule XXVI, paragraph 12, named for Senator Guy Cordon of Oregon. The Cordon rule imposes the same comparative print duty for Senate reports, requiring a Senate committee that reports a measure amending or repealing existing law to show the affected law and the changes the measure would make through the same kind of typographical display. The parallel requirement reflects a shared judgment in both chambers that an amending measure cannot be evaluated responsibly from its instructions alone. The Senate rule carries one qualification that the House rule lacks. The comparative print may be dispensed with under the Cordon rule only if the report states that dispensing with the print is necessary to expedite the business of the Senate. The escape is therefore narrow in form even where invoked, because the report must say on its face that expedition justified the omission, and the omission is recorded as a deliberate exception rather than a silent gap. A reader of a Senate report that invokes the exception knows that the comparison is absent by stated necessity, while a reader of a report that contains the print receives the same working tool that the Ramseyer rule guarantees in the House.

Senate reports carry additional prescribed material under Rule XXVI, including a Congressional Budget Office estimate and, under paragraph 11 of that rule, a regulatory impact evaluation. The regulatory impact evaluation directs the reporting committee to assess and state the regulatory consequences of the measure it reports, placing an account of expected regulatory effect inside the report beside the committee's explanation of the measure itself. The requirement fits the same pattern visible in the House rules. A report is expected to carry, within its own covers, the principal assessments on which a vote rests, fiscal assessment from the Budget Office and regulatory assessment from the committee, rather than leaving senators to assemble those judgments from separate documents of varying authority. For later readers, the placement matters as much as the content. An evaluation printed in the report shares the report's status as the committee's formal statement to the chamber, made at the time of reporting and directed to the text as reported, and it enters the interpretive record with that standing rather than as an unattributed estimate detached from the measure's passage.

A committee report's life does not end with the vote it informs, and its second life in interpretation rests on the qualities that made it authoritative in the chamber. After enactment, a statute's operative words may leave a question open, whether through a term the statute does not define, a provision whose reach is disputed, or a conflict between two plausible readings of the same sentence. Agencies charged with administering the statute, litigants disputing its application, and judges resolving those disputes then turn to the record of enactment for evidence of what the Legislature meant, and within that record the committee report occupies the highest place. The Supreme Court stated the hierarchy in Garcia v. United States, 469 U.S. 70 (1984), describing the committee reports as the authoritative source for finding the Legislature's intent. The Congressional Research Service, summarizing the practice of the courts, likewise records that among legislative history materials, courts give committee reports the greatest weight, because the report is the committee's formal explanation of the measure to the chamber that must vote on it. That ranking is a considered judgment about reliability, not a courtesy to committees, and it follows from the way a report is produced.

Reports outrank floor statements for reasons that the structure of the report makes plain. A floor statement speaks for one member, may be delivered to a nearly empty chamber, may never have been heard by the members whose votes enact the measure, and may reflect a purpose that the committee itself never adopted. A committee report, by contrast, issues from the committee as a body, is approved through the committee's own process, and presents the explanation on which the committee rested its recommendation. Reports also outrank floor statements on timing. A report is written after the text is fixed, because the committee reports a settled text and explains that text, whereas statements made during debate can address earlier versions, anticipate amendments never adopted, or describe hopes for the measure that its final language does not carry. Reports outrank hearing testimony for a related reason. Witnesses at a hearing speak for themselves, often in advocacy for an outcome, and the committee may accept, reject, or ignore their accounts; testimony records what the committee heard, while the report records what the committee concluded. Above all, the report is the document members had before them when voting. A member who read nothing else in the legislative record could read the report, with its text, explanation, votes, estimate, and comparative print, and the interpretive primacy of the report honors that reality by treating as most authoritative the explanation that was actually placed before the deciding chamber.

The practical consequences of that primacy run through every setting in which an enacted statute must be applied. An agency writing regulations under a statute consults the committee report to fix the boundaries of the authority the Legislature granted, to understand the problem the statute directed the agency to address, and to defend the agency's reading if that reading is challenged. A litigant arguing for one construction of a disputed provision builds the argument from the report first, quoting the section-by-section analysis that addresses the provision in dispute, because that analysis carries more weight than a favorable remark extracted from debate. Opposing counsel meets the argument on the same ground, searching the same report for language that supports a narrower or broader reading, and the contest over meaning becomes a contest over the committee's stated understanding. Researchers reconstructing the development of a statute rely on reports to connect enacted language to the choices that produced it, and guidance on [the sources for finding legislative history](/2022/05/15/finding-legislative-history-sources/) consistently places committee reports at the center of that reconstruction. In each setting, the report functions as the bridge between the text enacted and the reasoning that produced the text, and the absence of a report removes that bridge entirely.

A measure that passes through no committee markup produces no committee report, and therefore leaves no section-by-section explanation, no recorded committee division, and no Ramseyer or Cordon comparative print for later readers to consult. The bypass section weighs that cost in full, as part of its account of what unorthodox lawmaking surrenders when the committee stage is set aside.

## The Chair's Calendar: Gatekeeping Without a Vote

The namable claim of the committee system is a simple one, and the committee markup supplies the clearest place to state it. The chair's calendar is the veto. A measure that reaches a committee has passed referral, has acquired a number, a sponsor, and a place in the formal record of the chamber, and none of those steps carries the measure one inch toward a hearing. Advancement depends on a scheduling decision, and the scheduling decision belongs, in the ordinary course of committee business, to the chair. A chair who never places a measure on the calendar for a hearing and never places a measure on the calendar for a markup has defeated the measure as completely as a chamber majority voting against passage on the floor, and the defeat has required no motion, no debate, no amendment, and no recorded division. Gatekeeping of that kind is built into the structure of referral itself, because referral delivers a measure into custody without delivering any instruction about what the custodian must do with the measure, and custody without an instruction to act is authority to let the measure rest where referral placed it.

The absence at the center of the system deserves statement in the plainest terms the rules allow. No House rule requires a committee to hold a hearing on a referred measure. No Senate rule requires a committee to hold a hearing on a referred measure. No House or Senate rule requires a committee to conduct a committee markup on a referred measure, and no rule in either chamber compels a chair to schedule a hearing or a markup at any point after referral. The rules govern with considerable precision what happens after a committee decides to act. The rules prescribe quorums for reporting, entitlements to file minority and supplemental views, the contents of a committee report, and the comparative print that must accompany a report amending existing law. The rules govern the conduct of the markup, the reading of the text for amendment, and the recording of votes. The rules fall silent at the prior question, whether any of that machinery will be set in motion at all. That silence is the gate, and the chair, who controls the committee's agenda, holds the gate.

The consequence follows without exaggeration and without any need to attribute motive to any particular chair in any particular Congress. The decision not to schedule ends more legislation than any recorded vote. A recorded vote against a measure on the floor is a public event with a count, a journal entry, and a set of names attached to each side of the question. A recorded vote against reporting a measure in committee is likewise a public event, entered in the committee report under the requirements that the 104th Congress added in 1995 for recording votes on amendments and on the motion to report. A decision not to schedule produces none of that apparatus. The decision is exercised without a vote, without a record, and without any requirement to give a reason. A chair need not state that a measure failed any test of merit, cost, jurisdiction, or support, and need not distinguish a measure set aside after careful study from a measure that received no study at all. From the formal record, the two cases look identical, and that identity gives the calendar its force.

Volume converts that structural fact into the ordinary fate of legislation, and the figures for a single Congress make the scale concrete. In the 116th Congress, 16,602 bills were introduced and 344 public laws were enacted, about 2 percent of the measures introduced. The ratio matters less as a judgment on any one Congress than as a description of the terrain on which every committee operated. Inaction was the default outcome for nearly every measure introduced, not because every measure was examined and found wanting, but because the number of measures seeking a hearing, a markup, a report, and floor time exceeded by an order of magnitude the number the committee system and the two chambers could process. On such terrain, scheduling is the scarce grant. A place on a committee calendar is a rationed good, allocated among competing claims that cannot all be honored, and the allocation decision determines, in the overwhelming run of cases, whether a measure receives scrutiny that might improve or expose the measure, or receives no scrutiny and ends in silence.

The House maintains one formal workaround for a committee that refuses to act, and the workaround's design states, in procedural language, how seriously the chamber takes the gate the workaround must open. The discharge petition, governed by House Rule XV, clause 2, allows members to extract a measure from a committee that has held the measure and to bring the question of discharge before the House itself. The device is formal, public, and demanding by intention, and each element raises the cost of using the device above the cost of ordinary committee action. A petition cannot begin the moment referral occurs. A committee must first have held the measure for 30 legislative days, a threshold that gives the committee a defined period of exclusive custody before any effort to displace the committee may proceed. Only after that period has run does the petition route open, and the opening leads not to a vote but to a campaign for signatures, conducted in the open and measured against the highest count the House knows.

The signature requirement gives the discharge petition its character, and the requirement deserves precise statement because approximations drain the device of its meaning. A discharge petition requires 218 signatures, and 218 is not a plurality of members voting on a given day or a majority of a quorum assembled for business. The figure represents a majority of the whole House. A signature campaign must therefore assemble, in advance and in writing, the same number that passage itself would require from a fully attended chamber, for the limited purpose of forcing a question onto the floor, before any debate on the merits of the underlying measure has occurred. The publicity of the signatures compounds the difficulty. Signatures were once secret until 218 was reached. Since 1993, the signatures on a discharge petition have been public as the signatures are collected. Each member who signs therefore takes a visible position against the judgment of a committee chair and, in the usual alignment of party government, against the scheduling judgment of the majority leadership that sustains the chair, at a moment when the petition may fall short and produce nothing beyond the record of having signed.

The remaining mechanics of House Rule XV, clause 2, complete a picture of a device built to be available in principle and burdensome in practice. When 218 signatures have been gathered, the motion to discharge is placed on the Discharge Calendar, a separate calendar that exists for that single class of business. Placement does not produce immediate consideration. The motion may be called up only on the second and fourth Mondays of the month, and only after the motion has rested on the calendar for seven legislative days. The calendar therefore imposes a further wait after the signature threshold has been met, and confines the opportunity to call the motion up to two designated days in each month. When a motion to discharge is called up, debate is limited to 20 minutes per side, a ration that marks the procedural character of the question. The debate concerns whether the committee should be discharged, compressed into a span that permits each case to be stated and permits little beyond a statement of each case. Every stage adds friction, and the accumulation of friction is the point.

The historical record of the discharge petition, stated at the strength of the Congressional Research Service account that covers the period from 1931 to 2002, shows a device whose friction produced exactly the rarity the design predicts. During that period, 563 petitions were filed. Of those filings, 47 reached the signature threshold of 218. The fall from filings to completed petitions is the first measure of the device, because a filing costs little and a completed petition costs the organized support of a majority of the whole House. Of the petitions that reached the threshold, 26 discharge motions were voted. Nineteen measures passed the House after discharge. Only two measures became law through the discharge route itself. Each step narrows the count, filings to completed petitions, completed petitions to votes, votes to measures passed, and measures passed to laws enacted through the route, and the final figure stands as the summary of the whole account.

The campaign finance statute of 2002 supplies the modern illustration of the record, and the illustration fits the pattern of rarity rather than breaking that pattern. The discharge petition for the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act gathered the signatures required to force the question, and the petition stands in the series account as the second successful House discharge petition since the 1980s. A success of that kind demonstrates that the device functions, that a determined cross party coalition, assembled signature by signature in public, can pry a measure out of a committee that has declined to release the measure. The same success also demonstrates the conditions that success demands: majority support in the whole House declared in writing against the committee position, and support that persists through the calendar's waiting periods and designated days. Ordinary majority sympathy without that organized commitment gains nothing from the petition.

The Senate offers no discharge petition of comparable force, and the contrast sharpens the account of what the House device is and is not. Senate Rule XVII provides a motion to discharge a committee from consideration of a measure, and the motion exists as a formal possibility in the Senate's rules. The decisive difference lies in the treatment of debate. A Senate motion to discharge is debatable, and a debatable motion in the Senate is exposed to extended debate, the chamber's characteristic instrument for preventing a question from reaching a vote. A majority seeking to discharge a Senate committee must therefore contemplate not a single vote on discharge but an open ended contest over whether the vote will occur. That exposure is why the Senate motion is almost never effective. The House device, for all its friction, at least promises a vote on a designated day, after a defined wait, with debate confined to 20 minutes per side. The Senate device promises a debate about whether a vote will be permitted, and a promise of that kind leaves a committee's custody of a measure substantially undisturbed.

### Can a committee be forced to act on a bill?

No. No House or Senate rule requires a committee to hold a hearing, schedule a committee markup, or report a referred measure, and no rule compels a chair to place any measure on the committee calendar. A majority may seek a special meeting, but that right guarantees no markup.

The special meeting provision illustrates the limits of the formal checks that committee rules supply. House Rule XI, clause 2(c) allows a majority of a committee to call a special meeting on three days' notice, upon a signed written request. A committee majority acting together therefore possesses a means of convening the committee over a chair's reluctance. The provision is a meeting right and not a markup guarantee. A special meeting assembles the committee. Assembly alone does not place a particular measure on the agenda for amendment, does not order a text read for amendment by section, does not produce a motion to report, and does not generate a committee report. The counterweight addresses the convening of the committee rather than the consideration of a measure, a distinction that leaves the gate standing after the counterweight has been given full weight.

Against that account stands a counter reading that deserves presentation at full strength, on its own terms, without a rebuttal arranged to weaken the reading before the reading has been heard. The filter performs a genuine function. A legislature that receives measures in large numbers across a Congress, drafted with every degree of care and every degree of support, requires a mechanism that separates the small number of measures fit for the chamber's scarce attention from the large number that are duplicative, premature, symbolic, defective in drafting, or confined in appeal to a single district or interest. Referral to committee, followed by a chair's judgment about which referred measures merit the committee's hours, performs that separation. Abolish the filter, compel a hearing and a committee markup for every referred measure, and the result would not be universal consideration. The result would be the collapse of consideration itself, as committees consumed the session processing measures in an order determined by filing rather than by judgment, and measures of genuine consequence waited in the same queue as measures introduced to make a statement and abandoned by their own sponsors within a week.

The expertise argument strengthens the counter reading beyond the argument from capacity alone. Committees hold the subject expertise of the legislature, accumulated through the jurisdiction assignments that the Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946 wrote into the rules, through the professional staff that the same statute authorized, and through the sustained attention that members and staff give to a defined field across successive Congresses. A tax committee that has examined the revenue code through repeated markups, a defense committee that has written authorization measures year after year, and an agriculture committee within whose jurisdiction the nutrition programs reside each bring to a referred measure a body of knowledge that the chamber as a whole does not possess and cannot improvise on the floor. The chair's scheduling judgment, exercised from within that body of knowledge, reflects an assessment of how a measure fits the existing law in the committee's field, what a measure would cost, and what competing measures in the same field demand attention first. The counter reading insists that the judgment being exercised is, in the ordinary case, an expert judgment, and that expertise is precisely the asset the committee system was organized to create.

The capacity argument, stated in numbers rather than in abstractions, completes the counter reading. The chamber cannot mark up thousands of measures. A committee markup is a labor intensive proceeding that consumes member time, staff time, and the attention of the agencies and interests affected, and a markup conducted without preparation produces the drafting errors and ambiguities that markup scrutiny exists to catch. The 116th Congress figures that frame this section, 16,602 bills introduced against 344 public laws enacted, describe a ratio that no rearrangement of committee calendars could convert into comprehensive consideration. Even a committee system working at the outer limit of diligence would leave the great majority of referred measures without a hearing, because the hours required for a competent hearing and markup on each measure exceed the hours a Congress contains. Selection is therefore not a policy choice that a more virtuous set of chairs might decline. Selection is a mathematical necessity imposed by volume, and the only genuine questions concern who selects, by what lights, and subject to what check.

The criticism of gatekeeping, properly framed, concerns the absence of a check on the decision rather than the existence of the filter, and the distinction between those two objects of criticism is the most important analytical distinction the subject offers. A filter with a check would look different without the system's defenders needing to surrender the filter itself. Reasons could be required for a refusal to schedule after a defined period. A signature threshold lower than a majority of the whole House could align the discharge device with the scale of the decision the device reviews. A committee majority's power to call a special meeting could extend to placing a measure on the agenda of the meeting called. Each proposal accepts selection as necessary and addresses only the insulation of the selection from examination. The discharge record from 1931 to 2002, with 563 filings producing two laws through the route itself, can be read in that light as evidence about the calibration of the check rather than evidence about the legitimacy of the filter.

The section ends where the claim began, with the calendar. The chair's calendar is the veto because the rules that govern every later stage of a measure's life, the markup, the report, the floor, govern stages that a measure reaches only after the calendar has admitted the measure to consideration. That veto operates without a vote, without a record, and without a required reason, in a system whose volume makes some veto of the kind unavoidable and whose distribution of expertise gives the veto a defensible rationale in the hands of the committees that hold the veto. What the system lacks, and what the history of the discharge petition measures, is a check proportioned to the decision, a means by which the chamber that refers a measure can call for an account of a refusal to act without first assembling, in public and in writing, the full majority that enactment itself would demand. Gatekeeping names the arrangement whole, the necessary filter, the expert custodian, the silent decision, and the missing account.

## Two Reform Waves and the Redistribution of Committee Power

Reform of the committee system has rarely meant abolition of gatekeeping, and the history of committee markup offers the clearest evidence for that pattern. Each major reform wave altered who controlled the agenda, who possessed the staff and information needed to draft and defend a measure, whose attendance counted when a vote was taken, and which panels survived to receive referrals at all. The power exercised through referral, markup procedure, and report writing did not disappear under reform. Authority moved from one set of hands to another, and the gates that a measure had to pass were rebuilt in different places, under different rules, and with different keepers. The Legislative Reorganization Act of 1970, Public Law 91-510, signed on October 26, 1970, made committees rule bound, visible, and better staffed. The rules package adopted by the House at the opening of the 104th Congress on January 4, 1995, House Resolution 6, attacked the tenure, staff, tools, and existence of committees themselves.

The 1970 Act treated organizational reform as a response to an imbalance that had become too visible to defend. The chair stood at the center of the older accumulation, setting the calendar, recognizing members, directing staff, and deciding whether a referred measure received a hearing or a markup at all. Reformers who sought to weaken that discretion faced a constraint, because a legislature that abolished committees or stripped them of referral and reporting functions would lose the specialized scrutiny and drafting capacity that the committee markup existed to supply. The chosen method was therefore regulation rather than removal. Rules would be written down, meetings would occur on predictable days, votes would be recorded and published, and hearings would be open unless a majority voted to close them. The statute preserved committee authority while narrowing the private space in which that authority had been exercised, and that settlement explains both the reach and the limit of the first wave.

The requirement that each standing committee adopt written rules and publish them altered the most basic condition of committee government. A committee could operate through custom, precedent known mainly to its senior members, and the preferences of its chair, so that a member joining a panel encountered procedures that were difficult to state with confidence. Written rules changed the standing of every participant, because a member who believed that notice had been defective or that an amendment procedure had been mishandled could point to a published text rather than to an unwritten understanding that the chair alone was positioned to interpret. The chair retained the agenda and the gavel, but discretion operated inside a framework that other members could read, cite, and invoke. A rule bound committee did not cease to be a gatekeeper. It became a gatekeeper whose gate had measurements that outsiders could inspect.

Regular meeting days carried the same principle into the calendar. A committee that met only when its chair chose to summon it converted scheduling itself into a form of substantive power, because delay and inaction required no vote, left no record, and offered no occasion on which members could force a question. The establishment of regular meeting days under the 1970 Act gave committee business a predictable rhythm against which exceptions became visible. A chair who declined to place a measure on that recurring calendar made a choice that colleagues could identify as a choice rather than absorb as the natural absence of an occasion. No rule required a hearing or a markup on a referred measure, and the reform did not create such a duty. The reform instead attacked the invisibility of refusal, leaving scheduling power intact inside a calendar that had become a public and recurring fact.

The presumption of open hearings and open meetings pressed further into the territory that discretionary secrecy had occupied. Under the 1970 Act, closure ceased to be a unilateral act or a default habit and became a decision that a recorded majority vote had to support. It reversed the burden, so that openness was the expected condition and departure from that condition required the committee to act affirmatively and on the record. A chair who preferred to negotiate or to hear sensitive testimony behind closed doors had to assemble a majority willing to be counted in favor of closure, and that requirement raised the political price of secrecy even when the vote to close succeeded. Open proceedings also changed the informational position of everyone outside the room, because other members of Congress, executive agencies, affected interests, and the press could follow testimony and debate as those events unfolded. The chair lost a portion of the freedom that private management had supplied, even though extensive control over witnesses, recognition, and the order of business remained.

Recorded committee votes, made public under the 1970 Act, completed the transformation of committee action into committee record. An unrecorded division allowed a panel to move a measure, reject an amendment, or order a bill reported without fixing individual responsibility for the outcome. Recorded votes that entered the public domain attached names to positions, and that attachment changed the calculations of members and chairs alike. A member could be asked by constituents, by party leaders, and by colleagues on the floor to explain a committee vote that contradicted a later public stance, and the record gave such questions a documentary foundation. For the chair, the requirement reduced the value of quiet disposal, because an amendment defeated on a recorded vote had been defeated openly. The recorded vote strengthened the committee report as an interpretive document, because the report operated alongside a published voting history rather than in place of one. The 1970 provision established the governing idea that a committee exercised public power and that the exercise deserved a public tally.

Expanded committee staffing and the renaming of the Legislative Reference Service as the Congressional Research Service with an expanded research mission addressed the material foundations of committee power. Expertise determines who can draft a credible substitute, who can test an agency claim, who can assess the effect of an amendment during a committee markup, and who can prepare the section by section explanation that a report requires. A committee dependent on the chair for staff direction and dependent on executive agencies or outside interests for analysis possessed formal jurisdiction without an independent capacity to use it. The 1970 expansion increased the professional resources available to committees, and the enlarged research mission of the renamed Congressional Research Service gave members and committees an institutional source of analysis housed inside the legislative branch. The balance nevertheless moved, because committee members beyond the chair gained a stronger claim on professional assistance. Power that depends on information follows the distribution of information, and the staffing provisions of 1970 distributed information more widely than the arrangements they replaced.

Taken together, the committee provisions of the 1970 Act reduced discretionary secrecy without displacing the chair from the center of committee life. Control of the agenda, influence over staff, command of procedure at hearings and markups, and the prestige of senior position on a panel all survived the statute. What diminished was the freedom to govern a committee through unpublished custom, irregular summons, closed proceedings chosen without a recorded majority, and votes that left no public trace. The committee that emerged from the 1970 settlement was a rule bound body whose procedures were published and whose votes were on record, and that description applied to the most powerful chair as fully as to the newest member seeking to invoke a rule. Reform of that kind redistributed power downward and outward inside the panel, toward members who could cite the rules, toward staff who served the institution, and toward observers who could read a record. It did not redistribute much power toward the chamber floor or toward party leadership.

### Why were committee chairs term-limited in the House?

House chairs were term-limited in 1995 to break the link between long committee service and permanent control of a panel's agenda. A limit of three terms forced rotation, weakened the independence that seniority had built, and shifted influence toward party leadership, the steering process, and members seeking favor between chairmanships.

The second wave arrived as a rules package rather than as a freestanding reorganization statute, and its form revealed its purpose. House Resolution 6, adopted on January 4, 1995, at the opening of the 104th Congress, rewrote the rules under which House committees operated in a single opening day action. Congressional Research Service reports on the committee changes of the 104th Congress describe a package that reached tenure, voting method, staffing, committee structure, assignments, reporting content, and quorum practice at once. Where the 1970 Act had regulated the committee from within, the 1995 package reorganized the environment around the committee and subordinated long standing panel autonomy to a party program carried through the chamber rules. The elements belonged together, because a term limit on chairs had a harsher meaning when staff shrank, proxies vanished, committees disappeared, and assignments narrowed at the same moment. Gatekeeping itself was never the target, and the target was the identity and independence of the gatekeeper.

The three term limit on service as a committee or subcommittee chair, placed in House Rule X, clause 5(c)(2), converted the chairmanship from a position held for as long as seniority and party tolerance allowed into a position held for a bounded tenure. A chair approaching the end of eligibility had reason to consider the judgment of the party leadership that would shape assignments, advancement, and reputation after the gavel passed to another member. Seniority had traditionally been the strongest predictor of elevation to a chairmanship without ever having been a rule of the House, and the term limit weakened the practical force of that predictor by guaranteeing vacancies that seniority alone could not fill or prevent. Rotation also altered the relationship between a chair and the committee jurisdiction, because long tenure had allowed a chair to become the chamber's resident authority on a subject, courted by agencies and interests as a durable point of access.

The prohibition of proxy voting in committees and subcommittees changed the physical arithmetic of the committee markup. Before 1995, House committees permitted proxy votes under conditions, and a member absent from the room could authorize another member to cast a vote on the absent member's behalf. Proxies softened the discipline of attendance and allowed voting weight to accumulate beyond the members actually present and engaged in the proceeding. The ban ended that accumulation, so that a member who wished to affect an amendment vote or a vote on ordering a measure reported had to attend. A chair lost the convenience of harvesting proxies to settle questions without assembling the panel, and a closely divided markup required genuine presence rather than paper strength. At the same time, the chair's whip operation gained importance, because ensuring that supporters appeared, remained, and voted together fell to the chair and to the leadership that backed the chair. The proxy ban therefore shifted power toward attendance and toward the organizers who could produce attendance on schedule.

The reduction of committee staff by at least one third from the levels of the 103rd Congress struck at the resource that the 1970 Act had expanded. A cut of that magnitude reduced the independent analytical capacity of panels at the moment when term limits were shortening the experience of their chairs, and the combination mattered more than either element standing alone. A short tenure chair supported by a diminished staff faced a steeper dependence on other sources of expertise. Party leadership staff gained relative weight because leadership offices retained a coordinating role across committees and could supply priorities, language, and political direction to panels whose own capacity had thinned. Executive agencies and outside experts also gained relative weight, since a committee with fewer professional employees had fewer hands available to test claims, draft alternatives, and pursue oversight questions that originated inside the panel. Their remaining power became more borrowed, and borrowed expertise carries the lender's perspective into the borrowing institution.

The abolition of three standing committees and the caps placed on assignments restructured the map on which gatekeeping operated. The committees for the District of Columbia, for Merchant Marine and Fisheries, and for Post Office and Civil Service ceased to exist as standing panels under the 1995 package, and their jurisdictions had to be absorbed elsewhere in the committee system rather than vanishing with them. A subject that loses its committee loses a dedicated chair, a dedicated staff, a dedicated hearing record, and a membership that has chosen that subject as a principal assignment. A measure that once received a hearing from specialists could instead compete for attention inside a surviving committee with a broader and more crowded jurisdiction. Members were limited to two standing committees and four subcommittees, and subcommittee numbers were capped, narrowing the spread of assignments through which members had accumulated multiple footholds across the system. The gates that disappeared in 1995 did not open the road, and their traffic was redirected to gates that remained.

The requirement that committee reports record the votes cast for and against amendments and on the motion to report, with members' names attached, extended the logic of the recorded vote into the document that the chamber itself received. Adding named votes on amendments and on reporting changed the report into a fuller record of internal division and support. A member of the House who had not attended the committee markup could read not only what the committee recommended but also which alternatives the committee had rejected, by what margin, and which members had supported or opposed the decision to send the measure to the floor. For chairs, the requirement narrowed the space in which a markup outcome could be presented to the floor as a consensus that the internal record would not have supported. For party leadership, the report became a monitoring instrument and a published account of loyalty and defection inside the panels.

The elimination of rolling quorums addressed a practice through which committee business had been conducted without the continuous presence that formal quorum language suggested. A rolling quorum allowed a vote to accumulate as members arrived and departed, so that a decision could be assembled across a period of time rather than rendered by members present together for the question. House Resolution 6 eliminated rolling quorums, and the reporting safeguard that accompanied the change preserved the requirement that a majority of the committee be present for a measure to be ordered reported, with a point of order available against a measure reported without a majority present. Senate Rule XXVI, paragraph 7(a)(1) carried the parallel principle in the other chamber by providing that no measure shall be reported unless a majority of the committee was physically present. The provision joined the proxy ban as an attendance reform, and together the two changes made the committee vote a more demanding event to stage and a more meaningful event to win.

The elements of the 1995 package acquire their full significance when the destinations of the displaced power are named together. Term limits shifted influence toward party leadership and the steering process, because rotation made chairs more dependent on the favor that governed selection and life after the gavel. Staff reductions shifted influence toward leadership staff and outside expertise, because panels with fewer professional employees relied more heavily on analysis and language supplied from elsewhere. The proxy ban shifted influence toward attendance and toward the chair's whip operation, because outcomes turned on assembling supporters in the room. The abolition of committees shifted jurisdiction and attention toward the surviving panels, whose broader gates became harder to avoid and whose chairs inherited subjects that had once possessed advocates of their own. Report vote requirements shifted information toward the floor, the leadership, and the public record, weakening the chair's control over the story of the markup. None of those shifts abolished the committee's power to hear, to amend, to report, or to refuse, and reform instead redistributed the power to perform those acts under altered conditions. The later history of the chair term limit supplied a brief confirmation of that pattern at exactly the strength the Congressional Research Service chairs report supports. The limit was removed from House rules in 2009 and reinstated in 2013, with the chair of the Rules Committee exempted from its operation.

## The Leadership Bypass and the Record It Leaves Behind

The textbook route depends on a sequence that gives the committee system its meaning. A measure is referred, hearings test its premises, a committee markup settles its language amendment by amendment, and a committee report explains the result before a vote. The leadership bypass breaks that sequence at one or more points without abolishing it. A measure may be drafted outside the committee of jurisdiction, altered after reporting, carried inside a vehicle assembled elsewhere, or placed before the chamber under procedures that leave little room for amendment. The result is lawmaking with a different author, a different negotiating table, and a far thinner record.

Barbara Sinclair gave that pattern its enduring name in Unorthodox Lawmaking, first published in 1997 and issued in a fifth edition in 2016. Sinclair treated departure from the textbook process not as an occasional emergency device but as a recurring route for major legislation. Leadership task forces and leadership-drafted measures stood near the center of the account, alongside postcommittee adjustments negotiated after a committee had reported, omnibus vehicles, and restrictive floor procedures that displaced open amendment. The account is qualitative. No percentage for the trend belongs to Sinclair, and precision about scale requires a different source.

A leadership task force alters the committee system before a bill reaches any markup table. Its membership and its charge come from the party leadership rather than from a referral or from the jurisdictional logic of the standing committees. The committee route begins with a chair, a ranking member, a staff structure, and a jurisdiction defined in the chamber rules. A task force begins with a leadership objective and a deadline. Its negotiations can cross boundaries that referral divides, but responsibility becomes harder to trace, because no single committee owns the text in the way a reporting committee owns a reported bill.

Leadership-drafted measures carry the displacement one step further. Under the textbook account, the introduced bill supplies a starting point, committee consideration reshapes it, and the chairman's mark or an amendment in the nature of a substitute often supplies the text actually marked up. Under the bypass, leadership offices prepare the operative text before committee consideration begins, and in the limiting case the committee stage disappears altogether. The measure requires floor passage and may command genuine support. What changes is the location of the decisive choices. Language allocating burdens, defining terms, and reconciling competing provisions is settled in leadership negotiations rather than tested through the amendment votes that a committee markup places on the record.

Both parties have used leadership drafting when in the majority, and the practice resists description as the habit of a single party. Majority control supplies the incentive and the instruments regardless of party label. A leadership team answerable for a governing program faces pressure to produce a text that a floor coalition can accept without reopening every bargain on the floor. Minority parties have objected to particular bypasses in opposition and have reached for comparable devices after winning control. The bypass grew from the rewards attached to majority leadership, not from a lapse by one set of actors.

Postcommittee adjustments occupy a middle position between the committee route and its displacement, and Sinclair treated them as a distinct device. A committee holds hearings, completes a markup, and reports a measure, after which leadership negotiates changes to the reported text before floor consideration. Some adjustments answer objections from other committees, the administration, or members whose votes the leadership counts as uncertain. Others rework compromises the committee itself struck. Whatever their purpose, the adjustments separate the text the committee voted to report from the text the chamber is asked to pass. The report explains the first text, and the floor votes on the second. A section-by-section analysis written for superseded language cannot account for provisions inserted or qualified after reporting.

Omnibus vehicles extend the bypass by changing the unit on which the chamber votes. A large package combines provisions that would otherwise travel through several committees and several votes. The packaging changes the politics of each component, because a provision that might fail alone can survive inside a bargain many members support for other reasons, and rejection of the whole carries costs that rejection of a single measure does not. That logic explains the durability of [the omnibus bills that carry measures past the committee stage](/2022/04/15/omnibus-bills-and-riders/) as instruments for measures whose sponsors value enactment inside a package over examination as freestanding bills. The vehicle also compresses scrutiny. Separate markups divide a measure into provisions small enough to test in sequence and amend on recorded votes, while a single vote on a combined text cannot reproduce that testing.

Restrictive floor procedures complete the bypass on the House side by controlling what the chamber may do with a text once debate begins. A special rule can structure debate time, designate the text treated as the original bill for amendment purposes, and bar floor amendments altogether or admit only amendments the rule specifies. Each element has a scheduling rationale, because floor time is finite and open amendment can unravel bargains among committees, between the chambers, or with the administration. The institutional effect survives the rationale. When a rule prohibits amendments, the chamber's choice narrows to acceptance or rejection of a text fixed before debate, and members who identify a defect have no amendment through which to repair it. Debate becomes persuasion about a settled document rather than a stage of drafting.

The Senate developed parallel devices within a chamber whose rules rest heavily on unanimous consent. Senate Rule XIV allows a measure, after objection on its second reading, to be placed directly on the Calendar without committee referral. Placement does not guarantee consideration, and scheduling depends on motions to proceed and consent agreements, but the route removes the committee stage from the path of a measure the leadership is prepared to advance. Unanimous consent supplies related routes, including consent to discharge a committee from further consideration of a measure or consent to proceed to a measure without the ordinary sequence. The Congressional Research Service, in Report RS22299, treated Rule XIV and unanimous consent as the Senate's committee-bypass routes. A measure enacted after Rule XIV placement carries the same legal force as one enacted after full committee consideration, while the materials explaining its provisions differ sharply.

### Can a bill become law without a committee markup?

Yes. A measure may reach the floor without any committee markup through a leadership-drafted text, an omnibus vehicle, a special rule for an unreported bill in the House, or placement directly on the Senate Calendar under Rule XIV, and enactment requires only passage by both chambers and presidential action or an override.

The quantified trend, stated with its source and its periods, shows how far the House bypass extended beyond isolated cases. The Congressional Research Service, in "The 'Regular Order': A Perspective," Report R46597, dated November 6, 2020, found that in the early 1990s only about 9 percent of the bills the House considered under special rules had not been reported by a committee. By the 2010s, unreported measures accounted for about 30 percent of all bills with special rules. Most of those unreported measures were considered under rules that prohibited floor amendments, so the measures that skipped the committee stage also reached the floor in a form the chamber could not amend. The figures cover bills under special rules rather than all legislation, but that category governs contested measures that cannot rely on consent. Within it, unreported status changed from an exception attached to roughly one bill in eleven to a condition attached to nearly one bill in three.

The same Congressional Research Service account carried a counterweight that disciplines any claim of committee collapse. More than two thirds of major measures are reported from committee, a figure the report presented as a summary of expert assessment. Both findings can hold together because they measure different populations, and read together they describe concentration rather than disappearance. Committees handled most major legislation, while the bypass clustered on a substantial minority of measures, and Sinclair's account explains why that minority deserves attention disproportionate to its share. The measures routed around committees tended to be the measures leadership valued most.

The House supplied evidence of the trend's scale at the start of the 116th Congress. Section 103(i) of H.Res. 6, adopted for that Congress in 2019, made it not in order to consider a measure that had not been reported by a committee under a special rule unless a hearing list requirement attached to the procedure was satisfied, establishing a point of order against consideration of an unreported measure under a special rule without a hearing list. The Congressional Research Service discussed the provision in Report R46597 as part of the institutional response to unreported measures. A point of order is a modest instrument, because it can be waived and compels neither a markup nor a report. Its adoption records a chamber judgment that unreported consideration had become frequent enough to require a formal obstacle in the opening rules package. The instrument also has a limit. A hearing list can document testimony without establishing that a committee voted on amendments, so the provision protects the hearing stage more readily than the markup stage it leaves untouched.

The consequences begin with the arithmetic of the record. No markup means no amendment record. In a committee markup, amendments are offered, debated under committee rules, and voted, and the votes on amendments and on the motion to report are recorded for the committee report under requirements strengthened in 1995. That record shows which alternatives the committee considered and rejected, which compromises it accepted, and which members supported each step. A rejected amendment matters to later interpretation because rejection distinguishes a considered choice from an oversight. Without a markup, the distinction vanishes. No document establishes whether a phrase survived because drafters weighed an alternative and preferred it, or because no proceeding ever placed an alternative beside it.

No report means a second and larger set of losses. A House committee report under Rule XIII carries the text of the measure as reported, an explanation of the measure with a section-by-section analysis, the recorded votes from the markup, a Congressional Budget Office cost estimate where timely submitted, oversight and performance-goal statements, and the comparative print required when the measure amends existing law. A bypassed measure generates none of that apparatus as a committee product. Floor debate may explain parts of the text, but explanations scattered across debate lack the discipline of a document that must account for every section in order. The section-by-section form forces attention to provisions that attract no floor speech. Its absence leaves the most technical provisions, often those with the longest legal afterlife, with the least explanation.

The comparative print deserves separate attention because its loss is mechanical and verifiable. Under the Ramseyer rule, House Rule XIII, clause 3(e), a report on a bill or joint resolution that would repeal or amend existing law must reprint the affected portion of existing law and show, through struck text and italics, how that law would read if the measure were enacted. The Senate counterpart, the Cordon rule in Senate Rule XXVI, paragraph 12, imposes the same duty for Senate reports. A comparative print forces drafters to confront the measure as a set of changes to an existing legal structure rather than as a freestanding statement of intentions. Cross references and definitions borrowed from the amended statute become visible in the print. A measure that never receives a report never receives that confrontation in documentary form.

Drafting errors and ambiguities survive more readily in that environment, because markup scrutiny exists precisely to catch them. Committee staff, agency reviewers, and members from both parties read a mark with adversarial attention, hunting for undefined terms, inconsistent usage, faulty cross references, and deadlines that cannot be met. A text that passes through hearings, a markup, and a comparative print has survived several structured opportunities for a reader with opposing interests to object. A leadership text assembled under deadline pressure and protected from floor amendment has survived fewer, and each missed opportunity removes a chance to correct language before correction requires a new statute. Ambiguity costs more than error in one respect. An erroneous provision announces its defect when applied, while an ambiguous provision delegates its meaning to administrators and courts, which must choose among readings the legislative process never ranked.

The Supreme Court stated the premise behind that delegation in Garcia v. United States, 469 U.S. 70, decided in 1984, describing committee reports as the authoritative source for finding the intent of the Legislature. Courts give committee reports the greatest weight among legislative history materials because a report is the committee's formal explanation of a measure to the chamber that must vote on it. The reasoning presumes that a report exists. For a bypassed measure, the chamber voted on a text whose formal explanation was never written, and a court searching for the authoritative source finds floor statements and leadership summaries carrying less institutional authority than the missing report. Agencies inherit the same thin record earlier, as officials who did not draft the statute build a substitute record through rulemaking under language whose gaps markup scrutiny was meant to expose.

Against those consequences stands a counter-reading that deserves statement at full strength. Party leaders are accountable for outcomes in a way individual committee chairs are not. A chair answers for a committee's output and a jurisdiction, while the Speaker, the floor leaders, and the whip organizations answer for whether the majority governs at all. When a major measure fails, public judgment falls on the party and its leadership rather than on a chair whose committee declined to act. Leadership drafting aligns drafting authority with that accountability. The office that bears the cost of failure controls the text whose failure it fears, and the alignment gives voters a clearer target than a process dispersed among committees whose internal votes few outside the chamber follow.

Leadership drafting can also assemble coalitions that a single committee cannot. A major measure often crosses jurisdictions, implicates the budget, and requires commitments from members whose support depends on provisions outside any one committee's reach. A committee acting alone cannot trade across those boundaries with authority, and sequential consideration by several committees can produce texts that contradict one another or that no floor majority accepts. A leadership negotiation can weigh the whole coalition at once, balance concessions across jurisdictions, and fix a text that the counting of votes supports. On this reading, the bypass aggregates representation at the level where enactment is decided.

The counter-reading draws support from the survival of the committee route itself. Committees accounted for the reporting of more than two thirds of major measures in the Congressional Research Service's summary of expert assessment, and ordinary legislation moved through referral, hearings, markups, and reports in large volume. The bypass concentrated on measures where leadership judged committee handling inadequate to the political problem, whether because of deadline, jurisdiction, or coalition arithmetic. A system in which committees process the great majority of measures and leadership reserves a direct route for the hardest cases can be defended as a division of labor rather than a usurpation. The defense is strongest where leadership drafting remains exceptional and visible, and weakest where the exception becomes the routine path for every measure that matters.

The stakes finally settle on the readers a statute acquires after its sponsors leave the chamber. Every enacted measure outlives the negotiations that produced it, and its later readers approach it through whatever record the process left behind. A committee-reported measure hands those readers a marked-up text, recorded votes, a section-by-section explanation, and a comparative print showing the measure against the law it changed. A bypassed measure hands them a text, a closed rule or a consent agreement, and the knowledge that the decisive choices occurred in rooms whose reasoning no report preserves. The difference decides how much of a statute's meaning was settled by the institution elected to settle it, and how much was left, by silence, to whoever interprets the text next.

## Committees, Markups and Gatekeeping: The Chain in Full

The committee system is best understood as a chain of scheduled decisions, each resting with an identifiable officeholder and each producing a document that later readers can examine. Referral assigns a measure to a committee under a written jurisdictional map drawn in 1946 and maintained in the rules of the two chambers. The chair decides whether the measure receives a hearing and whether it receives a markup, and that scheduling decision, taken without a vote and defended without a record, disposes of the overwhelming majority of everything the chambers are asked to enact. Where a markup occurs, the committee works not on the bill as introduced but on a chairman's mark or a substitute, amends that text under recorded votes, and reports the measure with a report that explains it section by section, prices it through the Congressional Budget Office, records the votes by name, and prints against existing law the changes the measure would make. Where the two chambers pass different texts, the differences pass to the stage treated in [the account of conference committees and amendments between the houses](/2022/03/15/conference-committees-explained/), and the interpretive trail the committee stage laid down follows the statute for the rest of its life.

The chain has a weak link, and the weak link is the first one. Every later stage of the process creates a record that disciplines the actors at that stage: amendments are debated in the open, votes are taken by name, reports are published, and floor proceedings are transcribed. The decision to hold no markup creates nothing. The gatekeeping claim at the center of the committee system's study therefore survives every counter-reading offered in its defense, not because the filter lacks a function but because the function is discharged, at the decisive moment, by a single officeholder whose refusal leaves no trace for any later reader to weigh. Reform waves in 1970 and 1995 published the committee's rules, recorded its votes, limited its chairs, and cut its staff, and each reform accepted the gate while rearranging the gatehouse. The leadership bypass then demonstrated the final possibility, that the chamber's own majority, acting through its leaders, might route around the gate altogether on the measures it values most, trading the committee's scrutiny and the report's interpretive wealth for speed and control.

For study purposes the committee system reduces to four linked propositions, each of which the sections of the account established from the rules and the record. The markup is where operative text is set, and the text set is rarely the text introduced. The chair's decision to schedule the markup is the single most consequential gatekeeping act in the process, and the only formal remedy against its refusal, the discharge petition, has produced law through its own route twice in the span the Congressional Research Service counted. The committee report is the most heavily weighted document in the later interpretation of any statute that has one, which is why measures enacted without a markup govern with a thinner record behind them. And the system's written jurisdictions explain the map of American legislation itself, from farm bills carrying nutrition programs to defense authorization acts carrying education benefits for service members. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What happens at a committee markup?

At a committee markup, the members of a committee meet in a working session to consider a measure section by section and to decide its text. The text before the committee is usually a draft prepared under the chair's direction or a substitute amendment, rather than the bill as introduced. Members offer amendments to that text, debate each amendment under the committee's rules, and vote, with the votes recorded. Amendments adopted are incorporated, and amendments rejected fall away. When the committee has worked through the text, the members vote on whether to order the measure reported to the chamber, and members who dissent may file written views for the committee report that accompanies the measure to the floor.

### Q: Who decides whether a committee markup happens?

The chair of the committee decides. The chair controls the committee's agenda and schedule, and no rule of the House or the Senate requires a committee to hold a hearing or a markup on any measure referred to it. A chair who declines to schedule a markup therefore ends a measure without any vote taking place, and the rules provide no mechanism by which the committee's members, acting inside the committee, can compel the session. The single formal remedy lies outside the committee altogether, in a discharge petition signed by a majority of the whole House, a device whose rarity in practice measures how completely the scheduling decision belongs to the chair.

### Q: What is a chairman's mark before a committee markup?

A chairman's mark is a complete draft of a measure prepared under the chair's direction and laid before the committee as the text to be amended when the markup convenes. Committee staff draft the mark, and its language ordinarily embodies negotiation conducted before the session, together with technical and policy input from the agencies that administer the affected programs. The mark often differs substantially from the bill as introduced, because it represents the text the chair and the committee majority are prepared to defend. Amendments at the markup then amend the mark, so the mark, although it has no formal status in the chamber's rules, functions as the real starting point of the committee's work.

### Q: Why does the committee markup report matter later?

The committee report matters later because it is the committee's formal explanation of the measure to the chamber, and later interpreters treat it as the most authoritative guide to what the enacted text means. The report explains the measure's purpose, analyzes it section by section, prints the cost estimate, records the votes taken at the markup, and shows how the measure changes existing law. Agencies administering the statute, lawyers litigating its meaning, and judges construing ambiguous provisions all consult the report before any other legislative material, and the Supreme Court has described committee reports as the authoritative source for finding the intent of the Legislature.

### Q: Can a bill skip the committee markup stage?

A bill can skip the committee stage entirely, and on major legislation the route around committee has grown from an exception into a standard alternative. Party leaders may draft a measure themselves and bring it to the floor under a special rule without any committee having reported it, and the Congressional Research Service found that unreported measures rose from about 9 percent of the bills the House considered under special rules in the early 1990s to about 30 percent by the 2010s. The Senate reaches the same result through placement of a measure directly on its Calendar. A measure enacted by such a route carries no markup record and no committee report into its later interpretation.

### Q: How did 1995 reforms change committee markup power?

The rules package adopted by the House at the opening of the 104th Congress, on January 4, 1995, redistributed committee power in several directions at once. Chairs of committees and subcommittees were limited to three terms in the chair. Proxy voting, under which absent members had cast votes through colleagues, was prohibited in committee proceedings, so amendment and reporting votes required members to attend in person. Committee staff was reduced by at least one third from the levels of the preceding Congress. Committee reports were required to print the votes cast on amendments and on the motion to report, with members' names. Joint referrals of bills to two committees ended, and rolling quorums were eliminated, leaving a majority of the committee as the quorum for reporting.

### Q: What is the Ramseyer rule in a committee markup report?

The Ramseyer rule is the requirement, carried in House Rule XIII, clause 3(e) and first established in 1929, that a committee report show exactly how a reported measure changes existing law. Where a bill would repeal or amend a statute, the report must reprint the affected portion of that statute and present it in comparative form, with language proposed for removal struck through and new language set in italics, so that a reader can see the existing law and the amended law side by side in a single document. The rule takes its name from Representative Christian W. Ramseyer of Iowa. The Senate applies a counterpart requirement, the Cordon rule in Rule XXVI, named for Senator Guy Cordon of Oregon.

### Q: How are committee markup chairs chosen?

Committee chairs emerge from the party organizations of the two chambers rather than from any election by the chamber at large. In the House, the majority party's steering committee nominates a chair for each committee, the party conference ratifies the nominations, and the House adopts its committee assignments by resolution at the opening of a Congress. Seniority on the committee has traditionally been the strongest predictor of nomination, although seniority has never been a rule, and party caucuses have on occasion rejected senior members, as the House Democratic Caucus did with three prospective chairs in 1975. The Senate follows the same pattern through its party conferences, and the 1995 House reforms added a three-term limit to the tenure of House chairs.

### Q: What is the difference between a hearing and a markup?

A hearing and a markup are the committee's two distinct working sessions, and they serve opposite purposes. At a hearing, the committee receives information: witnesses from agencies, affected industries, expert bodies, and the public testify, members question them, and the committee builds a record on which judgment can rest, without taking any decision on text. At a markup, the committee decides: a prepared text lies on the table, members offer amendments, votes are recorded, and the measure is shaped into the form in which the committee will report it, or the effort fails. Hearings ordinarily precede markups on significant measures, although no rule requires a hearing before a markup, and the absence of a hearing leaves the markup record thinner for later readers.

### Q: What does it mean when a bill is ordered reported?

A bill is ordered reported when a committee, at the close of a markup, votes to send the measure to the chamber with the committee's recommendation. The phrase describes the committee's formal act of reporting: the members present, with a majority of the committee constituting the quorum the House rules require for that business, vote on the motion to report, and the measure then leaves the committee's custody. The committee staff prepares the written report that accompanies it, including the section-by-section analysis, the cost estimate, the recorded votes, and any minority or dissenting views members have filed. Ordering a measure reported makes it eligible for floor consideration, although the chamber's leadership decides whether and when the floor takes it up.

### Q: What is an amendment in the nature of a substitute?

An amendment in the nature of a substitute is an amendment that replaces the entire text of a measure after its enacting clause with a complete new text. Offered at a markup, it substitutes one full draft for another, and once it is pending, further amendments amend the substitute rather than the original bill. Committees use the device when negotiation has produced a comprehensive revision that piecemeal amendment cannot express, and a chairman's mark frequently takes this form. If the committee adopts the substitute and orders the measure reported, the reported bill carries the substitute's text. The device explains why the bill that leaves a committee may share little beyond its title and number with the bill that entered it.

### Q: Can a bill be referred to more than one committee?

A bill can be referred to more than one committee in the House, under a practice authorized by the Committee Reform Amendments of 1974 and effective from January 3, 1975. Referral may be split, with designated portions sent to different committees, or sequential, with a second committee considering the measure after the first has reported. Joint referral, under which two committees held a measure at the same time, was abolished in 1995, and since that year the Speaker has been required to designate one committee of primary jurisdiction whenever several committees receive a measure. The Speaker may also attach a time limit to a sequential referral. In the Senate, multiple referral occurs only by joint motion of the two party leaders or by unanimous consent, and remains rare.

### Q: How many members must be present for a committee to report a bill?

A majority of the committee must be present for the committee to report a measure, in both chambers, although the two rules are phrased differently. House Rule XI, clause 2(h) provides that a majority of a committee constitutes a quorum for the purpose of reporting a measure, while committees may set lower quorums, down to one third of the membership, for other items of committee business. Senate Rule XXVI, paragraph 7(a)(1) states the requirement in physical terms: no measure shall be reported from any committee unless a majority of the committee was physically present. The strictness of the reporting quorum reflects the weight of the act, since a reported measure carries the committee's authority onto the chamber floor.

### Q: What are minority views in a committee report?

Minority views are the written statements of disagreement that members of a committee append to the committee report when a measure is reported over their opposition. House Rule XI, clause 2(l) entitles any member who gives notice during consideration to not less than two additional calendar days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays on which the House is not in session, to file supplemental, minority, or dissenting views, and the views are printed as part of the report. The statements serve two audiences: the chamber, which reads the objections beside the majority's explanation before voting, and later interpreters, for whom the dissenting analysis often identifies the ambiguities and contested choices the enacted text left unresolved.

### Q: Can the minority party force a committee markup?

The minority party cannot force a markup through any procedure inside the committee. Scheduling belongs to the chair, and the minority holds no vote, petition, or motion within the committee's own process that can place a measure on the markup calendar against the chair's refusal. House Rule XI, clause 2(c) does allow a majority of the committee's members to demand a special meeting when the chair declines to call one, but a majority of the committee ordinarily includes members of the chair's own party, so the provision operates as a check by the committee's majority rather than as a minority instrument. The genuine remedy is the discharge petition, which belongs to the whole House and requires signatures from a majority of its members, including defectors from the majority party.

### Q: Does a reported bill always receive a floor vote?

A reported bill does not always receive a floor vote, and a large share of reported measures never reach one. Reporting makes a measure eligible for consideration and places it on one of the chamber's calendars, but the decision to schedule floor time belongs to the majority leadership in the House, acting through the Rules Committee and the Speaker, and to the majority leader in the Senate. Leadership weighs floor time, which is scarce, against the measure's support, its urgency, and the amendments its consideration might invite. Measures may wait for a larger vehicle, be folded into other legislation, or simply lapse at the end of a Congress, their committee reports standing as the fullest record of how far they traveled.

### Q: How did the Legislative Reorganization Act reshape the committee system?

The Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946 gave the standing committee system the shape it has carried since the 80th Congress. Enacted on the recommendations of the Joint Committee on the Organization of Congress, the Act reduced the House's standing committees from 48 to 19 and the Senate's from 33 to 15, defined each committee's jurisdiction in writing, ended much of the overlap under which several panels claimed the same subjects, and authorized professional staff for every standing committee. The companion Act of 1970 then opened the system, requiring written committee rules, regular meeting days, open proceedings, and public recorded votes. Between the two statutes, the committee became a smaller, staffed, rule-bound institution with a published territory to defend.

### Q: What is the difference between a standing committee and a subcommittee?

A standing committee is a permanent panel created by the rules of its chamber, holding a defined jurisdiction, receiving referrals directly from the chamber, and reporting measures to the chamber floor. A subcommittee is a subdivision that a standing committee creates within itself, drawing its members from the parent committee and receiving only the measures the parent committee chooses to send down. A subcommittee holds hearings and conducts markups of its own, but its work returns to the full committee, which may amend it further before reporting. The distinction matters for gatekeeping because each level carries its own chair and its own calendar, so a measure can be stopped at the subcommittee gate before the full committee ever sees it.

### Q: Why do agriculture committees write nutrition legislation?

Agriculture committees write nutrition legislation because committee jurisdiction follows the written jurisdictional map rather than the subject a program serves in the abstract. Federal nutrition assistance programs developed inside the farm legislation tradition, administered alongside the agricultural programs, and the great farm bills that authorize farm supports carry the nutrition programs in the same statutory vehicle. The agriculture committees therefore draft the food assistance titles of farm bills, even though the beneficiaries are households rather than farms. The pattern is general: the education benefits of the Montgomery GI Bill reached the statute book inside a defense authorization act because the armed services committees held the measure. Jurisdiction is a matter of accumulated assignment, precedent, and statutory placement, not of intuitive subject matching.

### Q: What should a student remember about committees and markups for an exam?

An examination answer on the committee system should hold four propositions together. The markup is the session at which a committee amends a prepared text and votes to report it, and operative statutory language is usually fixed there rather than on the floor. The chair's power to refuse a markup is the system's central gate, exercised without a vote and answerable only to the rarely successful discharge petition. The committee report that follows a markup is the document later interpreters weight most heavily, with its section-by-section analysis and its Ramseyer showing of changes to existing law. And a growing share of major legislation reaches the floor without any markup at all, leaving later readers a thinner record to interpret.
