## The Report She Wrote Around

The long gallery of the Helden Collection smelled of cold stone and old paper and the faint oil used on the hinges of the strong room door. Evening light came through the tall windows in narrow bands and fell across the floorboards, and dust moved slowly in each band. Ilva Marrow sat at the reading table with the provenance file open before her and the Vey Coffer standing on a cloth to her left, and she listened to the house settle. Below, a door closed. Above, in the storerooms on the top floor, a shutter tapped once and then was still. The gallery held shelves along both walls and a row of bound objects in their places, and Ilva had worked among such rooms for thirty years and knew how to be patient with them.

She was in her fifties and she had spent her working life among other people's stored objects, first as a junior clerk in a Valtessa warehouse where bound crates arrived with seals intact and papers in three tongues, then in a chancery archive where the ink had faded to brown on the oldest pages, and then here in Melora, as archivist to the Helden Collection, a family trust collection kept in a tall house above the court quarter. The trust paid her to keep the records straight and to say when a paper did not match the thing it claimed to describe. She had learned early that a trust did not want a problem unless the problem could be solved without noise, and that a quiet house preferred a quiet report. She had learned to leave the truth where a careful reader could find it, and she had learned that most readers were not careful.

![A tall house of stored objects above a court quarter at dusk - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The coffer had come to the collection on deposit, held for a seller who wished to put it through the Melora court sale. It was a small iron bound coffer, plain in its making, with a binding that fixed the lid so that it could not be opened without the binding showing the opening forever. Its iron bands were dull in the evening light and the wood beneath them was dark with age and handling. It looked like a thing carried under seal for a long time, and that was part of its value. A coffer like it had once carried Accord instruments between members in the early Sessions, and the trust papers said so in a careful hand on the first page of the file.

The provenance ran to seven transfers across two centuries, written in four hands and four tongues as the object crossed members. Ilva read it slowly on the first evening, page by page, with a lamp beside her and the windows darkening. The first page was a Valtessa trade hand, quick and slanted, with a seal pressed beside the signature. The second was a Dravin chancery hand, formal and upright. The third was a Melora court hand, older than the fifth page and steadier in its forms. The fourth was a Barrow mill hand, rough and practical, with figures in a margin. Ilva turned the pages with a bone folder and made notes in pencil on a separate sheet, and she did not hurry. Hurry was how mistakes survived.

On the first evening she found nothing wrong. The hands matched their periods. The seals were worn in the right places. The early pages were thin where they had been folded and refolded, and the later pages were thicker, as paper became when the mills changed their pressing. She read the transfers in order and wrote down the holders and the places where the coffer had rested, and she felt the small satisfaction of a chain that held. She closed the file when the lamp began to smoke and walked home through the court quarter, with the streets quiet and the lamps lit in the windows of the advocates houses.

On the second evening she began again at the fifth transfer, because something about the page had stayed with her through the night. The page was a Melora court receipt, written in a court hand that imitated its period well. The letters were formed with care, the spacing was even, the signature sat in the right place, and the seal beside it was clear. At first glance it was a sound page, and a reader in a hurry would have passed it without a second thought. Ilva laid it beside the third page, also a Melora court hand from an earlier year, and looked at the two together under the lamp, and then she saw it.

The fifth page lifted the pen where the period hand pressed. It was a small thing, a habit of the hand rather than a shape of the letters, and only a person who had read court hands for thirty years would have noticed it. In the third page the writer pressed through the downstrokes and kept the pen on the paper through the joins, so the ink pooled slightly at the turns. In the fifth page the writer lifted the pen at the same places, leaving a small break in the stroke and a lighter touch at the end of each word. The imitation was careful and the letters were right, but the hand behind them was not a court hand of that year. It was a later hand copying a court hand, and it had copied the shapes without copying the pressure.

She sat still with the two pages side by side and felt the cold of the gallery settle into her shoulders. She looked at the toll entry in the margin of the fifth page, and there was the second flaw, plainer once she saw it. The toll entry was written in a form the court had stopped using before the page's claimed date. She knew the forms by heart, because the collection held receipts from every stretch of the last two centuries, and the form on the fifth page belonged to an earlier period. It was not a mistake a clerk of the claimed year could have made. A clerk wrote the form he had been taught, every day, until the court changed it, and after the change he wrote the new form. The form on the page had been retired before the date written at its head.

She checked herself twice, because such a finding could not rest on memory. She took the trust's own book of court forms from the strong room, a bound volume kept for just this purpose, and found when the form had been retired and when its replacement had come into use, and wrote both down. She laid the fifth page beside a receipt from the claimed year, written by a different clerk in the same court, and the difference was clear. That receipt used the new form. The fifth page used the old one. The page was wrong in its habit and wrong in its form, and the two faults pointed the same way.

The coffer stood beside her, plain and quiet, and Ilva thought about what the flaw meant. Every other page was sound. The first four transfers held, and the sixth and seventh held. Only the fifth page was false, and it covered the transfer joining the fourth holder to the fifth, a Barrow mill holder whose own records ended and a court receipt that claimed to close the gap. If the page was false, the chain did not document what had happened between the fourth transfer and the fifth, and the years between were unaccounted for. She did not know where the coffer had been in those years, or who had written the page. She knew only that the page was false, and that the sale would go forward on the strength of the whole chain unless someone stopped it.

She knew the trust, and she knew how a family trust handled a flaw in a paper it wished to sell. A plain report would have been buried. The trust would have thanked her, filed her report where no court officer would read it, set her aside, and found another archivist who would write a smoother report. The object would have been sold through a quieter house, and the lie would have travelled with it into a private vault forever. The system rewarded the smooth report and punished the honest one, and she knew it as surely as she knew the court forms. She had seen archivists in other houses write plainly and be set aside, their names dropped from the lists. A plain report was not a protection. It was a way of losing the only chance to stop the sale.

Ilva did not write a plain report. She wrote a report that recorded the flaw in words a careful reader could find and a hurried reader would slide past. She wrote that the fifth transfer page showed a court hand of good period imitation, with a pen habit that differed from the period hand in its lifts, and that the toll entry appeared in a form the court registers showed in use in an earlier period, and she set those sentences among longer sentences about the soundness of the other pages and the general condition of the file. She wrote that the provenance as a whole presented as coherent, and that the fifth page merited close attention from any reader concerned with the continuity of the chain. She did not call the page false, and she did not call it a forgery. She wrote a reservation, placed where a person looking for it could find it, and signed the report with her full name and the years she had served the collection.

She read it through twice before she sealed it, weighing each sentence. The reservation was there. It was not hidden, and it was not shouted. A court officer reading in haste would see only a sound provenance and a sale ready to proceed. A reader who had come to the file because something in the sale filings had caught her eye would stop at the sentences about the pen habit and the toll form and would know what Ilva had found. Ilva did not know who that reader would be. She knew only that the report had to reach a desk that would act on it, and that a plain report would never reach such a desk at all.

When she finished, the gallery was dark except for her lamp and the strong room door stood locked. She put the file back in its box and the coffer back on its shelf in the strong room, and left the report where it would be copied into the sale filings in the ordinary course. She walked home through the court quarter with the streets empty and the lamps burning low. She did not sleep well. She lay awake thinking about the pen that had lifted where it should have pressed, and about the clerk who would copy her report in the morning and might smooth the reservation away. She had done what she could. She had written the flaw in words that would survive a hurried reading, and she had put the report where it would enter the record. The rest was out of her hands.

The report went into the sale filings, reservation and all, because the clerk copied it in full, as clerks do when they are not told to do otherwise. Ilva watched it go and said nothing to anyone in the house, and she kept her pencil notes folded in her desk drawer, where they would wait for the reader who came looking for them. She had written around the flaw because a plain report would have been buried and the object sold quietly elsewhere. Her careful wording was the only reason the file would ever reach a desk that would act on it, and she went on with her work in the long gallery, among the bound objects and the paper and the cold stone, and waited.

## Damages the Accord Cannot Afford

Petra Dunmore's room at the Ninth Office in Carrowmere smelled of lamp oil and paper dust and the iron of the stove that never quite warmed the far corner. Rain touched the high windows in a thin, uneven patter and the light in the room was grey and steady, the kind of light that made every ink colour look the same. Mira Kovac stood just inside the door with her coat still damp at the shoulders and listened to the building around them, clerks moving in the corridor, a drawer closing, a low voice in the next room stopping mid sentence. Dunmore sat at her desk with two files open side by side and a third closed under her hand, and she did not look up until Mira had shut the door.

The seller was Edric Vay, a dealer in restricted objects acting for an unnamed principal, polished and unhurried in every dealing the Office had recorded. Dunmore said his name first, before she said anything about the object, because his rights governed the shape of the whole case. Under the Melora court sale rules Vay held a seller's right of withdrawal, his to use at any point before the sale hour, for any reason he chose to give or for none at all. If he withdrew, the object left the court sale and returned to private keeping and the Office lost its handle on it. The sale itself was set for the third day, in Melora, at the tenth hour of the morning, in the court sale room above the arbitration courts where the Helden Collection itself stood a short walk away. Dunmore laid those facts down the way she laid down everything, in order, without decoration, and Mira memorised them in the order they were given.

Then Dunmore named the cost, up front, before she asked Mira to do anything at all. If the Office intervened in the sale and the Office was wrong, the Accord would pay damages it could not afford, assessed against the sale price of the object and the trust's costs, and the money was only the first part of the loss. The Office's right to intervene in court sales would go with the money. A failed intervention in Melora, in the member that hosted the arbitration courts, would be read by every other member as a reason to close a door the Office had spent years keeping open. Dunmore said it plainly and then waited, because she wanted Mira to carry the cost into every judgement that followed rather than discover it at the end.

Mira was at full strength that morning and she knew it the way she knew the weight of a cup in her hand. She had slept, she had eaten, and her hands answered when she asked them to. She entered the case at full endurance, and the only weight she carried in with her was old and settled. She carried Edran Voss's last ten minutes, from the first case of the season, lightly now, a cold patch she had learned to walk around rather than through. She carried the keeper's shame from the Corra Lock case still with her, fresher than the rest, a shame that was not hers and that she had taken on in a lock chamber and had not yet set down. It sat behind her other thoughts and coloured a room full of other people's paperwork before she had read a word of it. Dunmore knew about the lock case and did not mention it. She pushed the first file across the desk instead.

The first file was the sale filings, the ordinary papers of a court sale for a restricted object, copied into the Office in the ordinary course because Dunmore read court sale filings the way other analysts read correspondence, line by line, looking for the inconsistency in other people's paperwork. The object was the Vey Coffer, a small iron bound coffer held on deposit in the Helden Collection in Melora, offered by Vay for an unnamed principal, its binding fixing its lid so that it could not be opened without the binding showing the opening forever. Objects of its kind were held only under licence, and a sale through the Melora court required an authenticated provenance. The filings gave the provenance in summary, seven transfers across two centuries, and gave the sale day and the sale hour and the seller's name, and most readers in the Office would have passed the file to the registry without stopping.

Dunmore had stopped. She pushed the second file across, and that file was the archivist's report, written by Ilva Marrow of the Helden Collection, who had been engaged and paid by the trust to authenticate the provenance. Mira read it standing, because Dunmore had not offered her a chair and because some reports were better read on her feet. Marrow's prose was smooth and careful and it praised the soundness of the chain in the way such reports always did, page by page, holder by holder. Halfway down the second page Mira felt the texture of the sentences change under her eye, the way a dock inspector felt a seam change under a thumb. Two sentences sat among the longer ones, plain and unadorned, recording that the fifth transfer page showed a court hand of good period imitation whose pen habit differed from the period hand in its lifts, and that its toll entry appeared in a form the registers showed in use in an earlier period. Mira read those sentences twice. A hurried reader would have slid past them. Dunmore had not slid past them, and neither, standing there, did she.

Dunmore watched her reach the sentences and said that Marrow had written her report around a flaw she had seen, and had chosen, or been forced, to record it in a reservation rather than in plain words. Whether the wording had been protection or caution or something less clean was a question for later. The immediate question was what the Office could stand behind. A reservation in an archivist's report was not proof, and Dunmore was dry and precise about the difference. It was a reason to look, not a reason to stop a sale, and if the Office treated a reason to look as a reason to stop, Vay would withdraw or the court would rule, and the damages Dunmore had named would fall due in full.

It was not the first time a true thing handed to the Accord had become heavy in the using, and she thought of [the trade lever Commissioner Harrand had pulled at Melora](/2026/11/02/nightjar-lever-at-melora-003/) and of the delegate who had nearly resigned over a settled debt. Mira set the report down square with the edge of the desk and thought about the weight of a reservation. Marrow had put the flaw in the record where it could not be unsaid, and in doing so she had made the flaw the Office's problem as much as her own. A smooth report would have let the sale pass unremarked. A plain report, Dunmore agreed when Mira said it aloud, might never have reached Carrowmere at all. The reservation had travelled because it had been written to travel, tucked into filings that clerks copied in full, and it had landed on the one desk in the Office most likely to stop at it.

Dunmore set out the task without raising her voice. Mira was to go to Melora, read the provenance in full in the collection, read the coffer itself if the trust and the archivist allowed it, and establish whether the reservation rested on anything the Office could carry into a court room before the tenth hour on the third day. She was not to requisition the object. A requisition would be served on the seller as well as the trust, and Vay was not a man to wait politely under a requisition when withdrawal cost him nothing. She was to work through Marrow where she could, and through the papers where she could not, and she was to keep the seller unwarned for as long as the work allowed. Dunmore gave her the filings to carry and a copy of the report, and she kept the originals on her own desk, squared under her hand, in case anything that left the building failed to return.

Mira asked what degree of wrong the fifth page had to carry before Dunmore would act on it, and Dunmore answered as an analyst answers, by separating the questions the file had actually opened from the ones it had not. The filings seeded a suspicion that a page in the chain might be forged, and no more than a suspicion yet. The other possibilities belonged to the evidence in Melora and Mira was not to close any of them in Carrowmere on the strength of two careful sentences. She was to hold the forgery suspicion seriously and hold it lightly at the same time, which Dunmore acknowledged was an uncomfortable instruction and gave anyway. What could be settled in Carrowmere, Dunmore would settle herself, checking forms against registers and dates against years, and she would have her part ready before the sale hour if her part could be made ready at all.

The keeper's shame stirred as Mira picked up the files, not at anything in the papers but at the thought of the Helden gallery itself, a room full of other people's stored centuries waiting to be read. She had carried the shame out of the Corra Lock chamber on her own shoulders and it had thinned since, and it had not gone. She noted its presence the way she noted the rain and the damp in her coat, as a condition of the day rather than a warning, and her endurance held steady under it. Voss's last ten minutes stayed light where she had put them. She was as ready as the Office could ask her to be, and Dunmore, who measured readiness in other people with the same care she measured paperwork, seemed to reach the same conclusion and did not waste words on encouragement.

She left the Office with the sale filings under her arm and the archivist's careful sentences fixed in her memory, and she took the road for Melora with two days of work between her and a sale hour that would not move. The cost stayed where Dunmore had put it, at the front of the case, where it belonged. If she was wrong, the Accord paid damages it could not afford and the Office lost its right to intervene in court sales. If she was right and slow, Vay withdrew and the coffer vanished into private keeping with its papers unchallenged. If she was right and in time, the sale stopped and the papers broke in public, in the one court where such a breaking carried weight with every other member. Mira walked through the rain to the carriage point with the files kept dry inside her coat, and she began, as she always began, by reading the filings again from the first page while the road out of Carrowmere opened ahead of her.

## Seven Transfers and One Object

The tall house of the Helden Collection stood above the court quarter of Melora, its windows looking down over tiled roofs toward the arbitration courts, and Mira climbed its stone stair with the cold of the Corra Lock chamber still sitting low in her chest and the keeper's shame from the night before riding with it like a second coat she had not asked to wear. Ilva Marrow met her at the door of the long gallery, a room that ran the full length of the house with tall cases down both walls, and beyond it lay the strong room behind its iron door, and above both ran the stair to the top floor storerooms where the trust kept what the gallery could not hold. Beeswax and dust and old paper filled the air, and the light that came through the high windows fell in long pale bars across a floor of dark wood worn smooth by a century of careful feet, and the whole house held the hush of a place where things were kept rather than used.

Ilva Marrow was a woman in her fifties with grey threading her dark hair and ink on the edge of one thumb, and she carried herself with the quiet economy of someone who had spent thirty years among other people's stored objects and had learned to move among them without disturbing anything. The trust had engaged her and paid her to authenticate the provenance of the Vey Coffer, and she had done the work and found the flaw, and she had written her report around it in wording that recorded a reservation a careful reader could find and a hurried reader would slide past. She did not apologise for that wording and she did not defend it either, and Mira understood the shape of it at once, because a woman who wrote plainly about a fault in a thing her employer meant to sell would find her report buried and herself dismissed, and the object would travel on through a quieter house into a private vault where no desk would ever see it again. Ilva had chosen the one form of honesty the house would let through, and the choice had cost her sleep, and Mira could see the cost in the set of her shoulders.

They walked the long gallery together, and Ilva named nothing until Mira asked, and Mira asked about very little, because the gallery was a room to look at first and to touch only when the looking had done its work. The cases held bound objects and restricted things in rows, small iron boxes and sealed caskets and wrapped bundles with tags tied in faded ribbon, and each one sat in its own shallow pool of stillness, and the glass of the cases caught the light and threw it back in thin bright lines. Two centuries of keeping pressed on the room in a way Mira felt in her teeth before she felt it anywhere else, and she kept her hands at her sides and her attention on the wood and the glass and the iron, because a room full of other people's stored centuries was not a thing to take in all at once, and she had learned long ago that patience was the only way to stand in a place like this and come out of it with her judgement intact. Edran Voss's last ten minutes came back to her on the stairwell between the cases, uninvited and brief, the way such things did when a house asked her to stand still, and she let it pass through her and kept walking, and the keeper's shame from the Corra Lock chamber walked beside it, quieter and heavier, a borrowed feeling she had not yet set down.

The strong room stood at the end of the gallery behind a door of iron-bound oak, and Ilva opened it with a key from a ring she wore at her waist, and the room inside was small and windowless and lined with shelves of dark wood, and the air in it was cooler and drier than the air in the gallery, and it smelled of iron and cedar and old paper. The Vey Coffer stood on a middle shelf at the height of Mira's chest, alone on a square of undyed cloth, and it was smaller than she had expected, no longer than her forearm and no wider than both her hands set side by side, and its iron bands were plain and dark with age, and its lid sat flush against its body with a seam so fine she had to look twice to find it. The binding fixed the lid so that it could not be opened without the binding showing the opening forever, and the knots of the binding ran in a pattern she had seen drawn in books, a patient interlaced working that repeated itself around the hasp and down the sides, and the whole object had the unadorned look of a thing made to carry papers between members in the early Sessions, when the Accord was young and its instruments travelled in boxes rather than in satchels.

Ilva stood back and let her look, and Mira looked for a long time, because the coffer was the centre of the case and everything else in the room was arranged around it, and the plainness of it was the first thing that troubled her. A Binder of two centuries ago, working for the Accord in its early Sessions, would have had the skill to make something finer, and the ironwork here was honest and unadorned and spare, the kind of work a later hand could manage with patience and a good pattern book. The knots were the same story, a pattern a later Binder could copy if he studied the old work closely enough and had the time to practise it, and the pattern repeated without variation around the whole coffer, which was either the mark of a master working in a settled tradition or the mark of a copyist who had learned one pattern well and had not dared to vary it. She turned the thought over and set it down again, because a thought was not evidence, and the coffer sat on its cloth and gave her nothing but its shape.

Edric Vay had offered the coffer for sale through the Melora court sale on the third story day, acting for an unnamed principal, and everything Mira had been told about him fitted the shape of a man who could sell a made thing on beautiful papers. He was polished and unhurried, a dealer in restricted objects who knew the court sale rules the way a customs man knew the bonded cargo lists, and he held a seller's right of withdrawal under the Melora court sale rules, which meant he could take the coffer back before the sale hour and walk away without charge, and the Office would have no hold on it and no record of where it went. A man like that did not need to shout or hurry, and he did not need to forge anything with his own hands either, because a dealer of his polish could find the hands and pay for them and keep his own distance from the work. The thought sat beside the plain ironwork and the copyable knots and made a neat and persuasive shape, and Mira did not like neat shapes, because neat shapes were usually the ones someone had built for her to find.

Ilva laid the provenance out on the strong room table in the order of its transfers, seven documents in four hands and four tongues, and the papers had the soft worn feel of documents that had been folded and unfolded many times by many hands. The earliest was written in a Valtessa trade hand, quick and slanted, the letters leaning forward as if they were in a hurry to get to the next port, and the second and third were written in a Dravin chancery hand, slow and upright, each letter set down with the care of a clerk who knew his work would be read by men above him. The fourth was written in a Barrow mill hand, rough and practical, the ink blotted in places where the writer had pressed too hard, and the last three were written in a Melora court hand, neat and narrow, the letters standing in even rows like soldiers on parade. Four tongues and four hands across two centuries, and the object had crossed members with each transfer, and the papers told the story of those crossings in the voices of the people who had recorded them.

The flaw sat in the fifth transfer, and Ilva showed it to her without pointing at it, the way a good archivist showed a thing, by laying the page beside the others and letting the difference speak. The page was a Melora court receipt, written in a court hand that imitated its period well, and it was wrong in one habit, a lift of the pen where the period hand pressed, a small lightness at the end of certain strokes that no clerk of that year would have made. Mira held the page to the light and saw it, and then she saw it again on the next line, and then she could not stop seeing it, and the page seemed to change under her eyes from a record into a performance. The page also carried a toll entry in a form the court had stopped using before the page's claimed date, and Ilva named the form and the year it had been retired, and Mira wrote both down in her notebook in her own small hand, because a form was a thing that could be checked against a register, and a register was a thing that did not lie about its own years.

Ilva gave her leave to handle the coffer, and Mira lifted it from its cloth with both hands the way she had lifted bonded cargo at the Valtessa docks in her customs years, palms flat and fingers spread, and she made the read a preliminary handling read only, the lightest kind she knew, the kind that took in the surface of a thing and stopped there. The coffer was heavy. That was all the read gave her, and she let it be all, because heaviness was a fact a scale could have told her and she did not trust a fact that cost nothing. The weight sat in her forearms and in the small bones of her wrists, and it was more weight than the iron and the wood of the box seemed to account for, and she set the coffer down again on its cloth with care and stood with her hands open at her sides while the cold of the handling settled into her fingers. The cost arrived the way it always arrived, small and unmistakable, a chill that found the joints of her hands and stayed there, and a sense of old keeping pressing at the back of her eyes, grief and fear and long repetition held in metal the way metal held such things best. She did not push further. A full read of the binding would be a comparative read, the coffer set against other bound objects whose ages no one disputed, and that was work for its proper hour, with Ilva present and the method named aloud before the result. What she held from the handling was a single plain fact, that the object was heavy, and she wrote the fact down and wrote nothing beside it, because a fact without a conclusion was the only honest kind she had.

She went back to the papers and worked through them document by document, the way she had worked through bonded cargo manifests at Valtessa, one line at a time and never trusting the shape of the whole until the parts had been checked. The Valtessa trade bill recorded the coffer passing from a factor's hands into the keeping of a merchant house, and it rested on a ledger entry and a factor's seal, and the seal was worn and the ink of it had spread into the fibres of the page with age, and the bill named the coffer by its marks and its weight and the pattern of its binding. The Dravin chancery receipt that followed was a slower and prouder document, and it rested on a roll copy kept in the chancery itself, and its wording treated the coffer as a thing of consequence, an instrument box of the early Accord, and it listed the seals that had been placed on it and the hands that had witnessed the placing. The Dravin warrant that came third rested on the keeper's mark and the warrant itself, and it moved the coffer from the chancery's keeping into the hands of a private holder travelling south, and its language had the dry completeness of a clerk closing a file he expected to be read after his death. Each of those three documents held together, and each one agreed with the others about the marks and the weight and the pattern, and the agreement was the kind that came from men recording the same object rather than the kind that came from men copying each other's words.

The fourth document was the Barrow mill record, and it was the plainest of the seven, a day book entry in a rough hand that recorded the coffer arriving at the mill in the keeping of a man who stored things for people who could not store them at home, and the mill record rested on the day book alone and on the mill holder's habit of writing down everything that came through his door, whether it mattered or not. The entry named the coffer and its marks and said it had been left for safekeeping, and the pages after it in the day book went on recording grain and cloth and tools and the ordinary traffic of a mill, and then the record ended, not in a flourish and not in a disaster, but the way records ended when a man died or grew old or stopped writing. Nothing after that entry spoke of the coffer at all, and the silence in the mill record stretched forward across forty years that no document in the chain covered, and Mira sat with that silence for a while and let it be as large as it was. A mill holder's records ending proved nothing about the object, because records ended for a dozen ordinary reasons, and yet the gap sat in the middle of the chain like a missing stair, and everything on the far side of it had to be reached by stepping over a space where no paper bore any weight at all.

The fifth document was the Melora court receipt, and it claimed to bring the coffer out of that silence and into the court's keeping in a single neat step, and it rested on itself alone, one receipt in a court hand with no register entry cited beside it and no second document to agree with it. Mira read it three times, and each time it told her less than the page before it had told her, because the receipt named the coffer and claimed its transfer and offered no witness and no roll and no seal beyond its own. The sixth document, a Melora sale bill, rested on witness marks and a bill of sale in proper form, and it moved the coffer from the court's orbit into the hands of a private holder who kept it for the rest of his life, and the seventh, the Helden deposit note, rested on the trust's own seal and the note itself, and it recorded the object coming into the collection on deposit, where it had stayed while the decades turned. What the seven documents settled, set down together, was the shape of the whole claim, each transfer resting on what it rested on, and the thinness of the fifth step visible only when the seven stood side by side.

She sat with the seven documents in front of her and held the three readings of them apart in her mind the way she had been taught to hold cargo claims apart, giving each one its full weight before letting any of them lean on the others. One reading said the object was a forgery, made to be sold on its papers, and the reading had real strength in it, because the ironwork was plain for its claimed period and the knots were of a pattern a later Binder could copy with study and patience, and a dealer of Vay's polish was exactly the man to carry a made thing into a court sale on beautiful papers and to do it with a straight face and an unhurried voice. A thing did not have to be old to be sold as old, it only had to be sold by a man whom other men believed, and Vay was believed the way polished men were believed, without anyone quite remembering when the believing had started. That reading explained the false page as well, because a forged object needed forged papers, and a single false page among six true ones was a subtler thing than a whole false chain, and subtlety was the mark of a man who understood how buyers read.

A second reading said the object was real and the papers had been improved, polished somewhere along the chain to raise the price, and that reading had its own strength, because genuine objects gathered improved papers the way old houses gathered new roofs, and a coffer with a gap in its story sold for less than a coffer with a clean chain, and men who held real things had been tidying their papers since papers began. Under that reading the fifth page was a repair rather than a foundation, a patch laid over a hole by a holder who had the true object in his hands and needed the court to see a smooth road behind it, and the pen that lifted where the period hand pressed belonged to a hired clerk working carefully and working late, copying a form he had studied and getting one habit wrong. That reading explained why six of the seven documents held together so honestly, because a chain that had been repaired in one place still carried the truth in the others, and it explained the toll entry as the kind of detail a copyist got wrong when he worked from an older example than the year he claimed. It also left a question standing in the middle of the room that Mira could not put down, which was whether the seller knew the papers had been improved, because a dealer could sell a polished chain in honest ignorance, and the Office's case changed shape depending on the answer, and nothing in the papers told her which man Vay was.

A third reading said the object was real and the papers concealed where it had been for forty years, and that reading was the one that sat heaviest with her, because the gap between the fourth transfer and the fifth was not a thin place in the paperwork but a whole stretch of the object's life that no document covered at all. The mill record ended and the court receipt began, and between the ending and the beginning lay forty years in which a bound and restricted object, an instrument box of the early Accord, had been somewhere and had been held by someone and had been carried across distances by hands no page named. Objects like the coffer did not sit still for forty years by accident, and the years it had spent unrecorded were years in which it could have carried anything the Accord's early instruments had carried, under seal, in the keeping of men who had reasons of their own for leaving no paper. Under that reading the false page was not a polish and not a foundation but a lid, laid over the gap to make the gap look like a step, and the man who had laid it had known exactly which forty years he was covering and had chosen a court receipt to cover them with, because a court paper was the kind other men stopped questioning. Mira held all three readings open and let none of them close, because the heaviness of the coffer in her hands argued for age and argued for nothing else, and heaviness alone could not choose between a real object with polished papers and a real object with a buried middle, and choosing early was the mistake that had ended more cases than any forgery.

The toll entry was the one thread in the fifth page that led out of the room and into a register, and she set the checking of it in motion before she left the strong room. She wrote a message to Petra Dunmore at the Carrowmere desk, short and precise in the way Dunmore liked messages to be, naming the form of the toll entry and the year the court had retired it and the claimed date of the receipt, and asking for the form to be checked against the court registers in Carrowmere and in Melora, entry by entry, against the years the form had been in use. Dunmore was the right reader for such work, dry and exact and happiest when the inconsistency sat in someone else's paperwork, and a register either held the form in the claimed year or it did not, and no amount of polish could argue with a register once the comparison had been run. Mira sealed the message and gave it to the house porter for the court quarter post, and then she asked Ilva to show her the top floor, because a collection was a whole house and not only the room where its prize object stood, and she wanted the measure of the storerooms before she left Melora with her judgement half formed.

The top floor ran the length of the house under the slope of the roof, and the storerooms stood in a row along its corridor, narrow doors of painted wood with iron latches and small paper labels tied to their handles in Ilva's neat hand. The corridor smelled of warm dust and stored cloth and the faint sweetness of old timber heated by the roof above it, and the light came in at the far end through a round window that threw a pale disc onto the boards, and the boards creaked under their feet in a rhythm that changed as they passed each door. Ilva named what each room held as they walked, wrapped furniture and boxed instruments and the overflow of two centuries of keeping, things too plain for the gallery and too bound for any other house, and she spoke of them with the flat affection of a woman describing shelves she had dusted for thirty years. The storerooms were the collection's memory of itself, the place where the Helden trust kept what it could not display and would not sell, and Mira walked the corridor slowly and took in the doors and the labels and the latches and the smell of stored years, and she understood that a house which kept this much had kept its habits too, and that one of its habits was silence about the things that arrived without papers. The north storeroom stood at the end of the top floor with its door shut, and it put her in mind of [the transit room at Carrowmere where a supervisor died](/2026/11/01/nightjar-clean-room-001/) and the room had kept nothing of it at all. She stopped in front of that door for a moment and looked at its latch and its label, and she did not touch the latch and she did not reach for the room in any way at all, because the day had given her a heavy coffer and a broken chain and three readings that all still stood, and a shut door at the end of a corridor was a thing to note and to leave standing until its hour came.

Before she left the strong room she went over the coffer itself once more, slowly and without touching it, checking the object against the descriptions the documents carried, because the papers and the thing had travelled together for two centuries and each was a witness to the other. The trade bill named three marks, a stamped crescent on the hasp plate, a maker's punch low on the back band, and a shallow groove worn into the wood of the lid where a strap had run for years, and all three marks were there on the coffer in front of her, the crescent softened at one edge, the punch half filled with the dark of age, the groove polished by the passage of a strap long gone. The chancery receipt named the same marks and added the weight, written in the Dravin measure of the period, and the warrant repeated the weight in a second measure, and the two figures agreed with each other the way honest conversions agreed, near and not exact, with the small untidiness of real arithmetic. The mill record gave no weight at all, which fitted a mill holder who weighed grain and cloth for a living and saw no reason to weigh a box left in his keeping, and the court receipt, the fifth document, gave the weight in the Melora measure and gave it exactly, to the smallest fraction the measure allowed, and the exactness sat oddly with her for a moment before she understood why. A clerk recording a real transfer weighed a thing or copied a figure from an earlier page, and either way his number carried the smudge of its origin, while the figure on the fifth page had the clean look of a number made to be believed, worked out backwards from the Dravin figures by a man who wanted no questions asked about it. She wrote the observation down and wrote no conclusion beside it, because an exact number was a suspicion and not a proof, and suspicions were the currency she spent most carefully, but the observation joined the lifted pen and the retired toll form on the fifth page, and three small wrongnesses on one page were a pattern, and patterns were the beginning of cases.

Ilva walked her back through the strong room and named the other bound objects on the shelves as they passed, a sealed despatch box older than the coffer by a generation, a pair of instrument cases whose bindings had been repaired twice in living memory, and a small iron casket that had come into the collection with its papers complete and its age disputed by nobody. Mira looked at each of them and kept her hands at her sides, because those objects had a part to play in the work ahead and she wanted to meet them first as shapes and weights on a shelf before she met them any other way. The collection held its restricted things the way the Accord held its instruments, under licence and under lock, and the trust's licence depended on the honesty of its papers, which was why a single false page in a deposit chain mattered out of all proportion to its size, and why Ilva had written her reservation the careful way she had, in the one gap between honesty and survival that the house had left her. Thirty years among stored objects had taught the archivist which silences were safe and which were not, and Mira found herself trusting the woman in the way she trusted very few witnesses, not because Ilva had told her everything, but because Ilva had told her exactly as much as her position allowed and had arranged for the rest to be findable. The flaw had been findable for anyone willing to read a report slowly, and the fact that it had reached Dunmore's desk at all was the proof that Ilva's careful wording had done its work, and Mira carried that thought with her as a reminder that the case existed because one woman had chosen the narrow honest path through a house that rewarded smooth reports and punished plain ones.

She went back down the stair with Ilva and stood once more in the long gallery, and the light had moved across the floor while they worked and lay in longer bars against the cases, and the bound objects sat in their rows behind their glass in the deepening quiet of the house. The coffer waited in the strong room on its square of undyed cloth, heavy in the way she had felt it heavy, and its papers lay on the table beside it in the order of their transfers, seven documents in four hands and four tongues, and the fifth of them lifted its pen where the period hand pressed and carried a toll entry in a retired form, and the fourth of them ended in a mill holder's silence that ran for forty years. Mira had come to Melora to learn what the object could tell a reader, and the object had told her one fact and held the rest behind its binding, and the papers had told her a story with one false page and one long gap, and between the fact and the story stood the seller, polished and unhurried, holding his right of withdrawal like a door he could close at the sale hour. She thanked Ilva for the care of the report as much as for the care of the objects, and she left the house with the cold still in the joints of her hands and the three readings standing side by side in her mind, none of them dismissed and none of them believed, waiting on a register in Carrowmere and on a read she had not yet made.

## A Binding No Forger Would Bother With
The strong room smelled of cold stone and old iron and the faint oil the trust used on hinges that had not been asked to move in a long while. The air sat still against Mira's face, cool enough to make her aware of her own breathing, and the shelves rose on three sides in tight rows, bound boxes and iron strapped cases and leather wrapped bundles packed close under a low ceiling. A single lamp burned on the reading table in the centre, its light pooling on pale wood and leaving the corners of the room in a grey that seemed to hold its own weight. Dust lay thin on the upper shelves, undisturbed except where Ilva's hands had passed. The door behind them was thick, banded, shut, and the quiet of the room had the particular quality of a place built to keep things from being heard as much as from being taken. Mira set her satchel down without sound and let the room settle around her, taking in the shelf ends and the labels in Ilva's neat hand and the way the cold came up through the soles of her boots from the stone floor. Somewhere below, the house creaked as it adjusted to the evening, a low sound that travelled through timber and stopped. Ilva stood beside the table with her hands folded, watching Mira the way a woman watches a stranger who has been given leave to touch what she has guarded for thirty years.

On the table, under the lamp, stood the Vey Coffer. It was smaller than Mira had expected, no larger than a box made to hold a pair of folded documents and a seal, its iron dark with age and its lid held by a binding that crossed it in a pattern of knots and turns she had studied in the file photographs and still did not know in the way that knowing came from standing beside a thing. Beside it, at Ilva's elbow, lay the provenance papers in their folder, the seven transfers in four hands, the chain she had already read in the gallery and carried in her head since the morning. Ilva had placed a clean cloth under the coffer and had set out two other objects from the collection on the far side of the table, both bound, both old, both kept here under the same roof and the same licence. One was a deed box of dark wood bound in iron straps, its lid fixed by a binding of the same school as the coffer's, and the other was a dispatch case of stiff leather with a binding wound tight around its hasp, its surface worn smooth where hands had carried it for generations. Mira looked at each in turn and then at Ilva, and Ilva gave a small nod, as if the arrangement itself were a sentence she had already spoken and was waiting for Mira to answer.

Mira spoke the method before she touched anything, because Ilva had asked her to, and because a read done without a stated method was only a feeling dressed as evidence. She would read by comparison, she said, the coffer against the two bound objects whose ages no one in the collection disputed, all three read in the same hour and in the same room, so that the cold of the stone and the lamplight and her own state were the same for each. She would read the deed box first, then the dispatch case, then the coffer last, and she would take each binding in her hands in turn, palm flat against the knots where a Binder's work held, and listen for what the weight of each object carried. She would not ask the weight for names or for dates or for the story of any hand that had touched them, because weight did not give such things, and she would not ask it for the content of any message the coffer had once carried, because two centuries left almost nothing of content behind. She would ask only for age, for the simple fact of whether the binding had been laid down long ago and left to gather the slow accumulation of years, or whether it had been laid down within living memory and dressed to look older than it was. Ilva listened without interrupting, her eyes on Mira's hands, and when Mira finished she said that the method was fair and that the two comparison pieces were the right ones, because their histories in the collection were unbroken and their ages were a matter of record rather than argument. Mira nodded and turned to the deed box, and set her hand upon its binding.

The deed box gave her age at once, in the way that old bound things did, not as a picture or a word but as a pressure against her palm, a heaviness that sat low and steady and seemed to have settled into the iron over a long span of use. It was the weight of a thing that had been kept and carried and set down again across many hands, the accumulated intent of people who had trusted a binding to hold what mattered to them and had checked it and tightened it and repaired it when it frayed. She felt the faint trace of old repairs under her fingers, places where a later hand had rewound a strand or replaced a knot with a patient imitation of the first maker's work, and beneath that the older layer, worn smooth by handling and by the slow passage of time in a room like this one. The feeling was not sharp. It was broad and low, like the sound of a room full of people speaking at once from a great distance, and it left a coldness in her palm that spread up her wrist and settled in her forearm. She lifted her hand and flexed her fingers, and the cold stayed, a small cost taken and kept. The keeper's shame from the lock chamber in Carrowmere was still with her too, a quiet weight at the back of her thoughts that she had carried into the house and had not set down, and for a moment the two feelings sat side by side, the old shame and the old box, and she had to separate them by an act of attention before she could go on.

She moved to the dispatch case and laid her hand on its binding in the same way, palm flat, fingers spread, her breathing slowed until the room seemed to narrow to the lamp and the leather under her hand. The case answered in a different register, lighter in one sense and sharper in another, the weight of a thing that had travelled rather than rested, carried on roads and set down in inns and offices and taken up again in a hurry more than once. There was fear in it, faint and old, the kind that belonged to a messenger who had known the value of what he carried, and there was the steady intent of the men and women who had sealed it and expected it to arrive unopened. The binding itself held the same layered quality as the deed box, old work under old repair, a knot replaced here and a strand doubled there, each repair laid down long enough ago that its own weight had settled into the whole. She felt the years in it as a depth rather than a date, a thickness that no fresh work could imitate because fresh work had not yet been lived with. When she lifted her hand the cold followed, a little deeper than before, and her left hand answered a fraction late when she curled it, a small lag she noted and put aside. She stood for a moment with both hands at her sides, letting the two readings settle, and then she turned to the coffer.

The coffer's binding was heavy under her palm in a way that matched the two objects she had just read and surpassed them in depth, a weight that seemed to press down through her hand and into the table beneath it. It was not the weight of a single moment or a single hand. It was the weight of two centuries of keeping, layered like the rings of a tree, old repairs over older work, the accumulated intent of everyone who had ever carried the coffer under seal and set it down and picked it up again with care. She felt the places where a Binder long dead had first laid the knots, and the places where a later hand had repaired them with the same patience, and the places where a third hand had tightened what had loosened, each repair itself now old enough to carry its own faint charge. The iron was cold beneath the binding, and the binding itself seemed to hold the cold of every room it had rested in, a slow accumulation that no maker working in haste could lay down in a season. What she felt was the fact of age, and nothing more. The weight did not tell her who had carried the coffer, or where it had travelled, or what instrument it had once held between members of the Accord. Two centuries was too long a span for content to survive in any form she could read, and the fainter traces had faded until only the broad fact remained, that this binding had been laid down long ago and had been lived with ever since. The fact was enough. She stood with her hand on the coffer and let the weight settle into her, and the cold spread from her palm up her arm and into her shoulder, and a tiredness came with it that was not the tiredness of a long day but the tiredness of carrying too much that belonged to other people at once.

She lifted her hand at last and stepped back from the table, and the room seemed to tilt a little before it steadied. Ilva watched her face and did not speak, and Mira was grateful for the silence, because the read had taken more from her than the two comparison reads had. The shame from the lock chamber was still there, a small hard thing at the edge of her thoughts, and the coffer's weight sat beside it, heavier and older. She drew a slow breath and let it out, and the feeling eased enough for her to speak. She told Ilva what she had found, in plain words and in order, the deed box old, the dispatch case old, the coffer old in the same way and to the same depth, its binding a piece with theirs and unlike anything made within living memory. She said that weight faded with the years and that two centuries left almost nothing, and that what remained was only the fact of age, but that the fact was sufficient for the question they had brought her here to answer. Ilva listened with her hands still folded, and Mira understood that the archivist had expected this answer and had also feared it, because an old object made the papers harder to explain rather than easier.

The craftsmanship argument had been persuasive in the file, and Mira had carried it with her into the strong room as a live possibility, not as a settled conclusion. The coffer's ironwork was plain for its claimed period, plainer than a casual eye might expect from a Binder working two centuries ago, and the binding's knots were of a pattern that a later Binder could study and copy, given time and a steady hand and a set of good photographs. A dealer of Vay's polish was exactly the man to sell a made thing on beautiful papers, and the papers themselves were beautiful, seven transfers in four hands and four tongues, a chain that read like a history written to be admired. The argument had the shape of a complete story. A forger makes a coffer, a forger writes a provenance, a dealer sells the pair through a court that trusts the papers, and the money changes hands before anyone looks closely enough to see the join. Mira had rehearsed it on the road to Melora and had found it difficult to answer, because it explained the plainness of the iron and the elegance of the papers in a single stroke, and because it gave the case a villain whose motives were easy to understand. Standing in the strong room with her hand still cold from the reading, she could see where the argument failed, and the failure was not a small one. It failed on the binding.

A forger able to fake that binding would have spent years making an object worth less than the labour. The work under her palm was not a surface effect laid down in a workshop over a winter. It was two centuries of keeping, repair upon repair, each layer laid down by a different hand in a different decade, each repair itself grown old. To imitate it, a maker would have to lay down a binding, then wait, then repair it as if a generation had passed, then wait again, then repair it a second time, and a third, each time with the patience of a person who believed the object would outlive him. He would have to live with the thing for years, carrying it and setting it down and letting it gather the slow weight of use, and at the end of those years he would hold a small iron coffer of plain workmanship, worth a fraction of the time he had given it. No maker with that skill would choose such a poor return. A man who could lay a binding like that could earn his living ten times over making bindings for the living, for collectors and houses and offices that paid for the work itself, and he would not need to sell a forgery through a court on papers, or to wait for a sale day and a court's examiner and a trust's archivist to pass his work. The economics of the forgery did not hold. The binding was too costly to fake and too cheap to sell, and the man who could fake it had no reason to try.

Mira said this aloud, working through it in front of Ilva rather than presenting it as a finished judgment, because Ilva had lived with the coffer longer than she had and deserved to hear the reasoning rather than the verdict. She spoke of the plain iron and the copied knots and the polish of the dealer, and she gave each point its full weight before she answered it, because a case built on a dismissed argument was a case that would fall the first time a careful examiner asked the same question. She said that the plainness of the ironwork was a fact, and that the knots could be copied in pattern, and that Vay was polished enough to sell a made thing, and that none of those facts survived the binding. The binding was not a pattern. It was a history, laid down in layers that could not be hurried, and the history was old. She watched Ilva's face as she spoke, and saw the archivist's shoulders ease a fraction, as if a weight she had carried alone for weeks had been shared at last, and Mira understood that Ilva had known the craftsmanship argument was the one the trust would raise first, and had been waiting for someone to answer it on the object itself rather than on the papers.

Ilva answered when Mira finished, and her answer came slowly, in the measured voice of a woman who had spent thirty years among other people's stored objects and had learned to speak only when she was certain of her words. She said that she had raised the craftsmanship point herself in her own mind, when she first took the coffer in her hands, and that she had turned it over for days before she wrote her report. She had looked at the plain iron and the knots and had asked herself whether a later hand could have made the whole thing, and she had concluded that it could not, not in the time a forger would have, and not for the price a court sale would bring. She conceded the point without reservation, and Mira noted the concession, because it mattered that the archivist who had found the flaw in the papers had also considered the object itself and had found it sound. Ilva said that her reservation had concerned the papers, never the object, and that she had never doubted the coffer's age or its making, only the chain that claimed to trace its passage from hand to hand. Her words were careful, as they had been in her report, and Mira heard the care in them, the way a woman who had been paid by a trust to authenticate a provenance chose each phrase so that it recorded what she had seen without inviting the trust to bury her for seeing it. Ilva did not state her reservation in plain words, and Mira did not ask her to, not in that room and not in writing, because the plain statement belonged to the close of the case, after the night's work and after the register check had landed, when Ilva could sign her name to it with the Office standing beside her. For the moment, the concession was enough. The object was genuine. The papers were the question.

Mira turned back to the folder on the table and laid her hand on it, not to read it, for paper held weight worst of all and gave her nothing she could trust, but to mark the turn the case had taken. The provenance chain was the frame they had carried since the morning, seven transfers across two centuries, each resting on a document and each document resting on something else, and the chain had held together as a story until the binding broke it open. The object was old. The papers claimed to trace it, and six of the seven transfers rested on documents that bore the marks of their periods and their hands. The lie was in one page. It was the page that covered the fifth transfer, the Melora court receipt, the page Ilva had written around and Dunmore had found by reading the reservation a hurried reader would slide past. The page was the narrow place in the chain, the single sheet where the hand lifted where the period hand pressed and where a toll entry appeared in a form the court had stopped using before the page's claimed date. Everything else in the chain could stand. The fifth transfer could not, and the fifth transfer was the one that covered the gap, the forty years between the fourth transfer, to a Barrow mill holder whose own records ended, and the fifth, the court's receipt. Mira said this to Ilva, naming the gap page as the place where the case would be won or lost, and Ilva nodded, her hand resting on the folder beside Mira's, and the two women stood for a moment in the lamplight with the coffer between them and the papers beneath their hands, and the case seemed to narrow to a single sheet of paper on a table in a cold room.

Dunmore's toll form register check was still running. Mira knew it, because Dunmore had told her before she left Carrowmere that the check would take the evening and perhaps the night, a comparison of the form on the gap page against the court registers entry by entry, against the years the form was in use, carried out in Carrowmere and in Melora both, so that no single archive could be said to have decided the matter alone. The check had not landed, and Mira did not pretend that it had. The binding had killed the forgery reading, and the gap page was the lie, but the proof that would break the provenance before a court's examiner was the register, and the register was still being read. She told Ilva this plainly, because Ilva deserved to know what was settled and what was not, and because a case built on a read alone would not hold before a court that trusted paper over weight. The coffer was genuine. The page was false. The register would say when the form had been retired, and by how long, and whether any genuine clerk of the claimed year could have written it. Until that finding arrived, the case rested on the binding and on Ilva's reservation, and Mira was content to let it rest there for the evening, because the evening had its own work to do.

What remained unknown was larger than what was known. The binding had removed the possibility that the object itself was made to be sold, but it had not settled whether the papers had been improved to raise a price or written to conceal a gap, or whether the seller knew they were false, or who had written the gap page and when. Vay might have known, or he might have bought the papers in good faith from a principal who had kept the object in private keeping for forty years and had needed a page to cover the silence. The storerooms above held their own question, what the collection's upper floor kept. She did not try to answer these questions in the strong room. She let them stand, open and unforced, because the night ahead would answer at least one of them, whether the coffer was still in the strong room when she returned and whether the seller would move before the sale if he felt watched.

The decision that would earn the pressure of the night ahead was made in that room, at the end of the reading, with Ilva present and the lamp still burning and the coffer standing on its cloth under the light. Mira could have requisitioned the object, and the Office had the right to do so, a formal order served on the trust and on the seller both, requiring the coffer to be held and produced for examination. She weighed it, and she set it aside, because a requisition was a warning as much as an order. It would be served on Vay as well as on the trust, and it would tell the seller a full day before the Office could act that the provenance was under challenge. A warned seller withdraws. He exercises his right under the Melora court sale rules, takes the object back into private keeping, and the lie goes with it, unrecorded and unexamined, into a vault or a house where no examiner would ever see it again. The sale would stop, but the case would die with it, and the forty years would remain covered, and the next buyer would face the same papers with no Office file to warn him. Mira said this to Ilva, laying out the choice in the open, so that the archivist could see the reasoning and could weigh it herself, and Ilva listened and did not argue, because she knew the trust and the court and the seller better than Mira did, and she knew that a warned seller would not wait to be examined.

Instead, Mira decided to enter the collection in person that night, on Ilva's key, and to read the rooms above the strong room while the house slept, and to see for herself whether the coffer was still where it stood and what the upper floor held. The decision was watched by the reader of the case, and by Ilva, and by Mira herself, because it was a choice made in the open, with its reasons stated and its risks named, and because it committed her to a night in a house full of stored centuries with a hand that was already slow and a body already carrying the cold of the strong room. Ilva would admit her, on her own key, and would wait below, and Mira would go up alone, because a second person in the storerooms would double the weight she had to carry and would give the house another pair of feet to hear. The toll form register check would go on in Carrowmere and in Melora while she was in the house, Dunmore reading entry by entry against the years the form was in use, and Mira would carry the binding's answer with her up the stairs, the fact of age that had killed the forgery reading and narrowed the case to a single page. She looked at the coffer once more, standing on its cloth under the lamp, its binding dark and old and patient, and she felt the cold in her left hand answer late when she curled it, and she knew that the night would cost her more before it was done. She nodded to Ilva, and Ilva nodded back, and the decision stood, made and witnessed, and the strong room held its quiet around them as the lamp burned on.

## The Collection at Night

Ilva Marrow turned her own key in the side door of the Helden house and stood aside to let Mira pass into the dark hall, and the smell of the place came up at once, cold stone and old wax and dust that had settled into wood over more seasons than Mira could count by scent alone. The lamps in the hall burned low behind glass, and the stair rose ahead of her in a slow turn, and the long gallery ran to the left under a ceiling that held the last of the evening in its high windows. Ilva said nothing after the door shut. She had given Mira the evening and the house and the key, and she had stayed only long enough to see the bolt drawn behind them, and then her footsteps went back down the front steps and out into the court quarter, leaving Mira alone with the collection and the quiet that belonged to a house full of kept things.

Mira stood in the hall until her eyes learned the dark. The cold from the Corra Lock chamber still sat low in her chest, a flat chill that had not left her since the water, and the keeper's shame from that room still moved under it, a dull weight she carried without naming it to herself, a shame that was not hers and had settled into her all the same. She had carried Edran Voss's last ten minutes longer, and she knew the shape of that carrying, and she knew that a room full of other people's stored years would land on top of it and press it down harder. She set her hand on the newel post and felt the stone hold a faint warmth from the day, and she started up the stair toward the gallery with her steps kept soft on the wood.

The long gallery opened before her as a single run of shelves and cases and low chests, two centuries of bound and restricted objects kept in rows under glass and iron, and the air in it was thick with keeping. She did not touch anything at first. She walked the length of it slowly, letting the weight come to her in the way it always came, as feeling left in things by the people who had held them and hidden them and guarded them and grieved over them. A shelf of ledgers bound in iron gave off a steady pressure of care, the long patience of hands that had turned pages in the same order for years. A case of small stone figures held a sharper grief, brief and hard, the kind that comes from a loss kept close and never set down. Cloth and paper along the lower shelves read faint, their weight thinned by the years until only a trace remained, while metal and stone held their charge, iron clasps and carved weights and seals that still carried the intent of the men and women who had closed them and locked them and meant them to stay closed.

She worked shelf after shelf, and the cost came with each read in the way it always came, not as a blow but as an addition, cold settling deeper and a tiredness gathering behind her eyes and the feelings of strangers pressing in until they sat inside her own chest as if they belonged there. Fear from a locked box that had been hidden in a hurry. Keeping from a chest that had been watched through a long winter by someone who had not slept well beside it. Grief from a bundle of letters tied with cord, grief that had been folded and refolded until the paper itself seemed to hold the shape of it. She took it in and kept walking, because that was the work, and because the only way through a room like this was to let it land and carry it and keep her feet moving. Her breath grew shorter in the close air. Her shoulders grew heavy. She stopped at the far end of the gallery and rested her hand on a shelf edge and felt the wood give back nothing but its own age, and she counted her own pulse against the quiet and went on.

The strong room stood at the end of the gallery behind a door of iron-bound oak, and she opened it with the second key Ilva had pressed into her palm and stepped inside into a smaller dark that smelled of iron and dust and cold stone. The Vey Coffer stood on its shelf among other bound objects, a small iron-bound coffer with its lid fixed by a binding that had held for two centuries, and around it the other objects of the room stood in their own places, each holding its own weight in the dark. She read the room the same way she had read the gallery, shelf by shelf and object by object, letting the accumulated intent of the room come to her in a single slow press rather than in separate pieces. Old repairs lay in the weight of the coffer like layers in stone, the care of hands that had mended and sealed and carried it under seal across distances she could not name and did not try to name. The undisputed bound objects beside it read in the same register, heavy with age and with the long intent of keeping, and the coffer sat among them as a thing of the same kind, a thing that had been carried and guarded and meant. She took the read and let it settle, and the cost of it settled with it, a further cold in her fingers and a further weight in her chest, and she stood a moment with her hand open at her side and felt her left hand answer late when she tried to close it, the fingers slow to obey, as if the signal had to travel further than it should.

She moved along the shelf once more before she left, reading each bound object in turn and taking the measure of the room as a whole, and the room gave her the same answer from every side, a long kept weight of intent and grief and fear held in iron and stone and leather, the stored feeling of people who had trusted these shelves to keep what they could not carry themselves. A small casket at the end of the row held a thin bright fear, and a stone weight beside it held only patience, and she took both in and let them settle with the rest, and the settling cost her a further measure of warmth and left her hands cold to the wrist.

She left the strong room and climbed to the top floor, and the climb cost her more than it should have. The stair turned twice in the dark, and each turn asked her to lift her feet and set them down with care, and her legs felt the weight of the evening in them, the gallery and the strong room both still sitting on her shoulders as she rose. She kept one hand on the rail and felt the rail give back a faint trace of the many hands that had used it, a thin worn feeling, and she reached the top landing and stopped to let her breath come back to her. The corridor up here ran the length of the house, with a row of storerooms on the north side, their doors shut and their keys hanging on hooks beside them in a neat line. The air was colder here, and the dust lay thicker on the floor, and the lamps burned lower still, as if the house kept its top floor for storage and for silence.

She opened the first storeroom on the left and read it standing in the doorway. It read normally, a room full of stored things giving off the ordinary weight of two centuries of keeping, boxes and bundles and wrapped parcels holding grief and fear and patient intent in the measure she expected from a house like this. She closed the door and opened the storeroom on the right of the north room and read that one too, and it read normally as well, a shelf of bound volumes holding the steady pressure of long use, a chest holding a sharp bright fear that had been set down and left to fade over years. Both rooms gave her something to hold, both rooms answered when she reached for them with her reading, and both rooms left their small cost in her, a further cold and a further tiredness that she took and carried without stopping.

Then she opened the north storeroom, the middle room of the row, and found nothing there at all. She stood in the doorway and reached for the weight of the room, and there was no weight to reach for, no grief and no fear and no keeping and no intent, no trace of the people who had stored things here and no trace of the things themselves, only a clean empty volume that held its shape across the whole room from floor to ceiling and from wall to wall. She stepped inside and the nothing stayed whole around her, a volume with a sharp edge at the doorway, so that the corridor behind her still read normally and the rooms either side still read normally and this room read as nothing at all. She moved her hand through the air in front of her and felt only air, and she set her palm flat on a shelf and felt only wood and dust, and the shelf held no charge and the boxes on it held no charge and the wrapped parcels held no charge, as if the room had been swept clean of every feeling ever left in it. She walked to the far wall and back, and the nothing held across the whole of it, and her left hand answered late again when she reached for the door frame, the fingers slow and clumsy, and she had to look at her hand to make it close around the wood.

She stood in the corridor with the door of the north storeroom shut behind her and the fact of it sitting in her chest beside the gallery and the strong room and the keeper's shame and the cold from the lock chamber, and she did not try to name what it was. She knew only that one room in this house read as nothing while the rooms either side read as rooms should, and that the nothing had an edge she could feel and a volume she could walk through, and that it gave her nothing at all to carry except the fact of itself. She turned toward the stair to go back down to the strong room and the coffer, and as she turned she heard the service stair door open below.

The sound came up through the house as a soft click and a tread on the lower steps, and then the rustle of packing straw, dry and light, the sound of a man carrying a crate. Mira held still on the landing and listened, and the tread came up another flight, steady and unhurried, a man who knew the stair and knew the house and had climbed it often enough to climb it in the dark. She moved down the main stair without a sound, keeping to the edge of each tread where the wood was firm, and she slipped into the strong room and stood among the shelves in the dark with the door left a finger open, and she waited with her breathing kept slow and her hands open at her sides. The tread reached the gallery floor and crossed it, and the straw rustled again, and the door of the strong room opened wider, and the night porter stepped inside with a crate under one arm and a bundle of packing straw under the other, and he set the crate down on the floor and looked toward the shelf where the coffer stood.

Mira knew him by the shape of his coming. He was the collection's night porter, a man the trust paid by the week and the seller Vay paid by the month, and he had come up the service stair with a crate and packing straw to take the coffer out of the strong room before the sale, to lift it from its shelf and pack it and carry it down and out before the court could hold it. She did not call out. She did not give him a warning he could use. She stepped out from among the shelves and put herself between the man and the shelf where the coffer stood, and she said his task back to him in a low voice, that he had come for the coffer and that the coffer was staying where it stood.

He moved fast. He dropped the straw and reached past her for the shelf, and she caught his arm and turned it and held it against his back with a control hold, her body set firm and her weight set low, and he twisted against the hold and drove them both sideways into the shelves. The crate went over. Straw spilled across the floor. A shelf shook and a bound volume slid and fell with a flat sound, and the dark closed in around them as they grappled among the iron and the wood. Her left hand answered late. It would not close properly around his wrist, and the grip cost her a sharp effort that ran up her arm and into her shoulder, and she had to set her right hand over it and hold with both hands where one should have been enough. She held. She kept her body between the man and the shelf. She kept the coffer behind her where his hands could not reach it.

He pushed hard. She pushed back. The struggle stayed close and short, a press of shoulders and hands in the dark, his breath loud in her ear and her own breath short in her chest, and the shelves pressed at their backs and the crate lay on its side with straw scattered over the floor. She held the control hold and did not let it go, and she did not reach for a weapon, and she did not strike first, and she kept the man turned away from the shelf with the coffer on it, her feet set and her weight set, until he broke free of the hold with a twist that sent him stumbling back toward the door. He did not go for the coffer again. He went for the stair. He ran down the service stair with his feet loud on the steps, and the sound of him went down through the house, and below them the house woke, a door opening and a voice calling out and feet moving on the floor below, lamps being lit and voices rising in the hall.

Mira stood among the shelves in the dark with the crate on its side and the straw scattered and the coffer still on its shelf behind her, and the cost of the struggle sat in her left hand and in her shoulder and in her chest. Her left hand would not close properly. She tried to make a fist and the fingers answered late and would not come together, and it cost her to lift her arm and cost her to turn her head toward the door and cost her to stand upright in the dark with the house waking below. She reached back with her right hand and felt the coffer where it stood, iron-bound and steady on its shelf, and she kept her body between it and the door until the voices below grew louder and the light from the hall reached up the stair and into the gallery. The house was awake. The coffer was still on its shelf. She stood there and waited for the house to come up to her.

## Forty Years Missing

Mira sat on the house stairs above the hall, in the cold that gathered on the upper steps where the lamps below could not reach, and she listened to the house settle below her while the stone of the stair pressed through her skirt and the dust of the landing left its dry smell in her throat. Her left hand rested open on her knee, the fingers unwilling to close properly, and she kept it there without forcing it, while the weight of the gallery and the strong room still sat in her chest and the keeper's shame from the lock chamber still moved under that weight, a quiet pressure she had carried into the house and would carry out of it. She had not yet spoken to Dunmore. She had not yet spoken to the court's officer. She sat in the hour she had left before either of those conversations, and she held three things together in her mind and turned them slowly, the way she turned a bound object to read its faces in order.

She held the archivist's careful report first. Ilva Marrow had found the flaw in the provenance and had written her report around it, in wording that recorded a reservation a careful reader could find and a hurried reader would slide past, and Mira understood the care in that wording and what it had cost Ilva to choose it. Had Ilva written the flaw plainly, the trust would have buried the report and dismissed her, and the object would have been sold through a quieter house, and the lie in its papers would have travelled with it into a private vault where no reader would ever reach it. The careful report was therefore the only reason the file had ever reached a desk that would act on it, and Mira would say that plainly when she spoke, because it was true and because it was owed.

She held the forty year gap second. The provenance ran in four hands and four tongues across two centuries, and every page of it held except the page that covered the fifth transfer, and behind that page lay forty years that no document in the chain covered, years in which the coffer had been somewhere and held by someone and kept for reasons the papers did not record. The gap was the case. The gap was what broke the provenance, and it was what she would lay before the court, together with the register finding and the false form on the page, in words that proved a page false without claiming to prove who had written it.

She held the north storeroom third. In a [lock chamber on the north cut](/2026/11/06/nightjar-charge-that-failed-006/) the nothing had stood in the air with a ceiling above her head, and she had mapped it with her hands in cold water and come up the ladder with the shape of it whole. She had met that shape again on the top floor of this house, a whole room reading as nothing while the rooms either side read normally, and she turned that fact over carefully and set it apart from the evidence, because it was not evidence. She would not name the storeroom's nothing to the court. It was not theirs to weigh, and it proved nothing about the coffer or the page or the seller, and naming it would give the court a mystery to chase in place of the gap it could act on. She would keep the nothing in her own keeping, as she kept the cold from the lock chamber and the shame that was not hers, and she would say only what the papers and the registers and the coffer itself could bear, and no more.

## The Page That Breaks

The court room in Melora smelled of cold stone and old paper, and the morning light came in through tall windows in pale bars that fell across a long table of dark wood. Mira stood at the side of the room with her hands clasped behind her back, because her left hand still answered late and she did not want the examiner to watch it fail. The gallery of the Helden Collection had stayed with her through the hours without sleep, shelf after shelf of stored intent pressed into her shoulders like a weight she could not set down. Dunmore sat at the table with two folders laid square in front of her. The examiner, a man in his sixties with ink on his cuffs and a clerk waiting at his elbow, read without hurry, and the silence in the room had the quality of a place where a decision was being weighed rather than announced.

Mira had slept none of the hours since the top of the house stairs. She had stood there in the dark with the archivist report in her hand and the forty year gap laid out plain in her mind, and she had chosen the order in which the facts would be spoken. The night attempt in the collection had sharpened the choice rather than changing it. A man had come up the service stair to take the coffer before the sale, and the house had woken, and the coffer had stayed where it stood. That fact belonged in the file as a fact, and it did not belong in the argument about the page. The page would stand or fall on its own ink and its own form. The weight of the gallery pressed at the back of her neck, a dull ache that moved when she turned her head, and she let it press. It was the cost of the evening reads, and the cost was hers to carry into the room.

Dunmore opened the first folder and laid the register finding on the table in the plain language she used when a fact could not be softened. The toll form on the gap page, the form that gave the fifth transfer its court stamp and its date, had been retired a decade before the page claimed to have been written. Dunmore had checked it entry by entry against the court registers in Carrowmere and in Melora, year by year, through the span in which the form had been in use, and the registers agreed with each other. The form belonged to an earlier span of court practice, and it had passed out of use long before the claimed year arrived. No genuine court clerk of that year could have written it, because no clerk of that year had that form on his desk to write with. The finding did not rest on a single register or a single hand. It rested on two sets of registers kept in two cities, and on the plain absence of the form from every entry of the claimed year.

The examiner listened with his pen still, and his clerk wrote the finding down in a hand that did not hurry. Mira watched the man read the copies Dunmore had set before him, the page itself and the register entries beside it, and she watched him compare the form on the one with the forms in the others. He asked one question, about the span of years in which the retired form had last been used, and Dunmore answered with the dates from the registers without reaching for her notes. He asked a second question, about whether any court office had kept an old stock of the form past its retirement, and Dunmore answered that the registers showed no entry in the old form after the year of retirement, in either city, across the whole span she had checked. The room stayed quiet while he read again, and the pale light moved a little further across the table.

Ilva Marrow sat at the far end of the table with her hands folded on a second folder, and when the examiner turned to her she opened it and took out a single sheet. The sheet was her supplementary note, written after the night attempt, and it stated her reservation in plain words. It said that the page covering the fifth transfer carried a toll entry in a form the court had stopped using before the page's claimed date, and that the hand on the page lifted the pen where the period hand pressed, and that she had recorded both facts in her first report in wording a careful reader could find. It said that she had not stated them plainly in that first report because she had been engaged and paid by the trust, and because a plain statement would have ended her engagement and buried the report. It said that she stated them plainly in this note, and she had signed it at the foot of the page in a firm hand that did not tremble. The examiner read the note twice, and then he read it a third time, and Ilva sat still under his reading with the stillness of a woman who had waited thirty years among other people's stored objects to be asked a direct question and had decided to answer it.

Mira thought of the archivist's first report as she watched the examiner read the note. The first report had been careful in the way of a woman who knew the system rewarded a smooth report and punished an honest one, and Ilva had been right about the price of plain words. Had she written the flaw plainly at the start, the trust would have buried her report, dismissed her, and sold the object through a quieter house, and the lie would have travelled with it into a private vault forever. Her careful wording had been the only reason the file ever reached a desk that would act on it. The supplementary note did not erase that first caution. It stood beside it as a second act, and it gave the examiner a statement he could rely on without guessing at a reservation hidden between lines.

Dunmore set the two findings side by side on the table, the register finding and the signed note, and she laid the provenance chain beside them so the examiner could see where the false page sat in the sequence of seven transfers. The chain ran from the first holder to the seventh across two centuries, and the fifth transfer was the one that covered the gap between the fourth transfer, to a Barrow mill holder whose own records ended, and the fifth, the court's receipt. The gap was forty years wide, and no document in the chain covered it. The false page had been written to cover that span, and the form on it gave the lie away. The examiner traced the sequence with his finger, stopping at the fifth row, and his clerk marked the place with a small slip of paper. The rest of the chain was not in question in this room. The question was whether one page could be trusted, and the registers and the signed note answered that question together.

The examiner asked Mira what the Office could say about who had written the page, and she answered him plainly, because the answer was the limit of what the Office could claim. The evidence proved a page false. It did not prove who wrote it. The binding on the coffer proved the object old and genuine, and the registers proved the page could not have been written in its claimed year, and neither fact named a hand. Weight could not supply a name. A read of an object recorded intensity and affect left in places and things, and it never identified a person, and it never detected truth in speech, and it could not be asked to point at a writer across two centuries. Mira said that the Office would not ask the court to find a writer on evidence that did not reach that far. The examiner nodded once, and his clerk wrote the limit down beside the finding, and the room seemed to settle a little, as if a boundary had been marked that would hold.

Edric Vay stood by the window through the whole of the reading, polished and unhurried, and he did not speak until the examiner turned to him. When he spoke, his voice was even, and he exercised the right the court sale rules gave a seller, the right of withdrawal that had been established at the start of the filing. He withdrew the coffer from the sale without charge, in the form the rules required, and the examiner accepted the withdrawal and had the clerk record it. Vay did not argue with the register finding, and he did not argue with Ilva's note. He said only that the sale would not go forward, and that he would leave Melora, and he did leave, walking out of the court room with the same unhurried step he had brought into it.

The sale was stopped before its hour. The examiner said the words in the formal phrasing of the court, and his clerk wrote them, and the hour of the sale passed with no bidding and no transfer. The Vey Coffer was held under Accord seal in the collection's strong room, pending an inquiry into the gap years, and the seal was set on the shelf where the coffer stood. It was neither sold nor returned. It would stay under seal while the inquiry looked into the forty years no document covered. Mira stood in the doorway of the strong room later in the morning and looked at the seal, and the coffer sat behind it in the dim light, iron bound and old, and the room around it held the weight of two centuries of stored things in a way that made her shoulders ache.

No charge was brought in the room. The examiner did not name a writer, and the Office did not ask him to, and Vay left Melora without a charge against his name. The cost of the morning was the fact that the sale had been stopped, that the object was held, and that the lie in the papers had been named in a court room where it could not be quietly filed away. The price of being right was a held object and an open inquiry, and a seller who walked away free to sell nothing that day.

Dunmore found her in the corridor and entered a day of rest on her file, a full day out of the field, written in the analyst's neat hand. Mira read the entry and declined it. She had worked the close on no sleep, and the two centuries of the gallery were still on her, in the ache at the back of her neck and in the way her left hand answered late when she reached for a cup, and she knew the rest was owed to her. She declined it all the same, because the inquiry into the gap years would need a reader who had stood in the rooms and read them. Dunmore looked at her for a long moment, and then she left the entry on the file as written, and the two entries stood side by side as the record of a cost accepted and a rest not taken.

She walked out of the court building into the pale light of Melora with the weight of the gallery still on her, and the city sounded ordinary around her, carts and voices and the clatter of a court quarter going about its business. The coffer was held, the sale was stopped, the page was broken, and the seller was gone. The forty years were still missing, and no one in the room had named who wrote the page. She did not sleep on the road back, and she did not expect to. The work of the close was done, and the work of the gap years was still to come.

## In Quotation Marks

Carrowmere received her with grey light and the smell of rain on stone, and the Ninth Office building held its familiar quiet, paper and dust and the faint tang of the metal cabinets along the archive wall. Mira sat at Dunmore's desk in a chair that had been drawn up for her, her coat still damp at the shoulders, and she turned a cup in her hands without drinking from it, because the gallery weight had eased on the road and left a thin tiredness in its place. Dunmore's desk was ordered in the way of a woman who kept her work in rows, files stacked by year with their labels facing out, a pen laid straight beside a blotter, and the window behind her showing a piece of the courtyard where the rain had begun to spot the flags. Mira had slept a little on the road and woken with the phrase of the morning still in her head, the examiner's formal words, the sale stopped before its hour.

Dunmore had searched the archive while Mira was still in Melora, looking for any earlier Office dealing with the coffer's chain, any filing or note in which the object or its holders had crossed a desk before. She described the search in the spare language she used for a hunt through paper, shelf by shelf, through the sale filings and the older cases that touched the Helden Collection and the names in the chain. Most of what she found was the ordinary record of a restricted object kept on deposit, licences and inspections and the dull correspondence of a trust accounting for a holding. One file did not belong to that ordinary record. It was a Ninth Office file from eighteen years ago, kept in an old hand on paper that had thinned at the folds, and Dunmore had pulled it because the chain of the coffer ran through its second page.

Mira opened the file when Dunmore set it in front of her, and the old hand was careful and upright, the hand of an officer who had written for a record that would outlast him. The file dealt with a dealing she did not need to follow in detail, a matter of custody and a room in which an object had been kept. On the second page the writer had used a phrase she had not seen in an Office file before. He had written the words clean room, and he had set them in quotation marks, as if borrowing them from someone. The marks were small and deliberate, two strokes at the start of the phrase and two at the end, and they changed the sense of the sentence they sat in. The phrase did not read as a description the writer owned. It read as a term he had been given, a term that belonged to another voice, and he had marked the borrowing honestly rather than taking the words as his own.

Mira read the sentence again, and then she read the page around it, looking for the place where the borrowed phrase might be explained. The file gave her nothing more than the phrase itself. It did not say who had first used the words, and it did not say what room the words had named, and it did not say why the writer had felt the term needed marks around it. The pages around the phrase were ordinary, custody and correspondence, and they led away from the sentence in every direction except the one she wanted. Nothing more was learned in that hour. No further dealing surfaced from the archive search, and Dunmore set the other files aside in a neat stack and left the old file open between them. Mira sat with the borrowed phrase in front of her and felt the tiredness settle behind her eyes, and she understood that the file had given her a question rather than an answer, a phrase in another officer's hand with its quotation marks still on it after eighteen years.

She asked Dunmore where the file had been kept, and Dunmore told her the shelf and the box, and the answer was ordinary, and ordinariness settled nothing. Mira turned the page back and looked at the quotation marks once more. They were the kind of marks a careful writer made when the words mattered and the source did not belong in the record. Here the distance was older, and the hand that had marked it was gone from the Office, and the phrase waited in its marks without explanation.

Dunmore did not offer a reading of the phrase, and Mira did not ask her for one. The desk between them held the old file and the stack of ordinary records, and the rain had begun in earnest against the window, a soft sound on the glass that filled the quiet of the office. Mira closed her hands around the cup and found it cold, and she set it down beside the blotter. The phrase stayed with her as she looked at the open page, two words in quotation marks, borrowed from a voice the file did not name.

Dunmore sliding the old file across her desk, already open, saying nothing.

## The Case File

### Q: What is a bound object, and why is it restricted?
A bound object is an article whose closure, casing, or seal has been worked by a Binder so that any opening leaves a permanent mark on the binding itself. The restriction follows from that property. Because the binding records interference, the object can carry instruments, seals, and undertakings between members with a degree of assurance that ordinary containers cannot provide. That assurance creates misuse. A bound object may be used to give false confidence to a forged instrument, or to conceal movement that would otherwise require declaration. For these reasons bound objects are held only under licence, transferred only under recorded conditions, and examined by authorized readers when their custody is in dispute. Licensing and record keeping give that assurance a lawful and supervised setting for use.

### Q: How does the Melora court sale system treat restricted objects?
The Melora court sale system provides a supervised route by which restricted objects may change hands without passing into unrecorded private keeping. A seller files notice of the object, its licence history, and its supporting papers with the court before any sale may be listed. The court examines the filing for completeness and may require further evidence where a chain appears defective. Sales under this system take place at a stated hour before an examiner, and the transfer is recorded by the court at the time it occurs. The system exists to keep restricted objects visible. An object sold outside it is not made safe by private agreement between buyer and seller. The record remains available to the parties and to any later inquiry into the transfer.

### Q: How is a provenance authenticated for a court sale?
Authentication of a provenance proceeds by comparison rather than by assertion. Each document in the chain is checked against independent records that cover the same transfer, including court registers, toll books, licence rolls, and the internal papers of any collection that held the object. The hand, form, and language of each document are compared with examples from the claimed period and place. A document that agrees with its claimed date in every material particular supports the chain. A document that departs in form, in hand, or in recorded practice weakens the chain at that transfer, and the weakness is reported to the court whether or not the object itself is found to be genuine. The examiner receives the comparison as a whole, not as isolated points of style.

### Q: What may the Accord seize or hold, and under what showing?
The Accord may hold a restricted object under seal when a filing before a competent court shows a material defect in the papers on which a sale depends. The showing required is specific. It must identify the defective document, state the ground on which it is said to be defective, and provide supporting records capable of independent checking. Suspicion alone does not support a holding. Neither does a general concern about a seller or a collection. Where the showing is made, the object is held in place under seal pending inquiry, and it may not be sold, returned, or removed until the court discharges the seal. A holding preserves the position. It does not determine ownership and it does not establish fault. The holding is reviewed when the inquiry closes.

### Q: What is the private collections problem in relation to restricted objects?
The private collections problem arises when restricted objects are held on deposit by family trusts and similar bodies over long periods without active supervision. Such collections often keep sound internal records, and their objects remain physically secure. The difficulty is legal visibility. An object on deposit may change its effective control through trust decisions, family settlements, or informal loans that never reach a court filing. Over decades, a chain that was complete at deposit may acquire gaps that no single keeper intended to create. When the object is later offered for sale, those gaps surface as defects in the papers. The problem is therefore one of record, not necessarily of dishonesty, and it is addressed by inquiry rather than by assumption. That distinction governs the remedy in most such cases.

### Q: What is a Binder, and how does a binding age over time?
A Binder is a practitioner licensed to work bindings on objects intended to carry seals, instruments, or restricted contents. The work combines material craft with a set form that fixes the closure in a manner the craft recognizes. A binding ages in layers. Early wear appears at points of handling and at the lid or clasp where stress repeats. Repairs made under licence settle into the work and take on the character of the surrounding material over long periods. Later repairs remain distinguishable for longer. A binding of great age therefore presents a continuous history rather than a single surface, and that continuity is part of what an examiner and a reader assess when age is in question. Age alone does not authenticate a document attached to the object.

### Q: What do toll registers and court forms tell an examiner?
Toll registers record charges, receipts, and movements passing through a court or a collection point at a stated time, and they are kept in sequence with little reason for later alteration. Court forms reflect administrative practice in use during a defined span. Each form has a period during which clerks employed it, after which a successor form replaced it in ordinary business. Comparison of a disputed document against these records can test its claimed date without reliance on any single witness. A document bearing a form retired before its claimed date fails that test. A document whose toll entry corresponds in amount, wording, and sequence with the register gains support that private papers alone cannot supply. Both sources are strongest where they were kept independently of the sale.

### Q: What duty does an archivist owe when engaged by a seller's trust?
An archivist engaged by a trust to examine a collection owes a duty to the record as well as to the party paying for the engagement. The engagement may define the scope of the examination, but it does not authorize suppression of a material finding. Where an archivist identifies a defect in papers supporting a sale, the finding must appear in the report in terms a careful reader can act upon. Commercial sensitivity may affect presentation, but it may not remove substance. If a trust directs an archivist to omit a material defect, the archivist should decline the direction, record the direction, and consider whether the engagement can continue. Professional standing depends on the report meaning what it appears to mean. A report that hides a defect fails that standard.

### Q: What is a seller's right of withdrawal, and what does it protect?
The right of withdrawal permits a seller who has filed a restricted object for court sale to remove the object from that sale before the stated hour, in the form prescribed by the court, without charge arising from the withdrawal itself. The right protects sellers against being bound to a sale whose conditions have changed, whose papers have been challenged, or whose price environment has altered materially after filing. It also protects the court from conducting a sale that a principal no longer authorizes. Withdrawal does not cure a defect in the papers, and it does not prevent a court from holding the object under seal on a separate showing. The object withdrawn remains subject to licence and to any inquiry already begun. Notice must be given before the hour.

### Q: How are supplementary notes and corrected reports treated by a court?
A supplementary note is an addition to an earlier report, made by the same author, that clarifies, expands, or states plainly a matter the earlier report recorded in qualified terms. A corrected report replaces an erroneous statement with an accurate one and identifies the change. Courts treat both instruments as part of the record, alongside the original report, and they assess the whole sequence when weighing reliability. A supplementary note does not erase the earlier wording, and a correction does not conceal that an error was made. Where an author signs a note that strengthens an earlier reservation, the court may rely on the note as the author's considered position, provided authorship and date are established. Plain correction is preferred to silent substitution in every case.

### Q: How are paper, ink, and hands used in examining old documents?
Examiners consider paper, ink, and hand together, because each element belongs to a period and a place in a different way. Paper shows its making in its texture, sizing, and ageing, and stocks changed as mills and supply routes changed. Ink varies in composition and in the manner in which it sits on a given paper, and older work often displays penetration and fading patterns that later imitation reproduces imperfectly. Handwriting is assessed by habit rather than by single letters. Pressure, lift, spacing, and the formation of repeated characters reveal training and period practice. No single element should decide authenticity alone. Agreement among all three supports a document, and disagreement in any one requires explanation. The three are reported together in a single reasoned finding.

### Q: How must restricted objects be stored, and what is deposit keeping?
Restricted objects must be stored in a manner that preserves both the object and the evidence of its custody. Deposit keeping requires a secure room, a recorded location within that room, controlled access, and an entry in the deposit register each time the object is moved, inspected, or produced for examination. Collections holding objects on deposit act as keepers rather than owners, even where families regard the collection as their own. The keeper must be able to account for the object's place and condition across the whole deposit period. Gaps in deposit records create the same difficulties as gaps in transfer papers, because a later sale depends on a continuous account of where the object rested. Regular inspection confirms that the register and the room agree.

### Q: What damages arise when the Accord intervenes wrongly in a sale?
When the Accord intervenes in a court sale on a showing that later proves unfounded, the affected parties may claim damages assessed by reference to the sale price the object would have achieved and the costs thrown away by the trust and the seller. The principle is restorative rather than punitive. It recognizes that a stopped sale carries real loss, including professional fees, storage charges, and the effect of delay on a market that may not recur. The existence of this liability disciplines intervention. An office that seeks a holding must therefore be able to support its showing with records capable of independent checking, and it must accept that error in such a matter is expensive in both money and authority. Claims are filed through the court that stopped the sale.

### Q: What is the Helden Collection, and how is its trust constituted?
The Helden Collection is a family trust collection held in Melora, comprising bound and restricted objects kept on deposit and under licence over an extended period. The trust is constituted by a settlement that appoints trustees to hold legal responsibility for the collection, while archivists and keepers manage the objects and their records on a daily basis. Objects in the collection may belong beneficially to families, to the trust itself, or to depositors who placed them there for safekeeping. That mixed character requires careful records distinguishing ownership, deposit, and licence. The collection occupies premises above the court quarter, including gallery space, a strong room, and storerooms, and it sells or releases objects only through authorized channels. Trustees answer for gaps in that distinction when a sale is filed.

### Q: How does the Ninth Office read sale filings in the ordinary course?
In the ordinary course, the Ninth Office reviews sale filings for restricted objects as part of its supervision of transfers between members. Analysts read the filings against licence records, deposit registers, and prior dealings known to the Office. Most filings require no action. A filing attracts attention where a report contains qualified language that appears to reserve a material point, where a chain shows an interval without supporting documents, or where a form or hand described in the papers appears inconsistent with its claimed date. Attention at this stage is inquiry, not accusation. The Office may seek registers, request a plainer statement from a reporting archivist, or send an officer to examine the object and its papers in place. No action follows unless the concern can be tested against records.

### Q: What does a holding seal do to the legal state of an object?
A holding seal fixes the legal state of a restricted object at the moment the seal is set. While the seal remains in place, the object may not be sold, pledged, lent, returned to a depositor, or removed from the room in which it was sealed, except by order of the court that authorized the holding. The seal does not transfer ownership, and it does not decide whether the papers supporting a proposed sale were genuine. It preserves the object and its position so that inquiry may proceed without the risk of disappearance into private keeping. Breaking or circumventing a seal is treated as a separate matter from any defect in the original papers, and it carries its own consequences. Removal after discharge is recorded in the deposit register.

### Q: Through what four hands does a long provenance pass as objects cross members?
A long provenance commonly passes through four kinds of hand as an object crosses between members over an extended period. A trade hand records the commercial transfer in the language and form of a market or dock office. A chancery hand records official receipt or authorization in the formal style of an administration. A court hand records a judicial or sale proceeding in the forms the court then employed. A local or mill hand records private keeping where an object rested outside formal institutions. Each hand brings different conventions for dates, names, and charges. Authentication must allow for those differences while testing whether each document belongs to the period and office it claims. A chain is read with those differences in mind from the start.

### Q: Why is a span of forty years a dangerous interval in a chain of custody?
A span of forty years is dangerous because it exceeds the working memory of most keepers and outlasts many of the records that would otherwise fill it. Clerks retire, archivists change, mills and small offices close, and registers pass into storage where later searchers may not find them. Within such an interval an object may have rested harmlessly in a single place, or it may have been lent, pledged, copied, or sold without record. The danger lies in the inability to distinguish between those possibilities from the papers alone. A later document that purports to cover the entire interval therefore attracts close scrutiny, particularly where its form or hand belongs more naturally to a different period than the transfer it records. Length alone creates no presumption, but it demands a fuller account. A chain is read with those differences in mind from the start.

### Q: What does an examiner of the Melora court do in a disputed sale?
An examiner of the Melora court is the officer charged with testing whether a filed sale may proceed on its papers. The examiner receives the filing, the supporting provenance, and any report prepared by an archivist engaged for the object. Where a defect is alleged, the examiner receives the evidence supporting the allegation, hears the reporting archivist if a supplementary statement is offered, and allows the seller to exercise any right the sale rules provide. The examiner may stop a sale before its hour, authorize a holding under seal, or permit the sale to proceed where a challenge fails. The examiner decides questions of filing and sale. The examiner does not determine criminal responsibility for a defective document. The decision is recorded and served on all parties to the filing.

### Q: How does the Ninth Office record a read of an object as evidence?
A read of an object is recorded as evidence by a structured entry made as soon as practicable after the reading takes place. The entry identifies the object, the place, the persons present, and the method employed, including any comparative objects used where age or character is in issue. It states what the read established and, with equal care, what it did not establish. A read may support a conclusion about age, continuity, or accumulated affect, but the record must not extend that support to identity or intent beyond the limits of the method. The officer signs the entry, and any later reliance on the read in court proceedings proceeds from that written record rather than from recollection. A copy is retained with the case file for later reference.
