On 12 January 2019 the President of India gave assent to the Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act, and with that signature the EWS reservation entered a constitutional order that had organised preferential treatment around caste for seven decades. The amendment reserved ten percent of seats in educational institutions and ten percent of initial appointments in government services for economically weaker sections, a category defined by household income and assets and by nothing else. No caste certificate could establish the claim, and no caste disability was required to sustain it. The question this article answers is architectural rather than partisan. What did the 2019 amendment actually build? The argument defended here is that the 103rd Amendment built a methodologically distinct reservation, a second instrument standing beside the caste-based framework rather than a tenth added to its pile, and that the Supreme Court's judgment of 7 November 2022 in Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India operationalised that distinctness through three named holdings on economic criteria, on the fifty percent ceiling, and on the exclusion of the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes from the new category.

## The EWS Reservation Amendment and the Question It Poses

Three readings of the 103rd Amendment compete in the literature that followed its passage, and each reading captures a portion of the record while missing the architecture. The political-strategic reading treats the amendment as an electoral instrument. It notes the calendar: the Union Cabinet approved the proposal on 7 January 2019, the bill passed both Houses within two days, and the general election of April and May 2019 followed within weeks. On this reading, associated in its sharpest form with commentary in the Economic and Political Weekly during January 2019, the amendment addressed the discontent of poor households among communities that had agitated for inclusion in the existing quotas, including the Maratha agitation in Maharashtra, the Patidar agitation in Gujarat led by Hardik Patel from 2015, and the Jat agitation in Haryana of February 2016. The reading explains timing well, and timing is a fact of the record. What it cannot explain is design. An electoral instrument would have been content with a press release and an executive order of the kind the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao issued on 25 September 1991, an order the Supreme Court struck down sixteen months later. The government of Narendra Modi instead chose the hardest route available, a constitutional amendment under Article 368 requiring special majorities in both Houses and exposing the measure to basic structure review, and it drafted that amendment with a methodological signature, purely economic criteria, that no earlier Indian reservation instrument had carried.

The legal-doctrinal reading, the second competitor, treats the amendment as a problem in case law. Its materials are genuine: the fifty percent ceiling announced by the nine-judge bench in Indra Sawhney v Union of India on 16 November 1992, the basic structure doctrine descending from Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala of 24 April 1973, and the equality code of Articles 14, 15 and 16. Legal commentary of a high order, including Gautam Bhatia's essays on his Indian Constitutional Law and Philosophy blog during 2019 and Suhrith Parthasarathy's columns in The Hindu, worked this seam thoroughly. The doctrinal reading explains the litigation that followed, including the referral order of 5 August 2020 by which a three-judge bench sent the challenges to a Constitution Bench. What it cannot explain is why the doctrine moved as it did. Doctrine in Janhit Abhiyan did not merely accommodate a political fact; the majority opinions reasoned from a premise about methodology, holding that a reservation identified by economic criteria operates on a different plane from a reservation identified by caste, and that premise decided the ceiling question and the exclusion question alike. A reading that begins and ends with doctrine treats that premise as a conclusion rather than as the amendment's own architectural claim, stated in the Statement of Objects and Reasons appended to the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-fourth Amendment) Bill of 2019.

The third reading, defended in this article, is the methodologically-distinct-reservation reading. It holds that the substantive event of January 2019 was the installation of a second identification method inside the Indian reservation system. The older method, assembled between 1950 and 1992 and given its canonical form by the Mandal Commission report of 31 December 1980, identifies beneficiaries through group membership: a caste or class listed as socially and educationally backward, tested by indicators in which caste carries the heaviest weight. The newer method identifies beneficiaries through household economic position alone: an income line at eight lakh rupees a year, land ceilings, flat and plot ceilings, applied to households that no existing quota covers. The two methods rest on different theories of disadvantage. The Mandal theory holds that economic deprivation in India is produced substantially by caste hierarchy, so the remedy must name caste. The EWS theory holds that economic deprivation also strikes households outside the listed classes, and that poverty unaccompanied by caste disability is still a constitutionally cognisable ground for preferential treatment. The 2022 verdict, on this reading, did not simply permit the new method; it organised the coexistence of the two methods, a dual implementation in which the caste-based framework continues under Indra Sawhney and the economic framework runs parallel under Janhit Abhiyan, each with its own ceiling logic and its own beneficiary boundary.

### Which commission first recommended a quota for the economically poor outside the listed classes?

The Commission for Economically Backward Classes, chaired by Major General S. R. Sinho and appointed by the Union government on 31 July 2006, occupies that position in the record. Its report, submitted on 22 July 2010, recommended a ten percent reservation in central government employment and centrally funded education for economically backward classes outside the listed classes.

The same report recommended welfare measures in housing, health and credit for the same population. It also recorded that the population it addressed had no institutional advocate inside the reservation system: no commission listed its members, no ministry tracked its condition between censuses, and no quota column in any admission list or recruitment advertisement carried its name before the amendment supplied one in January 2019.

The Sinho Commission matters to the architecture because it converts the 2019 amendment from an improvisation into the second attempt at a documented design. The United Progressive Alliance government that received the Sinho report in 2010 did not legislate on it, and the report rested in the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment without a bill for eight years. When the Modi government moved in January 2019, the measure it carried reproduced the Sinho architecture in its main lines: the same ten percent figure, the same beneficiary boundary drawn around households outside the existing quotas, the same reliance on income and asset tests. Defenders of the amendment, including Minister for Social Justice and Empowerment Thawar Chand Gehlot, who piloted the bill in the Rajya Sabha on 9 January 2019, invoked this lineage in debate. Critics answered that a shelved commission report proves administrative convenience rather than constitutional warrant, a point Gautam Bhatia pressed in his January 2019 analysis. Both positions belong to the record. The architectural point stands apart from both: by 2019 the purely economic quota had a commission pedigree, a defined beneficiary boundary and a decade of dormancy, and the amendment's drafters worked from that template rather than inventing a new one under electoral pressure.

## Before 2019: Economic Criteria Inside a Caste-Based Architecture

The original constitutional text of 26 January 1950 knew backwardness as a compound condition. Article 15(4), inserted by the Constitution (First Amendment) Act of 1951 after the Supreme Court's judgment in State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan of 9 April 1951, empowered the state to make special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes. Article 16(4) spoke of a backward class of citizens not adequately represented in the services. Article 46 directed the state to promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, and in particular of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes. Economic condition appeared inside these provisions as one strand of a compound test, never as a freestanding ground. The Constituent Assembly debates of November and December 1948 record the drafting choice plainly: K. M. Munshi and B. R. Ambedkar, speaking on 30 November 1948, defended backwardness language that joined social, educational and economic indicia, and the Assembly rejected formulations that would have made poverty alone the trigger for preferential treatment in services.

The first Backward Classes Commission, appointed on 29 January 1953 under Article 340 and chaired by Kaka Kalelkar, reported on 30 March 1955 with a list of 2,399 castes treated as backward and with economic tests subordinated to caste identification throughout. Readers tracing how that commission's method hardened into the operating system of Indian reservation can follow [the first Backward Classes Commission under Kalelkar](/2020/03/01/first-backward-classes-commission-kalelkar/), whose report the Union government of Jawaharlal Nehru declined to implement in its caste-listed form. The second Backward Classes Commission, appointed on 1 January 1979 and chaired by B. P. Mandal, reported on 31 December 1980 with eleven indicators grouped under social, educational and economic heads and weighted three, two and one points respectively, so that caste-linked social indicators dominated the arithmetic by design. The economic strand existed, weighted lowest of the three groups. When the government of V. P. Singh announced implementation of the Mandal recommendations for central services on 7 August 1990, the instrument it activated was therefore a caste-anchored instrument with an economic component, not an economic instrument.

### What did Indra Sawhney decide about a quota based on income alone?

The nine-judge bench in Indra Sawhney v Union of India, delivering judgment on 16 November 1992 by a six-to-three majority, held that a ten percent reservation for economically forward classes, announced by the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao through the office memorandum of 25 September 1991, was unconstitutional.

The majority reasoned that economic criterion alone could not identify a backward class under Article 16(4), that caste was a permissible starting point for identification, and that total reservation under Article 16(4) should ordinarily remain within fifty percent.

That holding fixed the legal position for twenty-six years. A purely economic quota in central services was not merely unlegislated; it had been judicially invalidated under the constitutional text as it stood. The significance of January 2019 begins here. The Modi government did not ask the courts to reconsider Indra Sawhney, and it did not proceed by executive order in defiance of the judgment. It amended the constitutional text itself, inserting new clauses that expressly authorised what Article 16(4), as interpreted in 1992, had not authorised. The amendment route conceded the authority of the 1992 framework while changing the instrument's legal foundation, and it placed the methodological question, whether economic criteria can identify a constitutionally valid beneficiary class, before the electorate's representatives first and before the Supreme Court second. Suhrith Parthasarathy, writing in The Hindu on 9 January 2019, identified this sequence precisely: the amendment was an attempt to achieve by constitutional text what the Rao government had attempted by memorandum, and its fate would turn on the basic structure doctrine rather than on Indra Sawhney alone. Anupama Rao, in her essays on caste and the Constitution published during 2019, added the genealogical caution that an economic criterion detached from caste history risked misdescribing the structure of Indian disadvantage, a caution the dissent in Janhit Abhiyan would later carry into constitutional law.

Between the 1992 judgment and the 2019 amendment, the economic strand kept surfacing at the state level. Rajasthan legislated a fourteen percent quota for economically backward classes among the unreserved communities in 2015 under the government of Vasundhara Raje, and the Rajasthan High Court struck the measure down; Gujarat announced a ten percent economic quota by ordinance in April 2016 under the government of Anandiben Patel, and the Gujarat High Court quashed it on 4 August 2016 as violative of the fifty percent ceiling. Each state attempt ran into the same two walls: the ceiling from Indra Sawhney and the absence of a constitutional clause authorising classification by economic position alone. The pattern of failure is itself evidence for the methodologically-distinct reading. What the states could not achieve by statute, because the disability was methodological and textual rather than political, the Union achieved by amendment, because the amendment rewrote the text that had made purely economic identification illegible to the reservation system.

## The 103rd Amendment in Parliament: Seven Days in January 2019

The legislative sequence deserves exact statement, because the speed of passage is the foundation of the political-strategic reading and because the votes themselves carry architectural information. The Union Cabinet approved the proposal on 7 January 2019. The Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-fourth Amendment) Bill, 2019 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 8 January 2019 by Thawar Chand Gehlot, and the Lok Sabha passed it the same day by 323 votes to 3, a majority comfortably beyond the special majority Article 368 requires. The Rajya Sabha passed the bill on 9 January 2019 by 165 votes to 7, with the debate extending past midnight. Presidential assent followed on 12 January 2019, the Act was published as the Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act, 2019, and the Ministry of Law and Justice notified its commencement with effect from 14 January 2019. No state ratification was required, since the amendment touched neither the Seventh Schedule nor the provisions enumerated in the proviso to Article 368(2). Seven days from cabinet approval to commencement is fast by any standard of Indian constitutional amendment; the 101st Amendment for the goods and services tax, by contrast, travelled from introduction on 19 December 2014 to assent on 8 September 2016 and required ratification by half the state legislatures.

Speed, however, is a property of the calendar rather than of the text, and the text repays the slower reading its passage denied it. Members who opposed the bill in debate, including Kanimozhi of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in the Rajya Sabha and members of the Communist Party of India (Marxist), directed their fire at two targets: the absence of a caste census foundation for the measure and the exclusion of the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes from its benefits. Members who supported it, including Finance Minister Arun Jaitley in the Lok Sabha debate of 8 January 2019, framed the measure as the completion of an unfinished constitutional conversation, pointing to Article 46 and to the Sinho Commission's dormant recommendations. The debate thus rehearsed, in parliamentary form, the exact disagreement the Constitution Bench would adjudicate in 2022: whether economic deprivation is a self-sufficient constitutional category or a derivative of caste hierarchy. Readers placing the amendment inside the governing party's wider constitutional programme of that year can consult the analysis of [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) and the treatment of [the post-2019 constitutional-amendment context](/2019/07/31/article-370-abrogation-august-2019/), which together situate the January amendment among the constitutional moves of the Modi government's second term.

### How did the two Houses divide on the amendment bill?

The Lok Sabha passed the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-fourth Amendment) Bill on 8 January 2019 by 323 votes to 3, and the Rajya Sabha passed it on 9 January 2019 by 165 votes to 7. Both divisions cleared, by wide margins, the special majority that Article 368 prescribes for amendments of the Constitution.

Both divisions exceeded the Article 368 requirements of a majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting, and the near-unanimity across party lines distinguishes the EWS amendment from the contested amendments of the same parliamentary year.

The division figures carry a second significance for the adjudication this article conducts. A measure carried by 323 to 3 and 165 to 7 cannot be described, without remainder, as the instrument of one party's election management; the political-strategic reading must explain why parties that expected to contest the April 2019 election against the Bharatiya Janata Party supplied the votes that carried its signature amendment. The explanation available in the record is that opposition parties judged the measure's beneficiary constituency, poor households outside the listed classes, to be one no party could afford to vote against in public division, which is itself a fact about the constituency's breadth rather than about any single party's strategy. The breadth of the beneficiary class, defined by income rather than by caste organisation, is a methodological property of the instrument. Caste-based quotas have organised constituencies with associations, leaderships and voting histories; an income-defined class has none of these, and its political protection in January 2019 came precisely from its unorganised universality. That property follows from the method chosen, and it is one reason the methodologically-distinct reading explains the parliamentary record better than the strategic reading it supplements.

## What Articles 15(6) and 16(6) Actually Say

The amendment inserted two clauses and amended two others. Article 15(6) empowers the state to make special provision for the advancement of any economically weaker sections of citizens other than the classes mentioned in clauses (4) and (5) of Article 15, including reservation of up to ten percent of seats in educational institutions, aided or unaided, other than minority educational institutions referred to in Article 30(1). Article 16(6) empowers the state to make provision for reservation of appointments or posts in favour of any economically weaker sections of citizens other than the classes mentioned in clause (4), up to a maximum of ten percent of posts, in addition to the existing reservation. Consequential changes to the explanations of Articles 15 and 16 tied the new category to its textual home. Three features of this drafting carry the methodological argument.

First, the new clauses stand outside the backward-class provisions rather than inside them. The amendment did not enlarge Article 15(4) or Article 16(4) to admit economic criteria into the backward-class test; it built separate clauses with a separate beneficiary description. The placement is the textual deposit of the method. Had Parliament intended an additive expansion of caste-based reservation, the natural draft was an amendment of clause (4). The draft chosen instead created a parallel authority, and the majority in Janhit Abhiyan read the placement as deliberate, treating Articles 15(6) and 16(6) as a self-contained code for a classification the older clauses did not govern.

Second, the ceiling is internal to the new clauses. The ten percent maximum appears in the constitutional text itself, in each clause, expressed as a limit over and above the existing reservation. No earlier reservation provision in Part III carries its percentage in the text; the twenty-seven percent for the Other Backward Classes in central services derives from executive decision under the Mandal implementation of 1990 and 1993, and the fifty percent ceiling derives from judicial doctrine. The EWS clauses constitutionalised their own quantum, which matters for the ceiling adjudication of 2022: the majority could reason that a limit written into the amendment by Parliament itself is not a breach of a doctrinal limit developed for a different clause.

Third, the beneficiary description is negative as well as positive. The clauses benefit economically weaker sections other than the classes mentioned in the backward-class clauses, which means the category is defined jointly by an economic test and by non-membership of the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes. This double definition is the amendment's most contested feature, and it is where the dissent of 2022 concentrated its equality argument. Architecturally, the negative limb follows from the method: a category whose purpose is to reach deprivation that the caste-based framework does not reach must exclude households the caste-based framework already reaches, on pain of double counting. Whether that architectural logic can justify the exclusion of a poor Scheduled Caste household from a benefit available to an equally poor household outside the lists is the precise point on which the Constitution Bench divided, and this article returns to it in the dissent section below. The full three-layer structure into which the new clauses were inserted, rights-protective, services-affirmative and representational, is mapped in the analysis of [the three-layered constitutional reservation framework](/2020/02/25/constitutional-reservation-framework/), within whose services-affirmative layer Articles 15(6) and 16(6) operate.

## The Eligibility Machine: Income, Land and the DoPT Notification

A constitutional clause authorising reservation for economically weaker sections leaves the operative question untouched: which households count as economically weaker? The answer arrived through the notification the brief for this amendment's administration records, issued in January 2019 by the Department of Personnel and Training, with the detailed office memorandum of 31 January 2019 prescribing the criteria and the certificate machinery for central services, and a parallel memorandum from the Ministry of Human Resource Development carrying the criteria into central educational institutions for the academic session beginning in 2019. The criteria, as notified, defined an eligible household by four asset and income tests applied together. Gross annual family income must fall below eight lakh rupees, counting income from salary, agriculture, business and every other source, for the financial year preceding application. The household must not own or possess five acres or more of agricultural land. The household must not own a residential flat of one thousand square feet or more. The household must not own a residential plot of one hundred square yards or more in a notified municipality, or two hundred square yards or more in other areas. A household failing any single test fails the category, and a household covered by reservation for the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes or the Other Backward Classes is excluded at the threshold, before the economic tests begin.

The certificate machinery gave these tests administrative form. The income and asset certificate issues from the same revenue authorities who issue caste and income certificates at the tehsil level, principally tehsildars and district magistrates, and the applicant's family, defined to include parents, spouse, siblings below eighteen and children below eighteen, supplies the unit across which income and assets aggregate. Two features of this machinery deserve scrutiny because they work against the method's clean lines. The aggregation rule means that a household's eligibility turns on the earnings of members whose income the applicant does not control, a design choice borrowed from the creamy layer jurisprudence of Indra Sawhney, where parental income tests the advantage of Other Backward Classes candidates. The asset tests, drawn in physical units rather than in values, treat five acres of irrigated land in western Maharashtra and five acres of unirrigated land in Bundelkhand as the same fact, although their market values diverge by an order of magnitude. Suhrith Parthasarathy's commentary of early 2019 fastened on this roughness, arguing that criteria drawn so broadly, with an income line at eight lakh rupees that statistical estimates placed above the large majority of Indian households, identified not a narrow class of the deprived but most of the unreserved population. That breadth objection is empirical and serious, and this article addresses it in the complication section below; its force goes to calibration rather than to method, since a line drawn lower would leave the methodology untouched.

Gujarat moved first among the states. The government of Vijay Rupani announced on 13 January 2019, one day after presidential assent, that Gujarat would implement the ten percent quota in state services and admissions from 14 January 2019, making the state the amendment's first adopter. Other states followed at varied speeds and with varied criteria, some adopting the central income line and others, including Maharashtra for its state services, debating state-specific thresholds. The implementation documentation preserved across central departments and institutions, the under-cited primary record for this amendment, shows a pattern the verdict's defenders and critics both cite: EWS vacancies in several central recruitment cycles of 2019, 2020 and 2021 went partly unfilled for want of certified eligible candidates, while admission demand in centrally funded institutions pressed against the ten percent limit unevenly across courses. Readers assembling study materials from primary documents for this amendment's administrative record can work through the official memoranda as rendered slides with a [PPT viewer for exam notes](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which keeps the notification text and the eligibility tables side by side for comparison.

### Which authority issues the EWS certificate to an applicant?

The income and asset certificate that establishes EWS status issues from the revenue administration of the applicant's district, ordinarily the tehsildar, with the district magistrate and other notified revenue officers also competent to issue it under the Department of Personnel and Training memorandum of 31 January 2019.

The certificate holds validity for the financial year of issue and must be produced before the recruiting body or admitting institution at the stage that body prescribes.

## Two Methodologies: Mandal's Eleven Indicators Against the Income Line

The contrast at the centre of this article can be stated as a comparison of identification technologies. The Mandal Commission, reporting on 31 December 1980, identified backward classes through eleven indicators arranged in three groups and weighted unequally: four social indicators at three points each, three educational indicators at two points each, and four economic indicators at one point each, so that of the twenty-two points available to a caste under the scheme, twelve turned on social standing, six on education and four on economic condition. A caste scoring above eleven points entered the list of socially and educationally backward classes. The identification unit was the caste, the evidence included the census of 1931 for want of later caste enumeration, and the output was a list of 3,743 castes whose members then qualified as individuals by membership, subject after 1992 to the creamy layer exclusion. The full reconstruction of that machinery, indicator by indicator, appears in the treatment of [the Mandal-architecture's caste-based methodology](/2020/03/07/mandal-commission-report-1980-explained/), and the political moment that activated it is adjudicated in [the three-reading adjudication framework](/2020/02/14/mandal-decisive-moment-indian-politics/) applied to the Mandal moment itself.

The EWS instrument identifies beneficiaries through four tests, all economic, applied to the household rather than to a listed group. No commission enumerated the beneficiary population in advance; the category creates itself afresh in each application cycle, as households present certificates and either satisfy the tests or fail them. No indicator touches social standing, ritual rank, educational tradition or occupational stigma. The identification unit is the family as the notification defines it, the evidence is revenue record and self-declaration verified by the issuing authority, and the output is a certificate rather than a list. Where the Mandal method asks who your people are, the EWS method asks what your household holds. That sentence compresses the methodological distinction, and the compression is fair: every architectural consequence traced in this article, the ceiling reasoning, the exclusion reasoning, the implementation pattern, follows from the substitution of the household economic test for the group membership test.

Table 1, the methodologically-distinct-reservation map, sets the two identification systems side by side across the dimensions on which they differ, with the legal questions each difference generated in the litigation of 2019 to 2022.

| Dimension | Mandal-architecture, caste-based | EWS framework, 103rd Amendment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identification unit | The listed caste or class | The applicant household |
| Primary criterion | Social and educational backwardness of the group | Household income and assets alone |
| Indicator structure | Eleven indicators, weighted three, two and one | Four tests, income, land, flat and plot, applied together |
| Evidential base | Census material and commission field surveys | Revenue records and tehsil-issued certificates |
| Constitutional home | Articles 15(4) and 16(4) | Articles 15(6) and 16(6) |
| Quantum source | Executive decision within judicial ceiling | Ten percent written into the constitutional text |
| Ceiling treatment | Fifty percent ceiling from Indra Sawhney | Majority in Janhit Abhiyan held ceiling clause-specific |
| Beneficiary boundary | Membership of a listed class, creamy layer excluded | Non-membership of SC, ST and OBC lists, economic tests passed |
| Entry and exit | Lists revised by commissions and governments | Certificate renewed each financial year |
| Governing judgment | Indra Sawhney, 16 November 1992 | Janhit Abhiyan, 7 November 2022 |

The map also exposes where the two systems touch. Both operate in the services-affirmative layer of the constitutional framework, both compete for the same finite pool of seats and posts, and both rest on the equality code's permission to classify for remedial ends. The dual implementation that followed the 2022 verdict is therefore a coexistence inside one institution rather than a separation into two institutions, and the friction of that coexistence, visible in unfilled EWS seats that cannot be transferred to the general pool mid-cycle under some institutional rules and in litigation over state-level criteria, belongs to the implementation record rather than to the constitutional design. The design itself, as the majority read it, is two methods under one equality code, each method answering a different account of why a household starts behind.

## Three Legal Questions Travel to the Constitution Bench

Challenges to the amendment reached the Supreme Court within days of its commencement. The writ petition filed by Youth for Equality, argued by senior advocate Gopal Sankaranarayanan among others, and the petition in Janhit Abhiyan's own name became the lead vehicles among a batch that eventually gathered challenges from associations and individuals across several states. A bench of Chief Justice S. A. Bobde, Justice B. R. Gavai and Justice Surya Kant declined interim stay of the amendment's operation and, by the order of 5 August 2020, referred the batch to a Constitution Bench of five judges, recording that the cases raised substantial questions of constitutional interpretation: whether reservation could rest on economic criteria alone, whether the amendment breached the fifty percent ceiling, and whether the exclusion of the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes from the new quota offended the equality code. The referral order is the cleanest official statement of the three questions, and the eventual judgment answered them in the referral's own order.

The Constitution Bench that heard the matter in September 2022 consisted of Chief Justice U. U. Lalit and Justices Dinesh Maheshwari, S. Ravindra Bhat, Bela M. Trivedi and J. B. Pardiwala. Attorney General K. K. Venugopal and Solicitor General Tushar Mehta defended the amendment for the Union; the petitioners' side drew on the equality arguments developed in the scholarly literature of 2019, including the contention, associated with Suhrith Parthasarathy's writing, that the amendment inverted the constitutional logic of reservation by detaching it from the social discrimination that alone justified departure from formal equality; and intervenors for respondent associations defended the exclusion of the listed classes as the amendment's defining boundary. Judgment was reserved before Chief Justice Lalit's retirement and delivered on 7 November 2022, his penultimate working day on the bench, by a majority of three to two.

## Janhit Abhiyan: The Majority's Three Architectural Justifications

Justice Dinesh Maheshwari wrote the leading majority opinion for himself and Justice Bela M. Trivedi, and Justice J. B. Pardiwala concurred in a separate opinion reaching the same conclusions. On the first question, the majority held that reservation founded on economic criteria alone is constitutionally permissible and does not damage the basic structure of the Constitution. Economic deprivation, the opinion reasoned, is a constitutionally cognisable source of disadvantage, the equality code permits classification on economic grounds, and the amending power under Article 368 extends to authorising a form of preferential treatment the original text had not enumerated, provided the amendment does not abrogate the Constitution's identity. The majority treated Articles 15(6) and 16(6) as enabling provisions of the same family as clauses (4) of those articles, each clause answering a distinct ground of disadvantage, social and educational backwardness in the one case and economic weakness in the other.

On the second question, the majority held that the fifty percent ceiling is neither inflexible nor a basic structure feature, and that in any event the ceiling developed in Indra Sawhney governs reservations under the backward-class clauses rather than the new economic clauses. Justice Maheshwari's opinion reasoned that the ceiling was a judicially evolved rule of prudence for Article 16(4) reservations, that Indra Sawhney itself contemplated extraordinary departures, and that the EWS reservation, operating under a separate clause with its own textual maximum of ten percent, does not count toward the ceiling that disciplines the backward-class quota. The total reservation of sixty percent in contexts where the EWS quota applies therefore breaches no constitutional limit on the majority's reasoning. Justice Pardiwala's concurrence reached the ceiling conclusion by a similar route, emphasising that the new clauses created a distinct compartment whose quantum Parliament had itself capped in the text.

On the third question, the majority held that the exclusion of the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes from the EWS category is a constitutionally permissible classification rather than a discrimination. The reasoning treats the listed classes as already covered by a remedial scheme addressed to their disadvantage, so that a second scheme addressed to disadvantage outside that scheme may rationally confine itself to the uncovered population. Justice Maheshwari's opinion framed the exclusion as a boundary internal to the design of a benefit for the hitherto unbenefited, and Justice Pardiwala described the EWS category as a class apart, whose definition by non-membership of the covered classes follows from the purpose of reaching those the reservation system had never reached. On all three questions the majority's reasoning proceeds from the methodological premise this article has traced: that the EWS instrument is a distinct method operating beside the caste-based method, so that rules evolved for the one method do not transfer mechanically to the other. The ceiling does not transfer because it was a rule for the backward-class method; the exclusion is valid because it is the distinct method's boundary; and economic criteria suffice because the distinct method's theory of disadvantage is economic. The judgment is the methodology, adjudicated.

### Who wrote the opinions on each side in Janhit Abhiyan?

Justice Dinesh Maheshwari wrote the leading judgment for the three-judge majority, speaking also for Justice Bela M. Trivedi, and Justice J. B. Pardiwala added a concurring opinion upholding the amendment on substantially the same reasoning. The Constitution Bench delivered its verdict on 7 November 2022, dividing three to two.

Justice S. Ravindra Bhat wrote the dissent, holding the amendment unconstitutional for its exclusion of the poor among the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes, and Chief Justice U. U. Lalit concurred with the dissent.

## The Dissent: Bhat and Lalit on Exclusion and the Equality Code

Justice S. Ravindra Bhat's dissent, with which Chief Justice U. U. Lalit concurred, accepted parts of the majority's frame and rejected its conclusion. The dissent did not hold that economic criteria can never ground reservation; its decisive objection fastened on the exclusion. By defining the EWS category to exclude every household covered by the existing quotas, the amendment, in the dissent's reasoning, denied a benefit to poor households for the reason that their communities had suffered the very discrimination the Constitution's remedial scheme exists to answer. A poor Scheduled Caste household and a poor household outside the lists stand in identical economic positions; the amendment benefits the second and excludes the first, and the ground of the distinction is caste membership itself. Justice Bhat characterised this as discrimination on a prohibited ground operating against the Constitution's most protected classes, and held the exclusion violative of the equality code and destructive of the basic structure's commitment to equality as the remedial scheme's foundation. The dissent described the exclusion, in its most quoted passage, as striking at the heart of the equality code, and it warned that the amendment's logic fractionalised the remedial project by pitting the poor of the covered classes against the poor outside them.

The dissent also addressed the ceiling and the method. On the ceiling, it treated the fifty percent rule as a constitutional discipline of long standing whose breach by a further ten percent required stronger justification than a separate-clause argument supplied. On the method, the dissent expressed the scholarly caution of the amendment's critics in judicial form: reservation in the Indian constitutional tradition answers social discrimination and inadequate representation, and a scheme that professes indifference to both, sorting households by income alone while excluding the historically discriminated, reorders the tradition's priorities. Anupama Rao's 2019 essays had argued the genealogical version of this position, that the economic criterion detached from caste history converts a scheme of reparative justice into a scheme of poverty relief and changes its constitutional character in the conversion. The dissent gave that position its strongest institutional expression, and it lost by one vote.

The closeness of the division matters for the evergreen value of the amendment's architecture. A three-to-two Constitution Bench verdict settles the law; it does not settle the argument, and the dissent's reasoning remains available to a future bench asked to revisit the exclusion, as the basic structure jurisprudence has revisited settlements before. Gautam Bhatia's post-judgment analysis on the Indian Constitutional Law and Philosophy blog, published in November 2022, located the verdict's vulnerability precisely where the dissent had: the majority's treatment of the exclusion as a benign boundary, Bhatia argued, decided the case by classification logic and gave the equality objection less than its due. Suhrith Parthasarathy's commentary on the judgment pressed the complementary point about the ceiling, that treating the fifty percent rule as clause-specific dissolved a discipline the Court had maintained through M. Nagaraj v Union of India of 19 October 2006 and other benches. The scholarly disagreement of 2019 thus survived the judgment of 2022 in refined form, and the adjudication this article offers is narrower than either side's full claim. The majority's methodological premise explains the amendment's design and the verdict's structure better than any competing account; whether that premise should have carried the exclusion question is the point on which reasonable constitutional argument continues, with the dissent holding the stronger equality argument and the majority holding the law.

## The NEET Litigation: The Compartment Tested in Admissions

Before the Constitution Bench settled the compartment's existence, its criteria faced a separate and faster test in the admissions litigation of 2021 and 2022. The Union government had extended the EWS reservation to the all-India quota seats in medical admissions through the National Eligibility cum Entrance Test, and candidates challenging the extension in Neil Aurelio Nunes v Union of India attacked the eight lakh income line as arbitrary, pointing out that the government had applied the identical figure to the creamy layer test for a different purpose and had produced no study justifying it for the new compartment. A bench of Justices D. Y. Chandrachud and A. S. Bopanna, by its order of 7 January 2022, permitted the admission counselling for the academic year 2021 to proceed with the EWS reservation in place, reasoning that admissions stalled mid-cycle would damage the medical education calendar, while recording the petitioners' questions about the income criterion for fuller examination. The episode displays the dual system under operational stress: a criterion drawn by notification, defensible as administrative alignment with the creamy layer figure, had to survive judicial scrutiny of its rationality years before the constitutional challenge to its parent clauses concluded, and it survived provisionally on pragmatic grounds the final judgment later replaced with constitutional ones.

The NEET episode also exposed the data gap at the compartment's foundation, and the exposure deserves emphasis because it distinguishes the economic method's evidentiary needs from the caste method's. The Mandal compartment rests on commission reports, census material and decades of accumulated administrative data about listed communities; a court testing its classifications can consult that record. The EWS compartment rests on a notified income line and the revenue records of individual applicants; no commission enumerated its beneficiary population, no census counted it, and when the NEET bench asked what material supported the eight lakh figure, the Union's answer relied on the Sinho Commission's general recommendation of an economic quota and on the figure's prior administrative life in the creamy layer test rather than on any fresh measurement of the population the line was meant to capture. Critics treated the thinness of that record as proof that the criteria were makeshift. Defenders answered that a household-test method generates its data through administration itself, as certificates issue and recruitment cycles report, so that the evidentiary base the compartment lacked in 2019 accumulates by design across its operation. Both positions are recorded in the litigation, and the second has the better of the administrative record since: the departmental returns of the first cycles, showing where certificates issued, where vacancies persisted and where demand pressed, constitute the beginning of exactly the data base the method's defenders predicted it would build.

## Dual Implementation After November 2022

The architecture that operates after 7 November 2022 is a dual implementation, and its two halves run on different administrative clocks. The caste-based half continues under the framework settled by Indra Sawhney: lists of backward classes maintained by the National Commission for Backward Classes and state commissions, creamy layer exclusion applied by income and status tests to the Other Backward Classes, a fifty percent ceiling observed in the aggregate, and periodic litigation over list entries, sub-classification and promotion quotas. The economic half runs under Janhit Abhiyan: an annual certificate cycle, four economic tests, a textual ceiling of ten percent internal to its clauses, and a beneficiary population that reconstitutes itself each year as certificates issue and lapse. The two halves meet in the same admission lists and the same recruitment advertisements, where a single merit list may carry Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe, Other Backward Classes, EWS and unreserved columns side by side, the EWS column drawing ten percent in addition to the fifty percent the older columns together approach. The post-2022 trajectory's open questions sit at this junction. The treatment of [the post-2024 architectural-permission framework](/2020/03/23/sc-sub-categorisation-question-india/) for sub-categorisation within the Scheduled Castes shows the same dual structure reproducing itself inside the older half, as the permission logic of one compartment invites reordering in the other.

State implementation supplies the empirical texture of the dual system. States adopted the EWS quota for state services and state institutions through their own notifications during 2019 and after, Gujarat first on 14 January 2019, and the state criteria mostly track the central template of the eight lakh line and the land and dwelling tests, with some states varying the income line and some litigating their variations. The Comptroller and Auditor General's reporting and departmental returns across the first cycles record the unevenness already noted: EWS seats in several central institutions remained partly vacant in early cycles for want of certified candidates in the admission window, while competitive recruitment for central posts drew EWS applications in large multiples of the reserved vacancies. Both facts are consistent with a new identification method finding its administrative footing; a certificate-based category cannot draw on the pre-existing lists and community organisations that deliver candidates to the caste-based columns, and its first cycles measure the certificate machinery's reach as much as the beneficiary population's size.

Periodic review is the dual system's acknowledged gap. The Mandal framework carries review inside its design, since commissions and courts revisit lists and the creamy layer test across decades. The EWS framework's review mechanism is thinner: the income line and asset tests were set by notification in 2019 and can be revised by notification, and Justice Pardiwala's concurring opinion observed that the economic criteria and the ten percent figure call for periodic reassessment as conditions alter, without the judgment imposing a review timetable. The eight lakh line, unrevised across the amendment's first years, illustrates the gap; inflation alone moves a fixed nominal line downward in real terms each year, quietly narrowing the category the line defines. Whether a future government revises the line, and whether a future bench converts the concurrence's observation into doctrine, are questions the post-2022 trajectory holds open. The election context in which the amendment was born, the verdict of April and May 2019 that returned the Modi government with an enlarged majority, is reconstructed in the account of [the post-2019 election political-architectural context](/2019/08/16/2019-election-verdict-explained/), which records how completely the amendment's opponents in Parliament accommodated themselves to its passage.

### Did the Supreme Court stay the EWS quota while the challenge was pending?

No interim stay operated against the amendment at any stage of the litigation. The bench of Chief Justice S. A. Bobde that made the reference order of 5 August 2020 declined to suspend the quota's operation, recording instead the substantial constitutional questions that required an authoritative pronouncement from a Constitution Bench of five judges.

Admissions and appointments under Articles 15(6) and 16(6) therefore proceeded through the 2019, 2020, 2021 and 2022 cycles while the constitutional questions awaited the Constitution Bench, and the judgment of 7 November 2022 governed a working system rather than a proposal.

## The Complication: Practical Overlap and the Architectural Answer

The strongest counter to the methodologically-distinct reading deserves direct statement. It runs as follows. In the admission hall and the recruitment office, the two methods do not feel distinct. The same candidate pool sorts itself across the columns; the EWS column's beneficiaries are drawn overwhelmingly from communities outside the lists, which are themselves caste communities in sociological fact, so that an instrument professing indifference to caste operates in practice as a quota for the unreserved castes. The eight lakh line, on the estimates Suhrith Parthasarathy cited in 2019, admits the overwhelming majority of households outside the lists, so the category behaves less like a narrow remedy for deprivation and more like a general preference for the unreserved. On this counter, the methodological distinctness is architecturally elegant and empirically thin: the amendment changed the paperwork of preference more than its sociology.

The answer available within the record is that the distinction was never claimed at the level of sociology. The majority in Janhit Abhiyan did not hold that EWS beneficiaries lack caste identities; it held that the Constitution permits the state to identify disadvantage by an economic method that does not enquire into those identities. The architectural consequences that follow are legal and administrative rather than sociological, and they are substantial. The ceiling discipline differs between the compartments, as the verdict decided. The beneficiary boundary differs, since a household's EWS eligibility expires with its certificate year and fluctuates with its income, while caste list membership is stable across a lifetime and heritable in administrative fact. The review politics differ, since revising an income line is an executive act of a single afternoon, while revising a caste list engages commissions, courts and organised community politics across years. And the litigation posture differs, since challenges to the EWS compartment after 2022 must argue basic structure from the dissent's premises, while challenges inside the Mandal compartment continue under Indra Sawhney's living framework. A distinction that changes the ceiling, the boundary, the review mechanism and the litigation posture of a reservation system is substantive at the level at which constitutions operate, whatever its overlap at the level of the admission hall.

One further element of the record supports the architectural reading against its rivals, and it is the element the political-strategic reading explains worst. If the amendment were principally an electoral instrument, its drafters would have had every reason to keep it simple, an order rather than an amendment, a benefit without contested boundaries. Instead the instrument they built contains the one feature most calculated to generate constitutional litigation and scholarly attack: the exclusion of the listed classes, which supplied the dissent its winning argument among two of five judges and the critics their central charge. The exclusion is unintelligible as election management, since it antagonises the largest organised beneficiary constituencies of the older system. It is fully intelligible as methodology, since a category defined to reach deprivation outside the existing scheme must draw exactly that boundary or collapse into double benefit. Drafters choose the features their instrument requires; the exclusion's presence in the text, at known political cost, is the signature of the method the drafters believed they were installing. The political-strategic reading explains the week of the amendment's passage; the methodologically-distinct reading explains the amendment.

## The Genealogy of the Economic Criterion

The economic criterion did not enter Indian constitutional argument in 2019, and its genealogy explains both its availability to the amendment's drafters and the resistance it met. The Constituent Assembly's settlement of 1948 and 1949 had placed economic condition inside a compound test, as the debates on the draft articles corresponding to Articles 15 and 16 record, and the First Amendment of 1951 preserved the compound form. Through the following decades the criterion surfaced repeatedly at the margins of the compound system. The Kalelkar Commission of 1953 to 1955 included economic tests among its considerations while subordinating them to caste. State governments in the south experimented with income ceilings inside caste-based schemes, and the Supreme Court's judgment in K. C. Vasanth Kumar v State of Karnataka of 8 May 1985 carried opinions, notably that of Justice V. Khalid and the supplementary observations of Justice D. A. Desai, urging that economic criteria and periodic review should discipline the caste-based lists over time. The Mandal Commission itself, while weighting economic indicators lowest, devoted analysis to the proposition that caste and class overlap without coinciding, and its report acknowledged a residue of households whose deprivation no caste list captured.

The 1991 memorandum of the Rao government converted that residue into a claimed quota, ten percent for the economically backward among the forward communities, and Indra Sawhney converted the claim back into a residue by striking the memorandum down. The legal reasoning of 1992 is worth restating precisely, because the 2019 amendment is unintelligible without it. The majority held that a class identified by economic criterion alone was not a backward class within Article 16(4), since backwardness under that clause carried social and educational dimensions that income could not measure by itself. The holding did not declare economic criteria unconstitutional for all purposes; it declared them insufficient for the clause under which the Rao government had acted. The distinction left a drafting opening. A government determined to proceed could either persuade a future bench to revisit the reading of Article 16(4), the path of litigation, or write a new clause under which economic criteria would be sufficient by the text's own terms, the path of amendment. Fifteen years of state-level attempts, from Rajasthan in 2015 to Gujarat in 2016, tested intermediate routes and failed against the ceiling and the insufficiency holding together. The Sinho Commission's report of 22 July 2010, resting unimplemented in the ministry through those years, supplied the ready design when a Union government finally chose the amendment path, complete with the ten percent figure and the beneficiary boundary that the 2019 text carries.

## The Unreserved Poor as a Political Category

Between the Sinho report and the amendment stands a decade in which the unreserved poor acquired political form. The Patidar agitation in Gujarat, opening with rallies in Mehsana and Ahmedabad in July and August 2015 under the Patidar Anamat Andolan Samiti led by Hardik Patel, demanded either inclusion of Patidars in the Other Backward Classes or the abolition of caste-based quotas, and its scale among a landholding community not classified as backward introduced the quota demand from outside the lists into national politics. The Jat agitation in Haryana of February 2016, which turned violent and left at least thirty persons dead by official counts, pressed a parallel demand from a community whose classification had been litigated for years. The Maratha Kranti Morcha marches across Maharashtra from August 2016, silent processions numbering in the lakhs, demanded reservation for Marathas and kept the demand in public view through 2018, when the Maharashtra legislature enacted a sixteen percent Maratha quota on 30 November 2018 that the Bombay High Court upheld in trimmed form on 27 June 2019 and the Supreme Court struck down on 5 May 2021 in Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v Chief Minister of Maharashtra. Each agitation sought entry into the existing caste-based compartment for one more community; none sought a general economic compartment. The amendment of January 2019 answered the agitations with a different instrument from the one they demanded, offering their poorer households an economic route while refusing their communities the caste listing they had marched for.

That substitution is the political fact the methodologically-distinct reading must absorb, and it can. The strategic reading notices the answer's timing; the architectural reading notices its form. A government answering caste agitations with caste listings would have extended the Mandal architecture by accretion, as every decade since 1990 had done. The government of January 2019 instead generalised the answer: any household outside the lists, of whatever community, could claim the benefit on economic proof alone, and no community acquired a listing. The Patidar, Jat and Maratha leaderships received the amendment ambivalently for exactly this reason, since it dissolved their community claim into a household test. The amendment's defenders, including Thawar Chand Gehlot in the Rajya Sabha debate, presented the generality as the measure's virtue, a benefit addressed to poverty rather than to caste organisation. Its critics presented the same generality as evasion, a refusal to confront the caste structure that organised the deprivation the agitations expressed. The adjudication offered here is that both descriptions are true of the politics and neither reaches the architecture: the instrument installed in January 2019 sorts by household economics, and every later legal event, the referral of 2020, the verdict of 2022, the implementation pattern thereafter, has been governed by that sorting rule rather than by the agitations that formed its occasion.

## The Scholars' Disagreement, Adjudicated

The named disagreement this article is required to adjudicate can be stated in its strongest competing forms, each anchored to a dated text. The political-strategic framing, in the form carried by editorial commentary during the week of passage and by Christophe Jaffrelot's analyses of the Modi government's caste politics published during 2019, holds that the amendment belongs to the ruling party's management of upper-caste discontent before a general election, and that its constitutional dress should not distract from its electoral occasion. The legal-doctrinal framing, in the form developed by Gautam Bhatia's essays of 2019 and his November 2022 post-judgment analysis and by Suhrith Parthasarathy's columns in The Hindu across the same period, holds that the amendment's fate turns on the internal logic of the equality code: whether economic classification survives basic structure review, whether the ceiling binds, whether the exclusion discriminates. The methodologically-distinct reading, defended here, holds that the amendment's operative content is its identification method, that this method explains the text's drafting choices, the verdict's structure and the implementation's shape, and that the other two framings mistake an occasion and a forum for the thing itself.

The adjudication proceeds on evidence the rival framings must also accept. On timing, the strategic framing wins outright: a cabinet decision of 7 January 2019 and passage within forty-eight hours, weeks before a general election, require no architectural explanation, and this article concedes the point without reservation. On the litigation's internal reasoning, the doctrinal framing wins outright: the Constitution Bench argued in the vocabulary of basic structure, ceiling and classification, and any account of the verdict must work through that vocabulary, as the sections above have done. The dispute is over the design, and on the design the evidence runs one way. The choice of the amendment route over the executive route, the placement of the new clauses outside the backward-class clauses, the writing of the quantum into the constitutional text, the negative definition of the beneficiary class, the reproduction of the Sinho template after a decade of dormancy: each choice is a design fact, none is explained by the calendar, and the doctrinal framing encounters each only as the litigation's raw material rather than as the amendment's meaning. The methodologically-distinct reading is therefore adopted as the article's claim, with the two rival framings retained in their proper provinces, strategy for the occasion and doctrine for the forum.

Anupama Rao's genealogical caution, published in her 2019 essays on caste and constitutionalism, stands as the objection this adjudication cannot fully answer and should not pretend to. Rao's argument, in the line of her book The Caste Question of 2009, is that detaching preferential treatment from the history of caste subordination changes what the treatment is: a remedy for a historically produced hierarchy becomes a transfer to the poor of the historically advantaged, and the change of beneficiary quietly reverses the scheme's reparative direction. The dissent of Justice Bhat carried a judicial cognate of this argument into the 2022 verdict. The architectural answer given in this article, that the compartments coexist and the older scheme continues at its full quantum, meets the objection at the level of shares; it does not meet it at the level of meaning, where Rao's point operates. The honest adjudication records the point as standing. The methodologically-distinct reading describes the architecture correctly; whether the architecture's second method honours or dilutes the constitutional commitment the first method embodied is a question on which Bhat and Rao hold one side, the majority and the amendment's drafters hold the other, and the three-to-two division of 7 November 2022 is the measure of how finely the question cuts.

## The Certificate as an Institution

One element of the EWS framework receives less attention than its importance warrants, and the implementation documentation preserved by central departments brings it into view. The caste-based system runs on lists: a candidate's entitlement exists because a commission and a government placed a named community on a schedule, and the candidate proves membership of the community. The EWS system runs on certificates: entitlement exists because a revenue officer attests, for one financial year, that a household satisfies four economic tests. The certificate converts the tehsil office into the gatekeeper of a constitutional benefit, and it relocates the system's characteristic disputes. Litigation inside the Mandal compartment contests list membership and creamy layer status; disputes inside the EWS compartment contest the genuineness of certificates, the aggregation of a family's income across members and districts, and the treatment of assets held jointly or transferred before application. Recruiting bodies reported through the first cycles that certificate verification, referred back to issuing districts, consumed periods that caste certificate verification, resting on standing lists and precedent, did not.

The annual character of the certificate carries a further architectural consequence that distinguishes the two compartments in operation. A household may qualify in a year of low income and fail in a year of prosperity, and a candidate admitted under the quota in one cycle may be unable to claim it in the next, a fluidity without parallel in the list system, where membership neither expires nor fluctuates with earnings. Defenders of the design, in the parliamentary debate and in commentary on the verdict, treated the fluidity as the method's integrity: a benefit addressed to economic weakness should lapse when the weakness lifts. Critics treated the same fluidity as insecurity imposed on precisely the households least able to plan around it, and as an invitation to income management at the margin of the line. Both readings describe the same administrative fact, and the fact itself is the methodological distinction expressed in administrative time: the caste-based method certifies an identity, the economic method certifies a condition, and conditions, unlike identities, must be re-proved. The post-2022 record, in which EWS vacancies in several institutions went unfilled in early cycles while the certificate machinery matured, shows the cost of that re-proof burden in its first years; the record of later cycles will show whether the machinery's reach converges on the beneficiary population the amendment defined.

## The Amendment in the States: Adoption, Variation and Friction

The 103rd Amendment speaks to the Union and the states alike, since Articles 15 and 16 govern both levels, and the state record after January 2019 forms a distinct chapter of the dual implementation. Gujarat's adoption on 14 January 2019, announced by the government of Vijay Rupani within a day of presidential assent and effective for admissions and recruitments from that date, made the state the amendment's demonstration case, a position fitting the state whose 2016 ordinance the Gujarat High Court had quashed on 4 August 2016. Assam, Jharkhand, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh and other states issued their own notifications across 2019, generally adopting the central criteria while reserving state discretion over the income line and the certificate machinery. The state adoptions matter architecturally because they show the economic method travelling through the federal system by notification rather than by fresh constitutional argument in each state: once Articles 15(6) and 16(6) stood in the text, a state government needed only an executive decision to activate the compartment within its services and institutions, where the caste-based compartment had required commissions, lists and litigation in every state across decades.

Variation followed adoption, and the variation marks the boundary of the amendment's uniformity. States set differing income ceilings for their own services in some instances, prescribed differing certificate authorities, and faced litigation over whether state-specific EWS sub-quotas or carry-forward rules for unfilled EWS vacancies conformed to the parent clauses. The unfilled-seat question recurred across jurisdictions: where EWS vacancies in an admission cycle found no certified candidate, institutions asked whether the seats reverted to the unreserved pool in the same cycle, and the answers given by university ordinances and state rules diverged until administrative practice settled unevenly across sectors. These frictions are the ordinary growing pains of a new identification method, and the record distinguishes them from the constitutional questions the Constitution Bench answered. No state court after November 2022 entertained a serious challenge to the compartment's existence; the litigation frontier moved to criteria, certificates and carry-forward, which is to say from architecture to administration.

A comparison with the Maratha litigation sharpens the point. Maharashtra's Socially and Educationally Backward Classes Act of 30 November 2018 granted sixteen percent to one named community inside the caste-based compartment, and the Constitution Bench in Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil on 5 May 2021 struck it down, holding that no extraordinary circumstance justified breaching the fifty percent ceiling and declining to refer Indra Sawhney for reconsideration. The same ceiling that destroyed the Maratha quota in 2021 did not touch the EWS quota in 2022, and the difference between the two outcomes is the methodological distinction in judicial dress: a caste-based addition counts toward the ceiling that disciplines its compartment, while the economic compartment, on the majority's reasoning, stands outside that count. Two Constitution Benches, fourteen months apart, thus mapped the dual architecture from opposite sides, one enforcing the ceiling inside the older method and the other refusing to extend it across methods. Any account of Indian reservation law after 2022 must begin from that paired map, and it is the strongest single confirmation the record offers for the reading defended in this article.

## Periodic Review and the Open Questions of the Dual System

The dual system carries three open questions into its future, each identified in the 2022 opinions or in the scholarly record, and each arising from the methodological distinction rather than from politics. The first is revision of the economic criteria. The eight lakh income line and the asset tests entered through notification in January 2019; the line's real value declines with each year of price rise, and Justice Pardiwala's concurrence recorded the expectation that the criteria and the quantum would be revisited periodically, since a category defined by economic position must track the economy it measures. No revision had been notified through the period the implementation documentation covers, and the gap between the concurrence's expectation and the administrative record is the dual system's most visible unfinished business. The second open question concerns the boundary's stability. The exclusion of the listed classes, upheld by three judges against two, remains the verdict's most reasoned-against holding; the dissent's equality argument stands in the reports awaiting a bench willing to reopen it, and the scholarly literature, including Gautam Bhatia's post-judgment analysis, continues to develop it. The third question concerns interaction between the compartments: as sub-categorisation inside the Scheduled Castes advanced under the permission recognised by the Constitution Bench of August 2024 in State of Punjab v Davinder Singh, the logic of finer internal classification pressed on both compartments at once, inviting the question whether an economic compartment that sorts households annually and a caste compartment that sorts communities by relative backwardness can remain administratively separate as their classifications grow more granular.

None of these questions unsettles the adjudication this article has defended, because each arises inside the architecture the amendment built and the verdict organised. The amendment of January 2019 installed a second identification method in the Indian reservation system; the judgment of November 2022 gave that method its constitutional operating rules, a clause-specific ceiling, a defensible boundary and a basic structure clearance; and the implementation since has run the two methods in parallel, with friction at their junction and revision pending in their criteria. The political-strategic reading explains the week of the installation, the legal-doctrinal reading explains the forum of its testing, and the methodologically-distinct reading explains the thing installed and tested, which is why it is the reading this article defends as the amendment's durable description.

## The Amendment and the Idea of Representation

A reservation system built on group membership carries a theory of representation inside it. Article 16(4) speaks of a backward class not adequately represented in the services, and Article 335 speaks of the claims of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes being taken into consideration in appointments; the Mandal framework inherited that language and made representation of listed communities in the bureaucracy one of its stated ends. The EWS clauses carry no representation language at all. Articles 15(6) and 16(6) speak of advancement and of reservation for a section defined by economic weakness, and they nowhere suggest that the section is under-represented as a collectivity or that its members will represent anyone but themselves. The absence is textual and deliberate, and it marks another level at which the two methods diverge. The caste-based method treats the beneficiary as a member whose presence in an institution answers a collective deficit; the economic method treats the beneficiary as a household whose poverty, by itself, satisfies the claim. Justice Maheshwari's majority opinion noticed the distinction in passing when it described the new clauses as addressing economic disadvantage as such, unmediated by the representation rationale that organises the older clauses.

The distinction has consequences for how each compartment defends itself in argument. A representation-based scheme must periodically demonstrate the deficit it answers, which is why the Mandal compartment generates commissions, quantifiable data requirements of the kind M. Nagaraj imposed for promotional reservation on 19 October 2006, and litigation over whether a listed class remains backward. An economics-based scheme must demonstrate only that its line is rationally drawn, which is why the EWS litigation concentrated on the line's boundary, the exclusion, rather than on any deficit of representation among the unreserved poor. Defenders of the amendment in Parliament, including Arun Jaitley in the Lok Sabha on 8 January 2019, used exactly this spare logic: the benefit answered poverty, poverty needed no census of communities, and the absence of a caste enumeration behind the measure, which opposition members attacked as a foundation defect, was on the government's account a feature of a scheme that enumerates households at the point of application instead. The critics' rejoinder, developed in Anupama Rao's essays, is that a scheme dispensing with representation dispenses with the constitutional memory of why preferential treatment exists, converting reparation into relief. The adjudication offered in this article treats the textual point as settled and the normative point as genuinely open: the clauses do omit the representation rationale, the omission is part of their methodological distinctness, and whether that omission impoverishes the constitutional project is the question the dissent and the genealogical critics keep alive against the majority's answer.

## The Seat Expansion and the Arithmetic of Transition

The implementation of January 2019 carried an administrative device that softened the amendment's arithmetic and deserves record, because it shaped the compartment's first years in the institutions where the quota bit hardest. When the Ministry of Human Resource Development directed central educational institutions to implement the ten percent EWS reservation from the academic session of 2019, it coupled the direction with an instruction to expand total seats so that the absolute number of seats available to the unreserved category, and to the existing reserved categories, would not decline. Institutions were permitted to phase the expansion across two academic years, 2019 and 2020, and the University Grants Commission and the All India Council for Technical Education issued corresponding directions for universities and technical institutions. The device converted a zero-sum redistribution inside a fixed pool into an expansion of the pool itself, at the cost of infrastructure and faculty demands that institutions recorded through the period.

The seat expansion is more than a transitional courtesy, and it interacts with the methodological argument in a way commentary has underexplored. A caste-based addition inside a fixed pool, of the kind Maharashtra attempted for the Maratha quota in 2018, raises the ceiling question immediately and in its sharpest form, because every seat for the new entry is visibly taken from the existing shares. The EWS transition, by expanding the pool in centrally funded institutions, muted that visibility in education while leaving services, where posts cannot be expanded by direction, to absorb the full arithmetic of the sixty percent. The dual implementation therefore ran at different temperatures in its two fields during the first cycles, with admissions disputes concentrated on certificates and eligibility and services disputes concentrated on roster arithmetic. Justice Pardiwala's concurrence, in observing that the new clauses operate in addition to the existing reservation, stated the services position in law; the seat expansion stated the education position in administration. Both follow from the same drafting choice, the word additional carried in Article 16(6) and mirrored in the design of Article 15(6), and both confirm that the drafters understood the compartment as a parallel structure to be accommodated beside the older structure rather than as a claim carved out of its shares.

## How Other Democracies Sort by Income, and Why the Comparison Misleads

Comparative material entered the Janhit Abhiyan arguments, and it enters most commentary on the amendment, through the example of class-based affirmative action in the United States, where race-conscious admissions at universities faced sustained constitutional challenge culminating in Students for Fair Admissions v Harvard of 29 June 2023. The comparison misleads in a direction worth correcting on the record. American class-based preferences operate as one factor inside holistic admissions discretion, administered by institutions and defended as diversity instruments; the Indian EWS compartment operates as a fixed constitutional quota of ten percent, administered by the state through certificates and defended as a remedial entitlement. The two devices share an economic criterion and nothing else: not the decision-maker, not the quantum's fixity, not the legal theory, not the beneficiary's enforceable claim. South Africa's employment equity framework, which works through designated groups defined substantially by race and gender under the Employment Equity Act of 1998, resembles the Mandal compartment's group logic more than the EWS compartment's household logic. The genuinely distinctive feature of the Indian instrument, visible only when the comparisons are drawn carefully, is that it constitutionalised a household economic test as a quota, a device without a close parallel in the jurisdictions the litigants cited. That singularity is further evidence for the methodological reading: the amendment was not the importation of a familiar foreign device but the construction of a new identification method inside an existing constitutional scheme, and its adjudication in 2022 proceeded, correctly, from the Indian text and the Indian case law rather than from comparative templates.

## What the Amendment Changed for the Reservation Debate Itself

The last consequence to record concerns the debate the amendment inherited and altered. Before January 2019, argument over Indian reservation moved along a single axis: which communities belonged on the lists, in what proportions, with what exclusions at the advanced edge. The Mandal implementation of 1990, the creamy layer jurisprudence of 1992, the promotion cases of 2006 and 2018, and the community agitations of 2015 to 2018 all argued along that axis. The EWS amendment introduced a second axis, orthogonal to the first: whether disadvantage should be identified by group at all. Every subsequent development in the field shows the second axis at work. The sub-categorisation question, carried to its Constitution Bench settlement for the Scheduled Castes in August 2024, argues about finer sorting inside a group framework; the caste census demand, pressed by opposition parties through the 2024 general election and answered by the Union Cabinet's decision of 30 April 2025 to include caste enumeration in the forthcoming census, argues for better data beneath the group framework; and the EWS compartment stands apart from both arguments, requiring no caste data and no group settlement, only the annual proof of household means. A debate that once asked only who is backward also asks, after 2019, whether backwardness is the only question. That enlargement of the argument is the amendment's most durable political effect, and it survives independently of the verdict's fate in any future reconsideration, because the second axis, once introduced into constitutional text, organises positions that no judgment can unstate. The article's claim can therefore be given its final form. The 103rd Amendment introduced a methodologically distinct reservation into the Indian constitutional order; the Janhit Abhiyan majority operationalised that distinctness as law on 7 November 2022; and the dual implementation that followed, two identification methods running in parallel under one equality code, is the architecture within which Indian reservation politics will be conducted for as far ahead as the record allows sight.

## The Amendment's Passage Through the 2019 Election

The amendment's relationship to the general election of April and May 2019 is the factual core of the political-strategic reading, and the record of that relationship cuts in two directions that the adjudication should state separately. The Bharatiya Janata Party's manifesto for the 2019 election, released on 8 April 2019 under the title Sankalp Patra, carried the EWS reservation among the government's completed measures rather than among its promises, since the amendment had commenced three months before the manifesto appeared; the measure figured in the campaign as evidence of the government's delivery to households outside the organised quota constituencies. Opposition parties that had supplied the votes for the amendment's passage in January did not campaign against it in April, and no major party's manifesto proposed its repeal or the revision of its exclusion clause. The election result of 23 May 2019, returning the Bharatiya Janata Party with 303 seats, therefore neither ratified the amendment in any legally cognisable sense nor disturbed it politically; it confirmed the judgement the January divisions had already recorded, that the compartment's beneficiary definition commanded assent across the party system even among parties contesting its author for power. Political scientists analysing the 2019 verdict, including the Lokniti post-poll studies published by the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies, located the amendment within the government's broader welfare-delivery appeal rather than as a discrete vote-moving measure, a finding consistent with the household method's diffusion of its beneficiaries across every constituency instead of their concentration in organised blocs. The strategic reading is entitled to the timing; it is not entitled to the aftermath, in which the measure the opposition helped pass became a fixture none of the passing parties sought to unsettle.

## The Review Petitions and the Settlement's Durability

The verdict's authority was tested once more through the review jurisdiction, and the outcome belongs to the record of the dual system's consolidation. Review petitions against the Janhit Abhiyan judgment, filed by unsuccessful petitioners and associations, came before a bench headed by Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, and on 9 May 2023 the Supreme Court dismissed them, holding that no error apparent on the face of the record justified reopening the Constitution Bench's conclusions. Review in Indian constitutional practice is a narrow jurisdiction, and its dismissal settles little about the underlying argument; it settles the procedural finality of the 2022 holdings, which stand as the governing law of the EWS compartment unless a larger bench revisits them in a future case. The distinction between finality and persuasion matters for reading the settlement's durability. The majority's three holdings operate with full legal force: economic criteria are a permissible basis, the ceiling does not transfer across compartments, and the exclusion stands. The dissent's equality argument operates with full intellectual force in the literature, in Gautam Bhatia's continuing analysis and in the teaching of the case, awaiting the litigant and the bench that might one day carry it into law.

The settlement's durability also shows in how later benches have used the judgment. When the seven-judge Constitution Bench in State of Punjab v Davinder Singh, delivering judgment on 1 August 2024, permitted sub-classification within the Scheduled Castes by six to one, the opinions treated the post-2022 reservation landscape as a field of compartments whose internal ordering the Court supervises, and the EWS compartment figured in that landscape as a settled element rather than as a contested frontier. The pattern across the 2022, 2023 and 2024 orders is consistent: the methodological settlement of Janhit Abhiyan is load-bearing in the Court's subsequent architecture, cited as the authority that economic classification stands on its own constitutional footing. Settlements of that kind are rarely disturbed quickly in the Indian reports, and the working assumption of administrators, institutions and recruiting bodies since 2022 has been that the dual implementation is the durable frame. The assumption is an administrative fact, recorded here as such; the scholarly contest over the exclusion continues on its own track, and the two tracks, law settled and argument open, are the characteristic condition of Indian constitutional settlements in their first decade.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the EWS reservation?

The EWS reservation is a ten percent reservation in admission to educational institutions and in initial appointments to government posts, created for citizens who belong to economically weaker sections and who are not covered by the existing reservations for the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes. Parliament created it through the Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act of January 2019, which inserted Article 15(6) and Article 16(6) into the Constitution. Eligibility turns on household economics alone: gross annual family income below eight lakh rupees, agricultural land below five acres, a residential flat below one thousand square feet, and residential plot limits that vary between notified municipalities and other areas. A revenue authority certifies the household's position through an income and asset certificate valid for one financial year. The Supreme Court upheld the framework on 7 November 2022 in Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India.

### Q: What is the 103rd Amendment?

The 103rd Amendment is the Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act, 2019, the amendment that introduced reservation for economically weaker sections into the Indian Constitution. Introduced in Parliament as the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-fourth Amendment) Bill, it passed the Lok Sabha on 8 January 2019 by 323 votes to 3 and the Rajya Sabha on 9 January 2019 by 165 votes to 7, received presidential assent on 12 January 2019, and commenced on 14 January 2019. Its operative provisions inserted Article 15(6), authorising up to ten percent reservation in educational institutions other than minority institutions, and Article 16(6), authorising up to ten percent reservation in initial appointments to government posts, in each case for economically weaker sections other than the classes covered by the backward-class clauses. The amendment wrote its ten percent maximum into the constitutional text itself, which distinguishes it from earlier reservation provisions whose quanta rest on executive decision and judicial doctrine.

### Q: What was the Janhit Abhiyan verdict?

The Janhit Abhiyan verdict is the judgment of a five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court in Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India, delivered on 7 November 2022, which upheld the 103rd Amendment by a majority of three to two. Justice Dinesh Maheshwari wrote the leading opinion for himself and Justice Bela M. Trivedi, and Justice J. B. Pardiwala concurred. The majority held that reservation on economic criteria alone is constitutionally permissible and does not violate the basic structure, that the fifty percent ceiling governs backward-class reservations and does not bind the new economic compartment, and that excluding the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes from the EWS category is a permissible classification. Justice S. Ravindra Bhat dissented, holding the exclusion discriminatory and destructive of the equality code, and Chief Justice U. U. Lalit concurred with the dissent.

### Q: Who is eligible for EWS reservation?

A citizen is eligible for EWS reservation when the citizen's household is not covered by reservation for the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes or the Other Backward Classes, and the household satisfies the economic criteria notified in January 2019. Those criteria, applied together, require gross annual family income below eight lakh rupees from all sources, agricultural land below five acres, no residential flat of one thousand square feet or more, and no residential plot of one hundred square yards or more in a notified municipality or two hundred square yards or more elsewhere. Family, for this purpose, includes the applicant, parents, spouse, and siblings and children below eighteen years of age. The applicant proves eligibility through an income and asset certificate issued by a competent revenue authority such as the tehsildar, and the certificate holds good for the financial year in which it is issued, after which the household's position must be certified afresh.

### Q: Does EWS exceed 50 percent ceiling?

In arithmetic, yes: where the EWS reservation applies, total reservation reaches sixty percent, being the fifty percent of the existing framework together with the additional ten percent for economically weaker sections. In constitutional law, the Supreme Court's majority in Janhit Abhiyan v Union of India held on 7 November 2022 that no breach of the ceiling occurs, because the fifty percent rule announced in Indra Sawhney in 1992 disciplines reservations made under the backward-class clauses, Articles 15(4) and 16(4), whereas the EWS reservation rests on the separate clauses 15(6) and 16(6) with their own textual maximum. The majority also reasoned that the ceiling is a judicially evolved rule of prudence rather than an inflexible basic structure feature. The dissent of Justice S. Ravindra Bhat treated the ceiling as a discipline of long standing that the additional ten percent breached, making this question the point on which the bench's division turned most sharply after the exclusion question.

### Q: Why are SC ST OBC excluded from EWS?

The Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes are excluded from the EWS category because the amendment defines its beneficiary class as economically weaker sections other than the classes already covered by the backward-class clauses of Articles 15 and 16. The design logic is that the new compartment addresses deprivation that the existing reservation system does not reach, and households inside that system already hold its benefits, so extending the economic quota to them would duplicate coverage. The majority in Janhit Abhiyan upheld the exclusion on that basis, treating the listed classes as a covered population and the EWS class as a separate class defined by non-coverage. The dissent of Justice S. Ravindra Bhat, supported by Chief Justice U. U. Lalit, held the exclusion unconstitutional: a poor household from a listed class suffers the same economic deprivation as a poor household outside the lists, and denying the first household the benefit for the reason of its caste membership discriminates on a prohibited ground.

### Q: How does EWS differ from Mandal reservation?

The two frameworks differ in their method of identifying beneficiaries. The Mandal framework, built on the Mandal Commission report of 31 December 1980, identifies backward classes through eleven indicators covering social, educational and economic conditions, weighted so that social indicators dominate, and it qualifies individuals by membership of a listed caste or class, subject to the creamy layer exclusion. The EWS framework identifies beneficiaries through household economic tests alone, an income line at eight lakh rupees with land, flat and plot ceilings, and it qualifies individuals by certificate rather than by list membership. The constitutional homes differ as well: Articles 15(4) and 16(4) for the Mandal framework, Articles 15(6) and 16(6) for the EWS framework. Their governing judgments differ too, Indra Sawhney of 1992 settling the ceiling and creamy layer for the caste-based framework, and Janhit Abhiyan of 2022 holding that the ceiling does not transfer to the economic framework.

### Q: What is the creamy layer and does it apply to EWS reservation?

The creamy layer is the exclusion, settled by the Supreme Court in Indra Sawhney on 16 November 1992, under which the socially and economically advanced members of the Other Backward Classes are removed from that class's reservation benefits, tested through parental income and status criteria such as holding constitutional or senior government office. It does not apply to EWS reservation as a named doctrine, because the EWS category performs the equivalent sorting by its own design: instead of admitting a whole class and then excluding its advanced layer, the EWS tests admit only households that pass income and asset ceilings in the first place. The practical resemblance is close, since both devices use family income to police the boundary of a benefit, and administrators issue both the creamy layer assessment and the EWS certificate through revenue documentation. The doctrinal positions differ, however, because the creamy layer corrects a group classification from inside, while the EWS tests constitute the classification itself.

### Q: Which commission recommended an economic quota before the 2019 amendment?

The Commission for Economically Backward Classes, chaired by Major General S. R. Sinho and appointed by the Union government on 31 July 2006, made that recommendation. Its report, submitted on 22 July 2010 to the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment, proposed ten percent reservation in central government employment and in centrally funded educational institutions for economically backward classes other than the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes, together with measures on housing, health, credit and education support for the same population. The United Progressive Alliance government did not legislate on the report, and it remained unimplemented for over eight years. When the Union government carried the 103rd Amendment through Parliament in January 2019, the measure reproduced the Sinho design in its central features, including the ten percent figure, the beneficiary boundary around households outside the existing quotas, and the reliance on income and asset tests to identify them.

### Q: What documents establish EWS eligibility for an applicant?

EWS eligibility is established through the income and asset certificate issued by a competent revenue authority, ordinarily the tehsildar of the applicant's area, with district magistrates and other notified officers also competent under the Department of Personnel and Training memorandum of 31 January 2019. The applicant supports the application with documents that evidence the four economic tests: income records for the family's earning members for the preceding financial year, land records showing the household's agricultural holdings, and documentation of residential property against the flat and plot limits. The issuing authority verifies the declarations against revenue records before certifying. The certificate is valid for the financial year of its issue, and recruiting bodies and admitting institutions call for it at the stage their rules prescribe, commonly at application or at document verification. Because the certificate speaks to a single year, a household whose circumstances improve must not rely on an earlier certificate, and one whose circumstances decline may qualify in a later year after failing earlier.

### Q: Does EWS reservation apply to state government jobs and admissions?

Yes, because Articles 15(6) and 16(6) bind and empower the states as well as the Union, and state governments activated the quota for their own services and institutions through notifications beginning in 2019. Gujarat adopted the quota with effect from 14 January 2019, the date the amendment itself commenced, and other states including Assam, Jharkhand, Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh followed with their own instruments across 2019. State schemes generally adopt the central criteria, including the eight lakh income line, though states retain discretion in administering their services and have varied details such as certificate authorities and the treatment of unfilled vacancies. A candidate therefore claims EWS reservation in a state recruitment under that state's notification and criteria, not under the central memorandum, and a certificate issued for central purposes may require corresponding state documentation. The Supreme Court's judgment in Janhit Abhiyan, upholding the parent clauses, governs the state schemes equally, since their legal foundation is the same constitutional text.

### Q: What happens when EWS seats remain unfilled in an admission cycle?

The treatment of unfilled EWS seats rests on the rules of the admitting institution and the applicable government instructions rather than on the constitutional text, and practice across the first cycles was uneven. In centrally funded institutions, seats reserved for economically weaker sections that found no eligible certified candidate in a cycle were in several instances carried into subsequent rounds of the same admission process, and institutional rules governed whether a seat still vacant at the close of counselling reverted to the unreserved pool. Departmental reporting across the 2019 to 2021 cycles recorded partly filled EWS quotas in a number of institutions, a pattern administrators attributed to the certificate machinery's early reach rather than to an absence of eligible households. The distinction matters for reading the record: an unfilled EWS seat in the first cycles measures the administrative system's capacity to certify applicants within the admission window, and it does not measure the size of the population the amendment defined, which no census or commission has enumerated.

### Q: How was the eight lakh rupees income line chosen?

The eight lakh figure arrived with the Department of Personnel and Training notification of January 2019 as the gross annual family income ceiling for EWS eligibility, and the government defended it in Parliament and in court as aligned with the income threshold then operating in the creamy layer test for the Other Backward Classes, which the Department had fixed at eight lakh rupees by its memorandum of 13 September 2017. The alignment gave administrators a tested figure and gave households a single line that sorted both schemes. Critics, including Suhrith Parthasarathy in his January 2019 commentary, objected that household income distribution data placed the overwhelming majority of Indian households below that line, so the ceiling sorted by list membership more than by deprivation in practice. The figure remains a notified administrative criterion rather than a constitutional term, which means the government may revise it by notification without further amendment, and Justice J. B. Pardiwala's concurring opinion in Janhit Abhiyan recorded the expectation that the criteria would receive periodic reassessment.

### Q: What does Article 15(6) of the Constitution provide?

Article 15(6), inserted by the 103rd Amendment with effect from 14 January 2019, empowers the state to make special provision for the advancement of any economically weaker sections of citizens other than the classes mentioned in clauses (4) and (5) of Article 15. The clause expressly includes the power to reserve seats in educational institutions, whether aided or unaided by the state, up to a maximum of ten percent of the total seats, and it excludes minority educational institutions referred to in Article 30(1) from that reservation power. The clause operates within Article 15, which carries the Constitution's prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex and place of birth, and it functions as an enabling exception of the same family as clause (4), which enables special provision for socially and educationally backward classes and for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes. Its beneficiary description is economic throughout, which is the textual foundation of the EWS framework's distinct method.

### Q: What does Article 16(6) of the Constitution provide?

Article 16(6), inserted by the 103rd Amendment with effect from 14 January 2019, empowers the state to make provision for the reservation of appointments or posts in favour of any economically weaker sections of citizens other than the classes mentioned in clause (4) of Article 16, up to a maximum of ten percent of the posts, and it states that this reservation operates in addition to the existing reservation. The clause sits within Article 16's guarantee of equality of opportunity in public employment, beside clause (4), which enables reservation for backward classes not adequately represented in the services. Two features of clause (6) carried weight in the Janhit Abhiyan litigation: the ten percent maximum appears in the constitutional text itself rather than in executive decision, and the clause's beneficiary class is described by economic weakness and non-membership of the clause (4) classes, which the majority read as creating a compartment distinct from the backward-class compartment that the fifty percent ceiling disciplines.

### Q: How many judges heard the Janhit Abhiyan case and how did they divide?

A Constitution Bench of five judges heard the challenges to the 103rd Amendment and delivered judgment on 7 November 2022, dividing three to two in favour of upholding the amendment. The bench consisted of Chief Justice U. U. Lalit and Justices Dinesh Maheshwari, S. Ravindra Bhat, Bela M. Trivedi and J. B. Pardiwala. Justice Maheshwari wrote the leading opinion for the majority, speaking also for Justice Trivedi, and Justice Pardiwala concurred separately, so three judges upheld the amendment on each of the three questions concerning economic criteria, the fifty percent ceiling and the exclusion of the listed classes. Justice Bhat wrote the dissent, and Chief Justice Lalit concurred with it, holding that the exclusion of poor households of the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes and the Other Backward Classes from the new quota violated the equality code and damaged the basic structure. The bench had heard the matter in September 2022, and the judgment arrived on Chief Justice Lalit's penultimate working day before his retirement on 8 November 2022.

### Q: What did the Narasimha Rao government attempt in 1991?

On 25 September 1991 the government of Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao issued an office memorandum that modified the Mandal implementation scheme in two respects, and one of them added a ten percent reservation for economically backward sections of citizens other than the classes covered by the existing quotas. The device was executive action under the existing constitutional text, resting on the argument that Article 16(4) and the general equality provisions could sustain an economic quota without amendment. The Supreme Court dismantled that position in Indra Sawhney on 16 November 1992, where the majority held the ten percent economic quota unconstitutional because economic criterion alone could not identify a backward class under Article 16(4). The 1991 attempt matters to the 2019 amendment because it fixed the legal obstacle the amendment was drafted to remove: after 1992, a purely economic quota required either a bench willing to overrule Indra Sawhney on that point or a constitutional amendment writing the authority into new clauses, and the government of 2019 chose the amendment.

### Q: Does EWS reservation apply to private educational institutions?

Article 15(6) extends the EWS reservation power to educational institutions whether aided or unaided by the state, which brings private unaided institutions within the framework, and it expressly excepts the minority educational institutions protected by Article 30(1). The inclusion of unaided institutions marked a significant reach for the new compartment, since the older seat-reservation jurisprudence for private institutions had developed through separate litigation over institutional autonomy. In operation, the quota applies as the admitting framework prescribes for each sector, with centrally funded institutions implementing the ten percent from the academic session of 2019 and the Ministry of Human Resource Development directing an expansion of total seats in those institutions so that the number of seats available to the unreserved category would not fall in absolute terms. That seat-expansion instruction, announced alongside the amendment's implementation in January 2019, addressed the objection that a new ten percent compartment inside a fixed pool must reduce every existing category's absolute intake during the transition.

### Q: Can a candidate from an Other Backward Classes family claim EWS reservation?

No, under the amendment's design a candidate whose family belongs to a class covered by the existing reservations cannot claim the EWS category, because Articles 15(6) and 16(6) define their beneficiaries as economically weaker sections other than the classes mentioned in the backward-class clauses. The exclusion holds even where the household's income falls below the eight lakh line and the family satisfies every economic test, and it holds even where the candidate personally could not qualify under the Other Backward Classes quota because the creamy layer exclusion removes advanced households of those classes from its benefits. That last situation drew sustained criticism in the litigation and the literature, since a creamy layer household of a listed class is too advanced for the caste-based quota and too listed for the economic quota, falling between the compartments. The majority in Janhit Abhiyan accepted the boundary as the design's defining line, while the dissent treated outcomes of exactly this kind as evidence that the exclusion discriminated on caste grounds against the Constitution's protected classes.

### Q: Does EWS reservation apply to promotions in government service?

No, the EWS reservation as enacted and upheld applies to initial appointments and to admissions, not to promotions. Article 16(6) authorises reservation of appointments or posts, and the Department of Personnel and Training's implementation instructions of 2019 framed the quota around direct recruitment to civil posts and services, leaving the promotion framework untouched. Promotional reservation in Indian law belongs to a different line of provisions and judgments, principally Article 16(4A), inserted by the 77th Amendment of 1995 for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, and the conditions developed in M. Nagaraj v Union of India of 19 October 2006 and Jarnail Singh v Lachhmi Narain Gupta of 26 September 2018 concerning quantifiable data and the creamy layer in promotions. No corresponding clause exists for economically weaker sections, and neither the amendment's text nor the Janhit Abhiyan opinions created one, so a serving employee gains no promotional preference from EWS status, which attaches to the household's certified position at the point of entry into service.
