Everyone knows what Mohamed Salah is going to do. That is the most repeated observation in football and it is completely true, and it is also the reason so many sides arrive with a scouting report and leave with a defeat. Knowing the action is worth nothing on its own. The plan that works is a three-player geometry that closes the predicted route without opening the alternative, and the thing it gives away, deliberately and repeatedly, is the wide crossing position outside the full-back.

![How to stop Mohamed Salah, a counter blueprint for the predictability fork - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-53.webp)

This blueprint is built around a single argument. The reason prediction fails here is not that the prediction is wrong; it is that a lone full-back cannot act on it. Every stance he can adopt closes one route and opens another, and both routes are genuinely live, which means the information the analyst provided cannot be converted into a defensive action by the person who most needs it. Fix that and the scouting report becomes worth something. Leave it unfixed and you have a very well-informed defeat.

## The player in planning terms

An Egyptian left-footed attacker operating from the right, with a career through Basel, Chelsea, Fiorentina, Roma, and Liverpool. That is the whole of the biography that matters for a counter-plan.

What matters is the threat structure, and it has two primary components that must be treated as a pair rather than as a list.

The first is the cut inside onto the left foot: receiving wide or in the half-space, moving the ball toward the centre, and arriving at a shooting angle or a combination angle in the space between the full-back and the centre-back. This is the action everybody names.

The second is the run into the space behind the full-back's inside shoulder: a straight or slightly diagonal run into the channel, taken early, often before the ball has been played, and frequently while the defender's attention is on the ball rather than on him. This is the action everybody underrates, and it is the reason the first action works.

There is a third component that behaves differently enough to need its own owner: the arrival at the far post when the ball is on the opposite flank. That is not a wide-attacker threat at all in structural terms. It is a striker's threat performed by a wide player, and it is defended by different people using different cues. It is handled separately later.

For the category framing that places this profile alongside the other three types of elite wide attacker, [the cluster guide on stopping elite wingers](/2021/04/01/stopping-elite-wingers-complete-guide/) sets out the taxonomy. This article assumes it and goes considerably deeper into the inverted case.

## The useful-predictability test

Here is the organizing claim of this blueprint, and it generalizes well beyond one player:

Knowing what an attacker will do is worth nothing unless a third player can close the alternative. Scouting a pattern is only actionable when the shape can afford the cover.

Call it the useful-predictability test, and apply it to any scouting observation you are about to hand a defender. If the defender can act on the information without opening a worse option, the information is useful. If acting on it requires him to concede something equally dangerous, the information is not a plan, it is trivia, and giving it to him may make his defending worse rather than better.

That last point deserves emphasis because it is counterintuitive. A full-back who has been told all week that his opponent will cut inside is a full-back predisposed to protect the inside, which means he is a full-back who will be beaten on the outside and in behind more often than he otherwise would have been. The scouting report has degraded his performance. This happens constantly, it is invisible to everyone involved, and it is the direct consequence of failing the test.

The test also explains why some scouting reports transform a defence and others do nothing. The difference is almost never the quality of the observation. It is whether the shape can afford to act on it.

## The fork

The specific reason a lone full-back cannot act on the prediction is worth setting out in full, because every subsequent section is a response to it.

### Standing inside

The full-back positions himself so his body and his weight are angled to prevent the cut, roughly half a yard inside the attacker's inside shoulder. This is what the scouting report seems to ask for.

What it closes: the immediate cut inside becomes difficult, because the first touch toward the centre runs into the defender.

What it opens: two things, and both are dangerous. The run into the channel behind his inside shoulder is now available, because a defender angled inside is a defender whose recovery path into that channel is longer than it looks. And the route outside is open, which matters less on its own and matters a great deal when combined with an overlapping full-back, because the overlap turns an unthreatening outside route into a delivery from the byline.

### Standing outside

The full-back angles to protect the flank and the channel behind, showing the attacker toward the centre.

What it closes: the run in behind and the outside route.

What it opens: the inside lane, which is the route the attacker wanted, leading to a shooting angle in the most valuable area available to a wide player.

### Standing square

The compromise, and the worst of the three. A defender square to an attacker who can go either way has committed to nothing and closed nothing, and the attacker chooses at speed from a full menu. Square is what defenders do when they have been given contradictory instructions, and it is the position from which the most damaging sequences begin.

### Why the fork cannot be resolved individually

Each stance is a trade, and the trades are close enough in value that no stance is obviously correct. The attacker's advantage is not that his action is unpredictable; it is that both of his actions are viable, so his choice is made after the defender has already committed to something.

The alternative routes are also maintained deliberately. The run in behind is not a fallback that occurs when the cut is unavailable; it is a functional part of the primary threat, because it is what forces a defender to respect depth, and respecting depth is what makes the inside lane accessible. Remove the defender's fear of the run and the cut becomes far easier to defend, which is exactly why the run keeps happening.

So the fork is structural, not tactical. It cannot be resolved by a better stance, a quicker defender, or a more detailed briefing. It can only be resolved by adding players.

### Why does knowing what he will do not help?

Because both of his options are live, and a lone full-back closing one opens the other. The information only becomes actionable when a third player covers the option the full-back concedes. Prediction without cover changes which way a defender is beaten, not whether he is beaten.
## The predictability fork table

This is the reference artifact for the blueprint. Each row is a defensive stance, matched with what it denies, what it opens, the third player required to close the opened option, and the concession that remains after all three players are working.

| Full-back stance | What it denies | What it opens | Third player required | Concession that remains |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Angled inside | The cut inside onto the left foot | The run into the channel behind the inside shoulder; the outside route | Covering centre-back drops and narrows to own the channel before the run is made | Deep crosses from the corner; the overlap outside |
| Angled outside | The run in behind; the touchline route | The inside lane and the shooting angle | Near-side midfielder shades the entry lane so the ball arrives late, wide, and behind the hip | The recycle backward; possession in front of the block |
| Square | Nothing | Everything, at the attacker's choosing | No third player can rescue this | Not a plan; avoid |
| Neutral, delaying commitment | Nothing immediately; buys time for the other two | The immediate reception, briefly | Both: centre-back in the channel, midfielder on the entry | Wide crossing positions and a late, contested cut |

The fourth row is the recommended plan and it looks weakest on the table, which is the point worth understanding. A neutral full-back denies nothing by himself. What he does is refuse to resolve the fork, which forces the attacker to commit first, at which moment the two covering players have already taken positions that make both branches survivable.

That is the whole blueprint in one row. The full-back is not the solution; he is the delay that lets the solution arrive.

## The three-player resolution

Three jobs, and the plan fails entirely if any one of them is missing. This is not a plan that degrades gracefully with two players, which is worth saying at the outset because a coach with only two available bodies should choose a different plan rather than a diluted version of this one.

### The full-back: neutral, patient, close enough

His stance is deliberately uncommitted. He positions himself square-ish but slightly favouring the inside, close enough that the attacker cannot receive and turn unopposed, far enough that a single touch cannot eliminate him.

The distance is the technical heart of it. Too close and the first touch beats him past the shoulder, which against this profile means an immediate shooting position. Too far and the attacker receives, sets, and chooses at leisure. The workable gap is one where the defender could still adjust if the attacker accelerated, which is a feel rather than a measurement and which is why distance control is the primary attribute in the selection discussion below.

What he must not do is pick a side early. Every instinct trained into a full-back says to show one way, and showing one way here resolves the fork in the attacker's favour before the covering players have taken their positions. Delaying the commitment feels passive and it is the aggressive choice, because it forces the attacker to declare first.

The second thing he must not do is dive in. A tackle attempt against this profile at the moment of the first touch is a coin flip that, if lost, produces a shot from the inside lane with the defence unbalanced.

### The covering centre-back: the channel, not the line

The most important job in the plan, and the one most often left undone.

When the attacker receives wide, the centre-back's instinct is to hold the defensive line, stay square with his partner, and preserve the offside trap. Against this profile that instinct produces goals, because the dangerous space is not behind the line, it is the diagonal channel between the full-back and the centre-back: the corridor the attacker runs into after cutting inside, and the same corridor the run in behind the inside shoulder targets.

Those two threats use the same space. That is the single most useful sentence in this article for a centre-back, because it means one positional adjustment closes both branches of the fork. He drops and narrows into the channel early, before either action has been chosen, and he stays there.

He is not marking anybody. He is occupying a corridor. If the attacker cuts inside, he meets a defender. If the attacker runs in behind, he meets the same defender, arriving from a position that is already goal-side of the run rather than chasing it.

Two things make this hard to install. The first is that it looks like a positional error to anyone watching, because the centre-back appears to have broken the line. The second is that it genuinely does complicate the offside line, and centre-backs are held responsible for offside lines. Both problems are solved by explicit permission: he is allowed to leave the line, he will not be blamed for the offside consequences, and the corridor is his priority. Without that permission he will revert to the default under pressure, which is exactly when the plan is most needed.

### The near-side midfielder: the entry pass

The third job is the least visible and it changes more sequences than the other two combined.

The midfielder does not press the ball-carrier and does not mark the attacker. He positions himself so that his body shadow makes the direct entry pass into the attacker's feet unattractive, which forces the ball wider, later, flatter, or behind the receiving hip.

The effect is small on any single pass and compounds across a match. A pass that arrives half a second later is a pass that arrives after the covering centre-back has reached the channel. A pass that arrives behind the hip costs the attacker a touch to control, and the touch is the window in which the full-back closes the distance. A pass that goes wider than intended means the reception happens further from the shooting angle, which lengthens the cut inside and gives the covering defender time.

The shadow is an active position rather than a passive one. It has to be closer to the ball than instinct suggests, angled across the lane rather than facing the passer, and continuously adjusted as the ball circulates. A midfielder who stands still while the ball travels twenty yards sideways is shadowing a pass nobody intends to play.

### When the three are working

The attacker receives late, wider than he wanted, with a defender in range who has not committed, an occupied channel inside him, and a covering body already goal-side of the run. His best remaining option is the outside route toward a deep crossing position.

That is the plan succeeding. It will look, from the stands, like a side that keeps letting him cross, which is correct, and which is the concession named at the top of this article.

### What does the covering centre-back actually do?

He drops out of the defensive line and narrows into the diagonal corridor between himself and the full-back, before the attacker has chosen a route. Both of the attacker's primary threats use that corridor, so one position closes both. He is occupying space, not marking a player, and he needs explicit permission to leave the line.
## Shape and line height

Two structural decisions frame the three-player geometry, and both are less obvious against this profile than against a pure isolation attacker.

### Line height

The line height question here has a genuinely different answer from the one that applies to attackers whose only threat is running behind, and confusing the two produces the most common planning error against this profile.

A high line supplies the run in behind, which is one of his two primary threats. So the instinct is to drop, and dropping does reduce that threat, and dropping also shortens the distance between his receiving position and the goal, which makes the cut inside more valuable rather than less. A deep block converts a fifteen-yard carry into a five-yard carry, and the shooting position he arrives at is closer.

There is a second effect. A deep block compresses defenders into the area where the cutback and the low cross are most dangerous, which raises the value of the delivery you were trying to prevent.

The workable answer is a mid-block with a line that is neither high nor deep, and with the variability located in the covering centre-back rather than in the whole line. That is the structural insight specific to this profile: instead of moving four defenders up and down to manage the space behind, you move one defender into the corridor and leave the line alone. It is cheaper, more reliable, and it addresses both branches of the fork rather than one.

Where the line does need to move is in response to pressure on the ball. When the passer is genuinely pressed and cannot lift his head, the line can hold higher, because the ball in behind requires vision. When the passer is free, the line drops together on a call. That is the same principle any competent side uses and it carries more weight here because one of the two primary threats is a run.

### The block's horizontal behaviour

Unlike an isolation attacker, this profile is not primarily supplied by a switch after long circulation, which means the cap on the block's lateral shift is a smaller concern.

What matters more is the distance between the near-side midfielder and the full-back, and between the full-back and the centre-back. The plan depends on three players occupying positions that are close enough to interlock, and a block that is stretched horizontally cannot interlock no matter how well each individual understands his job.

The practical instruction is that on the side he occupies, the defensive unit plays narrower than it would otherwise, and accepts that the touchline is not defended by anyone until the attacker actually goes there. That is a deliberate inversion of the usual arrangement, where the flank is guarded and the inside is covered on arrival, and it follows from the fact that the inside is where the value is.

### The compactness point

The most useful single measurement, if you were going to take one, is the vertical distance between your defensive line and your midfield line on his side. A gap there is where the entry pass into his feet lives, and closing it is worth more than any adjustment to absolute depth.

That is a familiar principle applied in a specific place. Against this profile the compactness that matters is local rather than global: your near-side midfielder and your back line need to be tight to each other on that flank, and it matters much less what the far side is doing.

## Selecting the defender

The reflexes here are strong and mostly wrong, and they are worth dismantling individually.

### The left-footed defender myth

The most persistent belief about defending this profile is that a left-footed full-back solves it, on the theory that the natural body shape blocks the cut inside and the stronger foot is on the right side for the intervention.

The effect is real and small. Footedness affects a defender's comfort in tackling, clearing, and blocking on one side, and those actions occur after the important decisions have already been made. The attacker's route is determined by the defender's hips, his distance, and the moment he commits, none of which is a function of which foot he prefers.

More importantly, the belief is actively harmful when it becomes the plan. A coach who selects a left-footed full-back and considers the problem addressed has not built the three-player geometry, which means his left-footed full-back is standing on the same fork every other defender stands on, with a slightly better clearing foot.

The verdict: it is a marginal preference and it is not a solution, and any side that treats it as one has substituted a selection decision for a structural one.

### What the full-back actually needs

Distance control, first and by a distance. The plan asks him to hold a gap and delay a commitment, which is a technical and dispositional quality rather than an athletic one. A defender who closes the gap out of anxiety will be beaten past the shoulder; one who opens it out of fear will concede the reception and the turn.

Patience, second. The instruction to remain neutral until the attacker commits runs against everything a full-back has been taught, and it is only sustainable in a defender who can tolerate looking passive for the two seconds before the cover arrives.

Recovery pace, third. Being beaten is survivable if the defender can re-engage from behind and force a worse final action, and against a profile whose value is concentrated in the quality of the shooting angle, a defender pressing from behind at the moment of the strike is worth more than the same defender arriving a beat later.

Straight-line speed, fourth and least. This is not primarily a footrace, and selecting for it over the first three is how sides end up with a fast full-back standing square.

### What the covering centre-back needs

Willingness to leave the line, which is a disposition and a permission rather than a skill. Beyond that, the ability to defend facing his own goal, since the corridor position frequently means dealing with a runner arriving from behind him rather than in front, and comfort in one against one situations in a narrow space, because the cut inside brings the attacker directly to him.

Aerial ability matters less in this role than usual, which is worth knowing when the two centre-backs are being assigned. Give the corridor to the more mobile of the pair and the aerial responsibilities to the other, rather than assigning by side out of habit.

### What the near-side midfielder needs

Positional discipline and continuous attention. The shadow job is low-intensity, high-concentration work, which means it fades under fatigue faster than running-based roles do, and which means the player who does it best is often not the most physically impressive midfielder in the squad.

The second requirement is the willingness not to press. A midfielder whose instinct is to close down the ball will abandon the shadow constantly, and each abandonment opens the entry lane at exactly the moment the opponent wants it open.

### Which full-back profile actually copes?

The one who can hold a gap without closing it and delay a commitment without freezing, then re-engage from behind if beaten. Distance control and patience matter more than pace, and far more than footedness. A left-footed defender who commits early is a worse fit than a right-footed one who does not.
## The supply routes, and which one you are facing

The fork describes what happens after he receives. What determines how often he receives in a dangerous position is the supply, and three routes recur. A plan weighted toward the wrong one wastes its most demanding instruction.

### The half-space entry

The most common and the most dangerous. The ball is played from a central or inside position into his feet as he sits between the opposition full-back and centre-back, arriving over a short distance and quickly, which gives your full-back very little closing time.

This is the route the near-side midfielder's shadow exists to attack, and against a side that uses it heavily the midfielder is the most important player in your plan. The specific adjustment is that he sits narrower and deeper than he would otherwise, accepting that the opponent's own wide player has more freedom, because the entry into the feet is worth more than the circulation in front of him.

The full-back's adjustment against this route is to be tighter rather than deeper, because the reception is closer to him and the ball behind is less immediately available from that passing angle.

### The wide entry with time

Less common against this profile than against a pure isolation attacker, but it happens when a side circulates and switches. The ball arrives to him high and wide, with time, and the sequence resembles the isolation case: he receives facing the defender with room to work.

Against this route your covering centre-back is the key player rather than the midfielder, because there is time for the entry to be made and the question becomes what he finds when he cuts. The midfielder's shadow matters less, since a wide entry with time cannot be shaded effectively from an inside position.

### The ball played early into the channel

The third route skips the reception entirely: a straight or diagonal ball played into the space behind the full-back's inside shoulder, often before the attacker's run is obvious, and often from a deeper position than the other two.

This is the route that punishes a high line and a late-dropping centre-back, and it is the reason the corridor position should be pre-emptive rather than triggered. It is also the route most sensitive to pressure on the passer, since it requires vision and a clean striking position, which is why the line-height rule is tied to the passer's freedom rather than to the phase.

### Counting the supply

Two matches is enough. For every reception in a dangerous position, record which of the three routes produced it. A clear majority tells you where to put your weight. A three-way split tells you that you need all three components properly executed, which is a harder brief and useful to know in advance rather than to discover at half time.

### Which route should you defend first?

The one that produced most of the dangerous receptions in the video, not the one that produced the most memorable moment. Half-space entries point to the midfielder's shadow, wide entries point to the covering centre-back, and early balls into the channel point to line height and a pre-emptive drop. Weight the plan accordingly rather than executing all three at equal intensity.

## He is not only an individual threat

A blueprint that treats an elite inverted attacker as a solo problem will hold for a half and then be dismantled by a combination, because the players around him exist and are coached.

### The give and go into the channel

The most common combination is the short pass inside followed by an immediate run into the channel behind the full-back, receiving the return. This attacks the plan at its seam: your midfielder has shaded the entry and is now facing the ball, your centre-back is in the corridor and now has a runner arriving behind him, and your full-back has held his position against a player who no longer has the ball.

The answer is that the corridor position handles it, provided the centre-back's reference is the space rather than the ball. A defender occupying the channel is occupying the destination of the run regardless of whether the run comes with the ball or without it, which is another way of saying that this plan defends the space rather than the sequence.

The failure mode is a centre-back who steps toward the ball when the pass goes inside. That is the moment the run becomes free, and it is worth naming explicitly because stepping toward a ball played into your zone is a trained reflex.

### The overlapping full-back

Covered in the concessions and worth restating as a threat rather than as a price. When the inside is closed, the opponent's own full-back runs outside, and the delivery comes from a player arriving at speed rather than from an attacker who has been ushered wide.

The handover is triggered by the overlap committing, not by the ball. The near-side midfielder leaves the shadow and recovers to the overlapping player; the full-back stays with the attacker; the centre-back holds the corridor. A midfielder who holds the shadow while an unmarked full-back delivers from the corner has followed the instruction into a poor outcome, which is why the trigger has to be defined rather than assumed.

### The striker occupying your covering defender

The most direct attack on this plan is to occupy the covering centre-back so that he cannot take the corridor.

The answer is that the corridor role must be transferable. If the near centre-back is pinned, the far centre-back slides across to take the corridor and the holding midfielder drops into the vacated central position. That is a rehearsed rotation and sides that concede repeatedly in the inside lane are usually sides where the corridor is assigned to one player who is sometimes unavailable.

For squads that cannot execute the rotation, keep the corridor with the near centre-back and share the striker between the far centre-back and the holding midfielder. That leaves a marginal aerial advantage to the striker and protects the more valuable space, which is the correct priority.

## Rotations and the moving starting position

A plan tied to a location rather than to a player will be walked around by any side that rotates, and rotations against this plan are cheap for the attacking team.

The drift into the half-space is the most common and it is partly handled already, since the plan expects half-space receptions. What changes is the full-back's involvement: as the attacker moves further inside, the full-back's relevance falls and the holding midfielder's rises. The handover point should be defined by a zone rather than by a distance, and the full-back does not follow him into central areas under any circumstances, because following creates a hole on the flank that the overlap occupies immediately.

The switch of flanks is less common for this profile than for some and it does happen, usually in phases rather than permanently. The practical solution is to give both wide midfielders the shadow instruction from the start, triggered by the attacker's presence rather than by their own identity, so that a swap requires no communication.

The deep drop, where he comes toward the ball to receive facing his own goal, is the least dangerous rotation and should be treated as a small success. A wide attacker receiving with his back to goal thirty-five yards out is not doing the thing that makes him dangerous. Do not follow him. Hold the shape and prepare for the immediate return pass and the run in behind, which is the actual purpose of the drop.
## The plan, phase by phase

### Phase one: their build-up

Nothing in this plan requires a high press, and the decision about whether to press should be made on your side's general capability rather than on this opponent.

What does matter in this phase is that the three players are already in their relationships before anything happens. The near-side midfielder is already shading rather than pressing. The covering centre-back knows which of the two he is. The full-back has already noted where the attacker has positioned himself and whether he is holding width or drifting into the half-space.

If you do press, one instruction is specific to this profile: do not press in a way that leaves your defensive line high and unsupported while the ball can still be played forward. The run in behind the inside shoulder is available the moment your line is high and your presser has been bypassed, and it is available before the attacker has been involved in the possession at all.

### Phase two: the entry

This is the phase the plan is really about, and it is short.

The ball is being worked toward his side. The near-side midfielder shades the lane into his feet, adjusting continuously as the ball moves. The covering centre-back begins dropping and narrowing rather than waiting for a trigger, because the corridor is worth occupying pre-emptively and costs little when unoccupied. The full-back holds his distance.

The single most common failure here is the midfielder stepping to press the passer. It feels productive, it usually just moves the ball one player sideways, and it opens the lane. The instruction is that he engages the passer only when the passer is completely free and has time to play around the shadow anyway, which is a defined switch rather than a judgment call.

### Phase three: the reception

He has the ball, wide or in the half-space, facing the full-back.

The full-back does not commit. He holds the gap, shows nothing, and waits. The covering centre-back is already in the corridor. The midfielder now shifts from shading the entry to covering the return pass and the short combination, because a blocked inside route frequently produces a lay-off and a third-man run rather than an individual action.

The attacker's live options at this moment are: cut inside into an occupied corridor, run in behind into the same occupied corridor, go outside toward a deep crossing position, or recycle. Three of the four are outcomes you are content with.

### Phase four: he commits

Now the full-back reacts, and only now.

If the attacker goes inside, the full-back stays goal-side and presses from behind rather than reaching across, and the centre-back meets him. The objective is not to win the ball; it is to make the shooting angle worse and the strike later. A shot taken a half-second later, with a defender pressing from behind and a body in the lane, is a substantially different shot.

If the attacker goes outside, the full-back retreats at an angle that keeps the inside closed and ushers him toward the corner. The centre-back holds the corridor rather than following, because following into the corner is how the cutback zone empties.

If the attacker recycles, the shape resets and the plan has produced a possession that went nowhere, which is the most common successful outcome and the one that generates no highlight of any kind.

### Phase five: the delivery

Once a delivery is coming, the priorities change and the change should be called.

The cutback zone is owned by the covering centre-back, who has been in the corridor and is well positioned for it. The near post is owned by the goalkeeper and the front defender. The far post is owned by the far centre-back and the far full-back, and this is not a minor assignment against a side whose wide players attack the far post, which is the subject of the next section.

The near-side midfielder recovers toward the top of the box rather than following the ball, because the second ball and the cutback to the edge are live and because the space he vacates is exactly where a recycled attack re-enters.

### Phase six: the regain

The first pass after winning the ball goes forward or wide to the opposite flank, never square into the zone the attacker has just vacated. That zone is where his side's counter-press is densest and where losing the ball produces an immediate shooting position.

Your full-back needs a defined recovery position rather than a discretionary one, because he has just executed a demanding sequence and his instinct is to recover his breath at exactly the moment a counter-press might return the ball to the same flank.

## The far post: a separate threat with a separate owner

The most under-planned part of facing this profile has nothing to do with the fork.

When the ball is on the opposite flank, an inverted right-sided attacker frequently becomes a far-post arriving threat: drifting inside the far centre-back's shoulder, attacking the space at the back post, and finishing from close range. This is structurally a striker's action, it uses different cues, and it is defended by different people.

### Why it gets missed

Because the whole week of preparation has been about the fork, and the fork concerns the near-side full-back, the covering centre-back, and the near-side midfielder. None of those three is involved in a far-post arrival, and a defensive unit whose attention has been focused on one flank for four days will be looking the wrong way.

There is also a visual problem. When play is on the opposite flank, the far-side defenders are watching the ball, and the arrival happens in their blind side. That is not a concentration failure; it is a geometry that has to be actively countered.

### The answer

Assign it explicitly. The far-side full-back tucks inside when the ball is on the opposite flank, and his reference is the attacker rather than the ball. The far centre-back's job is to feel for the arrival at his shoulder rather than to watch the delivery.

The mechanical instruction that works is a scanning rhythm rather than a positional rule: the far-side defenders check over their shoulder at fixed moments, when the ball reaches the wide player, when the delivery is about to be struck, rather than continuously. Continuous scanning is impossible; rhythmic scanning is trainable.

The second instruction is that the far full-back does not get drawn to the ball side under any circumstances while an attacker of this type is in the box. His job during opposite-flank possession is not to help defend the ball; it is to be a body at the far post.

### Why is the far post his own threat?

Because it uses none of the mechanisms the rest of the plan addresses. It happens on the opposite flank, in the blind side of defenders watching the ball, and it is defended by the far full-back and far centre-back rather than by the three players who close the fork.
## Transition

The fork assumes a settled defence. In transition none of the three players is in position, the corridor is unoccupied, and the run in behind does not need to be manufactured because the space already exists.

### The rest-defence requirement

The specific requirement is that the flank he occupies always has a covering body during your own attacks. If your left-back pushes high, someone covers behind him, and the identity of that someone depends on your structure: a holding midfielder shifting across, a centre-back sliding wide, or a wide player staying back.

There is a second requirement particular to this profile. Because the run in behind is a primary threat rather than a secondary one, your defensive line during your own possession matters as much as your covering numbers. A back line pushed high to compress the pitch while attacking is a back line that concedes a fifty-yard run the moment the ball turns over, and against a profile that starts running before the turnover is complete, that run is frequently uncontested.

The workable compromise is a line that compresses less aggressively on the side he occupies, which produces a slightly staggered back line during your own attacks. That costs you some compactness in your attacking phase and it removes the highest-value transition outcome.

### The first pass

One player delays the first pass after a turnover. Not a full counter-press; a single immediate engagement whose only purpose is to prevent a clean forward ball in the first two seconds.

Against this profile the two seconds are worth more than usual, because the run starts early. A defender who is turning and running at the moment the pass is played has a chance. A defender who is still watching the ball when it is played does not.

The player who does the delaying is usually the one who just lost possession, which is a problem, because that player is typically reacting to his own error rather than to the situation. This is worth naming explicitly in the meeting: whoever loses it delays it, immediately, regardless of how the loss happened.

### The goalkeeper's role in transition

When the line is high and a ball is played in behind, the goalkeeper's decision has to be made during the pass rather than after it. Against a profile with this level of acceleration, a keeper who starts deciding late will arrive at the ball at the same moment the attacker does, inside his own box, which is the worst available outcome.

Decide early, commit fully or not at all, and if not, retreat and set for the one against one rather than hovering.

## Set pieces

### Their attacking corners and free kicks

He is a genuine box threat at dead balls rather than a marginal one, and the arrival pattern is typically a late one into the space rather than an aerial contest at the front post. Assign accordingly: a marker who tracks the movement rather than a zonal body who holds a spot, and preferably one who is comfortable defending a runner arriving from behind.

The second consideration is the short corner and the resulting delivery from a wide position, which reproduces the crossing geometry with your defence already in the box. A designated player should prevent the short option rather than leaving it to whoever notices.

### Your attacking corners and free kicks

Leave two players back rather than one, and position one of them toward the flank he occupies. This costs your attacking threat and it removes the two against one against your goalkeeper that a cleared corner otherwise produces.

The player positioned centrally has the same job as the transition delayer: pressure whoever plays the first pass forward. An unpressured outlet after a cleared corner is a fifty-yard run against a defence thirty yards out of position.

### Wide free kicks in your own half

The situation worth rehearsing is the quickly taken restart, which reproduces the reception without any of the shape you have built. One designated player stands over the ball on every free kick in your defensive half until the shape is set, and the near-side full-back does not join a wall or drift toward the ball until then.

## The goalkeeper's job in open play

Four specific responsibilities, each with a defined starting reference.

### The near post against the cut inside

The most important adjustment and the one most often got wrong. When an inverted right-sided attacker cuts onto his left foot, the shot comes from a position considerably more central than his starting position suggests, and the angle he shoots from is not the angle a goalkeeper instinctively sets for when the attacker is wide.

The reference is the near post as it stands at the moment of the strike, not at the moment of the reception. In practice that means the keeper sets narrower and earlier than his instinct suggests, and continues adjusting as the attacker moves inward rather than settling once and then reacting.

The specific error is setting for the wide position, then finding that the cut inside has moved the shooting angle before the feet have moved with it. The strike from that angle is frequently placed toward the far corner precisely because the goalkeeper's weight is still committed to the near side.

### The far post on the opposite flank

When play is on the other side, the goalkeeper's positioning has to account for the far-post arrival, which means starting slightly deeper and more central than he would against a side with no far-post threat, and communicating the arrival to his far-side defenders because he can see it and they cannot.

That communication is the highest-value goalkeeping action in the whole plan, and it is free.

### The one against one in behind

Delay rather than rush. Close ground while the ball is not under the attacker's foot, stop when it is, stay tall, and postpone the commitment, because delay brings recovering defenders into the frame. Rushing produces a finish around him or a foul.

### The cutback

Hold the position that covers both posts against a first-time strike, communicate the receiving runner to the covering centre-back, and resist advancing toward the receiving area. Advancing on a cutback is close to indefensible because the shot can be struck early while the keeper is between his line and the ball.

### How does a goalkeeper narrow the angle here?

By referencing the shooting position rather than the receiving position. Against a cut inside, set narrower and earlier than instinct suggests and keep adjusting inward as the attacker moves, because the angle at the moment of the strike is far more central than the angle at the moment of the reception.
## When the plan fails: the fallback ladder

Each component has a characteristic way of failing and each failure has an answer. Abandoning the plan wholesale because one part broke is the most common in-match error against this profile, and it usually happens at about the fiftieth minute.

### Fallback one: he receives cleanly and turns repeatedly

Signal: the entry pass keeps arriving on time, in his preferred body position, and he is facing the full-back with the ball settled.

This is the midfielder's shadow failing, and there are two causes. If the midfielder is too far from the ball, his shadow is too narrow to matter and the fix is to move him closer, which feels risky and is not. If he is stepping to press the passer, the fix is the instruction rather than the position, and it needs restating explicitly because the instinct returns under pressure.

If the shadow is correct and the entries are still clean, the passer is too free, and the answer is to engage the passer and accept that the lane is open for the two seconds the pass takes to arrive and be controlled. That is the defined switch described earlier, and using it is not an abandonment of the plan.

### Fallback two: he keeps arriving in the inside lane

Signal: sequences ending with shots from central positions rather than deliveries from wide.

This is the covering centre-back holding the line instead of occupying the corridor, and it is the most common single failure in this blueprint. The instruction has to be repeated with the permission attached: leave the line, the offside consequences are not your problem, the corridor is the priority.

If the centre-back is executing and the sequences still arrive inside, the full-back is committing outside too early. Move him back toward neutral and accept a slightly later engagement.

### Fallback three: the run in behind keeps coming

Signal: repeated balls into the channel behind the full-back's inside shoulder, arriving before the covering centre-back is set.

Two causes. If the line is too high, drop it, but drop it as a unit on a call rather than allowing individual defenders to drift, because a line that drops raggedly creates the exact gap the run targets. If the line is reasonable and the timing is the problem, the covering centre-back is dropping too late, and the fix is to make his drop pre-emptive rather than triggered: he goes to the corridor when the ball reaches the wide areas, not when the pass is played.

### Fallback four: the far post is being attacked successfully

Signal: arrivals at the back post from opposite-flank possession, with the far-side defenders reacting late.

This is a scanning failure and it is fixed with a rhythm rather than an instruction to concentrate. Give the far-side defenders two fixed moments to check their shoulder: when the ball reaches the opposite wide player, and when the delivery is about to be struck. If it persists, the far full-back is being drawn toward the ball side, and the fix is a blunt rule: he does not cross a defined line while the attacker is in the box.

### Fallback five: the plan works and you are under siege

Signal: the sequences are ending in wide deliveries as designed, and there are thirty of them, and the volume is starting to convert.

This is a box-exit problem rather than a wide problem. Deliveries pile up when the attacking side retains possession after each one, which means your clearances are being recycled. The fix is at the edge of the box: a player positioned to collect, a defined clearing direction, and a front player holding a position that gives the clearance somewhere to go. Adding another defender to the box makes this worse rather than better.

### Fallback six: nothing is holding

The controlled downgrade is a back five, with the wide centre-back occupying the corridor permanently and the wing-back handling the width. This concedes midfield and attacking structure, it removes the need for the three-player coordination that is failing, and it should be described to the players as a change of objective rather than a change of shape.

## What you concede on purpose

### The wide crossing position

The largest and most constant payment. A plan built to close the inside lane and the channel behind is a plan that leaves the outside route relatively open, which means deliveries from wide positions, in volume, all match.

The number to be comfortable with is high. If your box is organized and your second-ball structure holds, fifteen or twenty wide deliveries is a defensible match against this profile. The instinct to intervene, to send another body toward the flank, is the instinct that reopens the inside lane.

### The overlap outside

Closing the inside means the opponent's own full-back will overlap into the space, and he will deliver from a good position with momentum. This is a genuine escalation of the wide concession rather than a duplicate of it, and it should be handled by the near-side midfielder recovering to the overlapping player once the overlap has committed, rather than by the full-back trying to cover both.

### Possession in front of the block

The narrowness required on his side means the opponent will circulate comfortably in front of you, which produces a possession share that looks alarming and matters less than it appears. A side that has the ball for long periods in areas that do not produce shots is not dominating; it is being managed.

### A body in your own attack

The near-side midfielder is committed to a defensive relationship rather than to your build-up, which reduces your attacking presence on that side. If you are chasing the match, this becomes a genuine argument for changing the plan, and that decision should be pre-agreed rather than improvised.

### What you do not concede

Three things, and they are the reason the concessions above are worth making: the inside lane and the shooting angle at the end of it, the channel behind the full-back's inside shoulder, and an unowned far post. Every goal this profile generates comes from one of those three, and a plan that holds all three while giving away almost everything else is a plan that survives the match.
## The version for a side that cannot run three-player coordination

The honest problem with this blueprint is that it requires three players to hold interlocking positions simultaneously, and most part-time squads cannot do that reliably for ninety minutes. A diluted version of a three-player plan is not a two-player plan; it is a plan with a hole in it.

So for a side that cannot execute it, the answer is a different plan rather than a reduced one, and the different plan is built on a single decision that a lone defender can act on.

### Pick a route and defend it properly

The amateur version resolves the fork deliberately rather than avoiding it. Choose to close one route completely and accept the other, and choose based on your own personnel rather than on the attacker.

If your centre-backs are mobile and comfortable defending space, close the inside and accept the run in behind, because the run is then defended by a covering defender who is good at it. If your centre-backs are slow and better in a settled box, close the run in behind, accept the cut inside, and defend the resulting shot with numbers in the lane.

That is a worse plan than the three-player geometry and it is a real plan, which is more than a half-executed version of the better one would be. It also has the advantage of being a single instruction to a single player, which is the kind of instruction that survives the seventieth minute.

The general question of which way to show a wide attacker, and the arguments for each choice as a standing philosophy rather than as a response to one opponent, belongs to [the article that owns the inside-or-outside decision](/2022/01/15/show-inside-or-outside-defending/) and is treated there as the standalone problem it is.

### Keep the far post

Whatever else you drop, keep the far-post assignment, because it costs nothing. Your far full-back tucks in when the ball is on the opposite flank and does not get drawn toward the ball. That is a positional rule with no fitness cost, no coordination requirement, and no technical demand.

### Keep the no-tackle instruction

Also free, also effective. The full-back is judged on whether the sequence ends in a wide delivery rather than on whether he wins the ball, and changing the success criterion is what changes the behaviour.

### Keep the midfielder narrow

Even without a full shadowing instruction, a near-side midfielder who simply stands narrower than usual makes the entry pass harder. He does not need to shade a specific lane; he needs to be in the way more often than he would be by default.

## Five arguments about defending this profile, settled

### "He always does the same thing"

True and useless without cover, which is the whole subject of this article. The prediction is accurate; a lone full-back cannot act on it, because closing the predicted route opens a route that is equally dangerous and equally live.

There is a further point that gets missed. The alternative routes are maintained deliberately, as functional components of the primary threat rather than as fallbacks. The run in behind is what forces a defender to respect depth, and respecting depth is what makes the inside lane accessible. An attacker who genuinely stopped making the run would find the cut inside much harder, which is exactly why it keeps happening.

The verdict: predictability is an asset in proportion to the cover available. With three coordinated players it is worth a great deal. With one, it is worth nothing, and briefing the full-back on it without providing the cover makes his defending worse rather than better.

### "A left-footed full-back stops him"

The observation is that a left-footed defender's natural body shape and stronger foot favour blocking the cut inside.

The effect is real and small, because footedness governs actions that occur after the decisive decisions have been made. It becomes actively harmful when it substitutes for structure, and a coach who selects a left-footed defender and considers the problem solved has left the same fork in place with a marginally better clearing foot on it.

The verdict: a mild preference, never a plan.

### "He is only dangerous in one system"

The observation is that his output depends on the structure around him, which is true of every specialized attacker and is not a weakness in itself.

Where it becomes planning-relevant is narrower. The threat requires entries into the right half-space and channel, and a side that cannot generate those entries generates fewer dangerous sequences. That is a statement about how to defend, which is to deny the entries, and it is the reason the near-side midfielder's job matters so much.

The verdict: the dependence is real and the planning implication is to attack the supply rather than the player, which is what the shadow does.

### "Doubling him is straightforward"

Two problems. A reactive double, arriving during the carry, produces a second defender who can be eliminated with one pass while his own zone stands empty, and against a profile whose combination play is strong that is a poor exchange. A permanent double concedes a body elsewhere, and against a side that circulates well the location of the freed attacker is chosen rather than random.

The version that works is the structural one this blueprint uses: the covering centre-back in the corridor is functionally a second defender who cost nothing, because he was going to be on the pitch anyway and his position is a shape decision rather than a commitment.

The verdict: double structurally, in advance, never reactively.

### "His threat drops sharply against a deep block"

Partly true, and less than claimed. A deep block reduces the space for the run in behind, which is one of the two threats. It shortens the distance from his receiving position to the shooting angle, which strengthens the other, and it concentrates defenders in the cutback zone, which raises the value of the delivery.

There is also a compaction effect worth naming: a deep block that is compact vertically is frequently stretched horizontally, and the right half-space is exactly where the entry pass into his feet lives.

The verdict: depth helps against one threat and hurts against the other, which is why the recommendation here is a mid-block with the variability located in the covering centre-back rather than in the whole line. Where the limitation questions genuinely bear on the plan, they are examined properly in [the weakness anatomy for this subject](/2021/06/01/mohamed-salah-weaknesses-explained/) rather than asserted here.
## Managing the plan during the match

### The first twenty minutes: check the supply

The question is where the entries are coming from, because the plan's weight shifts depending on the answer.

If entries are arriving into his feet from the half-space, the midfielder's shadow is carrying the plan and should be reinforced. If they are arriving as balls in behind, the covering centre-back's pre-emptive drop and the line height are carrying it. If they are arriving as switches from the far flank, you have a different problem closer to the isolation case, and the near-side positioning needs to be set earlier because you have more warning.

Establish this before adjusting anything. A plan that is retuned in the twelfth minute on the basis of one dangerous moment is a plan that will be retuned four more times before half time.

### The concession check

At around the half hour, verify that what you are conceding is what you agreed to concede.

Are the deliveries coming from wide, or have they crept into the half-space? Has the volume of wide crosses become a second-ball problem? Is the far post still owned, or has your far full-back begun drifting toward the ball as the match has opened up? Plans degrade at their edges, and the far post is usually the first edge to go because it depends on a player doing something while the action is elsewhere.

### Half time

The diagnostic question is where the damage is coming from, and the three answers point to three different people.

Damage from the inside lane means the covering centre-back is not in the corridor, or the full-back is committing outside too early. Damage from the channel behind means the line height or the timing of the centre-back's drop. Damage from the far post means the far-side pair, and it is not a fork problem at all.

Sides frequently make the wrong substitution here, replacing the near-side full-back because he is the visible participant when the actual failure is thirty yards away. Locating the failure before changing the personnel is worth the two minutes it takes.

### Protecting a lead

The instinct is to drop deep, and against this profile that instinct is partially counterproductive for the reasons set out above: it shortens his route to the shooting angle and concentrates your defenders in the cutback zone.

The better late-match adjustment is to add a body to the structure rather than to drop the whole thing: move to a back five with the wide centre-back permanently in the corridor, keeping the block shape and the three relationships intact. Late in a match, add to a functioning plan rather than replacing it, because a tired squad can absorb one more instruction and cannot absorb a new system.

### Chasing a match

If you release the near-side midfielder forward and push the full-back high, expect the plan to stop working immediately and expect the transition version of the threat to become primary. That is a legitimate choice and it should be made deliberately, with the scoreline and the minute agreed in advance rather than negotiated in the moment.

## The patterns in how sides have coped

Rather than reason from individual fixtures, which is the least reliable form of evidence about wide attackers, it is more useful to name the structural features that recur when sides have contained this profile.

The first is a covering centre-back who leaves the defensive line to occupy the inside corridor. This is the most consistent marker and it is visible on any broadcast if you watch the defender rather than the ball. Sides that concede from the inside lane are almost always sides whose centre-backs prioritize the line; sides that cope are almost always the reverse.

The second is a near-side midfielder positioned narrow and low, shading the entry rather than pressing the ball. On camera this player looks uninvolved for long stretches, which is why containment is usually credited to the full-back who is visibly duelling.

The third is a high tolerance for wide deliveries. Sides that cope concede a lot of crosses and defend their box well. Sides that cope badly frequently defend the crosses better and concede the sequences that actually matter, which is a counterintuitive pattern until you notice that preventing crosses requires exactly the outward movement that opens the inside lane.

The fourth is an owned far post. Sides that concede to this profile from opposite-flank possession have almost always left the far-side full-back free to drift toward the ball, and sides that do not have a rule preventing it.

The fifth is conservative ball handling in their own half, which removes the transition version of the threat that no settled shape can solve after the fact.

Four of those five are coaching instructions rather than player qualities, which is the most encouraging thing in this article for a coach without elite personnel.

### What is the single most predictive marker?

The covering centre-back's position. If he holds the line while the attacker receives wide, the inside lane is open regardless of how well the full-back defends. If he drops into the corridor before the attacker commits, both branches of the fork run into a defender. One player's habit predicts more of the outcome than any other single variable.

## Rehearsing it

Session design belongs to the training articles in this series, and two rehearsal principles are specific enough to this plan to be worth naming here.

The first is that the three-player relationship cannot be trained as three separate jobs. Rehearsing the full-back's distance control in isolation, the centre-back's corridor position in isolation, and the midfielder's shadow in isolation produces three players who each understand their own task and cannot execute it together, because the whole point is the timing between them. It has to be rehearsed as a unit against a live attacker who is genuinely allowed to choose either route.

The second is that the attacker in the practice must be permitted to go outside and to run in behind, and must be rewarded for doing so. A practice where the attacker is instructed to cut inside trains the defenders against a threat that has been artificially simplified, and it produces exactly the false confidence that the useful-predictability test warns about. If your practice attacker only does one thing, your defenders are learning to defend a player who does not exist.

The duel-level technique underneath all of this, the body shape, the distance, the moment a defender is permitted to commit, is owned by [the article on defending inverted wingers one against one](/2021/08/15/defending-inverted-wingers-1v1/) and is not taught again here.
## The physical and attentional load

This plan has an unusual load profile because two of its three jobs are attentional rather than physical, and attentional jobs fail differently.

### The midfielder fails first

Shading an entry lane is low-intensity, high-concentration work: continuous small adjustments, sustained for ninety minutes, with no ball contact for long stretches and no obvious feedback when it is done well. That is precisely the kind of task that degrades under fatigue before running output does, and it is the first component of this plan to break.

The observable warning sign is that the midfielder starts pressing the ball. When concentration fades, players revert to the more instinctive action, and the more instinctive action here is to close down the passer. A midfielder who begins doing that in the sixty-fifth minute is not disobeying; he is tired in a way that does not show up as heavy legs.

The practical response is to substitute that role earlier than seems warranted. It is a counterintuitive substitution because the player will not look tired, and it protects the component that everything else depends on.

### The centre-back fails second

Leaving the defensive line is a deliberate decision taken repeatedly, and fatigue pushes players toward defaults. The default here is holding the line, so a tired covering centre-back drifts back into it without noticing.

Remind him at every stoppage. That is the entire intervention and it works, because the failure is a lapse rather than an inability.

### The full-back's load is different

His is the conventional load: repeated reactive accelerations and decelerations at moments chosen by somebody else, which is the most costly combination available. His decision quality degrades before his running output does, and the early signs are technical: committing early, standing square, reaching with the near foot.

Because the plan asks him to be patient rather than to be quick, the fatigue effect here is on patience specifically, and patience is the first thing a tired defender loses. A full-back who begins diving in at seventy minutes has not stopped believing the plan; he has stopped being able to execute the hardest part of it.

## Communicating the plan

Everything demanding in this blueprint feels wrong to the player being asked to do it, and an instruction a player does not believe in will not survive a difficult opening twenty minutes.

The full-back is being told not to show, not to tackle, and not to commit, which sounds like being told not to defend. The version that lands is a picture rather than a principle: if you pick a side, he goes the other way and there is nobody there yet, so hold and let the cover arrive.

The centre-back is being told to break the line he has spent his career holding. The version that lands includes explicit removal of blame: leave the line, and if the offside consequences go against us, that is my decision.

The midfielder is being told to stand narrow and not press, which feels like being told to do nothing. The version that lands is that he is the reason the ball never reaches the attacker in the position he wants it, and that the job is visible only in what does not happen. Name the role inside the squad if it helps, because a named role is defended by the player who holds it.

The far-side full-back is being told to ignore the ball. That is the hardest instruction on the list and the cheapest to execute, and it needs the reason attached: the goal comes from behind you, not from in front of you.

Then tell them what success will look like. He will get the ball often. He will cross a lot. The full-back will be the player everyone talks about and the least important of the three. Nothing dangerous will happen, and it will not feel like control. A squad that has been told this in advance holds the plan; a squad that has not begins improvising, and improvisation is what the fork punishes.

## What an analyst should count

Three counts across two or three matches, and they produce a report that changes Friday rather than one that gets filed.

The first is supply route, as set out above: half-space entry, wide entry with time, or early ball into the channel. This decides where the weight of the plan goes.

The second is sequence termination for every dangerous reception: shot from the inside lane, delivery from wide, delivery from the byline, run in behind contested, or recycled. This tells you what the defending side in the video actually achieved, and it lets you evaluate their plan rather than their result.

The third is far-post arrivals from opposite-flank possession, counted separately, because they are a different threat with different owners and they will be invisible if you fold them into a general tally.

### What should the report not contain?

Conclusions drawn from one fixture, and any judgment of the final action based on outcome rather than on the option set. Both are unreliable for wide attackers specifically, since output depends heavily on what the opponent's structure offered, and a single match tells you about that opponent rather than about the player.

Keeping the counts, the resulting instructions, and what actually happened attached to each fixture is what converts a scouting method into a system rather than a weekly improvisation, and it is the reason a coach who does this for a season arrives at the next fixture against a similar profile with his own evidence rather than a general principle.
## Why this plan differs from the plan against an isolation attacker

Coaches who have prepared for one type of elite wide attacker often assume the preparation transfers, and here it does not, which is worth setting out because the two plans conflict at their most important points.

Against an isolation attacker the primary intervention is upstream of the reception: capping the block's lateral shift so the switch never finds a lone full-back, and pre-positioning a midfielder in the space the attacker will attack. The duel is a fallback, the objective inside it is direction rather than possession, and the concession is the far side of the pitch.

Against an inverted finisher the primary intervention is at the reception and immediately after it, because the reception itself is rarely the problem. He can receive with a defender close and still be dangerous, since his threat begins with a short movement into a lane rather than with acceleration into open grass. The concession is not the far side of the pitch; it is the outside route on his own flank.

Three specific conflicts follow.

The first concerns the second defender's position. Against an isolation attacker the pre-positioned second stands in the inside channel to remove the route to space, and he can be a midfielder. Against an inverted finisher the equivalent body has to be a centre-back in the diagonal corridor, because the corridor is closer to goal and because the threat that arrives there is a shot rather than a carry. A midfielder standing in that space is not enough.

The second concerns line height. Against an isolation attacker depth is a partial answer that reduces the value of every sequence. Against an inverted finisher depth helps against one threat and actively assists the other, which is why the recommendation here locates the variability in one defender rather than in the whole line.

The third concerns the full-back's stance. Against an isolation attacker the full-back can and should close ground during the flight of a switch, arriving at an angle that pre-selects the outside route. Against an inverted finisher the equivalent aggression resolves the fork prematurely and is precisely the error to avoid.

A coach who applies the isolation plan to this profile will produce a well-drilled defence that concedes shots from the inside lane, and will conclude that his players executed badly. They executed a plan built for a different input. The taxonomy that keeps these straight is in [the cluster guide on stopping elite wingers](/2021/04/01/stopping-elite-wingers-complete-guide/), and getting the classification right before the preparation begins is worth more than any refinement afterward.

## The training week

Two structural points about the week, without straying into the session design that the training articles own.

The first is that the three-player relationship should be introduced early in the week and rehearsed daily in short blocks rather than installed in one long session. The reason is that the behaviour being trained is a timing relationship, and timing relationships decay quickly and rebuild quickly. Three short exposures across a week produce far better retention than one long one, and they also permit correction between exposures.

The second is that the far-post assignment should be rehearsed separately, with different players, at a different time. Folding it into the main session guarantees that it receives the least attention, because the main session is about the fork and everyone's attention is on the flank where the fork lives. It is a five-minute piece of work with the far-side pair and the goalkeeper, and it addresses the threat most likely to go unowned.

The third point is about the practice attacker, and it is the one most often got wrong. He must be genuinely allowed to go outside, to run in behind, and to recycle, and he must be rewarded for the routes that your defenders are conceding. A practice in which the attacker is told to cut inside trains your defenders against a simplified threat and produces false confidence, which is the exact failure the useful-predictability test describes. If your practice attacker only does one thing, your defenders are learning to defend a player who does not exist.

## The two errors that recur

Across every level, two errors dominate the failures against this profile, and both are instructional rather than technical.

The first is the full-back who commits early. He has been briefed, he knows what is coming, and he leans. Everything after that is downhill: the run goes behind his inside shoulder, or the overlap goes outside him, and the covering players are still arriving because the sequence began before they were set. This error is caused by the briefing, which is why the useful-predictability test matters as a coaching principle rather than as an analytical one. Do not give a defender a prediction he cannot act on.

The second is the centre-back who holds the line. He is doing what he was trained to do for years, the instruction to leave it conflicts with a deeply grooved habit, and under pressure the habit wins. This error is fixed by permission and repetition rather than by explanation, and it is worth a reminder at every stoppage because the failure is a lapse rather than a misunderstanding.

Neither error is a lack of ability, and both get diagnosed as one. A side that concedes repeatedly from the inside lane will usually conclude that its centre-back is not good enough, when the centre-back was standing exactly where his training told him to stand.

### What is the cheapest improvement available?

The centre-back's corridor position, with explicit permission attached. It requires no additional fitness, no coordination with a third player, and no new technical skill, and it closes both branches of the fork at once. Sides that install nothing else from this article and install that will concede meaningfully fewer shots from the inside lane.

## A closing note on what predictability is worth

The lesson here outlives this fixture, and it is the reason this article is built around a test rather than around a player.

Scouting produces predictions, and predictions feel like progress. A coach who leaves a video session knowing what an opponent will do feels prepared, and the feeling is frequently unearned, because the prediction has not yet been converted into something a player can execute without opening a worse problem.

The conversion is the work. It requires asking, for every observation, which player will act on this, what will he give up by acting, and who closes what he gives up. Observations that survive those three questions are worth putting in front of a squad. Observations that do not survive them should stay in the analyst's notes, because handing them to a defender does not neutralize a threat; it just decides which way he gets beaten.
## Facing him alongside a second wide threat

This blueprint assumes the dangerous flank is one flank. Against a side that carries a genuine threat on both, the plan becomes considerably harder, because the near-side midfielder and the covering centre-back are exactly the resources the other flank would need.

The decision has to be made before kick-off. Protect one side properly and give the other structure rather than bodies, because a plan that tries to run the three-player geometry on both flanks stretches the block horizontally and concedes centrally, which is the worst available outcome.

Protect the side with the inverted finisher, in almost every case. The reason is the value of the outcomes rather than the quality of the players: an inverted threat that succeeds produces a shot from a central area, while most other wide profiles that succeed produce a delivery. Given a choice, spend the resource where the failure mode is worse.

The unprotected flank gets three cheap things. The wide midfielder recovers inside and behind rather than ball-side. The centre-back on that side knows he may be the only cover and is not expected to hold a line. And the goalkeeper is briefed on the deliveries to expect from that side, which costs nothing and changes his starting positions.

Define in advance what would move the protection across, and make it repeated dangerous entries rather than a single alarming moment. Swinging the support back and forth in response to individual events protects neither side, and it is what a side with two strong flanks is built to provoke.

## Game state and the plan

The plan's viability changes with the scoreline, and pre-deciding the changes prevents the worst version of the decision, which is making it emotionally in the seventieth minute.

Level or ahead, the plan runs as written. The concessions are affordable, the near-side midfielder stays in his defensive relationship, and the far-side full-back holds his far-post responsibility.

Behind by one with time remaining, the honest position is that you can keep two of the three components. The covering centre-back's corridor position stays, because it costs nothing offensively. The far-post assignment stays, for the same reason. The near-side midfielder is the component you release, and releasing him means the entry passes arrive on time and the fork reopens.

That is a real trade and it should be described as one to the players rather than implemented silently. The version that works is a stated switch: at a defined minute, the midfielder pushes up, the full-back stops delaying and starts showing outside, and the centre-back keeps the corridor. It is a worse plan, chosen deliberately, and a squad that knows the switch is coming executes it far better than one that discovers it.

Behind by two, the plan is effectively abandoned, and the useful residue is the far-post rule and the goalkeeper's angle references, both of which survive any shape.

## Rotation across a season

One squad-level consequence is worth naming because it is invisible in most planning.

The near-side midfielder role in this plan is attentionally demanding rather than physically demanding, and attentional load does not recover on the same timescale as running load. A player who has held a shading role for ninety minutes has done something more tiring than the distance figures suggest, and a coach who rotates by minutes played will systematically under-rotate him.

The same applies to the covering centre-back in the corridor, whose job requires repeated deliberate decisions against a trained default. That is more depleting than holding a line, and it is not measured by anything.

The practical response over a congested run of fixtures is to treat those two roles as rotation candidates in the same way that a full-back facing an elite attacker is, and to accept that the player who looks freshest is not always the player who is.

### What is the one thing to keep in every version?

The covering centre-back in the corridor. It survives every game state, every level of squad quality, and every rotation, it costs nothing offensively, and it closes both branches of the fork with a single position. If a coach installs one instruction from this article and nothing else, that is the one worth installing.
## The one-page brief

For a coach who wants the whole plan on a single sheet before a meeting, this is it.

Shape: mid-block, narrow on his flank, defensive line neither high nor deep, with vertical compactness between the near-side midfielder and the back line as the measurement that matters.

Full-back: neutral stance, hold the distance, delay the commitment until he declares, never tackle at the moment of the first touch, re-engage from behind if beaten. Judged on where the sequence ends rather than on duels won.

Covering centre-back: drop out of the line into the diagonal corridor between yourself and the full-back, pre-emptively, when the ball reaches the wide areas. Both of his threats arrive there. You have permission to leave the line and you are not responsible for the offside consequences.

Near-side midfielder: shade the entry lane into his feet, closer to the ball than feels right, angled across the lane, adjusting continuously. Do not press the passer unless he is completely free. Recover to the overlapping full-back once the overlap commits.

Far-side full-back: tuck in when the ball is on the opposite flank, reference the attacker rather than the ball, check your shoulder when the ball reaches the wide player and again before the delivery, and do not drift toward the ball side.

Goalkeeper: near-post reference set narrow and early against the cut inside, adjusting inward as he moves. Deeper and more central when play is on the opposite flank, and communicate the far-post arrival. Delay rather than rush in the one against one. Hold your position on the cutback.

Concede: wide crosses in volume, the overlap outside, comfortable opponent possession in front of the block, and one attacking body on that flank.

Refuse: the inside lane, the channel behind the inside shoulder, and an unowned far post.

Fallback order: check the midfielder's distance, then the centre-back's corridor, then the line height, then the far-side scanning, then the box exits. Change to a back five only if several fail together.

That fits on a sheet, and every line of it has a mechanism behind it in the sections above.
## The verdict

The most repeated line about this player contains a real insight wrapped around a false conclusion, and separating them is the whole of the preparation.

The insight is that his actions are predictable. That is accurate. The false conclusion is that predictability makes him stoppable, and it is false because prediction is only useful when the predicted action can be closed without opening a worse one. A lone full-back cannot pass that test. Standing inside opens the run and the overlap; standing outside hands over the shooting lane; standing square offers everything. The information the scouting report provides cannot be converted into a defensive action by the person holding it.

That is the useful-predictability test, and it generalizes: scouting a pattern is only actionable when the shape can afford the cover. Handing a defender a prediction without the cover does not neutralize the threat, it selects which way he gets beaten, and it frequently makes him worse by predisposing him to protect the wrong thing.

The resolution is three players and it does not degrade to two. The full-back holds a neutral stance and delays his commitment, which feels passive and is the aggressive choice, because it forces the attacker to declare first. The covering centre-back drops out of the line into the diagonal corridor, before either route has been chosen, and because both of the primary threats use that corridor, one position closes both. The near-side midfielder shades the entry pass so that whatever arrives is later, wider, and in a worse body position, which buys the other two the time they need.

The far post is a fourth job and a different one. It belongs to the far-side full-back and the far centre-back, it uses different cues, and it is the part most likely to be left unowned by a week of preparation focused entirely on one flank. Assign it explicitly, give the far-side defenders a scanning rhythm rather than an instruction to concentrate, and forbid the far full-back from drifting toward the ball.

The payment is the wide crossing position, in volume, all afternoon, plus the overlap outside and a comfortable possession share for the opponent in front of your block. None of those three produces the chances that beat you. The inside lane, the channel behind the inside shoulder, and an unowned far post do, and a plan that holds all three while conceding almost everything else is a plan that survives ninety minutes.

For a side that cannot run the three-player version, the honest answer is a different plan rather than a diluted one: pick a route, close it properly, and choose which route based on your own centre-backs rather than on the attacker. That is worse and it is coherent, and coherent beats sophisticated-with-a-hole every time.

Where a limitation genuinely bears on the plan, [the weakness anatomy for this subject](/2021/06/01/mohamed-salah-weaknesses-explained/) examines it under a proper conditional standard rather than repeating what circulates. The category framing that places this profile alongside the other three kinds of elite wide attacker is in [the cluster guide on stopping elite wingers](/2021/04/01/stopping-elite-wingers-complete-guide/).

One last thing to expect. A working version of this plan produces a match in which he receives the ball frequently, crosses a great deal, and generates very little, and in which your full-back is the player everyone discusses despite being the least important of the three. That is what success looks like here, and a coach who has told his players in advance what it will look like is a coach whose players will still be executing it in the eightieth minute.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Mohamed Salah?

With three players, not one. His two primary threats, the cut inside onto the left foot and the run into the channel behind the full-back's inside shoulder, form a fork that no single defender can close: protecting one opens the other. The resolution is a full-back who holds a neutral stance and delays his commitment, a covering centre-back who drops out of the line into the diagonal corridor before either route is chosen, and a near-side midfielder who shades the entry pass so the ball arrives later and in a worse body position. Both threats use the same corridor, so one centre-back position closes both. The payment is a high volume of wide crosses.

### Q: How do you stop Salah cutting inside onto his left foot?

Not by standing inside, which closes the cut and opens the run in behind and the overlap outside. The cut is closed by the covering centre-back occupying the diagonal channel between himself and the full-back, so that the first touch inward arrives at a defender rather than in space. The full-back's contribution is to hold his distance and avoid committing early, since a defender who has already leaned inside is a defender whose recovery into the channel behind him is longer than it looks. The midfielder's shadow makes the entry pass arrive later, which gives the centre-back time to be in position before the cut begins.

### Q: Which full-back profile copes with Salah?

Distance control first: a defender who can hold a gap he could still adjust from if the attacker accelerated, without closing it anxiously or opening it fearfully. Patience second, because the plan asks him to remain neutral until the attacker commits, which runs against everything he has been taught and looks passive for two seconds. Recovery pace third, since being beaten is survivable if he can press from behind and make the strike later and worse. Straight-line speed matters least. Footedness is a mild preference and never a plan, and a left-footed defender who commits early is a worse fit than a right-footed one who does not.

### Q: Should you show Salah down the line?

Only if you have decided to resolve the fork deliberately and can defend what you have opened. Showing outside closes the run in behind and hands over the inside lane and the shooting angle, which is the more valuable of the two routes, so it is a poor default. The recommended approach is not to show either way: the full-back stays neutral and delays, and the routes are closed by the covering centre-back and the midfielder rather than by his stance. For a side that cannot coordinate three players, picking a route is better than half-executing the geometry, and the choice should follow your centre-backs' qualities rather than the attacker's.

### Q: How do you defend Salah's run in behind the full-back?

With the same corridor position that defends the cut inside, which is the structural gift of this plan. The covering centre-back drops and narrows pre-emptively when the ball reaches the wide areas, rather than reacting once the pass is played, and arrives goal-side of the run rather than chasing it. Line height matters too and it should move as a unit on a call: higher when the passer is genuinely pressed and cannot lift his head, dropped together the moment he is free. A line that drops raggedly creates exactly the gap the run targets, so the coordination matters more than the absolute depth.

### Q: Does doubling up on Salah work?

Reactively, no. A second defender arriving during the carry can be eliminated with one pass while his own zone stands empty, and against a profile with strong combination play that exchange is unfavourable. Permanently, also no, because committing two defenders to one flank frees an attacker elsewhere and a well-organized side chooses where that happens. The version that works is structural rather than reactive: the covering centre-back sitting in the corridor is functionally a second defender who cost nothing, since his position is a shape decision made before the ball arrives rather than a commitment made after it.

### Q: How do you stop the pass into Salah between the lines?

By degrading it rather than intercepting it. The near-side midfielder positions himself so his body shadow covers the direct lane into the attacker's feet, closer to the ball than instinct suggests, angled across the lane rather than facing the passer, and adjusting continuously as the ball circulates. The aim is a pass that arrives later, wider, flatter, or behind the receiving hip, because each of those costs a touch and the touch is the window in which the covering centre-back reaches the corridor. The one exception is a completely free passer, against whom shading is theatre and engaging is correct.

### Q: What does the centre-back do when Salah comes inside?

He should already be there. The corridor position is taken pre-emptively, when the ball reaches the wide areas, not in response to the cut, because reacting to the cut means arriving after the attacker has the angle. Once the attacker commits inside, the centre-back meets him in the channel while the full-back presses from behind rather than reaching across. The objective is not winning the ball; it is making the strike later and the angle worse. The instruction only survives contact with a match if the centre-back has explicit permission to leave the defensive line and is not held responsible for the offside consequences.

### Q: How do goalkeepers narrow the angle against Salah?

By referencing the shooting position rather than the receiving position. When an inverted right-sided attacker cuts onto his left foot, the strike comes from a considerably more central angle than his starting position suggests, so the keeper sets narrower and earlier than instinct dictates and keeps adjusting inward as the attacker moves rather than setting once and reacting. The specific error is setting for the wide position and then finding the angle has changed before the feet have. Strikes toward the far corner from that position frequently succeed precisely because the goalkeeper's weight is still committed near.

### Q: How do you defend Salah at the far post?

As a separate threat with separate owners, because it uses none of the mechanisms that close the fork. When play develops on the opposite flank he becomes a late arriving threat at the back post, in the blind side of defenders who are watching the ball. Assign it to the far-side full-back, who tucks inside and takes the attacker rather than the ball as his reference, and the far centre-back, who feels for the arrival at his shoulder. Give both a scanning rhythm at fixed moments rather than an instruction to concentrate, and forbid the far full-back from drifting toward the ball side.

### Q: What line height works against Salah?

A mid-block, with the variability located in the covering centre-back rather than in the whole line. A high line supplies the run in behind. A deep line reduces that threat and shortens his route to the shooting angle while concentrating your defenders in the cutback zone, which raises the value of the delivery you were trying to prevent. Moving one defender into the corridor is cheaper and more reliable than moving four defenders up and down, and it addresses both branches of the fork at once. Where the line does need to move is in response to pressure on the ball, dropping together on a call whenever the passer is free.

### Q: How do you stop Salah in transition?

During your own possession, because after the turnover the corridor is unoccupied and the space already exists. Never leave his flank with a single defender while you attack, and compress your back line less aggressively on that side, which produces a slightly staggered line and costs some attacking compactness. Have one player delay the first pass immediately after any loss, and make it a rule that whoever loses possession does the delaying regardless of how the loss happened. Two seconds matters more than usual here because the run starts before the turnover is complete, so a defender who is already turning has a chance and one still watching the ball does not.

### Q: What do you accept conceding in order to contain Salah?

Four things. Wide crossing positions in high volume, all match, because closing the inside lane leaves the outside route relatively open. The overlap outside, which escalates that concession and is handled by the midfielder recovering to the overlapping player rather than by the full-back covering both. Comfortable opponent possession in front of your block, since the narrowness on his side invites it and circulation that does not produce shots is being managed rather than dominating. And a body subtracted from your own attack on that flank. What you refuse to concede is the inside lane, the channel behind the inside shoulder, and an unowned far post.

### Q: What is the fallback if the plan against Salah fails?

Locate the failure before changing anything. Clean receptions and turns mean the midfielder's shadow is too far from the ball or he is stepping to press. Damage from the inside lane means the covering centre-back is holding the line, or the full-back is committing outside too early. Repeated balls into the channel mean the line height or a late centre-back drop, which should be pre-emptive rather than triggered. Far-post arrivals mean a scanning failure among the far-side pair, which is not a fork problem at all. Siege volume with correct concessions is a box-exit problem. Only if several fail together should you move to a back five.

### Q: How should an amateur side handle an attacker like Salah?

Do not attempt a diluted version of the three-player geometry, because a plan with a hole in it is worse than a simpler plan executed properly. Resolve the fork deliberately instead: choose one route to close completely and accept the other, and choose based on your own centre-backs. Mobile centre-backs who defend space well means close the inside and accept the run. Slower centre-backs who are better in a settled box means close the run and defend the resulting shot with numbers. Then keep the three free elements: the far-post assignment, the no-tackle instruction for the full-back, and a narrower near-side midfielder.

### Q: Which sides have coped with Salah and what did they do?

The recurring structural markers matter more than any individual fixture. Sides that cope share five features: a covering centre-back who leaves the defensive line to occupy the inside corridor, which is the single most predictive marker; a near-side midfielder positioned narrow and low, shading the entry rather than pressing the ball; a high tolerance for wide deliveries combined with a well-organized box; an explicitly owned far post with a rule preventing the far full-back drifting ball-side; and conservative ball handling in their own half that removes the transition version of the threat. Four of the five are coaching instructions rather than player qualities.
