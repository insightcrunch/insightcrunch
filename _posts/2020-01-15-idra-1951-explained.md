The Industries Act 1951 is remembered through a system that its text no longer imposes on most factories. The statute is Act 65 of 1951, titled the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951, and its long title states a double purpose: to provide for the development and regulation of certain industries. That pairing matters. The Act was not drafted as a prohibition code alone. It joined entry control, capacity control and price control to councils, research, training and industrial planning. Its best known mechanism, the industrial licence, decided whether a new undertaking could be established, whether an established undertaking could expand in a substantial way, and whether a registered undertaking could move into a different article. For four decades, those questions were not treated as ordinary investment choices. They were legal permissions.

![The Industries Act 1951 and the licence raj, showing industrial licensing, the First Schedule and the Section 29B exemption route - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-105.webp)

The central surprise is the manner of the change. Parliament did not repeal sections 11, 11A and 13, and it did not rewrite the basic prohibition on establishing an industrial undertaking without a licence. The Statement on Industrial Policy of July 1991 announced that licensing would be abolished for most industries, and a notification under section 29B then exempted the generality of undertakings from the licensing provisions. The statutory switch remained on the wall. The executive, using a power placed in the Act itself, turned it off for most of the field. That is why a reader who opens the Act can meet an apparently strict licensing code while a manufacturer outside the surviving notified heads faces no industrial licence requirement under that code. The wider field, including the monopolies statute and the competition statute that followed it, is mapped in [the industrial and competition law guide for India](/2020/01/01/industrial-competition-law-india-guide/). This pillar stays with one statute: what it built, how it operated, and what remains legally alive inside it.

Three distinctions prevent most errors. A scheduled industry is not the same thing as an industry subject to compulsory licensing. The First Schedule defines the industries over which the Act can operate, and that schedule remains broad. Compulsory licensing concerns the smaller set of industries for which the section 29B exemption was withheld or later withdrawn in part. A public sector reservation is different again: it concerns the sphere the industrial policy reserved to public ownership or operation, rather than the licence an entrepreneur needs before manufacture. Registration, licensing, investigation and takeover are also separate instruments. Registration recorded an existing undertaking and its productive capacity. A licence authorised entry, expansion, relocation or a new article. Investigation examined production, quality, price or management. Takeover displaced management for a defined period. The Act becomes intelligible when those instruments are kept apart.

This article explains public legal material and the history of a regulatory system. It is not advice on a particular plant, product or transaction. A project can be outside industrial licensing and still be governed by environmental clearance, land use, factory safety, product standards, foreign investment conditions and the special statute for the sector concerned.

## Before the Act: the 1948 resolution and the planning inheritance

The Act did not begin in 1951. Its constitutional device, a declaration that Union control of an industry is expedient in the public interest, carried a recognisable antecedent in the Government of India Act, 1935, which had distributed legislative power through lists and allowed federal control of industries on a declaration of public interest. Its policy device was older still in spirit. The National Planning Committee of 1938, chaired by Jawaharlal Nehru, had treated industrial development as a subject for state direction before independence, and the Congress governments of the provinces had experimented with planning language in the years before the war. The Industries (Development and Regulation) Act was the point at which that inheritance became a licensing statute.

The immediate predecessor was the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1948, the first industrial policy statement of independent India. The 1948 resolution divided industry into spheres, reserved an exclusive field to the State, marked a field for progressive State participation, and announced that the State would exercise control and regulation over the remainder in the interest of planned development. It contemplated that new undertakings in important industries would be established under licence and that the State would progressively assume a direct role in industrial development. The Bill that became the Industries Act translated that announcement into sections: a declaration, a schedule, registration, licensing and councils. The licensing idea was therefore not an improvisation of the 1950s. It was the statutory form of a policy announced within a year of independence.

The timing explains the design's confidence. The Second World War had accustomed the colonial state to controls over production, prices and imports, and the wartime machinery of control orders supplied both precedent and personnel. Capital was scarce, the market for industrial finance was shallow, and the new republic's Constitution had just committed the State to preventing concentration of wealth in the Directive Principles. A Parliament that had lived through shortage did not experience licensing as an intrusion on a functioning market order. It experienced licensing as the ordinary instrument by which a poor country decided what would be built, where, and by whom. Judging the Act fairly requires that starting point, even where the judgment ends against the system's operation.

## The Industries Act 1951 as a legal machine

The Act received its place in the statute book as Act 65 of 1951 and is dated 31 October 1951. Section 1 left commencement to a date appointed by the Central Government. The appointed date was 8 May 1952, given by Notification No. S.R.O. 811 dated 8 May 1952. The gap between enactment and commencement is more than a dating point. The Act created councils, registration duties, licensing procedures and investigative machinery, and commencement allowed the administrative structure to be put in place before the obligations began to bite. The statute then extended to the whole of India, subject to the territorial history recorded in its commencement footnotes.

### What changed when the Act commenced in 1952?

Commencement turned a policy design into enforceable duties for owners of undertakings in scheduled industries. Existing undertakings had to register, new undertakings required a Central Government licence, and the Centre gained linked powers to investigate production, issue directions, control specified articles and authorise a takeover of management in defined circumstances.

The opening chapters distribute power in a deliberate order. Chapter I contains the short title, the declaration of expediency and the definitions. Chapter II creates the Central Advisory Council and Development Councils and authorises a cess for development work. Chapter III regulates scheduled industries through registration, licensing, investigation and directions. Chapter IIIA addresses direct management or control by the Central Government. Chapter IIIAA deals with undertakings owned by companies in liquidation, Chapter IIIAB with relief for taken-over undertakings, and Chapter IIIAC with liquidation or reconstruction of managed companies. Chapter IIIB contains section 18G, the power to control supply, distribution and price. Chapter IV gathers inspection, penalties, delegation, exemption and rule-making.

The definitions do quiet but decisive work. An industrial undertaking is an undertaking pertaining to a scheduled industry carried on in one or more factories by a person or authority, including Government. A factory, for the Act, turns on a manufacturing process and the number of workers: fifty or more workers where power is used, and one hundred or more where the process is carried on without power, measured by the statutory wording and the preceding twelve months. Scheduled industry means an industry specified in the First Schedule. Owner means the person or authority with ultimate control over the affairs of the undertaking, and a manager, managing director or managing agent entrusted with those affairs is deemed to be the owner for the Act. Those definitions identify both the regulated unit and the person answerable for it.

The definition of new article is unusually revealing. An article can be new because it falls under a different First Schedule item from the articles ordinarily made by the undertaking. It can also be new because it bears a trade mark, or is the subject of a patent, and the undertaking was not making that marked or patented article at the relevant date. Industrial policy therefore reached beyond a change in plant or chemistry. A product identity recognised by trade mark or patent law could itself trigger the licensing question. The statute treated diversification as a matter for permission even where the factory, workforce and basic process remained familiar.

Chapter II is the part most often skipped, and skipping it distorts the Act. Section 5 permits a Central Advisory Council of a chairman and not more than thirty other members, appointed from persons capable of representing owners, employees, consumers and other classes including primary producers. The Central Government must consult the Council about rules other than the first rules, and may consult it on other matters of administration. Section 6 permits a Development Council for a scheduled industry or a group of scheduled industries. Its membership joins owners, persons with technical knowledge, employees and consumers. A Development Council is a body corporate, holds and transfers property, and performs functions assigned from the Second Schedule. Section 7 requires annual reports and accounts, with reports laid before Parliament. Section 8 permits dissolution, with remaining assets vesting in the Central Government for the purposes of the Act after liabilities are met.

The Second Schedule shows the intended developmental work in concrete terms. A council could be assigned functions concerning production targets, coordination of production programmes, efficiency norms, fuller use of installed capacity, marketing and distribution arrangements, standardisation, controlled materials, inquiry into materials and equipment, production methods, management and labour use, training, scientific and industrial research, accounting and costing methods, statistics, decentralisation of production stages and other matters directed toward efficiency and service to the community. The cess in section 9 supplied a possible fund. The Central Government could levy a duty of excise as a cess on goods made in a specified scheduled industry, at a rate fixed by notified order, subject to a ceiling of two annas per cent of the value of the goods. Proceeds handed to a Development Council were directed toward research, design and quality improvement, technician and labour training, and prescribed administrative and functional expenses.

The architecture therefore joined two conceptions of the state. One conception licensed and inspected. The other advised, coordinated, researched and trained. Later debate often reduced the Act to the first conception because licensing had the sharpest effect on investment. The text itself gives the second conception institutions, a schedule of functions and a funding route. Whether those institutions achieved enough is a separate question from whether they existed.

## One Act, three tempers

The sections fall into three tempers, and keeping them distinct is the key to the Act's reputation and its survival. The first temper is permission: registration, licensing, capacity endorsement and the policing of new articles and expansions. The permission temper governed the future, deciding what could come into existence and in what size. The second temper is intervention: investigation, directions, takeover and price control. The intervention temper governed the present, correcting or displacing management and setting terms on which articles reached the market. The third temper is development: councils, cess, research, standardisation and training. The development temper governed capability, attempting to make Indian industry better rather than merely licensed or corrected.

Each temper had its own theory of knowledge. Permission assumed the State could know in advance which capacity the economy needed. Intervention assumed the State could diagnose failure and mismanagement after the event. Development assumed the State could raise efficiency by coordination, standards and information. The three assumptions aged differently. Advance knowledge of needed capacity proved the weakest, because demand and technology moved faster than the file. Diagnosis after the event proved mixed, strongest where the problem was a discrete shock and weakest where the problem was deep obsolescence. Coordination aged best, because standardisation, statistics and training did not depend on the State outguessing the market. The reform history follows the ageing exactly: permission was switched off for the general field, intervention was retained for defined failures, and development passed into other institutions and policies without controversy.

The tempers also explain the Act's odd textual survival. Repeal would have required a judgment on all three at once. Exemption allowed a judgment on one. Section 29B could disapply the permission sections while leaving intervention and development untouched, and that is what the 1991 notification did. A statute book that keeps the Industries Act is therefore not evidence of an unreformed economy. It is evidence of a reform that proceeded power by power, keeping the powers whose justification did not depend on the discredited one.

## The declaration, the First Schedule and Union competence

Section 2 is short enough to quote in full: "It is hereby declared that it is expedient in the public interest that the Union should take under its control the industries specified in the First Schedule." Those words perform the constitutional step contemplated by Entry 52 of the Union List. Entry 52 covers industries, but only where control by the Union is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest. Entry 24 of the State List covers industries, subject to Entry 7 and Entry 52 of the Union List. The declaration in section 2 is therefore not decorative. It is the legal hinge on which the Act turns, and [the guide to the Seventh Schedule lists](/2018/09/15/seventh-schedule-lists-explained/) sets out the list structure within which that hinge operates.

### How does the First Schedule connect the declaration to the operative sections?

The First Schedule is more than a catalogue because section 2 ties Union control to the industries named there, and section 3 defines a scheduled industry by reference to it. Adding or reshaping an entry can alter the field over which registration, licensing, investigation and takeover powers may be exercised.

The amended First Schedule is arranged under thirty-eight headings. It begins with metallurgical industries, fuels, boilers and steam generating plants, prime movers and electrical equipment. It moves through telecommunications, transportation, industrial machinery, machine tools, agricultural machinery and earth-moving machinery. It covers commercial, office and household equipment, medical and surgical appliances, industrial and scientific instruments, fertilisers, chemicals, photographic raw film and paper, dye-stuffs, drugs and pharmaceuticals, textiles, paper and pulp, sugar, fermentation industries, food processing, vegetable oils and vanaspati, soaps and toilet preparations, rubber goods, leather, glue and gelatin, glass, ceramics, cement and gypsum products, timber products, defence industries and miscellaneous industries. Cigarettes appear in the miscellaneous heading, while explosives appear among chemicals. The schedule was substituted by the Industries (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 1956, with effect from 1 March 1957, and later amendments altered particular entries. A notable later change was made by Act 27 of 2016, which recast the fermentation heading as fermentation industries other than potable alcohol and inserted a validation provision in section 29E concerning the schedule amendment.

Breadth should not be confused with a licence obligation. The First Schedule remained the field of potential control even after the licensing exemption became general. A steel plant, a paper mill and a textile undertaking could all concern scheduled industries while facing different licensing consequences because the section 29B notification, rather than the schedule alone, decided whether the licensing sections applied to the project. The shrinkage after 1991 was principally a shrinkage in the reach of compulsory licensing, not a deletion of most First Schedule headings. That distinction is the cleanest answer to the common statement that the Act was cut down to five industries. Five heads describe the surviving compulsory licensing list in the DPIIT licensing guidance. They do not describe the First Schedule.

The constitutional effect of a declaration is powerful but bounded. In Ch. Tika Ramji v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in 1956, cane growers challenged the Uttar Pradesh Sugarcane (Regulation of Supply and Purchase) Act, 1953. One argument treated sugarcane as part of the sugar industry and therefore as a subject removed from the State by the declaration for sugar. The Supreme Court separated three aspects that the word industry might be asked to carry: the raw material, the process of manufacture or production, and the distribution of the finished product. The process belonged to the controlled industry field. Sugarcane, as raw material, and sugar, as product, could fall within other entries, including the Concurrent List field for trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, the products of a controlled industry. The State law on the supply and purchase of sugarcane was upheld. The declaration did not swallow every legal subject touching a sugar factory.

Ishwari Khetan Sugar Mills v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in 1980, pressed the boundary from a different direction. Uttar Pradesh had enacted an acquisition law for sugar undertakings. The challenge said that a declared industry left no room for the State Legislature. The Court treated the law, in pith and substance, as an acquisition law supported by the Concurrent List entry for acquisition and requisitioning of property. The Industries Act occupied control, management, regulation and development of a declared industry. Acquisition of the undertakings was a different field, and the Union control assumed under the Industries Act could operate before or after acquisition. The two statutes could coexist. Section 20 of the Industries Act barred a State Government or local authority from taking over management or control of an industrial undertaking under another law, but that bar did not convert every State acquisition statute into an Industries Act takeover.

The alcohol litigation shows that the boundary can move when another specific entry is read with greater width. In Synthetics and Chemicals Ltd. v. State of Uttar Pradesh, the Court had treated intoxicating liquor in Entry 8 of the State List as confined to potable liquor and had left industrial alcohol largely to the Union side of the constitutional argument. A nine-judge Bench in State of Uttar Pradesh v. M/s Lalta Prasad Vaish, decided in 2024 by an eight to one majority, overruled that limitation and held that intoxicating liquor in Entry 8 includes industrial alcohol. The majority reasoned that the specific State entry should not be emptied by a broad reading of Entry 52. Justice B. V. Nagarathna dissented in favour of Union control. For the Industries Act, the lesson is not that the declaration lacks force. The lesson is that its force is measured against the text of competing entries and the particular subject being regulated. A declaration gives Parliament the controlled industry field. It does not answer every dispute about raw materials, products, taxation or a subject separately named in the State List.

That bounded reading also explains why State industrial law did not vanish. States continued to legislate on land, factories in aspects not occupied by the declaration, local taxation within their entries, acquisition, labour subjects assigned by the lists, and the supply or distribution of goods under the Concurrent List where a valid entry supported the law. Conflict still had to be tested by pith and substance, the extent of the control taken and, in the Concurrent field, repugnancy. The Act created Union supremacy for the control it assumed. It did not create a single-code industrial constitution.

## Reading the First Schedule heading by heading

A heading by heading reading shows the industrial imagination of the schedule. The opening headings are heavy industry: ferrous and non-ferrous metals and alloys, castings, forgings, pipes and steel structurals under metallurgical industries; coal, lignite, coke and fuel gases under fuels; boilers and steam generating plants; internal combustion engines, turbines, compressors and pumps under prime movers other than electrical; and switchgear, transformers, motors, cables and batteries under electrical equipment. These headings describe the capital goods core the Second Plan was determined to build. An undertaking's place in the schedule was decided by the article and the process, and the heading chosen could decide whether a licence file was opened under one industry group or another.

The middle headings describe the machinery and consumer chain. Telecommunications equipment, electronics and radar instruments sit beside transportation: aircraft, ships, railway coaches and wagons, automobiles, bicycles and aircraft parts. Industrial machinery reaches the machinery used in food processing, chemicals, textiles, paper, mining, metallurgy and cement. Machine tools, agricultural machinery, construction machinery and earth-moving machinery connect the factory to the farm and the building site. Commercial and household equipment ranges from office equipment and duplicators to air conditioning equipment, refrigerators, typewriters and calculating machines. Medical and surgical appliances, scientific instruments and industrial instruments complete a picture of a state that expected industrial policy to reach the hospital, the laboratory and the drawing office as well as the mill.

The process industry headings carry the schedule into daily consumption. Fertilisers are listed in detail, nitrogenous, phosphatic and potassic. Chemicals other than fertilisers include inorganic chemicals, organic chemicals, fine and intermediate chemicals, synthetic rubber, man-made fibres, explosives, plastics and insecticides. Photographic paper and film, dye-stuffs, drugs and pharmaceuticals, textiles across cotton, silk, artificial silk, staple fibre, wool and jute, paper and pulp, sugar, fermentation industries, food processing, vegetable oils, soaps and toilet preparations, rubber goods, leather, glass, ceramics, cement and gypsum products and timber products follow. The miscellaneous heading closes the list with items as varied as matches, fireworks, fountain pen ink, clocks and cigarettes. The heterogeneity is the point. The schedule was written for an economy that planned at the level of the product, and the product level is where licensing bit.

Two headings repay attention because later litigation and policy kept returning to them. Fermentation industries produced the alcohol boundary dispute: the heading named alcohol and other products of fermentation industries, and the interaction between that heading, section 18G and Entry 8 of the State List supported decades of argument about industrial alcohol, resolved against the narrower reading of the State entry only in 2024. Defence industries, the penultimate heading, named arms and ammunition, explosives and allied items of defence equipment and defence aircraft and warships. That heading supplied the scheduled industry base for the defence licensing head that survived 1991, joined in the licensing schedule by electronic aerospace and defence equipment. The schedule's breadth also explains why the end of general licensing could not be inferred from the schedule: the list describes what the Union may control, not what an entrepreneur must license.

## Registration and the licensing core

Section 10 addressed undertakings that already existed. The owner of an existing industrial undertaking in a scheduled industry was required to register it with the Central Government within ninety days from commencement, in the manner provided by rules. A certificate of registration stated the productive capacity of the undertaking. That phrase is important. Industrial licensing was not only about permission to enter. It rested on a state record of capacity: what an undertaking was treated as able to produce, and therefore what expansion, diversification or additional article might amount to in legal terms.

The certificate could be amended. Section 10 authorised amendment where a change in capacity occurred because of factors other than a substantial expansion caught by section 13. It also authorised amendment where the certificate had been issued in the name of a deceased proprietor, where an undertaking had been transferred, or where the certificate required amendment for another recorded reason. Section 10A authorised revocation of registration where registration had been obtained by misrepresentation of an essential fact, or where the undertaking had ceased to be registrable. The scheme treated false or stale registration as a defect in the control record itself.

Registration also fixed the legal identity of the existing sector at the moment the system began. The Act's concept of an existing industrial undertaking fastened on the undertakings in existence when the licensing regime commenced, and registration was the process by which those undertakings entered the record with a stated capacity. Every later question in the system referred back to that record. Was this expansion substantial against the registered figure? Was this article within the registered and licensed range? Had this undertaking been transferred, and was the certificate amended to match? The commencement registration drive was therefore not a census taken for its own sake. It laid the baseline from which forty years of permissions would be measured, and errors or generosity in the baseline echoed through every later file.

For a registration certificate issued before the amending Act 67 of 1973 took effect on 7 February 1974, the amended text required a further step. The Central Government was to specify the productive capacity by endorsement on the existing certificate. The endorsement was to take account of prescribed factors, including the manner in which prescribed levels of production had been achieved, the extent to which production had fallen short of those levels, contribution to exports, conservation of production materials and energy, production capacity approved for the undertaking, and other matters the Government considered relevant after notice to the owner. Capacity was therefore not a neutral physical measurement. It was a legal figure shaped by output history, export performance, conservation and prior approvals.

### Is a certificate of registration the same as a licence?

A certificate of registration is not a licence to begin a new undertaking. Registration recorded an existing scheduled-industry undertaking and stated its productive capacity. A licence was the permission needed for entry, fresh investment in specified cases, a new article, a substantial expansion or a change of location under the licensing sections.

Section 11 stated the basic prohibition for new industrial undertakings. No person or authority other than the Central Government could establish a new industrial undertaking except under a licence issued by the Central Government. No person or authority other than the Central Government could also make a fresh investment, or cause a fresh investment to be made, for an industrial purpose in cases covered by an order under the section, except under a licence. The words did more than regulate a first factory. They allowed the licensing system to follow capital itself where the notified circumstances made fresh investment a controlled act.

Section 11A addressed diversification. The owner of an industrial undertaking could not manufacture a new article unless the licence issued for the undertaking authorised manufacture of that article, or unless the Central Government amended the licence to include it. The sanction was severe in form. Authority to manufacture articles covered by an unamended licence could be withheld where a new article had been manufactured without authority, and the Central Government could issue directions that it considered appropriate. Section 11B added requirements for production by small scale industries, a subject connected with the reservation machinery in section 29B. Section 12 dealt with revocation or amendment of licences and registration certificates. Grounds included misrepresentation of an essential fact, failure to implement a licence within the period fixed by the Central Government, failure to comply with a condition in the licence or a direction under the Act, and a material change in circumstances after issue that made continuance of the licence unjustified in the eyes of the authority.

Section 13 pulled five further acts into permission. A licence was required to effect a substantial expansion of an industrial undertaking, to change the location of the whole or part of an undertaking, to effect a merger or amalgamation connected with establishing an undertaking, or to erect a new factory for an existing undertaking in the circumstances specified by the section. The statutory Explanation to section 13 treated an expansion as substantial where the investment in the plant and machinery of the undertaking exceeded the percentage of the existing investment prescribed by rules, or where the undertaking was of a class to which prescribed rules applied. Location was not left as a commercial footnote. Moving an undertaking could reopen the licensing question even where the product and owner stayed the same.

Section 14 made investigation part of the grant process. Before granting a licence or permission, the Central Government could require a prescribed officer or body to investigate the scheduled industry or the proposed undertaking. The matters for investigation included the proprietor's technical and other qualifications, the quantity, quality and price of the articles the undertaking might produce, the effect of the undertaking on general economic conditions, capital required and its availability, demand for the article, and other relevant matters. The officer or body could examine witnesses on oath and call for documents, accounts and information. The Advisory Council or Development Council for the industry could be consulted, and the report was to be furnished within the time fixed by the Central Government.

Section 23 then made the Central Government's decision final on stated questions. Whether a substantial expansion was involved, whether an article was a new article, and whether a dispute concerning the grant of a licence had arisen were questions on which the statutory decision was declared final. Finality clauses of that kind show how much of the system turned on characterisation. A disagreement about whether investment was substantial, or whether a product was new, was not a preliminary detail. It decided whether permission was required at all. The same pattern appears in the takeover and price provisions: broad operative phrases, a Central Government decision, and a narrow express route for judicial correction.

A licence was also time bound in practice because section 12 allowed revocation where the project was not implemented within the period fixed by the licensing authority. The licence therefore operated as both permission and programme. It recorded the product, the capacity and the location, and it exposed the holder to loss of the permission if the approved plan was not carried out. An investor who treated the licence as a permanent property-like entitlement misunderstood the instrument. The entitlement was conditional, and the conditions reached into output and timing.

## The licence as a recorded permission

A licence under the Act was personal to the recorded undertaking in a way that commercial lawyers found restrictive and planners found essential. The control record identified the owner, the undertaking, the article, the capacity and the location, and the amendment machinery in sections 10 and 12 existed because changes in that record were legally significant. Transfer of an undertaking called for amendment of the registration certificate. Death of a proprietor called for amendment. A licence obtained by misrepresentation of an essential fact could be revoked, which meant the permission rested on the truth of the particulars on which it had been granted. The permission was not drafted as a freely tradeable asset detached from the project it authorised.

That design had a planning rationale. If a licence could be sold apart from implementation, the market in permissions would allocate capacity to the highest bidder rather than to the plan's chosen use, and the licensing authority would lose the connection between approval and execution on which section 12's implementation condition depended. The design also had a cost that grew with the economy. Undertakings are bought, merged, reorganised and inherited. Each corporate event in a licensed sector had a control record consequence, and a transaction team in the licence era had to diligence the licence file as carefully as the title to the land: Was the certificate in the correct name? Did the licence cover the articles actually made? Had capacity been endorsed after the 1973 exercise? Had any direction or investigation left a condition outstanding? The questions sound antiquarian. They were the ordinary due diligence of Indian manufacturing for forty years.

The exemption settlement changed the diligence rather than abolishing it. For exempt industries, the licence file lost its operative role and the Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum acknowledgement took its place as the central record, while sector licences, environmental consents and State approvals carried the diligence weight. For the five surviving heads, the old questions remain live in their original form. The continuity is instructive: a permission system creates a documentary estate around a factory, and any serious history of Indian industry has to read that estate, in licences, certificates, endorsements and acknowledgements, as carefully as it reads the balance sheet.

## Why capacity became the unit of control

The system's true unit was neither the factory nor the firm. It was capacity. Registration stated productive capacity. A licence authorised capacity for an article. A substantial expansion was judged by investment against existing capacity. A new article disturbed the capacity picture by adding a product the certificate and licence had not contemplated. Price control and investigation then policed the use made of the capacity that permission had created. Once capacity is seen as the unit, the sections stop looking like a miscellany and start looking like a single accounting system for national production, kept in licences and certificates rather than in a market.

Capacity control had a planning logic. In an economy short of machinery, steel, cement and foreign exchange, an approved tonne of capacity in one place was a claim on resources denied to another place. The planner's question was allocative: which industry, which region, which house and which product should receive the next unit of investment. The licence answered that question in advance. Demand forecasts, import content, raw material linkage and regional balance could all be attached to the answer. Where the forecast was sound and the administration quick, the system could coordinate complementary investments, a steel plant with its coal linkage, a fertiliser plant with its feedstock and port.

The same logic carried a structural weakness. Capacity on paper is not production. A licence could be held while finance was arranged, machinery ordered or a collaborator negotiated, and the licensed figure entered the plan as if supply had been secured. The Dutt Committee's finding of pre-emption without timely implementation was a finding about this gap. Capacity figures also froze technology at the date of approval. A process improvement that raised output beyond the licensed figure, or a product improvement that shaded into a new article, could require the producer to return to the file. Industrial systems learn by adjusting capacity and product continuously. A control system built on authorised figures learns only when the file is amended.

The 1973 endorsement exercise shows the state trying to repair the accounting from inside. Productive capacity was to be endorsed on pre-1974 certificates after considering achieved production levels, shortfalls, export contribution and conservation of materials and energy. The factors are an admission that the authorised figure and the real figure had drifted apart, and that drift mattered because expansion, reservation and control orders were calculated from the authorised figure. A legal system that must periodically re-measure its own permissions is telling on itself: the permission had become the fact, and the fact had to be corrected by another administrative act.

Capacity also explains the emotional register of the licence raj debate. To the planner, capacity was a scarce national resource to be husbanded. To the entrepreneur, authorised capacity was the ceiling on ambition, and a rival's unused licence was a locked door. To the consumer, the system appeared as shortage, queue and controlled price. Each description was accurate from its seat. The Act's sections are neutral machinery; the conflict came from a shortage economy asking one instrument to do the work of capital markets, regional policy, competition policy and industrial strategy at once.

## Through the consent state: one project, many gates

The lived experience of the system cannot be understood from the Industries Act alone. A manufacturing project in the decades before reform had to pass through a cluster of controls created by different statutes. The Industries Act decided entry, capacity, article and location. The monopolies statute dealt with concentration and large houses. Foreign exchange law governed foreign participation and the use of scarce foreign currency. The capital issues statute controlled the raising and pricing of capital. Import law controlled machinery and raw materials brought from abroad. The project was one commercial plan, but the state met it through several doors, and each door had its own file.

The monopolies door belonged to the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969. Large undertakings and undertakings connected with dominant undertakings faced prior approval requirements for substantial expansion, new undertakings, amalgamation, merger and takeover under the part of that Act later removed by the 1991 amendments. [The account of the MRTP Act and its repeal](/2020/02/01/mrtp-act-1969-repeal-explained/) follows that route in detail. The foreign exchange door belonged to the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1973, which had replaced the 1947 statute and treated foreign exchange as a resource to be authorised rather than freely used. [The passage from FERA to FEMA](/2013/02/01/fera-to-fema-transition/) explains the change from permission to management. The capital door belonged to the Capital Issues (Control) Act, 1947 and the Controller of Capital Issues, whose consent covered the issue and pricing of capital until the consent regime was removed in 1992 and replaced by a disclosure based framework under the securities regulator. The import door belonged to the Imports and Exports (Control) Act, 1947 and the successive export-import policies, under which machinery, components and raw materials could require licences even after the industrial licence for the factory itself had been obtained.

The table below compresses that journey. It is a map of instruments, not a claim that every project faced every consent in the same form. Sector, size, foreign participation, location and the import content of the plan could change the combination.

The artifact in the table is deliberately narrow. It shows the consent that disappeared or narrowed, and the route by which the change came. It does not suggest that manufacturing became unregulated. Pollution control, boiler safety, factory licensing under State administration, building permission, product standards, tax registrations and labour law continued to apply because they rested on different statutes and different public purposes. Delicensing removed a permission to invest and produce. It did not remove the law of the factory floor, the river, the worker or the consumer.

The psychology of the old system followed from the sequence. Because a licence fixed capacity and article, delay at the licensing stage could determine the economics of the whole project. A rival's product could reach the approved market first. A machinery order could wait on an import licence. A priced capital issue could wait on consent. An expansion justified by demand could wait on a judgment about whether the expansion was substantial. Each gate had a stated public reason: scarce capital, scarce foreign exchange, regional balance, prevention of monopoly, orderly capacity. The gates still met the entrepreneur as one cumulative burden, and the burden explains why the phrase licence raj carried a force beyond the Industries Act itself. The Act supplied the central gate, but the experience came from the cluster.

## Shortage as the climate of the system

No account of the Act's operation is complete without the climate in which every section worked: shortage. The control system was built for an economy in which steel, cement, foreign exchange, machinery and even paper fell short of demand at administered prices. Shortage gave each power its daily meaning. Licensing decided who would add to supply. Registration measured the supply that existed. Investigation asked why supply had fallen. Price control decided who would receive the inadequate supply and at what figure. Reservation decided which class of producer would make the smaller articles. The sections were answers to a single question that a market economy rarely has to ask in that form: how shall an insufficient quantity be divided?

Shortage also explains the system's moral economy. Where a licence, a quota or a controlled price decides access, the administrator's discretion is experienced as the boundary between obtaining and going without. Queues, waiting lists and informal payments gather at such boundaries in every country that has run a shortage economy, and India was no exception. The critique of the licence raj in its popular form was rarely about the text of section 11. It was about the lived experience of standing at a boundary the text had created. The Dutt Committee and the economists wrote about pre-emption and delay. The citizen experienced the same system as the scooter booked for years ahead, the cement permit, the telephone of a later control regime, and the sense that production was something the State allowed rather than something enterprise did.

The end of shortage in one sector after another did more to discredit the system than any argument. As supply expanded in the 1980s and markets for many consumer goods began to clear, capacity licensing lost its factual premise product by product. Broad banding and partial delicensing followed the goods that had become plentiful. The 1991 settlement generalised the point in a year when the foreign exchange shortage had become a crisis of the State itself, and the argument inverted: permission was no longer the cure for scarcity but one of its causes, because a system that rations entry rations the supply that would end the shortage. The Industries Act survived that inversion because its intervention and price powers could be justified in a shortage of a different, episodic kind. Its permission power, addressed to the general field, could not.

## Licensing in operation: discretion, pre-emption and delay

The administrative life of a licence began before the licence. Industrial licensing practice worked through a letter of intent followed, after conditions were met, by conversion into an industrial licence. The letter recorded in-principle approval for the project, its product, capacity and location, and gave the promoter a document against which finance, collaboration and machinery arrangements could be pursued. The licence followed when the steps attached to the letter were completed. The two-stage route had a screening purpose, but it also lengthened the road: a project could be approved in principle and unbuilt for years, while its capacity stood in the plan and its product stood against rival applications.

Applications were examined against the plan priorities of the industry, the availability of capacity already licensed, the import content of the proposal, the foreign collaboration terms where a collaborator was involved, and the claims of regional dispersal. The Industries Act file was therefore never the only file. A proposal with foreign collaboration engaged foreign exchange control. A proposal from a large house engaged the monopolies threshold. A proposal in a reserved small scale item engaged section 11B and the reservation list. A proposal in a city engaged location policy. The examining machinery consulted the administrative ministry for the industry, the planning authorities and the State Government of the proposed location, because land, power and water were State subjects in practice even where the licence was a Union instrument.

Discretion entered at characterisation. Whether an investment was a fresh investment, whether a product was a new article, whether an expansion was substantial and whether licensed capacity was already adequate for estimated demand were questions with legal form and economic consequence. Section 23 made the Central Government's decision final on substantial expansion and new article disputes. Finality protected the administration from paralysis by litigation, but it also meant that the most consequential judgments in the system were taken inside it. A producer who believed a rival's expansion escaped licensing, or whose own product improvement was treated as a new article, faced a decision structure designed to end the argument rather than to ventilate it.

Pre-emption followed from scarcity plus time. Where licensed capacity was treated as adequate to demand, a later applicant could be refused because the field was full. An early applicant therefore gained by filing broadly and early, and a house with a licensing department gained over a first generation entrepreneur without one. The Dutt Committee recorded the outcome in the distribution of licences among large houses and in the gap between licences issued and projects implemented. R. K. Hazari's study of industrial planning and licensing policy, prepared in the 1960s, reached the connected conclusion that licensing had not prevented the growth of the large houses it was meant to discipline, and that the pattern of approvals tracked the applicants with the strongest organisational presence. The committee and study literature matters because it removes the critique from hindsight: the system's own evaluators found the gap between object and outcome while the system was at full strength.

Delay had a price that compounded. Machinery prices moved, collaboration terms lapsed, demand shifted and working capital arrangements aged while files moved between the licensing authority, the import control authority and the capital issues authority. Bhagwati and Desai treated the control system as a tax on enterprise measured in time and administrative cost, and their account of detailed, overlapping controls remains the standard economic critique. The counterweight in the record is that some delay was the point: a licensing system that answers at once has abandoned screening. The failure was not screening as an idea. The failure was a screen fine enough to catch product improvements and capacity adjustments that posed no scarcity problem, operated by a machinery whose information about demand and cost was weaker than the information held by the applicants it screened.

Regional dispersal exposes the same tension in spatial form. Licensing was used to steer factories toward backward regions by refusing or conditioning approvals in congested centres and by favouring applications for underdeveloped areas. Land, power, transport and skills pulled the other way, and a licence could not manufacture the infrastructure that made a location viable. Approvals clustered where industry already stood, and the four industrially advanced States took a majority of the benefits the Dutt Committee measured. Location control survived in diluted form after 1991 through the locational condition in the exemption notification, and its removal in 2008 closed a chapter that had begun with the belief that a permission system could redraw the industrial map by refusal.

## Watching, directing and taking over

Licensing controlled entry. Sections 15 to 18 dealt with trouble after entry. The Central Government could cause a full and complete investigation where it formed an opinion on any of five grounds stated in section 15. The grounds were a substantial fall in production relative to the production the undertaking could reasonably achieve, a deterioration in the quality of an article that could have been avoided, an unjustified rise in the price of an article, a need for conservation of resources of national importance, or conduct detrimental to the scheduled industry or to the public interest. The investigator could be a person or body of persons, and the investigating authority could require information, documents and assistance. Section 15A added a route for undertakings owned by companies in liquidation where the Central Government considered that reconstruction or amalgamation in the public interest called for an investigation into the possibility of restarting production.

Section 16 converted investigation into directions. After an investigation under section 15, the Central Government could direct the owner to take a course of action calculated to regulate production, improve quality, bring price within reasonable limits, conserve resources or manage the undertaking in the public interest. Directions could include regulating production or supply, prohibiting specified acts or practices, and requiring the owner to do acts or things the Government considered necessary. Where an investigation was pending, section 16 also permitted interim directions for a reason stated in writing, limited to conserving resources or preventing conduct detrimental to the industry or public interest, for a period fixed by the direction. Section 18 armed the investigating process with the powers of a civil court for summoning and enforcing attendance, requiring discovery and production of documents, and receiving evidence on affidavit.

### Can the Centre act before a factory fails?

The Centre can act before failure because section 15 is triggered by an opinion on stated grounds, including falling production, avoidable quality deterioration, an unjustified price rise or conduct detrimental to the industry or public interest. Investigation, directions and takeover address ailing control as well as closure.

Section 18A is the takeover power after investigation. Where the Central Government was of opinion that an industrial undertaking in a scheduled industry had been, or was being, managed in a manner highly detrimental to the scheduled industry or to the public interest generally, it could declare by notified order that the undertaking would be conducted by a person or body of persons authorised by the Government, called the authorised person, for a specified period. The terms of management could be set in the order. The authorised person could be changed, and the period of continuance could be extended for periods not exceeding two years at a time, subject to the statutory ceiling on the aggregate period of continuance after the first five years. The owner could make representations, and the section required the authorised person to manage in accordance with the order and the purposes of the Act.

Section 18AA dispensed with a prior investigation. It applied where an undertaking had been closed for a period of not less than three months and the closure was prejudicial to the scheduled industry, restart was possible through Government financial assistance and restart was necessary in the interests of the general public. It also applied where the undertaking was being managed in a manner highly detrimental to the scheduled industry or the public interest, and where persons in charge had brought about a situation affecting production by reckless investment, creation of encumbrances on the assets, or diversion of funds. The same family of grounds appears in the official summaries of the provision: reckless investments, encumbering assets, diversion of funds, closure for three months or more, and management highly detrimental to the industry or the public interest. The authorised person took over management for a specified period, with the continuance machinery of section 18A applied through section 18AB.

Swadeshi Cotton Mills is the case that keeps section 18AA honest. The Central Government issued an order on 13 April 1978 taking over the management of Swadeshi Cotton Mills under section 18AA. The company challenged the order. In Swadeshi Cotton Mills Co. Ltd. v. Union of India, the Supreme Court accepted that urgency could justify dispensing with investigation, and it did not read a full prior hearing into section 18AA as a general rule. The majority still held that, on the facts, the denial of a pre-decisional hearing breached the rule of audi alteram partem, and the matter was remitted for a full, fair and effective hearing in connection with continuance. The judgment is a useful correction to two simplifications. The section is not invalid merely because it permits action without prior investigation. Nor does the absence of an investigation leave the owner without procedural protection where the facts call for a hearing.

Section 18B states the legal consequences of a section 18A or 18AA order in hard terms. On issue of the notified order, the persons in charge of management, including directors and any manager, administrative head or other officer, are deemed to have vacated office. A contract of management or other agreement, to the extent it was not a contract with Government, terminates. The authorised person or body takes over management and applies for registration as a government company where section 18B so requires in company cases. Section 18C allows a person whose contract of management is terminated, and in defined cases a managing agent, to claim compensation from the Central Government. Section 18D provides that no person is entitled to compensation for loss or damage sustained by the order, except as provided in the Act. Section 18E states the effect on shareholder meetings and company law: provisions of the Companies Act concerning meetings, election of directors and management stand displaced to the extent provided, and the Central Government may direct modifications for the period of management. Section 18F permits cancellation or variation of the order. Where an order is cancelled, section 18FB permits prescribed relief to shareholders in the form of shares in another company or compensation in defined circumstances.

The liquidation chapters answer a harder problem: takeover of management does not cure a company that is insolvent, wound up or incapable of lawful reconstruction. Section 18FA therefore required the permission of the High Court where a company owning an undertaking was in liquidation, or where specified proceedings were pending, before an order under section 18A or section 18AA could take effect in the cases covered. Chapter IIIAA then prescribed the position of undertakings owned by companies in liquidation. Chapter IIIAC, sections 18FC to 18FH, deals with decision making by the official in charge, recording an inventory, and staying suits and proceedings against a company whose undertaking had been taken over or was to be taken over. The drafting is tied to the Companies Act, 1956 and to institutions that have since changed, and a reader should treat those cross references as part of the Act's layered amendment history rather than as a description of insolvency practice under later company law.

The powers were not theoretical. Swadeshi Cotton Mills was itself a takeover case fought to the Supreme Court. The textile sector supplied the largest field of intervention because mills combined employment, urban land, working capital stress and obsolete machinery in one undertaking, and several taken-over textile undertakings later passed into public ownership schemes under separate legislation. The Act's takeover machinery did not nationalise an undertaking by itself. It displaced management, preserved the undertaking as a going concern in the cases where that was possible, and left transfer of ownership to acquisition or nationalisation law where Parliament or a State Legislature chose that route. Ishwari Khetan matters here for the same reason: takeover under the Industries Act and acquisition of title are different legal acts, and confusing them misstates both.

A takeover also had a political economy of its own. Wage arrears, supplier dues and bank exposure made closure costly to more than the owners. Government management could keep a mill running and protect employment in the short term, but it could also postpone a decision about capital, technology and demand. The Act allowed intervention in defined circumstances. It did not supply the money, the machinery or the market that a failing undertaking lacked. That difference between legal control and industrial revival runs through the takeover chapters and explains why the power was both necessary in argument and disappointing in many outcomes.

## Investigation and directions in detail

An investigation under section 15 was an inquiry into facts the administration could not see from the licence file. Production could have fallen because demand collapsed, because machinery failed, because working capital dried up or because the owner was diverting the undertaking's resources. Price could have risen because costs rose or because scarcity was being exploited. The section's five grounds are written to separate those possibilities only after evidence is taken: the investigating authority could require the owner and others to furnish information and documents, and its report grounded the choice between a direction under section 16, a takeover under section 18A and no action at all. The investigation was the Act's diagnostic instrument, and the quality of everything after it depended on the diagnosis.

Section 16 directions were correspondingly specific in design. A direction could regulate the production and supply of an article, prohibit an act or practice that the investigation had identified, or require the owner to take steps the Central Government considered necessary in the interests of the scheduled industry or the public. Directions could address quality deterioration by requiring the avoidance that section 15 treated as possible, and price by requiring the undertaking to bring its price within limits the Government considered reasonable. Non-compliance with a direction was not a mere administrative lapse. Contravention of a direction under section 16 stood in the list of penal contraventions in section 24, and persistent detrimental management after directions formed the natural record on which a section 18A opinion could rest. The sequence was graduated on paper: register, license, investigate, direct, take over. Each step assumed the failure or inadequacy of the step before it.

The weakness of the sequence lay in time and information. An investigation into a large undertaking took months, and a direction depended on cost and production data that the undertaking itself largely supplied. By the time a takeover order issued, machinery, markets and credit could all have moved. The 1971 insertion of section 15A and the 1978 litigation over section 18AA both respond to that weakness from opposite ends: one supplied an investigation route for undertakings trapped in liquidation, the other allowed management to be taken over without the delay of investigation where the grounds were urgent. Swadeshi Cotton Mills then supplied the procedural correction, insisting that speed did not abolish the hearing the affected owner was owed in the circumstances of that case. Read together, the three developments give the intervention chapters their final shape: a graduated ladder, an emergency stair built beside it, and a judicial handrail on the stair.

## Takeover in practice: mills, money and management

The takeover chapters were used most heavily where an industry's economics broke down at scale, and the textile industry supplied that condition for decades. Cotton textile mills combined old machinery, high labour intensity, controlled cloth obligations, working capital shortage and urban land value in a single balance sheet. A mill could be commercially unviable and socially impossible to close in the same year. Investigation under section 15 could establish falling production or detrimental conduct, and an order under section 18A or section 18AA could place the mill under an authorised person while wages, suppliers and the local economy were considered. Taken-over textile undertakings later passed in groups into public ownership under separate nationalisation legislation for sick textile undertakings, which confirms the division of labour described in Ishwari Khetan: the Industries Act managed, other statutes acquired.

Government management changed the boardroom faster than the factory. Section 18B vacated the offices of the persons in charge and terminated management contracts, so the legal control of the undertaking moved at once. The authorised person inherited the plant, the workforce, the debts and the order book. What the order could not supply was modernisation capital or a market for the mill's output at remunerative prices. Where the underlying problem was a shortage of cash for a sound business, management takeover could bridge the gap. Where the problem was obsolete machinery and a product the market had left, takeover preserved employment at fiscal cost and postponed restructuring. The record of taken-over mills contains both patterns, and a fair account should not let either stand for the whole.

Takeover also interacted with the credit system. Banks and public financial institutions holding exposure to a sick undertaking had reason to prefer Government management to liquidation, because a going concern offered some prospect of recovery and because closure crystallised loss. Workers had the strongest reason of all: arrears of wages and the absence of alternative employment in mill districts made continuation a livelihood question. The authorised person therefore managed under pressures the Act does not mention, from lenders, unions, State Governments and the ministry, and the five year initial period with extensions recognised that revival, where possible, was slow. The statutory ceiling on continuance after the first five years marks the point at which Parliament expected even a rescued undertaking to leave emergency management or pass to another legal solution.

The relief and liquidation chapters complete the picture of a state learning the limits of management control. Section 18FB relief, the High Court permission route in section 18FA and the managed company provisions in sections 18FC to 18FH all address situations in which management takeover met insolvency, shareholder claims or court proceedings. Their presence in the Act is an admission written into the statute book: taking over management is the beginning of a legal problem, not its solution. Title, debt, shareholder rights and winding up each required machinery of their own, and the amendments of the 1970s built that machinery piece by piece as cases exposed the gaps.

## Price, supply and distribution: section 18G and the Essential Commodities interface

Section 18G is the Act's market-facing control. Where the Central Government considers it necessary or expedient to secure the equitable distribution and availability at fair prices of an article or class of articles relatable to a scheduled industry, it may issue an order providing for regulation or prohibition of supply, distribution, trade and commerce in the article, and for matters connected with price. The order can fix maximum and minimum prices, regulate the period for which stocks may be held, require persons to sell to specified persons or classes, prohibit withholding from sale, and impose other controls specified in the section. Section 28 places the burden, in a prosecution or penalty proceeding connected with an order under section 18G, on the person concerned to prove a lawful authority or excuse where the section so provides. Section 18G also carries the Act's most sweeping exclusion language: an order under the section is declared not to be called in question in any court. Language of that kind does not remove constitutional review of legislative power, but it shows the draftsman's intention to leave price administration to the executive rather than to ordinary appellate correction.

The interface with the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 is one of overlap by subject rather than repeal by implication. The Essential Commodities Act, Act 10 of 1955, empowers control orders under section 3 where the Central Government considers it necessary or expedient to secure the availability of an essential commodity at fair prices, or to secure its equitable distribution. An article can be relatable to a scheduled industry under the Industries Act and also be an essential commodity under the 1955 Act. Cement, steel, drugs, fertilisers and food articles have all lived in fields where industrial control and commodity control could meet. The legal question in a given case is which statute and which order the authority invoked, because the definition, procedure and penal route follow the instrument chosen. Section 18G is tied to an article or class of articles relatable to a scheduled industry. Section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act is tied to a notified essential commodity. Neither power should be described as a copy of the other.

Section 16 and section 18G are also distinct. A direction under section 16 follows an investigation into an industrial undertaking and is addressed to the owner of that undertaking. An order under section 18G is a market instrument addressed to an article or class of articles and to the persons who supply, distribute or trade in them. A single factory can be directed after investigation while the whole market for its product is also price controlled, but the route, the addressee and the remedy differ. The distinction matters to drafting, to defence and to historical description. A price control order is not a takeover, and a direction to one undertaking is not a general price code.

One consequence of the exemption route deserves emphasis. Notification S.O. 477(E) disapplied sections 10, 11, 11A and 13. It did not touch section 18G. Price and distribution control therefore passed through the 1991 settlement intact, available for any article or class of articles relatable to a scheduled industry where the Central Government considered control necessary or expedient for equitable distribution at fair prices. Delicensing freed the decision to produce. It did not free the price of the thing produced where a control order was in force, and it did not repeal the power to make such an order in a future shortage. The distinction between entry freedom and price freedom is written into the settlement itself, and accounts of 1991 that speak of decontrol without that qualification overstate what the notification did.

Price control in operation is best understood through the commodities that organised Indian economic argument for a generation. Cement stood at the centre of construction costs, and price and distribution control for cement operated for decades before decontrol was carried through in stages in the 1980s, ending in 1989. Steel pricing was administered through industry mechanisms under Government oversight, and steel price decontrol followed in the reform period after 1991. Sugar combined a controlled levy portion with a free sale portion for decades, and drugs were controlled through price orders made under the Essential Commodities framework. The statutes differed by instrument, but the administrative habit was shared: a price was treated as a policy variable for articles whose shortage could transmit inflation or halt construction, and the producer's margin was set by order after cost examination rather than discovered in an uncontrolled market.

The economic objection to that habit mirrors the objection to capacity licensing. A controlled price set below the level that calls forth supply converts shortage into queue, diversion and quality shading, and the control order then has to police the consequences of the price it fixed. Section 18G's ancillary powers, over stock holding periods, withholding from sale and sale to specified persons, are the policing instruments of a controlled price, and their presence in the section shows that the draftsman understood the sequence. The defence mirrors the defence of licensing: in a shortage economy, an uncontrolled price allocates a scarce essential article by income, and control was defended as the instrument that kept cement, steel and sugar within reach of public works and ordinary households. The 18G power remains in force because the argument was never settled in the abstract. It was settled commodity by commodity, as supply conditions changed, and the power was left available for the articles and moments to which the argument might return.

## Development councils, small scale reservation and section 29B

The developmental half of the Act did not disappear when attention settled on licensing. Development Councils continued to be the institutional form through which an industry could be given targets, norms, research and training functions from the Second Schedule. The Central Advisory Council continued as the broader consultative body for rules and administration. Their practical influence varied by industry and period, and the Act does not support a claim that consultation controlled outcomes. What the text supports is narrower and more useful: licensing was created beside consultative and developmental machinery, not in place of it. A history that begins at section 11 and ends at section 13 misses the statute Parliament actually passed.

In practice, Development Councils were constituted for a number of scheduled industries in the first decades of the Act, and their assigned work followed the Second Schedule: production programmes, capacity utilisation, standardisation, statistics and the identification of shortages in materials and equipment. The councils gave the administration a structured channel to the industry it licensed, and gave owners, technicians, workers and consumers a seat in the same room. Their weakness was structural rather than personal. A council could recommend; it could not license, finance or build. Where the binding decisions were taken in the licensing file, the council's programme work ran beside the decision rather than through it. The Central Advisory Council carried the same design at the level of the whole Act: consultation on rules was obligatory, consultation on administration was available, and neither form of consultation transferred the decision. The developmental half of the Act therefore informed the regulatory half without governing it, and the imbalance between a council that advised and a licence that decided is one of the quieter explanations for why the developmental intention is so faint in the system's reputation.

Small scale reservation used a different technique. Instead of licensing entry for all producers, the State reserved specified products for manufacture in the small scale sector and restricted larger producers. Section 11B supplied the requirement hook for production by small scale industries. Section 29B(2A) to section 29B(2G), inserted by the Industries (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 1984, Act 4 of 1984, with effect from 12 January 1984, built the reservation machinery. The Central Government could reserve an article for the small scale sector only after considering the recommendations of an Advisory Committee appointed under section 29B(2C). The Committee reported on factors including whether the article could be produced on an economic scale in the small scale sector, employment potential, promotion of industrial dispersal and entrepreneurship, and prevention of concentration of economic power. Reservation orders were laid before Parliament. Producers who were already manufacturing a reserved article outside the small scale sector could be allowed to continue subject to conditions, and a reserved article regime carried licensing consequences for larger units through the linked provisions.

The reservation list grew large and then contracted in stages. The official MSME account records that the list once covered over 800 items and that dereservation proceeded by committee recommendations across the decades that followed. Notification S.O. 998(E) dated 10 April 2015 removed the remaining twenty items, including pickle, mustard oil except through the specified solvent extraction route, groundnut oil except through that route, wooden furniture, exercise books, registers, wax candles, laundry soap, glass bangles, steel almirahs, rolling shutters, steel chairs, steel tables, steel furniture, steel cabinets, padlocks, stainless steel utensils and aluminium utensils. With that notification, small scale reservation under the Act came to an end as an operative list, although the machinery in section 29B remains in the text. The end of reservation followed the same pattern as delicensing: the list was emptied by subordinate action under powers the Act already contained, without repeal of the enabling sections.

Reservation in operation had a middle position that the list alone does not reveal. A larger unit already manufacturing an item when the item was reserved was not simply ordered to stop. The reservation scheme allowed existing production to be carried on under conditions, and the developed form of the policy permitted manufacture of reserved items by undertakings outside the small scale sector subject to an export obligation, on the reasoning that foreign exchange earning production should not be sacrificed to a domestic market reservation. The Advisory Committee's criteria in section 29B(2C) explain the calibration: economic scale tested whether small units could make the article properly, employment and dispersal tested the social return, and prevention of concentration tested whether reservation was serving its stated purpose or merely sheltering inefficiency. Dereservation proceeded item by item on committee recommendations for the same reason reservation had been imposed item by item: each article had its own scale economics, and a judgement sound for glass bangles could be unsound for an item whose efficient plant size had grown.

Section 29B itself is the pivot of the whole Act in its amended form. Section 29B(1) empowers the Central Government, by notified order, to exempt an industrial undertaking, or a class of industrial undertakings, from the operation of all or any provisions of the Act, subject to conditions where the order imposes them. Section 29B(2) deals with cancellation: where an exemption is cancelled, an owner to whom sections 10, 11, 11A or 13 would have applied must obtain the necessary licence or permission before carrying on business after expiry of the period specified in the cancelling order. Section 29B(2B) extends the exemption power to the operation of section 11B and section 29B provisions for reservation purposes, and sections 29B(2C) to 29B(2G) govern the Advisory Committee, its report, laying before Parliament and the treatment of existing manufacturers. A provision drafted as an exemption clause therefore became the legal site of both reservation and delicensing. It allowed policy to move in opposite directions at different times without amendment of the licensing sections.

That drafting choice explains the Act's longevity. A statute that can be switched field by field invites the executive to preserve the framework while changing its incidence. It also creates a transparency cost. A reader who stops at sections 10 to 14 will describe a licensing regime that most manufacturers have not faced since 1991. A reader who stops at a policy summary will miss the fact that investigation, takeover, price control and exemption all remain in force. The legally accurate description has to hold the text and the notification together.

## 1991: delicensing without repeal

The 1991 settlement had a run-up, and the run-up matters because it shows the system loosening at its edges before the centre gave way. Policy changes in the 1980s widened the flexibility inside licensing: broad banding allowed a licence holder to produce a range of related articles within a band without treating each variation as a fresh licensing event, a number of industries were delicensed or opened to wider automatic treatment in the mid 1980s, and asset thresholds for monopolies clearance were raised. Each step conceded a part of the critique while preserving the structure. Broad banding conceded that article level permission was too fine a mesh for product variation. Threshold increases conceded that figures fixed in a growing economy swept ordinary investment into extraordinary control. The concessions also trained the administration in the exemption technique that 1991 would generalise: change the incidence of the sections by policy and notification, and leave the sections standing.

The Statement on Industrial Policy presented to Parliament on 24 July 1991 announced the end of industrial licensing for most industries. The timing was not chosen in calm conditions. India faced a balance of payments crisis in 1991, with foreign exchange reserves fallen, by common account, to a level covering a few weeks of imports, and the industrial policy statement formed part of a wider stabilisation and reform programme of that summer. The crisis mattered to the legal form of the change. A government legislating at leisure might have repealed and re-enacted. A government moving within a crisis used the instrument that lay ready: the policy statement for direction, and section 29B for legal effect. Speed is part of the explanation for delicensing by exemption, and the explanation has a corollary that still matters: what was done by notification can, in law, be revisited by notification, which is why the survival of the licensing sections is not a mere antiquarian point. The policy decisions, set out in the statement and its annexes, abolished licensing irrespective of investment levels except for a short list of industries where licensing was to continue for strategic or environmental reasons. The annexed list of industries for which licensing was compulsory contained eighteen industries. A further annex listed the industries reserved for the public sector, reduced from the longer list in the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956. The statement also removed the asset threshold route by which large houses had faced monopolies clearance, liberalised foreign investment and technology arrangements, and recast public sector policy. The longer passage [from the licence raj to liberalisation](/2020/03/15/licence-raj-to-liberalisation-laws/) is traced in the companion history of the reform period. The industrial policy statement was a policy instrument. Its legal effect for the Industries Act came through the exemption power.

Notification S.O. 477(E) dated 25 July 1991, issued under section 29B(1), exempted undertakings from sections 10, 11, 11A and 13 except undertakings manufacturing items in the schedule of industries for which licensing remained compulsory. The notification was amended repeatedly as the compulsory list was shortened, as reservation changed and as locational conditions were revised. The exact notification language matters less for most readers than the legal route: sections 10 to 14 were not repealed, the First Schedule was not cut to the licensing list, and the exemption could be varied by notification. Step by step, the eighteen-industry list of 1991 was reduced until the official DPIIT guidance settled on five heads for which an industrial licence remains compulsory.

The five heads, stated as notified and as reproduced in the DPIIT licensing guidance and in State single window guidance issued for investors, are these. First, distillation and brewing of alcoholic drinks. Second, cigars and cigarettes of tobacco and manufactured tobacco substitutes. Third, electronic aerospace and defence equipment. Fourth, industrial explosives, including detonating fuses, safety fuses, gun powder, nitrocellulose and matches. Fifth, specified hazardous chemicals, namely hydrocyanic acid and its derivatives, phosgene and its derivatives, and isocyanates and di-isocyanates of hydrocarbon not elsewhere specified, such as methyl isocyanate. A manufacturer should test a proposal against the notification schedules and the product classification for the exact item, because a head stated at policy level can turn on item codes and sub-descriptions in the schedule.

## The five surviving heads, examined one by one

Each surviving head rests on a reason that differs from the capacity logic of the old system, and the difference is the strongest evidence that the residue is a considered selection rather than an unfinished repeal. Distillation and brewing of alcoholic drinks sits at the meeting point of excise revenue, public health and the State List entry for intoxicating liquors. Manufacture of alcohol was never an ordinary entry question in India, and the licensing head operates beside State excise licensing of manufacture and sale. The 2024 judgment on industrial alcohol restated the States' constitutional field over intoxicating liquor, and the Industries Act licence for distillation and brewing should be read with that field in mind: two licensing systems, founded on different entries and different purposes, can touch the same distillery.

Cigars and cigarettes of tobacco and manufactured tobacco substitutes form the health head. Tobacco manufacture is also governed by product legislation, including the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products (Prohibition of Advertisement and Regulation of Trade and Commerce, Production, Supply and Distribution) Act, 2003, and by heavy indirect taxation. The Industries Act licence controls entry into manufacture itself. Electronic aerospace and defence equipment forms the security head. Defence manufacture also runs through the Arms Act, 1959 in its application to arms and ammunition and through defence procurement policy, and the industrial licence for this head is issued in coordination with the defence establishment. The head covers a field in which the State is simultaneously regulator, customer and security guarantor, and in which unrestricted entry was never the policy of any period.

Industrial explosives form the safety head in its most literal sense. Detonating fuses, safety fuses, gun powder, nitrocellulose and matches are articles whose manufacture, storage and transport are governed by the Explosives Act, 1884 and the rules under it, and the industrial licence adds an entry control to a safety code that mainly regulates handling. Specified hazardous chemicals form the process safety head. Hydrocyanic acid and its derivatives, phosgene and its derivatives, and the isocyanates named in the schedule are substances whose accidental release can kill at distance from the plant, a category written into Indian regulatory consciousness by the methyl isocyanate disaster at Bhopal in 1984. Manufacture, storage and handling of hazardous chemicals also attract the rules framed under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986. An entry licence for such substances answers a question the general delicensing settlement never posed: not whether capacity should be planned, but whether the State should know, in advance and as a condition of entry, who is making the most dangerous articles in the schedule.

Read in that order, the five heads have a pattern. None of them survives because the State wishes to fix the capacity of the industry. Alcohol and tobacco survive on health, revenue and federal grounds. Defence survives on security. Explosives and hazardous chemicals survive on catastrophic risk. The pattern supports the verdict recorded in this article: the failure of general capacity licensing did not discredit selective entry control for dangerous and sensitive manufacture, and Parliament's instrument for keeping the two ideas apart was the notification schedule under section 29B.

Locational control shows the same staged retreat. Under the 1991 settlement, a licence could still be required for projects in specified cases connected with location within the periphery of cities with a population above one million, subject to exceptions for non-polluting industries and for areas designated before 25 July 1991, and subject to the terms of the notification as amended. A Cabinet decision in July 2008 removed the central locational stipulation of that kind. The removal did not license a factory on any land. Zoning, planning permission, environmental clearance, factory approval and municipal law remained, and they remain the correct place to test a site.

For undertakings outside compulsory licensing, the Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum became the familiar filing. The memorandum is filed with the Secretariat for Industrial Assistance in the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade, and an acknowledgement is issued. Filing records the proposal. It is not a licence, and it does not certify that the project complies with environmental, land, safety or sector law. The distinction is the practical core of delicensing. The State stopped asking for prior permission to manufacture under the Industries Act for the exempt field. It did not stop regulating manufacture.

Public sector reservation should be read separately. Government single window guidance reproducing the consolidated industrial policy position lists three heads reserved for the public sector: atomic energy, the substances and goods specified in the Department of Atomic Energy Notification No. S.O. 212(E) dated 15 March 1995, and railway transport, subject to the openings later made in areas such as railway infrastructure through separate policy decisions. Reservation of that kind concerns who may undertake the activity as a matter of policy and sector law. It is not an item in the five-head industrial licence list, and an article that merges the two lists misstates the position.

The limits of the settlement are as instructive as its reach. Delicensing under the Industries Act did not open sectors closed or capped by other policies. Foreign investment remained subject to the foreign exchange framework and to sectoral caps and routes set by policy, so a project could need no industrial licence and still face an investment condition. Defence and atomic energy carried their own regimes on top of the licensing head and the public sector reservation. Environmental clearance, which expanded as a discipline in the years after 1991, grew on a separate statutory base and became, for many projects, the permission that replaced the licence in practical importance. The reform was therefore a change in the kind of gate, not the abolition of gates: from a general permission addressed to capacity, to specific permissions addressed to land, environment, safety, foreign capital and dangerous articles. The Industries Act supplied the legal form of the first kind. Its exemption clause supplied the legal form of the change.

## Defence, critique and verdict

The defence of the 1951 design begins with the world in which it was written. Independent India had scarce capital, scarce foreign exchange, a thin capital goods base and large regional disparities in industrial development. The Industrial Policy Resolution, 1956 committed the State to a pattern of society in which the commanding heights of the economy would be developed with a large public role, private industry would operate within a planned framework, concentration of wealth and economic power would be checked, and small and cottage industries would be supported as a source of employment and dispersed production. The Second Five Year Plan strategy associated with P. C. Mahalanobis placed weight on capital goods and heavy industry because machinery to make machinery was treated as the limiting factor in long term growth. Licensing, in that setting, was defended as a way to direct scarce investment toward plan priorities, prevent duplication in thin markets, spread factories across regions and keep essential capacity under public discipline.

The defence also has a comparative point. Postwar industrial states used entry controls, directed credit, import substitution and production targets in combinations that differed in law but shared a belief that markets alone would not allocate investment across sectors and regions at the speed development required. The Industries Act was India's legal form of that belief for scheduled industries. Its councils and cess show that the form included coordination and capability building, not permission alone. A fair reading has to give the design its developmental intention before measuring its operation.

The critique is strongest where it stays close to operation. The Industrial Licensing Policy Inquiry Committee, chaired by Subimal Dutt and reporting in 1969, examined whether licensing had served its stated objects. Its findings, as summarised in the Planning Commission tradition and later policy histories, were that large industrial houses had obtained a disproportionate share of licences, that licences were often pre-empted without timely implementation, that capacity authorised on paper did not always become production, and that industrially advanced regions gained a majority of the benefits while regional dispersal lagged. Jagdish Bhagwati and Padma Desai, in their 1970 study of planning for industrialisation, documented the costs of detailed controls, delay and discretionary administration in the licensing and import control system. The critique was therefore not a later invention of reformers. It was recorded inside the system while the system was still young.

Three mechanisms explain how a defensible instrument decayed. Scarcity made the licence valuable, so obtaining and holding a licence could be a competitive strategy in itself. Capacity figures fixed on paper rewarded pre-emption: a house that secured licensed capacity denied room to a rival even where its own project moved slowly. Discretion multiplied contact points between applicant and administrator, and each contact point carried delay, information cost and the risk of rent. Regional policy suffered because applicants proposed locations where infrastructure, suppliers and finance were already strong, and licences followed the proposals. The instrument meant to correct imbalance could record imbalance instead.

The verdict that fits the evidence separates text, operation and residue. The licensing core deserved abolition for routine manufacturing because it allocated entry, capacity and diversification by permission in markets where those choices are ordinarily better made by investors subject to competition, safety and environmental law. The Dutt and Bhagwati-Desai critiques concern administration and incentives more than the existence of sections 11 to 13 as text, which is why the reform could be carried out by notification under section 29B without proving the sections unconstitutional or unworkable in every field. The residue needs a different test. Licensing for explosives, specified hazardous chemicals, defence equipment, tobacco products and alcohol manufacture raises safety, security, health and revenue questions that are not answered by the failure of capacity licensing in textiles or engineering. Investigation, takeover and price control raise proportionality questions of their own: defined grounds, fair procedure, time limits and review. The Act survives because Indian industrial policy moved from general permission to selective control. The general permission failed its own stated objects. Selective control is a separate argument, and it has to be made sector by sector.

The decades after delicensing tested the verdict in the only way that matters, by entry. Manufacturing entry in the exempt field no longer waited on a capacity judgment, and the administrative energy of industrial policy moved to land, infrastructure, credit and State level clearances. That movement confirms the diagnosis rather than refuting it: the binding constraints on Indian manufacturing after 1991 were located outside the Industries Act, in the factors a licence had never been able to supply. It also explains the Act's quiet afterlife. A statute whose most intrusive general power had been switched off stopped generating the grievances that fill case reports and committee findings, and its remaining provisions operated at the margins of dangerous manufacture, failing undertakings and controlled prices, where the argument for a state power had never depended on the licence raj.

## Two industries under the Act: sugar and textiles

Two industries carry the Act's whole biography between them. Sugar produced the constitutional case law. The sugar industry stood in the First Schedule, its raw material was an agricultural crop grown by millions of farmers, its product was an essential article of mass consumption, and its factories sat in States whose politics ran through the cane field. Every boundary of the Act was litigated in sugar: the reach of the declaration in Tika Ramji, the survival of State acquisition power in Ishwari Khetan, levy and free sale pricing under commodity control, and the location of mills under licensing. To follow sugar through the Act is to watch the declaration meet agriculture, federalism and the consumer in turn, and to see why the courts, rather than the licensing file, wrote sugar's part of the story.

Textiles produced the intervention record. The cotton textile industry stood in the First Schedule across its cotton, silk, artificial silk, staple fibre, wool and jute headings, employed labour on a scale few industries matched, and declined for reasons that mixed machinery, management, controlled cloth obligations and shifting demand. Investigation, direction and takeover were textile instruments in practice long before they were textile examples in textbooks, Swadeshi Cotton Mills supplied the leading authority on takeover procedure, and the migration of taken-over mills into public ownership schemes supplied the factual background to the acquisition analysis in Ishwari Khetan. Sugar tested what the Act could reach. Textiles tested what the Act could rescue. The first test was answered in the Act's favour with boundaries. The second was answered with a distinction that runs through this article: management can be taken over by order, but an industry cannot be revived by one.

## The Act and the company

The Act speaks to companies in the vocabulary of its decade, and the vocabulary dates it as surely as the licence. The definition of owner deems a manager, managing director or managing agent entrusted with the affairs of an undertaking to be its owner for the Act's purposes. The managing agency system, under which a firm managed a stable of companies for commission, was a central institution of Indian business when the Act was passed and was abolished by company law amendment with effect in 1970. The Industries Act retained its references, and a reader meets them in the takeover provisions as fossils of the original corporate landscape: sections that terminate management contracts, compensate managing agents in defined cases and vacate the offices of those in charge were written against a world in which control of a company often sat in a managing agency house rather than in its board.

Section 18E shows the same relationship from the other side. A takeover order would be unworkable if the Companies Act machinery of meetings, director elections and board control continued to run against the authorised person, so the Act displaces that machinery to the extent provided and lets the Central Government direct modifications for the period of management. The managed company becomes a statutory hybrid: a company in form, administered under a public order in substance. The relief provisions for shareholders in section 18FB recognise the cost of the hybrid to those whose investment is governed, for a period, by an instrument they did not choose. Company law and industrial control law were not separate universes in this period. Each assumed the other, and the takeover chapters are the record of their negotiation.

The penal attribution in section 24(2) and (3) belongs to the same negotiation. A company contravenes through human hands, so the Act deems the person in charge of and responsible for the conduct of the business to be guilty with the company, subject to the defence of absence of knowledge or due diligence, and reaches the director, manager, secretary or other officer whose consent, connivance or neglect stands behind the offence. That structure, standard in regulatory statutes of the period, placed the compliance burden on the control room rather than the factory gate. Its survival in amended company law and regulatory practice long after the licence raj is a reminder that the Act's least remembered provisions were often its most imitated.

## How the Act is read: finality, burden and exclusion

Four interpretive habits run through the Act, and a reader who learns them can predict how its disputes were framed. The first is finality. Section 23 closes the questions of substantial expansion and new article to the Central Government's decision, and the licensing system depended on that closure because every file turned on characterisation. The second is burden. Section 28 places the burden of proving lawful authority for a contravention connected with a section 18G order on the person proceeded against, reversing the ordinary instinct of criminal law in favour of the control order's administration. The third is exclusion. Section 18G declares its orders not to be called in question in any court, and the takeover and licensing provisions minimise the space for interlocutory challenge, leaving constitutional judicial review as the owner's real remedy, as the Swadeshi Cotton Mills litigation illustrates. The fourth is protection. Section 29, before its omission in the Jan Vishwas amendments, barred suits and prosecutions against the Government and its officers for acts done in good faith under the Act, and cognizance under the former section 27 required the written report of a public servant, filtering prosecution through the administration itself.

Together, the four habits describe a statute designed to be administered more than litigated. Its characteristic legal events were the application, the investigation, the direction and the order, with the courtroom at the edge of the system rather than its centre. The case law that defines the Act in retrospect, Tika Ramji on competence, Ishwari Khetan on acquisition and Swadeshi Cotton Mills on hearing, reached court precisely at the points where the administrative design met a constitutional right or a competing legislative field. Inside the field, the file ruled. That is the deepest sense in which the licence raj was a raj: not merely that permission was required, but that the permission system carried its own finality, its own burden and its own protection, and answered most challenges from within.

## Amendment layers and the supporting powers

The Act reached its amended form in layers, and the layers explain provisions that puzzle a first reader. The Industries (Development and Regulation) Amendment Act, 1956, Act 71 of 1956, substituted the First Schedule with effect from 1 March 1957, giving the schedule the structure described in this article. The amendment Act 72 of 1971 inserted section 15A, the investigation route for undertakings owned by companies in liquidation. Act 67 of 1973 supplied the productive capacity endorsement for older registration certificates and substituted the twelve year ceiling language in the takeover continuance provisions. Act 32 of 1974 substituted part of the section 18FA permission machinery for undertakings connected with liquidation. The amendment Act 4 of 1984 inserted section 11B and the reservation machinery in section 29B(2A) to (2G). Act 27 of 2016 recast the fermentation heading and inserted section 29E to validate the schedule position, and Act 18 of 2023 rewrote the penalty clause and added the adjudication sections. A citation from the Act therefore needs a date discipline: the section number identifies the instrument, and the amendment history identifies the form in which it stood at the time in question.

The supporting powers are brief but load bearing. Section 19 authorises inspection of an industrial undertaking by an officer empowered by the Central Government, directed at verifying the position of the undertaking under the Act, its licence and any direction in force. Section 23, discussed with licensing, makes the Central Government's decision final on substantial expansion, new article and connected disputes arising in the grant process. Section 25 permits the Central Government to delegate its functions under the Act to subordinate authorities and to State Governments by notified order, while withholding the most intrusive powers, including directions under section 16 and takeover under sections 18A and 18AA, from that general delegation. The design is consistent with the rest of the Act: routine administration could travel downward, the decisions that displaced management could not.

Rule-making under section 30 supplied the working detail that the sections omit. The Registration and Licensing of Industrial Undertakings Rules, 1952 prescribed the procedure and forms through which registration was sought, licences were applied for and certificates were issued and amended, and rules governed council procedure, fees and the particulars to be furnished to the administration. The rule book is where the licence raj acquired its paper form: application particulars about capacity, capital structure, collaboration, import content and location entered the file because the rules asked for them. A history of the system written only from the Act understates the density of the control, because the density lived in the forms.

## The States inside a Union statute

A Union licensing statute still had to be operated in States. The factory stood on State land, drew State supplied power and water, employed workers governed in part by State administered factory and labour machinery, and paid State and local taxes. Licence applications for a location were examined with the State Government's knowledge because the State would carry the infrastructure consequences of approval or refusal. States competed for licensed projects, lobbied for capacity in plan discussions and used their own industrial promotion corporations, estates and incentives to attract the undertakings the Union licensed. The declaration centralised the permission. It could not centralise the conditions that made a permission worth having.

The constitutional cases kept a second State presence alive inside the scheme. After Tika Ramji, a State could legislate on the raw material and product sides of a controlled industry through entries outside the manufacturing process. After Ishwari Khetan, a State could acquire an undertaking under the acquisition entry without colliding with the Industries Act, while section 20 barred a State takeover of management that cut across the Union scheme. State industrial development law therefore grew in the spaces the declaration left: acquisition, land, incentives, estates, raw material supply and product distribution. The alcohol cases added a third presence, taxation and regulatory power over a substance that was also a scheduled industry product, contested for decades and restated by the nine-judge Bench in 2024. Federalism under the Act was not an exception to central control. It was the medium in which central control operated.

The Concurrent List supplied the shared ground on which much of that operation stood. Entry 33 of the Concurrent List covers trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, the products of an industry where control by the Union is declared expedient, together with imported goods of the same kind. Tika Ramji turned on that architecture: the process of manufacture belonged to the declared industry field, while the product and its raw material could be reached through the Concurrent entry by either legislature, subject to repugnancy. Price control practice reflected the sharing. The Essential Commodities Act is a Union statute whose control orders are administered in large measure through State machinery, and State Governments have long issued and enforced commodity orders under delegated power. The result was a system in which the Union declared, licensed and could take over, while the States supplied, distributed, inspected and prosecuted much of the daily control. Neither level's statute book tells the whole story alone.

## Offences, penalties and the Jan Vishwas substitution

The penal scheme in the Act as it stood for most of its life was criminal in form. Section 24 punished contravention of a provision of the Act, an order under the Act, a licence condition, a direction under section 16 or an order under section 18G, with imprisonment for a term which could extend to six months, or fine which could extend to five thousand rupees, or both, and with an additional fine for a continuing default. Section 24(2) deemed a company and every person in charge of and responsible for the conduct of its business to be guilty where the offence was committed by the company, subject to the defence that the offence was committed without knowledge or that due diligence had been exercised. Section 24(3) extended liability to a director, manager, secretary or other officer where the offence was committed with consent or connivance, or was attributable to neglect. Section 24A punished making a false statement in a matter required under the Act, an order or a licence, with imprisonment up to three months, or fine up to two thousand rupees, or both. Section 27 required cognizance on a report in writing of the facts by a public servant. Section 29 barred suits and prosecutions against Government and its officers for acts done in good faith under the Act.

The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023, Act 18 of 2023, changed the enforcement grammar for the provisions it amended, with effect from 12 December 2023 for the Industries Act entries. Section 24(1) was substituted so that a person who contravenes a provision of the Act, an order, a condition of a licence, a direction under section 16 or an order under section 18G is liable to a penalty which may extend to twenty-five lakh rupees, with a further penalty for a continuing contravention at the rate stated in the substituted clause. Section 24A was substituted as an adjudication provision: the District Magistrate or an Additional District Magistrate authorised by the Central Government acts as adjudicating officer, after giving the person concerned an opportunity of being heard. Section 24B provides an appeal to an officer not below the rank of Joint Secretary to the Government of India within thirty days, with disposal within sixty days. Section 24C provides recovery of penalty as arrears of land revenue where the penalty is not paid within ninety days. Sections 27, 29 and 29A were omitted, and section 28 was adjusted so that its burden language speaks of imposition of penalty rather than prosecution.

The change should be described with care. The substituted section 24(1) is monetary and adjudicatory in route. It raises the ceiling from five thousand rupees to twenty-five lakh rupees while removing the imprisonment limb from that clause. It does not rewrite every regulatory consequence in the Act. Licence revocation under section 12, cancellation of an exemption under section 29B, directions after investigation and takeover consequences continue to operate as regulatory measures, and conduct involving fraud, safety or another commodity can still attract other statutes. The Jan Vishwas change is therefore a decriminalisation of the stated penalty clause combined with a much larger monetary exposure and an administrative recovery route. It is not a repeal of enforcement.

The old figures had in any event decayed into symbolism. A maximum fine of five thousand rupees, fixed for an economy of the 1950s and left unadjusted through decades of inflation, could not price a contravention in an industrial sector where a single consignment could be worth many times the maximum. Enforcement had rested less on the fine than on the regulatory consequences around it: revocation, refusal, direction and the reputational cost of a prosecution sanctioned under section 27. The Jan Vishwas substitution recognised that structure honestly. It moved the money sanction to a level with meaning, placed its imposition in an adjudication with a hearing and an appeal, and attached recovery to the land revenue machinery that the Indian state has used for public dues for generations. The prosecution route it removed had been, for most contraventions of a regulatory statute of this kind, a heavier instrument than the conduct ordinarily warranted, and its removal follows the stated purpose of the 2023 Act: trust based governance and ease of doing business, pursued by substituting penalties for imprisonment across the statutes it amended.

## What remains, and how to read the Act

The Act remains in force. Parliament has not repealed it, and the reason is visible in the provisions that delicensing never touched. The declaration and First Schedule still define the field of Union control. The Central Advisory Council and Development Council provisions still stand. Registration still stands in the text for the field to which it applies. Sections 15 to 18FH still supply investigation, direction, takeover and company relief machinery. Section 18G still supplies price and distribution control for articles relatable to scheduled industries. Section 29B still supplies exemption and reservation machinery, and the penal provisions operate in the amended form described in the previous section. Repeal has an obvious attraction for a statute remembered through a discarded regime, but repeal would require Parliament to decide what happens to takeover, price control, councils and the surviving licences. Preservation has a cost too: a reader must always ask which provision has been exempted, reserved, amended or left untouched.

Reading the Act correctly therefore requires reading a notification with it. Notification S.O. 477(E) organised the exemption through schedules: a schedule of industries for which licensing remained compulsory, and a schedule connected with items reserved for the small scale sector, with the general field exempted from the licensing sections. The schedules were amended as heads were removed, items dereserved and conditions revised, so the notification in force at the relevant date is the operative document for the licensing question, and the Act is the operative document for everything else. Lawyers who cite sections 11 to 13 without the notification describe a regime that ended in 1991. Summaries that cite the policy without the notification miss the legal route by which the regime ended and could, in law, be revived for a field by cancellation of the exemption.

A practical reading order helps. Begin with the product and ask whether it falls within a scheduled industry. Then ask whether the undertaking is within the section 29B exemption or within a head for which licensing remains compulsory, testing the exact item description rather than a trade label. Then ask whether a separate sector statute governs the product: defence production, explosives, drugs, food safety, chemicals and tobacco each carry their own regimes. Then test location and site under land, planning and environmental law. A filing check also saves errors in administrative material: an acknowledgement, a memorandum or a compiled project note should be checked against the source notification before it is circulated, and a reader assembling records from scanned files may find it useful to open a compiled presentation through [an online PowerPoint viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) before relying on a figure quoted from it. That habit is clerical, not legal, but the history of this Act is full of figures that changed because a schedule was amended.

The repeal question deserves a direct answer, because it tests whether preservation is inertia or judgment. A repeal of the Act would have to decide the fate of at least five live inheritances. The declaration and schedule would fall, and with them the Union's Entry 52 footing for the field in the form the Constitution contemplates. The surviving licences for five heads would need a new statutory home or a saving. The takeover chapters would lapse, leaving a failing undertaking in a sensitive sector to insolvency and company law alone. Section 18G price control would lapse, leaving the Essential Commodities Act as the only general price instrument. The councils and the cess would lapse with little practical loss, and the reservation machinery would lapse with none, its list being empty. A Parliament that repeals is therefore making five decisions, of which at least three are contested, in a single clause. Preservation by exemption made one decision and postponed the rest. Postponement has a price in clarity, paid by every reader who meets sections 11 to 13 without the notification. It also has a logic that any honest account should record: sleeping powers cost little until the day they are wanted, and the day a power of this kind is wanted is rarely a day for drafting it afresh.

The Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum illustrates the reading order in miniature. The memorandum is filed in two parts in the established practice of the Secretariat for Industrial Assistance: one part before the project is established, and the second after commencement of commercial production, with an acknowledgement issued at each stage. Neither acknowledgement is an approval, and neither certifies compliance with the sector statutes the reading order identifies. A promoter who treats the acknowledgement as a clearance repeats, in miniature, the oldest error in the system's history: mistaking the document the centre issues for the legality of the project. The delicensed regime kept the record and removed the permission, and the discipline of the regime lies in remembering which of the two a given document is.

Three proposals show the reading order at work. A promoter planning a brewery begins in the First Schedule, under fermentation industries, and finds the project inside the first surviving licensing head, distillation and brewing of alcoholic drinks. The Industries Act licence question is therefore live, and beside it stand State excise licensing for manufacture, the food safety regime for the product and the alcohol jurisprudence on the Union and State fields. A promoter planning a unit for safety fuses finds industrial explosives in the licensing schedule and the Explosives Act, 1884 in the sector column, so entry permission and handling permission must both be obtained, each from its own authority and on its own criteria. A promoter planning an engineering plant outside the five heads finds the Industries Act licence question closed by the exemption, files the Industrial Entrepreneurs Memorandum, and spends the project's legal effort where the delicensed system intends it to be spent: land and planning, environmental clearance, factory and boiler approvals, product standards and the tax and labour registrations of the State of the site. The three files look nothing alike, and the difference between them is the post 1991 Act in operation: a licensing statute for the dangerous and the sensitive, a recording system for the general field, and a reserve of intervention powers behind both.

The Industries Act 1951 endures as a frame rather than as a general licence regime. It built the legal grammar of permission in Indian industry: declaration, schedule, registration, licence, investigation, direction, takeover, price control, exemption and penalty. Reform kept the grammar and changed the sentences to which it applied. That is the accurate way to describe the end of the licence raj under this statute. The raj was not repealed. It was exempted away, head by head and notification by notification, until the surviving controls were the ones the State was still willing to defend.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Industries Development and Regulation Act establish?

The Act established a Union framework for the development and regulation of industries specified in its First Schedule. It declared in section 2 that Union control over those industries was expedient in the public interest, created the Central Advisory Council and Development Councils, required registration of existing undertakings, and made a Central Government licence necessary for new undertakings, new articles and substantial expansions. It also supplied powers to investigate an undertaking, issue directions, take over management in defined circumstances, control the price and distribution of articles relatable to a scheduled industry, and penalise contravention. Development and regulation were designed as parts of one scheme.

### Q: How did industrial licensing actually work?

An applicant sought a licence from the Central Government before establishing a new industrial undertaking in a scheduled industry. Section 14 allowed the Government to require an investigation into the applicant's qualifications, the proposed articles, demand, capital needs and the effect on economic conditions, and the relevant council could be consulted. The licence authorised the undertaking, its product and its capacity at an approved location. Making a new article required the licence to cover that article or be amended under section 11A. A substantial expansion or a change of location required permission under section 13. Failure to implement within the fixed period exposed the licence to revocation under section 12.

### Q: Which industries still require a licence?

The DPIIT licensing guidance states five heads for which an industrial licence remains compulsory. They are distillation and brewing of alcoholic drinks; cigars and cigarettes of tobacco and manufactured tobacco substitutes; electronic aerospace and defence equipment; industrial explosives, including detonating fuses, safety fuses, gun powder, nitrocellulose and matches; and specified hazardous chemicals, namely hydrocyanic acid and its derivatives, phosgene and its derivatives, and isocyanates and di-isocyanates of hydrocarbon not elsewhere specified, such as methyl isocyanate. The exact item description and classification in the notification schedule decide a borderline case, because a trade label alone may not match the scheduled description.

### Q: How was industrial licensing dismantled?

Licensing was dismantled by exemption rather than repeal. The Statement on Industrial Policy of 24 July 1991 announced abolition for all industries except a short list where licensing would continue for strategic or environmental reasons, and the annexed list then contained eighteen industries. Notification S.O. 477(E) dated 25 July 1991, issued under section 29B(1), exempted undertakings from sections 10, 11, 11A and 13 except for the scheduled licensing heads. Later amendments shortened that list in stages until five heads remained. Sections 11 to 13 stayed in the statute book, the First Schedule stayed broad, and the section 29B exemption carried the legal effect of the reform.

### Q: What powers does the government have to take over an industrial undertaking?

After an investigation under section 15, section 18A lets the Central Government declare that an undertaking in a scheduled industry will be conducted by an authorised person where it is of opinion that the undertaking has been or is being managed in a manner highly detrimental to the scheduled industry or to the public interest. Section 18AA allows takeover without prior investigation on grounds including reckless investment, creation of encumbrances, diversion of funds, prejudicial closure for three months or more, and highly detrimental management. Section 18B deems existing managers to have vacated office, terminates management contracts and places the undertaking under the authorised person for the period specified.

### Q: What was small scale industry reservation?

Reservation was a policy under which specified products were kept for manufacture in the small scale sector, restricting production of those items by larger units. The machinery stood in section 11B and section 29B(2A) to (2G), inserted by the amending Act 4 of 1984. Reservation required consideration of an Advisory Committee report on economic scale, employment, dispersal of industry, entrepreneurship and prevention of concentration. The list once covered over 800 items and was reduced in stages over the decades that followed. Notification S.O. 998(E) dated 10 April 2015 removed the remaining twenty items, ending reservation as an operative list while the enabling sections remained in the Act.

### Q: Is the Industries Act still in force?

Yes. The Act has not been repealed. Delicensing changed the reach of sections 10, 11, 11A and 13 through exemption notifications under section 29B; it did not repeal the Act or the First Schedule. The declaration in section 2, the council provisions, the investigation and direction powers in sections 15 and 16, the takeover chapters, section 18G price and distribution control, the exemption and reservation machinery in section 29B, and the penal provisions in amended form all remain in the statute book. Repeal would require Parliament to decide the fate of each of those surviving powers, which is a separate legislative choice from the 1991 exemption.

### Q: What is the First Schedule and why does it matter?

The First Schedule lists the industries to which the Act applies, arranged under thirty-eight headings that run from metallurgical industries, fuels and electrical equipment through chemicals, textiles, paper, sugar, food processing, defence industries and miscellaneous industries. It matters because section 2 ties the declaration of Union control to the industries specified there, and section 3 defines a scheduled industry by reference to the schedule. Inclusion in the First Schedule makes an industry part of the field over which the Act's powers may operate. It does not by itself mean that a licence is compulsory, because the section 29B exemption decides whether the licensing sections apply to an undertaking.

### Q: What does section 29B allow the Central Government to exempt?

Section 29B(1) allows the Central Government, by notified order, to exempt an industrial undertaking or a class of industrial undertakings from the operation of all or any provisions of the Act, subject to conditions stated in the order. That single power carried the 1991 delicensing settlement, because Notification S.O. 477(E) used it to disapply sections 10, 11, 11A and 13 for undertakings outside the compulsory licensing schedule. Section 29B also houses the reservation machinery for the small scale sector in subsections (2A) to (2G). The provision is therefore both the Act's release valve and one of its control instruments, depending on the notification issued under it.

### Q: What does scheduled industry mean in the Act?

Section 3 defines scheduled industry as an industry specified in the First Schedule. The term fixes the field of the Act rather than the licence position of a particular factory. An undertaking pertaining to a scheduled industry and carried on in one or more factories falls within the definition of industrial undertaking, and the owner is the person or authority with ultimate control over its affairs. Because the First Schedule covers thirty-eight headings, from metallurgical industries to defence industries, scheduled industry is a wide category. Whether licensing sections apply to a given undertaking depends on the section 29B exemption and the notified licensing schedule, not on the schedule label alone.

### Q: What does a certificate of registration record?

A certificate of registration under section 10 records an existing industrial undertaking in a scheduled industry and states its productive capacity. Registration was required within ninety days from commencement for undertakings already in existence. The certificate could be amended where productive capacity changed for reasons other than a substantial expansion, where the proprietor named in it had died, where the undertaking was transferred, or where another recorded reason required correction. For certificates issued before the 1973 amendment took effect, the Central Government was to endorse productive capacity after considering production levels, export contribution, conservation and approved capacity. Registration was a record of capacity, not a permission to start a new undertaking.

### Q: When can a licence be revoked under section 12?

Section 12 allows the Central Government to revoke or amend a licence or a certificate of registration on stated grounds. The grounds include obtaining the licence or certificate by misrepresentation of an essential fact, failure to implement the licence within the period fixed by the Government, failure to comply with a condition of the licence or with a direction issued under the Act, and a material change in the circumstances in which the licence was granted that makes its continuance unjustified. Revocation under section 12 is a regulatory consequence separate from the penalty for contravention. A licence was a conditional permission tied to implementation, and the conditions survived the grant.

### Q: What counts as a substantial expansion under section 13?

Section 13 required a licence or permission for acts that changed the approved shape of an undertaking, including a substantial expansion and a change of location. The Explanation to section 13 measures substantiality principally by investment: an expansion is substantial where the investment in the plant and machinery of the undertaking exceeds the percentage of the existing investment prescribed by rules, or where the undertaking belongs to a class to which prescribed rules apply. Section 23 made the Central Government's decision on whether a substantial expansion was involved final for the purposes of the Act. The test therefore turned on a prescribed investment threshold and class rules, not on a manager's description of the project.

### Q: How do section 18A and section 18AA differ?

Section 18A follows an investigation. It applies where, after investigation under section 15, the Central Government is of opinion that an undertaking in a scheduled industry has been or is being managed in a manner highly detrimental to that industry or to the public interest, and it authorises management by an authorised person for a specified period. Section 18AA dispenses with prior investigation. It reaches reckless investment, encumbrances created on assets, diversion of funds, closure for three months or more where restart is possible and necessary in the public interest, and highly detrimental management. Swadeshi Cotton Mills shows that the absence of an investigation does not by itself make the section 18AA route unlawful.

### Q: What consequences follow a takeover order under section 18B?

Section 18B attaches automatic consequences to a notified order under section 18A or section 18AA. Persons in charge of the management of the undertaking, including directors and managers, are deemed to have vacated their offices. Contracts of management and similar agreements terminate, except contracts with the Government. The authorised person takes over management and control of the undertaking. Section 18C allows compensation claims for terminated management contracts in defined cases, while section 18D denies compensation for other loss except as the Act provides. Section 18E displaces parts of company law for the period of Government management, and section 18F permits the order to be cancelled or varied.

### Q: How do section 18G price orders differ from section 16 directions?

A section 16 direction follows an investigation into a particular industrial undertaking and is addressed to its owner. It can regulate production, require quality improvement, address price or require action in the public interest for that undertaking. A section 18G order is a market instrument. It applies to an article or class of articles relatable to a scheduled industry and can regulate or prohibit supply, distribution, trade and commerce, fix prices and control withholding from sale for the market generally. The two powers can overlap in effect where a directed undertaking sells a controlled article, but the trigger, the addressee and the legal route differ.

### Q: What changed in the Act's penalty provisions under the Jan Vishwas Act?

The Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act, 2023, Act 18 of 2023, substituted section 24(1) with effect from 12 December 2023. Contravention of the Act, an order, a licence condition, a section 16 direction or a section 18G order attracts a penalty which may extend to twenty-five lakh rupees, replacing the earlier clause that carried imprisonment up to six months or a fine up to five thousand rupees. New sections 24A to 24C created adjudication by the District Magistrate or an authorised Additional District Magistrate, an appeal to an officer not below Joint Secretary rank, and recovery of unpaid penalty as arrears of land revenue.

### Q: What do Development Councils do under the Act?

Development Councils are bodies corporate established under section 6 for a scheduled industry or a group of scheduled industries. Their membership represents owners, technical experts, employees and consumers. The Central Government assigns them functions from the Second Schedule, which include production targets, coordination of production plans, efficiency norms, fuller use of capacity, standardisation, marketing and distribution arrangements, research, training, costing methods and statistics. Section 9 allows a cess on goods of a specified scheduled industry to fund research, design and quality improvement, and training. Councils belong to the developmental half of the Act: they were built for coordination and capability, while licensing belonged to the regulatory half.

### Q: What happens to a manufacturer if a section 29B exemption is cancelled?

Section 29B(2) answers the question in the text. Where an exemption granted under section 29B is cancelled, an owner to whom sections 10, 11, 11A or 13 would have applied cannot carry on business after expiry of the period specified in the cancelling order except under and in accordance with a licence or permission obtained under the Act. Cancellation therefore revives the licensing requirement for the undertaking concerned rather than merely withdrawing a concession. The provision is the legal reason an exemption should never be described as a repeal: the sections waiting behind it were disapplied for the exempt field, and a cancelling order can bring them back into operation.

### Q: Why does section 20 bar a State Government from taking over an undertaking?

Section 20 provides that no State Government or local authority may take over the management or control of an industrial undertaking under any law for the time being in force, in a manner inconsistent with the scheme of management provided in the Act. The bar protects the Union scheme created after the section 2 declaration: once Parliament has taken a scheduled industry under Union control and supplied its own investigation and takeover machinery, a State route that displaces that machinery would fragment control. The bar is aimed at takeover of management or control. In Ishwari Khetan, the Supreme Court held that a State acquisition law stands on a different entry and is not a takeover of management barred by section 20.
