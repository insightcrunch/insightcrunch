On 12 December 1990, T.N. Seshan took charge as the tenth Chief Election Commissioner of India, and the office he entered had spent four decades as the quietest constitutional authority in New Delhi. The Election Commission of India conducted the largest elections on earth, yet it conducted them by deference: it announced dates after consulting the government of the day, it requested staff and police from the same governments whose candidates stood for election, and it treated the superintendence, direction and control that Article 324 of the Constitution vested in it as a procedural trust rather than as a governing power. This article defends one claim about what changed in the six years that followed, ending on 11 December 1996. Seshan's reforms operationalised an architectural shift in the positioning of the Election Commission within the constitutional order, from procedural deference toward political actors to an assertive-commissioner positioning that read Article 324 as substantive institutional content. The shift had four components: a Model Code of Conduct converted from a voluntary compact into an enforced discipline, a photo-identity-card requirement that rebuilt the electoral roll around photographic verification, an amended Election Symbols Order that hardened the rules of party recognition and symbol allotment, and a disqualification practice under Section 8 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 that treated conviction as an operative bar rather than a negotiable stigma. The Rao government answered the shift on 1 October 1993 by rebuilding the Commission itself as a three-member body, and the Supreme Court's Constitution Bench judgment of 14 July 1995 settled the resulting contest by holding. The strongest evidence for the architectural reading is the trajectory after 1996: commissioners of very different temperament have operated inside the pattern Seshan built, and none has returned the institution to the deference of the years before 1990.

## The Commission Seshan Inherited: Deference as an Operating System

The Commission that Seshan inherited had been designed in a hurry and then left to improvise. When Sukumar Sen, a member of the Indian Civil Service seconded from the West Bengal chief secretaryship, became the first Chief Election Commissioner on 21 March 1950, he had no permanent staff, no settled rules of procedure and roughly seventeen months to prepare a general election for an electorate of about 173 million voters, most of whom could not read the names on a ballot paper. The improvisation that solved that problem, examined in the account of [the founding-template improvised architecture](/2020/06/08/sukumar-sen-1951-1952-election/), produced durable inventions: a separate ballot box for each candidate in 1951 and 1952, a symbol system that let an illiterate elector vote by recognition, and a field machinery borrowed from the district administrations of the states. Sen's two reports on the general elections of 1951 to 1952 and of 1957 remain the founding texts of Indian election administration, and Sen himself recorded that the framers had given the Commission, through Article 324, a reservoir of power adequate to any contingency the statute book had failed to foresee.

The reservoir stayed capped for forty years. The nine Chief Election Commissioners who held the office between Sen's retirement in December 1958 and December 1990, from K.V.K. Sundaram through to R.V.S. Peri Sastri, ran elections of growing size and frequent violence without converting Article 324 into an instrument of discipline over the political class. The reasons were structural rather than temperamental. The Commission depended on the Union and state governments for every returning officer, every presiding officer and every policeman at every booth, and Article 324 itself obliged the President and the Governors to make staff available only when the Commission requested it. A Commission that quarrelled with a ruling party quarrelled with its own workforce. The convention that grew from this dependence was consultation shading into clearance: schedules were settled in a register of mutual convenience, the Model Code of Conduct bound ministers by courtesy, and the Commission's considerable formal powers were exercised at the margins, in the allotment of symbols and the settlement of party disputes, rather than against governments in office.

The one partial exception proved the rule. S.L. Shakdher, Chief Election Commissioner from 1977 to 1982, used the post-Emergency years to press a reform agenda on the government of Morarji Desai, and K.V.K. Sundaram before him had warned in print about the corrupting growth of election expenditure. Neither converted exhortation into enforcement. By the late 1980s the costs of deference stood in plain view: booth capturing in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh had become an organised trade, electoral rolls carried the dead and the duplicated alongside the living, and the election of 1989 returned a fractured verdict that opened [the post-1989 institutional-political-stress context](/2019/05/20/vp-singh-1989-verdict-india/) in which every constitutional office, the presidency, the governorships and the Commission included, was about to be tested by coalition instability. The government of V.P. Singh appointed a committee on electoral reforms under Law Minister Dinesh Goswami in January 1990, and the Goswami Committee's report of May 1990 recommended, among much else, that the Commission be made a multi-member body and that the photo identity card be issued to every elector. The recommendations slept in the file. The institution that would act on two of them within three years was, in December 1990, still waiting for its next head.

### Why did Article 324 remain a sleeping power for four decades?

Article 324 vested the superintendence, direction and control of elections in the Commission in the widest words available, yet Commissioners before 1990 treated the clause as administrative authority rather than governing power, because the institution depended for every polling official and policeman on the governments it was meant to discipline. Deference was the price of a borrowed workforce.

The dependence had shown itself even in the Commission's composition. In October 1989 the outgoing government of Rajiv Gandhi appointed two Election Commissioners, S.S. Dhanoa and V.S. Seigell, alongside Chief Election Commissioner R.V.S. Peri Sastri, converting the Commission into a multi-member body for the first time since a brief experiment in the founding years. The incoming government of V.P. Singh reversed the appointments by amending the service rules, and the two commissioners stood removed on 1 January 1990. Dhanoa carried the removal to the Supreme Court, which held in S.S. Dhanoa v Union of India, reported at (1991) 3 SCC 567, that the appointments and their termination both lay within the constitutional scheme, since Election Commissioners held office on terms the President could lawfully set. The episode matters for the Seshan years because it established, before Seshan took charge, that the size of the Commission was a lever a government could pull. What no one had yet tested was whether a Chief Election Commissioner would pull levers of his own, against governments, and keep pulling them for six years.

## The Reform Agenda Before Seshan: Ideas Without an Operator

Nothing Seshan did between 1990 and 1996 was intellectually new, and the architectural reading is strengthened, not weakened, by admitting it. The reform of Indian elections had been written down, in full and in detail, at least three times before he took office, and each draft had died in the custody of the governments it proposed to discipline. The first full draft came from outside the state altogether. In 1974 Jayaprakash Narayan's Citizens for Democracy appointed a committee under the retired Bombay High Court judge V.M. Tarkunde, and the Tarkunde Committee's report of 1975 recommended a three-member Election Commission appointed on the advice of a collegial body rather than by the government alone, the lowering of the voting age, identity documentation for voters, and curbs on the money and machinery advantages of ruling parties. The report arrived months before the Emergency and was buried beneath it. The second draft came from inside the Commission itself. S.L. Shakdher, who held the office from 1977 to 1982, submitted a set of reform proposals to the Janata government and renewed them thereafter, pressing among other things for the Commission's strengthening and for statutory backing to the conduct rules the Commission administered by consent. The proposals were received with respect and filed without action, by governments of two different parties, which established the pattern the third draft would repeat.

The third draft was the Goswami Committee's. Appointed in January 1990 by the V.P. Singh government and reporting in May of that year, the committee worked through the entire agenda, the multi-member Commission, the photo identity card, disqualification, expenditure, the misuse of official machinery, and recommended much of what the decade would later see enacted or enforced. The government that commissioned the report fell in November 1990 before it could act on a single major recommendation, and its successor, the minority ministry of Chandra Shekhar, had neither the numbers nor the lifespan to legislate an agenda whose first casualty would have been its own freedom of manoeuvre. The archive thus held, in December 1990, a complete reform programme endorsed in principle by a parliamentary committee, resting beside an Election Commission whose enforcement habits had been formed by forty years of deference. The distance between the programme on paper and the practice in the field is the space in which the Seshan tenure has to be explained, and it cannot be explained by the programme, which everyone already possessed. What the tenure added was not a new idea but a new location for the ideas: inside the Commission's own operational orders, where a committee's recommendation became a returning officer's instruction, a deadline in a letter to a chief secretary, a transfer order, a paragraph in an observer's reporting format. Reform as politics, the secondary framework of this article's brief, names exactly that relocation. A recommendation addressed to a government asks the government to restrain itself. An operational order addressed to the machinery restrains the machinery first and lets the government complain afterwards, and the order of those two events, reversed between the committee era and the tenure, is a large part of what changed in 1990.

## The Reservoir in the Text: Mohinder Singh Gill, 1978

The legal warrant for the assertive reading of Article 324 had also been on the record for more than a decade before Seshan used it, in a judgment the tenure's lawyers cited and its critics preferred to forget. In Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, decided by a Constitution Bench in 1977 and reported at (1978) 1 SCC 405, the Supreme Court considered the Commission's cancellation of a poll in the Firozpur parliamentary constituency after violence and irregularities. Justice Krishna Iyer's judgment for the Bench described Article 324 as a plenary provision, a reservoir of power in the Commission adequate to meet contingencies that the enacted election law had not foreseen, exercisable where the statute was silent and always in aid of the free and fair conduct of the election. The holding was not a blank charter: the same judgment insisted that the Commission, like every public authority, must act fairly, hear the affected where hearing was possible, and answer for its actions to the courts after the election process had run. But the direction of the doctrine was unmistakable. The widest electoral power in the Constitution was to be read widely, and its breadth was itself a considered part of the design.

Between 1978 and 1990 the reservoir doctrine sat in the reports largely undisturbed, because the Commissioners of those years did not draw on it for anything larger than the cancellation of individual polls. The Seshan tenure's legal originality, such as it was, consisted in treating Mohinder Singh Gill not as a shield for occasional interventions but as the charter of a standing enforcement practice, and in extending the same full-width reading across the whole of the Commission's jurisdiction: to the calendar, where the power to fix and to postpone followed from superintendence; to the Code, where the direction and control of the election's conduct embraced the conduct of governments during the election period; to the machinery, where the deputation discipline of the election staff gave the reading its hands; and to the roll, where the control of preparation embraced the method by which a voter's identity was established. Each extension was contested, and several were litigated, and the courts of the 1990s, up to and including the Constitution Bench of 1995, sustained the Commission's jurisdiction while cutting down its chief. That pattern of outcomes, jurisdiction expanded and personality checked in the same decade and sometimes in the same judgment, is the legal form of the distinction this article has defended throughout. The law was building an institution. It was only ever, at most, tolerating a man.

## The Man and the Moment: 12 December 1990

Thirunellai Narayana Iyer Seshan was born on 15 May 1933 at Thirunellai village in the Palakkad district of Kerala, took a physics degree from Madras Christian College, and entered the Indian Administrative Service in 1955 in the Tamil Nadu cadre. His service record before 1990 was already a study in institutional friction conducted from inside the machine. As transport commissioner in Madras in the mid-1960s he answered a strike of state bus drivers by driving a bus himself on a city route, producing the heavy vehicle licence that entitled him to do it, and the story followed him for fifty years because it contained his whole method: find the rule, hold the qualification the rule demands, and then act on the rule in public until the other side yields. He rose through the Tamil Nadu administration and the Union government to hold, among other posts, the secretaryships of agriculture and of environment and forests, a seat on the Planning Commission's predecessor arrangements, and finally the office of Cabinet Secretary, to which the government of V.P. Singh appointed him in December 1989. He served as the head of the civil service through the fall of that government in November 1990 and into the first weeks of the Chandra Shekhar ministry.

His appointment to the Election Commission was therefore not the arrival of an outsider. It was the transfer of the republic's senior-most serving bureaucrat, a man who knew exactly which files moved governments and which instructions district officers obeyed, into the one constitutional office whose occupant could not be removed except by the process reserved for a judge of the Supreme Court. Peri Sastri had died in office in November 1990, and V.S. Ramadevi, the Commission's secretary elevated as acting head, held the post for sixteen days, the shortest tenure in the institution's history, until Seshan assumed charge on 12 December 1990. The protection of Article 324, which shields the Chief Election Commissioner from removal except on proved misbehaviour or incapacity after a parliamentary address, had existed since 1950. Seshan was the first occupant of the office to organise his tenure around that protection, treating security of tenure not as a dignity of the post but as the working capital of an enforcement programme. The political moment matched the man. The Chandra Shekhar government that appointed him lived on the sufferance of the Congress party, the Mandal Commission's implementation had set north India alight in the autumn of 1990, and a general election was due within months whether the minority government survived or fell.

## The First Test: The General Election of 1991

The tenth general election reached Seshan five months into his tenure, and it gave him the emergency in which the assertive reading of Article 324 was first performed before a national audience. Polling for the Lok Sabha opened on 20 May 1991. On the night of 21 May 1991, a suicide bomber of the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam assassinated Rajiv Gandhi at Sriperumbudur in Tamil Nadu, with two phases of polling still to run. The Commission postponed the remaining phases, first by days and then, as the security and campaign consequences worked themselves out, into the middle of June 1991. The postponement was the Commission's decision, announced by the Commission, and it stood: no government instructed Seshan to suspend a national election in mid-course, and none dared instruct him to resume it before he judged the field ready. In Punjab, where insurgency had made a mockery of the electoral calendar, the Commission deferred polling altogether, and Punjab voted for the Lok Sabha and its assembly only in February 1992, under a security deployment of a scale that made the act of voting itself a state ceremony.

Within the rounds that did proceed, the Commission behaved as a combatant against malpractice rather than as its registrar. Repolls were ordered in constituency after constituency in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh where booth capturing had been reported, and the count of repolled stations ran into the thousands across the 1991 cycle. Seshan deployed central paramilitary forces at sensitive stations in numbers that state governments protested as an occupation of their own police powers, and he posted observers drawn from the all-India services of other states to watch district administrations whose loyalties he declined to presume. The message to the field machinery was the point of the exercise. A returning officer who connived at capture could expect the Commission itself, rather than the captured poll, to have the last word. When P.V. Narasimha Rao formed his minority government in June 1991 after the Congress emerged as the largest party, he inherited a Commission that had spent the election season demonstrating that the superintendence in Article 324 included the power to say no, in public, to the machinery of the state. The relationship that followed, traced in the account of [the Rao government's institutional-political context](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), was set in that summer.

### What turned the 1991 general election into Seshan's first institutional test?

The assassination of Rajiv Gandhi on 21 May 1991, after the first phase of polling and before the remaining phases, forced the Commission to postpone a national election in mid-course, defer Punjab's polling to February 1992, and order repolls across Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, converting Article 324 from a custody of procedure into an exercised power of decision.

## Component One: The Model Code Becomes a Discipline

The Model Code of Conduct was older than Seshan by three decades and weaker than its text suggested. Its ancestor was a code of conduct adopted for the Kerala assembly election of 1960, and the Commission had circulated consolidated versions from the early 1970s, securing the signatures of the political parties to undertakings that covered the familiar ground: no appeals to caste or communal feeling, no use of places of worship as campaign forums, no bribery or inducement of voters, no government machinery, vehicles, aircraft or rest houses for the campaign, no new schemes, grants or foundation stones after the announcement of elections, and restraint in criticism of private life and unverified allegation. What the Code lacked was a sanction. It bound by consent, and consent, in the heat of a campaign, was withdrawn precisely where the prize justified the breach. Ministers toured in official cars, governments announced sops after the notification of polls, and the Commission's protests travelled by letter and arrived after the advantage had been banked.

Seshan's first component rebuilt the Code as an enforcement architecture. The Commission under his direction asserted that the Code operated from the moment the Commission announced the election schedule at its press conference, not from the later formal notification, which closed the window in which governments had habitually announced schemes in the gap between announcement and notification. It asserted that the Commission would act on breaches itself: by directing the transfer of officers whose conduct compromised neutrality, by ordering repolls where the campaign's corruption had reached the booth, by postponing polling where the conditions of a free contest did not exist, and by public censure issued in the Commission's name and timed for the newspapers. Publicity was a deliberate instrument. The Commission's letters to chief ministers and party presidents were released to the press, and the political class discovered that a rebuke from Nirvachan Sadan carried an audience cost that a private protest never had. Expenditure monitoring acquired teeth through the same route: candidates' accounts were demanded and examined, and the threat of disqualification proceedings for false or unfiled accounts hung over the returns of 1991 and after.

The state elections of November and December 1993, held in Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh in the shadow of the demolition at Ayodhya, were the Code's first full-dress performance under the new architecture. The Commission staggered the polling, flooded the sensitive districts with central forces and observers, and held returning officers to account in a register that district officials across north India remembered for a generation. The enforcement reached upward as deliberately as it reached downward. Union and state ministers found their tours scrutinised for the mixing of official and campaign purposes, and the Commission's insistence that the caretaker conventions of the Code bound the whole government, not merely its election-facing parts, produced a standing quarrel with every ministry in office between 1991 and 1996. The quarrel was the architecture working as designed: the Code's obligations had ceased to depend on the goodwill of the obliged.

### How did Seshan stop ministers from campaigning on official machinery?

The Commission asserted that the Model Code bound the government from the moment the schedule was announced at its press conference, policed the use of official vehicles, aircraft, rest houses and staff for campaigning, directed transfers of officers for partisan conduct, released its rebukes, and treated announcements of schemes after the announcement as breaches to be answered.

The archival trail of this component is unusually rich, because an enforcement architecture conducted through public letters generates its own record. The Commission's correspondence with chief ministers, party presidents and chief secretaries across the 1991 to 1996 cycle, preserved in the Election Commission's archives, documents the sequence in the institution's own hand: the assertion of a rule, the political actor's protest against the rule's application to his case, and the Commission's refusal to bargain the rule away. Researchers working through that correspondence, and through the notifications and orders of the Seshan years that the Commission preserves in its archive, handle a large body of scanned office documents, the kind of material an [office file viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) exists to open, and the letters repay the reading. They show that the Code's transformation was not a matter of a louder voice reading an old text. The mechanisms were new: the announcement-date trigger, the transfer direction, the observer's report treated as actionable evidence, the repoll as a remedy for campaign corruption rather than for polling-day failure alone, and censure deployed as a sanction in itself. A norm that political actors had signed and ignored became, between 1991 and 1996, a discipline that returning officers enforced because the Commission had shown it would enforce the norm against the enforcers first.

## Component Two: The Photograph on the Roll

The second component attacked the document on which every other fraud depended: the electoral roll. A roll that carried dead men, duplicate entries and whole lanes of fictitious names made impersonation a low-risk trade, since the presiding officer at the booth had nothing against which to check the person standing before him except a printed name and the say-so of polling agents who might themselves be party to the arrangement. The Goswami Committee had recommended photo identity cards in May 1990. In 1993 the Commission under Seshan converted the recommendation into a decision of its own: the Electors Photo Identity Card, known by its acronym EPIC, would be issued to every registered elector in the country, and the roll would be rebuilt, through an intensive revision conducted across India in 1993, as a register of photographed persons rather than of unverified names. The architectural meaning of the decision deserves emphasis, because it is the clearest case of the tenure's method. Verification of the voter ceased to be an act of local recognition, embedded in the social knowledge of the village or the ward and open to all the pressures that knowledge carried. It became an act of documentary comparison, portable across every booth in the country and answerable to a card the state itself had issued after a house-to-house enumeration.

The politics of the card were ferocious, and they were fought on the terrain the Commission had chosen. State governments pleaded cost, scale and time. The programme required photographing hundreds of millions of electors, and chief ministers who had no objection to clean rolls in the abstract discovered objections of logistics in the particular. Seshan answered with deadlines. The Commission set dates by which states were to complete card coverage, announced that elections would not be held on rolls the Commission considered unfit, and let it be understood that a state which failed the programme faced the postponement of its polls. The threat was not empty in form, since Article 324's control of the electoral calendar was the one lever no state government could seize, but it collided with arithmetic: coverage lagged badly in the largest states, and as the assembly elections of 1995 approached, including the election in Bihar where Lalu Prasad Yadav's government had fallen furthest behind the deadlines, the Commission had to choose between its own ultimatum and the constitutional necessity of holding elections at all. The compulsion was progressively relaxed, the elections proceeded, and the card became a standing programme rather than a gate that could slam shut. Critics of the tenure cite the retreat as proof that the assertive style wrote cheques the institution could not cash. The architectural reading draws the opposite lesson from the same sequence: the card survived its own deadline. No successor Commission abandoned EPIC, coverage climbed decade by decade until the photographed elector became the default citizen of the roll, and the principle that identity at the booth is verified against a state-issued photograph passed from a Seshan ultimatum into the settled furniture of Indian elections.

### What happened when state governments missed the identity card deadline?

The Commission's deadlines for card coverage slipped in state after state between 1993 and 1995, the threatened postponement of the 1995 elections, Bihar's above all, never came, and the requirement became a standing programme whose coverage grew under every successor Commission until photographic verification became the ordinary test of identity at the booth.

Beneath the quarrel over deadlines sat the quieter labour of the rolls themselves. The intensive revision of 1993 sent enumerators house to house across the country to rebuild the register from the doorstep rather than from the previous list, deleting the dead and the shifted, entering the newly eligible, and tying each entry to the photograph that the card programme required. Revision of that kind is the least visible work a Commission does, and the Seshan years raised its status from clerical routine to constitutional front line. A Commission that controlled the roll controlled the electorate's composition, and every party that had profited from padded registers understood the threat precisely. The roll, like the Code, had been a formality administered by deference. It became, in the second component of the shift, a document the Commission claimed as its own work, verified by its own method, and defended with the one weapon the tenure never hesitated to use, the calendar.

## Component Three: Symbols, Recognition and the Hardened Order

The third component governed the signs under which Indian politics is actually fought. In a mass electorate, the party symbol on the ballot is the party for most practical purposes, and the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order of 1968 made the Commission the registrar, referee and court of that symbolic economy. The Order reserved symbols for recognised national and state parties, allotted free symbols to independents and unrecognised parties, set the conditions of recognition in votes polled and seats won, and empowered the Commission to decide, when a party split, which fragment was the party entitled to the reserved symbol. The adjudicatory power had produced landmark rulings before Seshan, above all the test laid down by the Supreme Court in Sadiq Ali v Election Commission in 1972, which directed the Commission to weigh both the strength of the rival groups in the party's organisation and their numbers in the legislatures. What the Seshan years added was systematisation with an edge. The Commission tightened the working of the Symbols Order in 1993 and after, applying the recognition conditions with a strictness that put parties on notice that reserved symbols were held on performance rather than on inheritance, and handling the decade's profusion of splits, mergers and breakaway formations as questions to be decided on the Order's tests, promptly and in the open, rather than as embarrassments to be managed by delay.

The significance of the component is easily missed because its theatre is technical. A reserved symbol is a party's accumulated capital, recognisable to a voter who knows nothing else on the ballot, and the power to freeze a symbol, to strip recognition, or to award the symbol of a divided party to one claimant is a power over the survival of political organisations themselves. Before 1990 the Commission had exercised that power as a registrar exercises discretion, carefully and late. The Seshan-era Commission treated the Symbols Order as a code to be enforced on schedule, and the parties adjusted their behaviour to the new certainty. Factions contemplating a split had to reckon with a Commission that would count heads in the organisational and legislative wings and publish its reasoning. Recognition reviews ceased to be formalities. The component lacks the drama of the postponed election and the identity card ultimatum, but it belongs in the architectural account for the same reason the roll does: it converted a discretionary custody into a rule-governed jurisdiction, and rule-governed jurisdictions outlast the temperaments of their occupants. The post-1996 Commission inherited the hardened Order along with the enforced Code and the photographed roll, and has applied all three as the ordinary law of its house.

## Component Four: Disqualification Treated as Law in Force

The fourth component concerned the candidates the law had already condemned. Section 8 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 disqualifies a person convicted of specified offences, a schedule running from offences against public order and electoral law to corruption and heinous crime, for six years from release in the graver cases and for the period of the sentence and beyond in others. Alongside the section stand Articles 103 and 192 of the Constitution, under which a question about the disqualification of a sitting member of Parliament or of a state legislature is decided by the President or the Governor in accordance with the opinion of the Election Commission, an opinion the deciding authority is bound to follow. The architecture on paper was complete before 1990. The practice was porous. Convictions were appealed and the appeals slept, disqualification references moved at the pace of the governments that forwarded them, and a political culture had formed in which a convicted candidate's prospects depended on his party's reading of his usefulness rather than on the statute's reading of his sentence.

The Seshan-led Commission pressed the disqualification architecture at every point where the Commission's hand touched it. In its opinions under Articles 103 and 192 the Commission treated the conviction, rather than the political convenience of the reference, as the operative fact, and it refused the leisurely timetable by which references had previously been allowed to outlive the assemblies they concerned. In its public advocacy, conducted through the Commission's reform proposals to the government, it demanded that the disqualification net be widened to reach candidates facing trial on grave charges, a demand the political class received with the enthusiasm it reserved for proposals to disqualify itself. The Vohra Committee, appointed by the Rao government in 1993 to examine the nexus of crime, politics and the bureaucracy, reported in October 1993 that a criminal syndicate was running a parallel state in parts of the country, and the Commission's insistence that convicted and chargesheeted politicians stood inside the election law's jurisdiction drew strength from the same public record. The component produced friction out of proportion to its case list, because it named the point at which electoral reform stops being administrative and becomes existential for a section of the political class. Governments did not enact the widened net. What the Commission could do without legislation, applying the disqualification law in force as law in force, with opinions delivered on the record and on time, it did, and the doing established the expectation that disqualification is a working part of the electoral machinery rather than a decorative clause. The long downstream history of the question, from the affidavit disclosures ordered by the courts in 2002 to the Supreme Court's strike-down of the convicted legislator's appellate shelter in Lily Thomas v Union of India in 2013, runs through terrain the Seshan Commission had already staked out as the Commission's business.

## The Four Components as One Architecture

The four components are usually catalogued as a list of reforms, and the list is accurate as far as it goes. Read as architecture, they are better understood as a single design applied to four surfaces of the same problem. The problem was deference: an institution armed with the widest electoral mandate in the Constitution had habitually asked permission. The Model Code component removed deference from the conduct of campaigns, by making the Commission the enforcing author of rules it had previously only circulated. The identity card component removed deference from the composition of the electorate, by making verification a documentary act of the Commission rather than a social act of the locality. The Symbols Order component removed deference from the organisation of parties, by deciding recognition and splits on published tests within published time. The disqualification component removed deference from the eligibility of candidates, by treating conviction as a fact with consequences rather than as a rumour with a lobby. Each component substituted a rule, a document or a holding for a relationship. That substitution, repeated across the whole surface of an election, is what the phrase assertive-commissioner architecture names, and it is why the tenure's product could be inherited: relationships die with their parties, while rules, documents and holdings are transferred by file.

The map below, labelled here as the Four-Component Assertive-Commissioner Map, sets the design out in the form in which the argument of this article can be cited against it.

| Component | Seshan-era instrument and date | Architectural change | Continuation after 1996 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model Code enforcement | Code applied from schedule announcement, 1991 to 1996 | Voluntary compact becomes enforced discipline with transfers, repolls, postponement and public censure | Enforced by every successor Commission as the standing law of campaigns |
| Photo identity card | EPIC decision and intensive roll revision, 1993 | Roll rebuilt on photographic verification rather than local recognition | Card coverage extended decade by decade until the photographed elector became the default |
| Symbols Order discipline | Tightened application of the 1968 Order from 1993 | Recognition and split disputes decided promptly on published tests | Recognition reviews and symbol adjudication continue as rule-governed jurisdiction |
| Section 8 disqualification | Opinions under Articles 103 and 192 and reform advocacy, 1991 to 1996 | Conviction treated as operative fact rather than negotiable stigma | Disclosure orders of 2002 and the Lily Thomas holding of 2013 extend the same terrain |

A map of this kind also disciplines the argument's critics, because it forces the charisma reading to specify which cell of the table a personality explains. The Commission of 1991 to 1996 unquestionably spoke in one man's voice, and that voice was the loudest instrument the tenure possessed. But the cells of the map are not voices. They are a trigger date for a code, a card in a voter's hand, a test for a symbol, a consequence for a conviction. When the voice fell silent in December 1996, the cells remained filled, and the commissions that followed found the triggers, the cards, the tests and the consequences waiting for them in the institution's ordinary papers. The sections that follow test that claim against the two hardest bodies of evidence: the reconfiguration of 1993, in which the political system struck back at the architecture by rebuilding the architect's office, and the record after 1996, in which the architecture had to survive occupants who had not designed it.

## 1 October 1993: The Government Rebuilds the Commission Around Him

The political system's answer to the assertive commissioner was delivered, with a certain elegance, through the Commission's own membership. On 1 October 1993 the government of P.V. Narasimha Rao promulgated an ordinance that amended the Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Conditions of Service) Act 1991, equalised the status and service conditions of the Chief Election Commissioner and the Election Commissioners, inserted a chapter on the transaction of business that required decisions to be taken by the Commission as a body, unanimously where possible and by majority where the members differed, and supplied the statutory basis on which the President appointed two Election Commissioners the same day: M.S. Gill, a former agriculture secretary of the Punjab cadre, and G.V.G. Krishnamurthy, a former law secretary. Parliament converted the ordinance into law in the winter session that followed. The reconfiguration is sometimes loosely described as a constitutional amendment, and the correction matters enough to state plainly: the Constitution (Sixty-first Amendment) Act was a measure of 1988 that lowered the voting age from twenty-one to eighteen under Article 326, and it had nothing to do with the events of 1993. The three-member Commission of 1993 was built by ordinance and statute under the appointment power of Article 324 itself, which is precisely why its legality could be, and was, fought in court.

The government's public case for the change was stated at its strongest by its defenders and deserves to be taken seriously rather than as a pretext. The Commission's workload had grown beyond the design capacity of a single office; the Goswami Committee had itself recommended a multi-member body in May 1990, before Seshan had conducted a single election; collegial decision-making is the Constitution's own preference for bodies exercising quasi-judicial power; and a Commission that could postpone elections, transfer officers and censure governments arguably required internal checks of the kind a three-member structure supplies. Seshan's reading of the same ordinance was stated at its strongest in his own challenge: the timing, in the middle of his enforcement campaigns and weeks before the five-state elections of November 1993, disclosed the purpose, and the purpose was to dilute a single inconvenient office-holder by outvoting him in his own institution. He declined to assign the new commissioners work of consequence, treated the appointments as an encroachment on the independence Article 324 guaranteed, and carried the ordinance to the Supreme Court. The Court's interim order, passed in the weeks after the appointments, directed that until further orders the Chief Election Commissioner would remain in complete overall control of the Commission's work, might consult his colleagues as he chose, and alone would issue instructions to the Commission's staff and to outside agencies. For twenty-one months the three-member Commission existed in a condition its statute did not describe: two commissioners in office, one commissioner in command, and a Constitution Bench deliberating over which of them the Constitution had meant.

### Can the Chief Election Commissioner be outvoted inside his own Commission?

Yes. The Constitution Bench held on 14 July 1995 that the Chief Election Commissioner is first among equals, with no superior vote and no veto, that the Commission's business is transacted unanimously where possible and by majority where members differ, and that the two Election Commissioners appointed in October 1993 held equal status in its decisions.

The institutional stress of those twenty-one months is the point at which the Seshan story joins the larger frame of [the institution-under-stress framework](/2020/06/03/election-commission-institution-under-stress/), because the stress ran in both directions at once. The Commission was stressing every government in India with its enforcement, and the government of India was stressing the Commission with a reconfiguration whose legality was unresolved while the enforcement continued. The elections of November and December 1993, among the most heavily policed of the decade, were conducted by a Commission whose own composition was sub judice, under an interim order that concentrated in one man the authority that the contested statute had just distributed among three. The episode supplies the charisma reading with its best material, and the architectural reading must absorb it honestly: between October 1993 and July 1995, the assertive Commission and the interim order's one-man control were practically indistinguishable. What separates the readings is what each predicts for the morning after the judgment. If the assertive Commission had been a personality with a court order, the judgment against the personality should have ended it. The judgment came, the personality lost on every substantial point, and the assertive Commission continued for the remaining seventeen months of the tenure and then for decades under other names. The explanation for that continuation lies in the components, which no part of the 1993 reconfiguration had touched: the ordinance changed who decided inside Nirvachan Sadan, and changed nothing about the Code's trigger, the card, the Order's tests or the disqualification practice those decisions administered.

## 14 July 1995: The Supreme Court States the Holding

The Constitution Bench of five judges delivered its judgment in T.N. Seshan, Chief Election Commissioner of India v Union of India on 14 July 1995, reported at (1995) 4 SCC 611, and the holdings can be stated with the precision the Court itself used. The Bench upheld the ordinance and the amending Act as constitutionally valid exercises of the powers conferred by Article 324, which contemplates a Commission consisting of the Chief Election Commissioner and such number of other Election Commissioners as the President may fix, and by the parliamentary power to regulate their conditions of service. It upheld the appointments of Gill and Krishnamurthy. It rejected the argument that the Chief Election Commissioner stood in a position of constitutional superiority over the Election Commissioners in the transaction of the Commission's business. It held that the Chief Election Commissioner is primus inter pares, first among equals: the chairman of the body, entitled to preside and to a position of precedence, but possessed of no casting superiority by which his view could prevail over the united view of his two colleagues. And it held that the statutory scheme of decision, unanimity as the endeavour and majority as the rule on difference, governed the Commission's functioning, so that the interim arrangement of one-man control stood dissolved by the judgment itself.

Seshan accepted the verdict in the only way open to him, which was to continue in office under it for the remainder of his term, and the final phase of the tenure, from July 1995 to December 1996, is the controlled experiment the architectural reading needed. The man was the same man. The voice was the same voice. The statutory ground had shifted beneath him: he could be outvoted, his colleagues held equal status by the authority of a Constitution Bench, and everyone in Indian politics knew it. Had the assertive Commission been the product of the interim order and the personality, the seventeen months after July 1995 should have shown the enforcement slackening into the old deference as governments learned that two votes in Nirvachan Sadan could restrain the third. The record runs the other way. The Commission conducted the general election of April and May 1996, the largest exercise of the tenure, under the full rigour of the Code as the Seshan years had rebuilt it: staggered phases, mass deployment of central forces, observers with reporting lines to the Commission, expenditure scrutiny, and a campaign discipline that the parties contested at the margins and obeyed in the main. The three-member body that the Rao government had built to dilute the assertive commissioner ended by administering his architecture, with M.S. Gill, one of the two commissioners appointed to check him, positioned as his successor and already formed inside the institution the tenure had made. The judgment of 1995 is usually read as Seshan's defeat. In the architectural account it is his vindication's hardest evidence, because it removed every personal prop, the interim order, the claim of superiority, the aura of the irremovable single will, and the architecture stood anyway.

## The Case Against, at Its Strongest

An adjudication that cannot state the losing side at its strongest has not adjudicated anything, and the case against the Seshan tenure, made by politicians, administrators and commentators across the 1990s, is a serious case. Its first strand is constitutional propriety. A Chief Election Commissioner, the critics argued, is an administrator of elections and not a tribune of the people; Seshan's press conferences, his public letters to chief ministers and his cultivation of a personal following converted a referee into a player, and the electorate's applause for the referee's theatre taught a dangerous lesson about where democratic legitimacy resides. The nickname the press fastened on him, Al-Seshan, a play on the gangster Al Capone, carried the compliment and the accusation in a single word: here was a feared enforcer, and fear is not a constitutional method. His reported remark that he had politicians for breakfast, repeated by admiring interviewers throughout the tenure, was quoted by his opponents as a confession that the enforcement was personal relish dressed as duty.

The second strand is institutional. Seshan's treatment of Gill and Krishnamurthy between 1993 and 1995, the refusal of files, of consultation and of any real share in the Commission's work, was defended by his partisans as fidelity to the interim order and condemned by others, including Navin Chawla in his later biography of a successor Commission, as shabby treatment of constitutional colleagues that the Supreme Court's final judgment repudiated in substance. The third strand is efficacy. The identity card ultimatum failed on its own terms and had to be abandoned; the money power in elections grew through the 1990s rather than shrinking; booth capturing declined for many reasons, including the spread of electronic voting experiments and the professionalisation of the paramilitary deployment, for which the tenure claimed sole credit. On this reading, Seshan was a gifted publicist of reforms that committees had recommended and successors would implement, and the cult of the man, sustained by a middle class hungry for a hero against the political class, has distorted the institution's history ever since.

Seshan's own answer was published while he still held office. The Degeneration of India, issued in 1995, set out his philosophy in his own words: a diagnosis of a republic whose institutions had been hollowed by the men who staffed them, and an insistence that the Constitution's provisions were adequate to their purposes if the occupants of office chose to operate them at full strength. The book is a polemic and reads as one, but its central contention is the architectural claim in autobiographical form, that the text of Article 324 had never been the constraint and that the constraint had always been the will of the office-holder. The adjudication between the critics and the book does not require choosing a hero. It requires noticing what each side's evidence actually shows. The critics' strongest exhibits, the failed ultimatum, the treated colleagues, the theatre, are exhibits about conduct. The book's strongest exhibit, the post-1995 record of a Commission enforcing the rebuilt Code under a judgment that had stripped its chief of superiority, is an exhibit about structure. Conduct explains how the tenure felt to live under. Structure explains what the tenure left behind, and it is the leaving behind that an architectural claim is about.

David Gilmartin's essay "One Day's Sultan: T.N. Seshan and Indian Democracy", published in Contributions to Indian Sociology in 2009, remains the deepest scholarly treatment of the tenure precisely because it takes the theatre seriously as evidence rather than as decoration. Gilmartin reads the Seshan years as a public argument about the meaning of popular sovereignty: the election as the one day on which the ordinary voter is sultan, crowned awkwardly in the presence of his usual masters, and the Commission under Seshan as the institution that dramatised that reversal by humbling the masters in public. The Deccan Chronicle cartoon of 1 December 1994 with which his essay opens, showing the dhoti-clad voter processing down a carpet while politicians abase themselves, captures the era's own sense that the flow of power had been theatrically reversed. Gilmartin's reading is neither charisma nor list; it is an account of what the tenure meant in the political culture. The architectural reading defended here is compatible with his at every point except the conclusion it draws about duration. A meaning performed in public can fade with the performer. A trigger date, a photograph, a test and a consequence do not need to be performed to persist, and the quarter-century since 1996 has been long enough to tell the two kinds of legacy apart.

## After 11 December 1996: The Pattern Outlives the Man

Seshan demitted office on 11 December 1996, received the Ramon Magsaysay Award for government service in that year, and in 1997 contested the presidential election against K.R. Narayanan, losing by the margin everyone expected. The succession inside the Commission is the first datum of the continuation. M.S. Gill, appointed in October 1993 as a check on Seshan, served as Chief Election Commissioner from December 1996 to June 2001 and administered the general elections of 1998 and 1999 inside the architecture he had been sent to dilute: the Code enforced from announcement, the photographed roll expanding, the Symbols Order applied as jurisdiction, and, in a development the Seshan years had prepared, the Commission's growing insistence on disclosure and discipline in campaign finance. The elections of 1998 were conducted in [the post-1998 NDA institutional-political context](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/) of a coalition system at its most fragmented, and the Commission's authority over schedule, forces and conduct was not seriously in question in any quarter. A pattern that survives transfer to the very man appointed to restrain its author has passed the strongest test institutional history offers.

The succession after Gill reads, for the argument of this article, as a series of variations played inside a settled form. J.M. Lyngdoh, Chief Election Commissioner from 2001 to 2004, brought a quieter manner and the same architecture to its most dramatic post-1996 test when, after the Gujarat violence of 2002, the Commission refused to be hurried into an early assembly election before the rolls could be revised and the displaced could be re-enrolled, and the Supreme Court's advisory opinion on the presidential reference that followed sustained the Commission's discretion over the calendar. T.S. Krishnamurthy, B.B. Tandon and N. Gopalaswami carried the institution through the full national conversion to electronic voting machines, a transformation of polling technology executed without any retreat from the enforcement posture on conduct, rolls and symbols. The controversies of the Navin Chawla years, including Gopalaswami's 2009 recommendation to the President for Chawla's removal and its rejection by the government of the day, were fought entirely inside the 1993 settlement, over the meaning of the Chief Election Commissioner's recommendatory power under Article 324, and are unintelligible except as disputes within the architecture. S.Y. Quraishi, V.S. Sampath and H.S. Brahma presided over general elections and assembly cycles in which the Code's enforcement, the observers' regime and the expenditure scrutiny were reported by the press as routine features of the season rather than as innovations of an incumbent. Nasim Zaidi's tenure carried the photographed roll toward saturation. Under Sunil Arora the Commission conducted the general election of 2019, and the public argument of that season, including Election Commissioner Ashok Lavasa's recorded dissents from the majority's handling of Model Code complaints, was an argument about the rigour of an enforcement everyone agreed existed. Sushil Chandra and Rajiv Kumar inherited the same furniture. The roster, read whole, makes the point that the brief for this article insists on: the names change, the temperaments range from the theatrical to the taciturn, and the assertive-commissioner pattern persists through all of them, because it was deposited in rules, documents and holdings rather than in a style.

The honest complication must be entered before the verdict, because the pattern's persistence is not uniformity. E. Sridharan's essays on the Commission's post-1996 trajectory document real variation in assertiveness between tenures: some commissions have pressed the Code harder than others, some chief commissioners have courted confrontation and others have avoided it, and the Lavasa dissents of 2019 showed a majority choosing a leniency in Code cases that a Seshan or a Lyngdoh might not have chosen. The complication defeats the architectural reading only if the reading claims that the architecture determines every outcome. It claims something more precise and more defensible. The variation after 1996 occurs inside the architecture, as argument about how its instruments should be used, and never as a return to the world before 1990, in which the Code bound by courtesy, the roll verified by recognition, the symbol disputes slept, and the calendar was settled by consultation with the government of the day. No commission since 1996 has proposed to unbuild the photographed roll, to restore the Code to a voluntary compact, or to surrender the calendar. The floor of the institution's behaviour was raised in six years and has not since been lowered, and a floor, unlike a performance, is exactly the kind of thing an institution can inherit. Read against [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) that organises this series, the Seshan tenure is the moment the referee of the competition among India's political forces stopped being one of the forces' conveniences.

## The Field Machinery: How the Architecture Reached the District

An architecture drawn in New Delhi governs nothing until it changes the incentives of the district officer on polling day, and the Seshan tenure's least theatrical work was done at that depth. The legal instrument was ready to hand. Section 13CC of the Representation of the People Act 1950 provides that the officers and staff employed in connection with the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections are deemed to be on deputation to the Election Commission for the election period and subject to its control, superintendence and discipline. Before 1990 the deputation was largely a formality of drafting: the district magistrate who served as returning officer took his real instructions, and his real career prospects, from the state government of the day. The Seshan Commission activated the clause as a chain of command. During an election period the machinery answered to Nirvachan Sadan; transfers of district magistrates, superintendents of police and returning officers were directed by the Commission over the heads of state governments; and officers against whom observers reported connivance or negligence faced the Commission's displeasure in forms that reached their service records. The transfer power, exercised repeatedly between 1991 and 1996 and protested each time as federal trespass, was the tenure's most resented instrument precisely because it was the one that bit at the level where elections are actually stolen or saved.

The observer system was rebuilt to feed that chain of command with evidence the Commission could act on. Observers drawn from the Indian Administrative Service and the Indian Police Service of other states had accompanied elections before 1990, but the Seshan years multiplied their numbers, sharpened their mandates and, above all, changed the destination of their reports: an observer's adverse finding could trigger a repoll, a transfer or a postponement, and observers learned that the Commission read what they filed. Expenditure observers followed in the same logic, charged with the shadow registers and the scrutiny of candidate accounts. Around the observers stood the deployment of central paramilitary forces, which the Commission demanded in quantities that state governments denounced as extravagant and which altered the physics of the sensitive booth: a station guarded by central forces answerable through the Commission's chain was a station at which the local strongman's arithmetic stopped working. The staggered phasing of elections, extended across the 1991, 1993 and 1996 cycles, belonged to the same design, since phasing is what made the concentration of forces and observers arithmetically possible across a subcontinent. None of these instruments appears in the popular portrait of the tenure, which prefers the press conference to the deployment chart. All of them appear in the institution's inheritance. The observer, the transfer, the phased calendar and the central force at the booth are the ordinary equipment of an Indian election in the decades after 1996, used by commissions that never met Seshan, because the equipment was written into the machinery's standing practice rather than into one man's habits.

## The Money Question and the Architecture's Limits

The component of the electoral problem that resisted the architecture most stubbornly was money, and candour about the failure is part of the adjudication. The Commission's statutory purchase on expenditure ran through the candidate's account: Section 77 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 required candidates to keep and lodge accounts of their election expenses within the ceiling the rules prescribed, and Section 10A empowered the Commission to disqualify a candidate for three years for failing to lodge the account in the required time and manner. The Seshan Commission worked this machinery as it had worked the Code, demanding the accounts, examining them, and treating the filing obligation as a real gate rather than as a ritual of the returning officer's office. What it could not reach by administration was the structure of political finance itself: the ceiling bound the candidate's declared spending while the party's spending stood outside the candidate's account by a statutory explanation added in the 1970s, donors gave in cash below disclosure thresholds, and the true economy of a campaign ran through channels no observer's register could capture. Seshan's Commission said so, in its reform proposals and in its public advocacy, pressing for state funding of elections in kind, for disclosure with teeth and for the disqualification of the chargesheeted, and the political class, which had united across parties against the identity card deadlines and the three-member dilution alike, united once more in letting the money proposals rest in committee.

The limits matter to the argument because they locate it. The architectural shift operated where the Commission's own authority ran: over the calendar, the roll, the code of campaign conduct, the symbols, the eligibility rules in force and the field machinery. Where a problem required legislation, as money did, the Commission could expose, propose and shame, and could not enact, and the exposure without enactment is the standing condition of Indian electoral reform after the Seshan years as much as during them. The Goswami Committee's agenda of 1990 was still being recycled through the Indrajit Gupta Committee on state funding in 1998 and through law commission reports in the decades after, mostly unimplemented. The architecture's defenders do not need to claim that the tenure solved the money question. They need only note that the boundary of its achievement coincides with the boundary of the Commission's lawful powers, which is what distinguishes an institutional shift from a crusade. A crusade fails where its energy ends. The architecture succeeded exactly where its authority ran, and failed only where authority had never been given, and that coincidence of boundary is among the strongest reasons to describe the tenure in architectural terms at all.


## The States Fight Back: Federal Friction as the Proving Ground

The architecture's hardest testing ground was not the Union government, which struck its blow in October 1993 and then litigated, but the states, inside whose territories every election is physically conducted. The constitutional geography of an Indian election is a deliberate provocation: the subject of elections to Parliament and the state legislatures sits with the Union's institutions, while police and public order sit with the states, and the entire field staff, from the district magistrate who returns the result to the constable at the booth door, draws salary and promotion from the state government whose party may be a contestant. The Commission's superintendence has to operate through that staff against, on occasion, the staff's own employers. Before 1990 the contradiction was managed by courtesy. The Seshan Commission managed it by command, and the states' resistance, sustained across the whole tenure and across party lines, is the measure of how much the management had changed.

The quarrels followed a repeating script, and the script is itself evidence of a rule at work. The Commission would direct the transfer of a district magistrate or a superintendent of police in the middle of an election period, on an observer's report or on its own assessment of the officer's neutrality. The state government would protest that law and order was a state subject, that the officer was indispensable, that the Commission was conducting a parallel administration. The Commission would repeat the direction, the officer would move, and the election would proceed under his replacement. Bihar under Lalu Prasad Yadav supplied the longest-running version of the quarrel, compounded by the identity card deadlines the state could not meet and by the Commission's refusal to accept the state's assurances about its rolls. Uttar Pradesh, whose verdicts of 1991 and 1993 were delivered under deployments of central forces that the state's politicians described as an occupation, supplied the loudest. West Bengal under Jyoti Basu, whose Left Front government prided itself on its administrative discipline, objected on principle to the scale of central force requisitioning as an affront to a state's competence, and Tamil Nadu's government complained in its turn when the Commission's hand fell on its officers during the 1996 cycle. The party in office in the state varied from quarrel to quarrel; the Commission's direction did not, and that indifference to the complainant's colour was the tenure's most persuasive credential.

The states' case deserves its strongest statement, because it identifies a genuine constitutional cost that the architectural reading must carry on its books. A Commission that transfers a state's senior police officers during the one season when the state's accountability for order is most exposed does blur the lines of federal responsibility, and a district administration that learns to look to New Delhi for its instructions during elections has absorbed a lesson that does not wholly expire when the election ends. The defenders of the tenure answered, also at full strength, that the alternative on offer in 1991 was not a tidy federalism but a captured one, in which the state's machinery delivered the state's ruling party's verdicts, and that Article 324's placement of superintendence in an all-India body was the framers' own judgment that the conduct of elections could not be left to the governments being elected. The framers' judgment had slept for forty years inside the same text. The states' protests of the 1990s are what it sounded like when the judgment woke, and the fact that no state, for all its protesting, carried the question to a constitutional amendment or refused the Commission's machinery outright suggests that the political system, at the level of its considered interests, preferred a feared referee to the remembered alternatives.

## The Final Examination: The General Election of 1996

Every tenure of an election commissioner is finally marked by one examination paper, and Seshan's was the general election of April and May 1996. The scale had grown beyond anything the founding Commission could have pictured: an electorate of about 592 million, voting in phased rounds across the country, with Jammu and Kashmir returning to a Lok Sabha poll after years in which insurgency had emptied the exercise of meaning. The political field was at its most fractured since 1989, with the Congress in visible decline, the Bharatiya Janata Party rising on the strength of its post-Ayodhya mobilisation, and the regional parties holding the balance in state after state. The Commission that conducted the election was, by the judgment of July 1995, a three-member body in which its chief could be outvoted, and it conducted the election, from the announcement of the schedule to the declaration of results, under the full discipline the tenure had built: the Code applied from the announcement and policed against governments and parties without favour, the photographed roll in use across the country though short of the coverage the Commission had demanded, observers reporting through the Commission's own chain, central forces concentrated by the phasing, and the counting safeguarded by the insistence that ballot papers from a constituency's booths be mixed before the count, so that no village or ward could be identified, and punished, for the way it had voted.

The campaign's visible texture had changed in ways a returning voter could read off the walls. The Commission's enforcement of the defacement laws and its restrictions on posters and wall writing, resisted by every party's publicity machinery and obeyed under protest, kept the public surfaces of the 1996 campaign measurably cleaner than the painted free-for-all of earlier cycles, and the restriction survived the tenure to become a standing feature of the Code's administration, contested in each generation and surrendered in none. Expenditure observers shadowed the money, the announcement of the schedule froze the announcement of schemes across every government in the country at a stroke, and the parties conducted the largest campaign in the republic's history to that date inside a discipline whose authorship was no longer attributed, in the press of the season, to any single man's vigilance. The machinery had begun to receive the credit, which is the precise moment at which an architecture can be told apart from a performance.

The verdict the election returned, a hung Lok Sabha in which the Bharatiya Janata Party emerged as the largest single party with 161 seats, the Congress fell to 140, and a United Front of regional and left parties formed the government under H.D. Deve Gowda after Atal Bihari Vajpayee's thirteen-day ministry resigned before its confidence vote, was a verdict against nearly every incumbent interest in the country. It was accepted everywhere. That acceptance, unremarkable in the retelling, is the datum the architectural reading prizes most in the whole record. Elections in India had been accepted before 1996, of course, and the republic's habit of honouring results was older than Seshan. But the election of 1996 was the first general election conducted entirely inside the rebuilt architecture, by a Commission whose chief had been judicially cut to size the previous summer, across a field in which the losing side included the government that had appointed half the Commission's members. No serious voice alleged that the machinery had been bent. The examination had been set by the decade's politics at its most suspicious, and the institution passed it in a register so matter-of-fact that the passing was barely news. Seshan demitted office seven months later, on 11 December 1996, and the quiet of that transition, a successor already in place, a calendar already fixed, a machinery already drilled, is the sound an architecture makes when it stops depending on its architect.

## Seshan in His Own Voice

A tenure conducted so largely in public generated a spoken and written record from its central figure that historians of most institutions can only envy, and the record matters to the adjudication because it lets the architectural claim be tested against the architect's own account of what he was doing. Seshan's stated philosophy, set out at length in The Degeneration of India in 1995 and repeated in lectures and interviews for the rest of his life, had three working parts. The first was textual confidence: the Constitution and the election statutes, read at their full width, already contained every power an honest election required, and the failure of earlier decades was a failure of reading rather than of drafting. The second was a doctrine of the office: the Chief Election Commissioner held a trust that ran to the Constitution and to the voter, not to the government that had signed his appointment, and the security of his tenure existed so that the trust could be discharged without calculation. The third was a theory of demonstration: a rule enforced once, visibly, against a powerful defaulter, taught the whole field more than a hundred circulars, which is why the tenure's enforcement so often chose the conspicuous case, the transferred officer in the big district, the censure addressed to the senior minister, the poll postponed in the state that had assumed its size exempted it.

Posterity's institutions ratified the performance in the vocabulary available to them. The Ramon Magsaysay Award of 1996, in the citation for his government service, praised the resolute action by which he had brought order, fairness and integrity to elections in India, and the citation's choice of nouns is worth noticing: order, fairness and integrity are properties of a process, not of a personality, and the award the tenure is remembered by honours the state of the elections rather than the style of the man. His detractors' vocabulary ran the other way, to temperament and appetite, and the quarrel between the two vocabularies is the historiographical quarrel this article has been adjudicating throughout. What the man's own record adds to the adjudication is a point in the architecture's favour that neither his admirers nor his critics usually press. Seshan never claimed to have invented his powers. His claim, in the book and out of it, was always the more modest and more radical one that the powers had been lying in the text since 1950, available to any occupant willing to read them at full width and accept the quarrels that followed. A charisma cannot be bequeathed, and a voice cannot be inherited. A reading can. The successors who administered the architecture after 1996 did not imitate Seshan's voice, several of them conspicuously declined to, and they did not need to, because what the tenure had transmitted was not a performance to copy but a construction of Article 324 to operate, a construction the Supreme Court had tested in 1995 and the electorate had tested in 1996, and one that remains, in the hands of commissioners of every temperament, the working constitution of the Indian election.

## The Voter at the Booth: What the Shift Felt Like from Below

The architectural argument has been conducted so far at the altitude of constitutions, commissions and governments, and it needs to land, before the verdict, at the altitude of the queue. For the ordinary elector, the Seshan years changed three experiences. The first was the experience of being counted. The intensive revision of 1993 and the card programme that rode on it meant that the state's knowledge of the voter ceased to be a list compiled by local intermediaries and became a record assembled by enumeration and fixed by a photograph, and the change cut in two directions that both deserve plain statement. For the voter whose name had been struck off by a hostile patwari or never entered by a negligent one, the photographed roll was a form of protection, since an entry with a face attached is harder to delete in silence. For the voter at the margins of documentation, the migrant worker, the pavement dweller, the widow whose papers had never matched her name, the new documentary rigour raised the threshold of citizenship's exercise, and the critics who warned that a verification drive administered in haste would disenfranchise the undocumented poor were making a point that the Commission's own deadline retreats tacitly conceded. The architecture's honest history holds both: the card defended the roll against the fictitious, and its demands fell hardest on the real voters least equipped to satisfy them, a tension the successor commissions inherited and spent decades administering through safeguards, alternative documents and door-to-door verification rounds.

The second experience was the experience of the day itself. The voter who reached a booth in the elections of 1993 or 1996 entered a space that had been rearranged: central forces at the sensitive stations rather than the local constabulary alone, an observer's authority standing behind the presiding officer, a count conducted after the mixing of ballots so that the booth's verdict could not be read back to the village that delivered it. Booth capturing, which in the elections of the 1980s had been a recognised trade with recognised contractors in the worst districts of the north, became a riskier business in exact proportion to those rearrangements, and the decline of the open capture, attested by every observer corps of the decade, belongs to the tenure's credit side whatever share of the credit the phasing and the forces must take. The third experience was subtler and Gilmartin's essay names it best: the experience of being courted by the humbled. A Code enforced against ministers in mid-campaign altered the theatre of the election season, since the candidate who could no longer arrive on the state's helicopter, announce the district's new scheme from the official dais or count on the district magistrate's complaisance had to address the elector as a supplicant earlier and more often. The sultan-for-a-day reversal was never more than a day, and the morning after the count the hierarchies of the village and the ministry resumed their places. But a democracy is partly made of the days it sets aside for reversal, and the tenure's work at the altitude of the queue was to make the reversal harder to counterfeit: the counted name harder to invent, the captured booth harder to hold, the official advantage harder to spend. The architectural claim, translated downward, is simply that these hardenings were deposited in procedure, where they survived, rather than in the vigilance of one commissioner, where they would not have.

## The Hardest Polls: Bihar 1995 and Kashmir 1996

Two elections at the tenure's end test the architecture at its extremes, one against the administrative collapse of a great state and the other against an insurgency's veto. The Bihar assembly election fell due in early 1995, in the state where the Commission's quarrels with the government of Lalu Prasad Yadav had run longest, where the identity card programme had fallen furthest behind its deadlines, and where the booth-capturing economy the tenure existed to break had its deepest roots. The Commission postponed the polling from its first schedule into a phased calendar across March and April 1995, citing the deployment of forces and the state of preparation, and held the election under a weight of central paramilitary presence and observer scrutiny that the state's politicians denounced as a tutelage and the Commission defended as the minimum condition of a poll worth the name. The verdict, a renewed majority for Lalu Prasad Yadav's Janata Dal, was the answer to the denunciation that mattered most: a machinery accused of partisan tutelage had delivered the accuser's own re-election, and the result was accepted by the losers without a serious challenge to the conduct of the count. The episode fixed the mature form of the tenure's federal doctrine. The Commission would set the calendar against a state government's wishes, flood the state's districts with forces the state had not requested, and then hand the state, impartially, to whichever of its politicians the voters chose, including the chief minister who had fought the Commission at every step.

The Jammu and Kashmir assembly election of September and October 1996 was the harder examination, because the obstacle was not a government's obstruction but a population's fear and an armed movement's threat. The state had not elected an assembly since 1987, in a poll whose widely disbelieved conduct had fed the insurgency that followed, and Parliament had governed the state through President's rule across the years of the militancy's peak. The Commission under Seshan, in the last months of his tenure, fixed the election in phases across September and October 1996, preceded by the Lok Sabha polling in the state that spring, and conducted it under security arrangements of an extraordinary density, with the rolls revised, the displaced Kashmiri Pandit electorate provided postal and camp arrangements, and the count held under the same discipline of mixing and supervision applied in the plains. Turnout varied sharply by district, and no honest account calls the conditions normal. What the Commission claimed, and what the acceptance of the result by the National Conference government of Farooq Abdullah that took office in October 1996 bore out, was narrower and more durable: an election had been held at all, on a calendar the Commission set, by procedures the Commission defended in public, in a state where the gun had claimed a veto over the ballot for nine years. For the architectural argument the two polls bookend the tenure's meaning. Bihar showed the architecture prevailing over a hostile elected government and surviving its own victory's impartiality. Kashmir showed it prevailing over the absence of government altogether. Institutions are what remain when both the obstruction of friends and the violence of enemies have been priced in, and by December 1996 the Commission's rebuilt practice had been priced in by both.

## The Public as a Constituency: Press, Polls and the Household Name

No account of the tenure's mechanics is complete without the resource its critics most resented and its defenders most relied on: the Commission acquired, for the first time in its history, a public. Before 1990 the institution had been a connoisseur's subject, known to election lawyers, returning officers and the readers of its annual reports. The Seshan years made it a household presence, through a combination the tenure cultivated deliberately. The press conference replaced the press release as the Commission's native instrument, and a chief commissioner who answered questions at length, in quotable sentences, supplied the newspapers with a running story that needed no investigation to print. The cartoonists took him up, which in the India of R.K. Laxman and his successors is the certificate of arrival: the Deccan Chronicle cartoon of 1 December 1994 that opens Gilmartin's essay is one of hundreds in which the Commission's chief figures as the one official before whom the political class stands with folded hands. Opinion surveys of the mid-1990s repeatedly placed Seshan among the most admired public figures in the country, and the admiration had a precise social address. It was the salaried, newspaper-reading, queue-standing middle class, the class that experienced elections as voters rather than as brokers, that recognised its own grievances in the Commission's targets: the minister's cavalcade, the captured booth in the ancestral village, the name missing from the roll.

The political significance of the public constituency is that it changed the cost accounting of defiance. A government that quarrelled with the pre-1990 Commission quarrelled with a file. A government that quarrelled with the Seshan Commission quarrelled on the front pages, against an official whose approval ratings its own strategists could read, and the Rao government's preference for the indirect remedy of October 1993, diluting the office rather than confronting its occupant, is unintelligible without that arithmetic. The charisma reading treats the publicity as the tenure itself, and the architectural reading must again absorb the strongest part of the rival case: the public was attached to the man, not to the institution, and it dispersed when he left, leaving his successors to operate the architecture in the obscurity from which he had rescued it. The absorption, though, runs in the architecture's favour on the longer view. The public's attention was the scaffolding, necessary while the structure was being altered in the open and removable when the alteration was complete. What the attention built, the triggers, the card, the tests, the consequences, the drilled machinery, did not require an audience to keep standing, and the proof is that they have stood through decades of the institution's restored obscurity. Institutions that need a hero in every generation are not institutions but cults with intervals. The Commission after 1996 needed no hero, because the hero's generation had been spent, in part, on making heroism unnecessary.

## The House Behind the Commissioner: Secretariat, Money and Unfinished Independence

Behind the assertive commissioner stood a house whose own unfinished state marks the boundary of what the tenure could rebuild by will alone. The Election Commission's permanent secretariat at Nirvachan Sadan in New Delhi was, at the start of the 1990s, a modest establishment staffed substantially by officers on deputation from the government the Commission was meant to hold at arm's length, and its budget travelled through the ordinary demands of the ministry that administered the election law. The Commission's reform proposals of the period, renewed from its own earlier submissions, asked for the two instruments by which the Constitution protects the independence of its other referees: an independent secretariat of the kind the Lok Sabha and the higher judiciary possess, recruited and controlled by the Commission itself, and the charging of the Commission's expenditure on the Consolidated Fund of India, so that the money for elections would not depend on the annual goodwill of a finance ministry answerable to a contesting government. Neither demand was granted in the tenure, and neither has been granted in full since, which is why the Seshan years read, in the institution's internal history, as a demonstration of how far practice can outrun structure. The field machinery the Commission commanded during elections it borrowed under the deputation clauses and returned when the polls closed. The secretariat that drafted its orders served on sufferance. The assertive architecture was raised, in other words, inside a building whose title deeds the Commission did not hold, and its survival owes as much to the conventions the tenure hardened, the expectation that governments do not starve or staff-capture the referee, as to any parchment guarantee. The unfinished items on that ledger, the secretariat, the charged budget, and the method of appointing commissioners, which the Goswami Committee had wanted placed in collegial hands and which remained the President's act on the government's advice throughout the tenure, constitute the reform agenda the next generation inherited alongside the architecture. An inheritance with items outstanding is still an inheritance, and the adjudication that follows weighs the tenure on what it transferred, not on what it merely requested.

## Adjudication: Architecture Against Charisma and the List

The three readings of the tenure can be judged on the evidence assembled. The reforms-list reading is true and insufficient: the Code's enforcement, the identity card, the Symbols Order's discipline and the disqualification practice all happened, and a chronicle of them describes the tenure without explaining its consequence, since lists do not distinguish the reforms that died with their author from the reforms that became the institution's permanent equipment. The charisma reading explains the tenure's atmosphere and fails its sequel: it cannot account for the Commission of 1995 and 1996 enforcing the rebuilt order under a judgment that had dismantled the chief's superiority, for Gill administering the architecture he was appointed to dilute, for Lyngdoh's calendar stand of 2002, or for the plain fact that the Code, the card, the tests and the consequences are all still in force under commissioners whose names the public barely knows. The architectural reading accounts for the atmosphere as the instrument by which the architecture was installed, for the backlash of 1993 as the political system's testimony to the architecture's reality, and for the continuation after 1996 as the architecture's ordinary operation. Its claim is therefore adopted here in the terms stated at the outset. Seshan's reforms operationalised an architectural shift from procedural deference to assertive-commissioner positioning, and the trajectory since 1996 operates through the shift. The sultan of Gilmartin's title reigned, as sultans of a day do, briefly. The palace he rearranged is still standing, and every election in India is still conducted in its rooms. A reader who wishes to test the claim against the primary record need not take this article's word for any of it: the notifications and orders of the Seshan years rest in the Commission's archive, the Constitution Bench's holdings of 1991 and 1995 rest in the law reports, the Goswami Committee's buried agenda of May 1990 rests in the parliamentary record, and the succession of commissions that administered the inheritance rests in the results of every general election from 1998 onward, each accepted, each conducted inside the rooms. The evidence was never hidden. It was only, for forty years, unread at full width, and the tenure that taught the republic to read it is the tenure whose consequences have not yet finished unfolding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was T.N. Seshan?

Thirunellai Narayana Iyer Seshan, born on 15 May 1933 at Thirunellai in the Palakkad district of Kerala, was a Tamil Nadu cadre officer of the Indian Administrative Service from the 1955 batch who rose to become Cabinet Secretary of India in December 1989 before his appointment as the tenth Chief Election Commissioner. His tenure of 1990 to 1996 rebuilt the Election Commission's enforcement of the Model Code, launched the photo identity card, hardened the Symbols Order and applied disqualification law with new rigour, making him the most consequential occupant of the office since its founding. He received the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 1996 and died on 10 November 2019.

### Q: When was Seshan the CEC?

T.N. Seshan served as Chief Election Commissioner from 12 December 1990 to 11 December 1996, a full six-year term that ranks among the longest in the institution's history. He succeeded V.S. Ramadevi, who had held the office for sixteen days after the death of R.V.S. Peri Sastri, and he was himself succeeded by M.S. Gill, one of the two Election Commissioners appointed in October 1993. His tenure covered the general elections of 1991 and 1996, the five-state assembly elections of November and December 1993, the assembly elections of 1994 and 1995, and the long litigation over the three-member Commission that the Supreme Court settled in July 1995.

### Q: What is the Model Code of Conduct?

The Model Code of Conduct is the set of norms, first developed for the Kerala election of 1960 and later adopted by the Election Commission for all elections, that governs the behaviour of political parties, candidates and governments during an election period. It bars appeals to caste and communal sentiment, the use of places of worship for campaigning, bribery and inducement of voters, the use of official machinery and vehicles for the campaign, and the announcement of new schemes or grants by governments once elections are declared. It carries no statutory penalty of its own, which is why its history divides into a period of voluntary compliance before 1991 and the enforcement architecture built under Seshan.

### Q: What is the three-member commission?

The three-member Commission is the composition the Election Commission of India acquired on 1 October 1993, when the President appointed M.S. Gill and G.V.G. Krishnamurthy as Election Commissioners alongside Chief Election Commissioner T.N. Seshan, under an ordinance that amended the service conditions Act of 1991 and required the Commission's business to be transacted by the body acting unanimously or by majority. Before that date the Commission had functioned, apart from a brief episode in 1989 and 1990, as a single-member body headed by the Chief Election Commissioner alone. The composition has remained three-member ever since, and the Supreme Court upheld its legality in July 1995.

### Q: Why was Seshan controversial?

Seshan was controversial because he enforced rules that the political class had been content to leave unenforced, and he did so in public. Parties of every colour resented the Model Code applied from the announcement of the schedule, the transfer of their favoured officers, the postponement of polls in disturbed regions, the deadlines for photo identity cards that state governments could not meet, and the Commission's insistence that disqualification law applied to convicted politicians. His abrasive manner, his press conferences and his reported remark that he had politicians for breakfast deepened the resentment, and the Rao government's decision of October 1993 to surround him with two commissioners of equal status was the controversy's institutional peak.

### Q: What is the photo-identity-card requirement?

The photo-identity-card requirement is the Election Commission's decision of 1993 that every registered elector be issued an Electors Photo Identity Card, known as EPIC, carrying the elector's photograph, so that identity at the polling station could be verified against a state-issued document rather than by local recognition. The decision accompanied an intensive revision of the electoral rolls in 1993 and aimed at the bogus voting and impersonation that padded rolls had made easy. State governments resisted the Commission's deadlines and the threatened postponement of elections was not carried out, but the card became a standing programme, and photographic verification grew into the ordinary test of identity at Indian polling stations.

### Q: How did Seshan change the ECI?

Seshan changed the Election Commission by converting the powers of Article 324 from a procedural trust into an enforced jurisdiction. He applied the Model Code from the moment the schedule was announced and backed it with transfers, repolls, postponements and public censure; he rebuilt the electoral roll around the photo identity card decided in 1993; he applied the Symbols Order's recognition and split tests as a rule-governed jurisdiction; and he treated disqualification under the election law as an operative consequence of conviction. After the Supreme Court settled the three-member question in 1995, the rebuilt practices continued under his successors, which is why the tenure is best read as an architectural shift rather than a personal episode.

### Q: What did Seshan do before he became Chief Election Commissioner?

Before December 1990 Seshan spent thirty-five years inside the administration he later confronted. He served in the Tamil Nadu cadre from 1955, where as transport commissioner in Madras in the mid-1960s he famously drove a state bus himself during a drivers' strike, producing the heavy vehicle licence that entitled him to drive it. He went on to hold Union secretaryships including agriculture and environment and forests, and in December 1989 the government of V.P. Singh appointed him Cabinet Secretary, the head of the Indian civil service. He served through that government's fall and into the Chandra Shekhar ministry before moving to the Election Commission.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court hold in T.N. Seshan v. Union of India in 1995?

In its judgment of 14 July 1995, reported at (1995) 4 SCC 611, a five-judge Constitution Bench upheld the ordinance and Act that had created the three-member Commission and confirmed the appointments of M.S. Gill and G.V.G. Krishnamurthy as valid. The Bench rejected Seshan's claim of constitutional superiority over the Election Commissioners, held that the Chief Election Commissioner is first among equals, a chairman with precedence but no power to override his colleagues, and ruled that the Commission's business must be transacted unanimously where possible and by majority where the members differ. The interim order that had left Seshan in sole control stood dissolved by the judgment.

### Q: Why did the Rao government appoint two Election Commissioners in October 1993?

The government of P.V. Narasimha Rao gave public reasons that had independent support: the Commission's workload had outgrown a single office, the Goswami Committee had recommended a multi-member body in May 1990, and collegial decision-making suits a body wielding quasi-judicial power. The timing, however, was widely read as the operative motive. The appointments of 1 October 1993 came in the middle of Seshan's enforcement campaigns and weeks before the five-state elections, and the ordinance that accompanied them equalised the new commissioners' status and imposed majority decision-making, diluting the authority of a Chief Election Commissioner whose assertiveness the government had experienced at close quarters for three years.

### Q: Who were M.S. Gill and G.V.G. Krishnamurthy?

M.S. Gill and G.V.G. Krishnamurthy were the two Election Commissioners appointed on 1 October 1993 under the ordinance that converted the Election Commission into a three-member body. Gill, a Punjab cadre civil servant who had served as Union agriculture secretary, worked under Seshan's shadow through the litigation that followed and then succeeded him as Chief Election Commissioner in December 1996, serving until June 2001 and conducting the general elections of 1998 and 1999. Krishnamurthy, a former Union law secretary, served as Election Commissioner until 1999. Their appointments, intended by the Rao government to check Seshan, instead inducted both men into the assertive institution his tenure had built.

### Q: What happened to the Punjab election in 1991?

Punjab did not vote with the rest of India in the general election of May and June 1991. The state was in the grip of a separatist insurgency, its assembly stood dissolved, and the Commission judged that conditions for a credible poll did not exist, deferring the Punjab elections while polling proceeded elsewhere. Punjab finally voted for the Lok Sabha and for its state assembly in February 1992, under an extraordinary security deployment, and the election returned an Akali Dal boycott-marred verdict with a very low turnout before a Congress government under Beant Singh took office. The deferral was an early demonstration that the Commission under Seshan would treat the calendar itself as an instrument of electoral integrity.

### Q: What is the Election Symbols Order and what does it decide?

The Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order 1968 is the Election Commission's own code for the signs under which Indian parties fight elections. It reserves exclusive symbols for recognised national and state parties, allots free symbols to other candidates, fixes the vote and seat conditions a party must meet to win or retain recognition, and empowers the Commission to decide which faction of a split party is entitled to the reserved symbol, applying the test the Supreme Court laid down in Sadiq Ali in 1972. Because a symbol is a party's recognisable capital before a mass electorate, the Order makes the Commission the registrar and referee of party identity itself.

### Q: What does Section 8 of the Representation of the People Act 1951 disqualify?

Section 8 disqualifies a person from voting and from membership of Parliament and the state legislatures upon conviction for a schedule of offences that includes offences against public order, electoral offences such as bribery and undue influence, corruption, and specified grave crimes. The disqualification runs for six years from conviction in some categories, and in others for the period of imprisonment and a further six years from release. The section works alongside Articles 103 and 192 of the Constitution, under which questions about a sitting member's disqualification are decided by the President or the Governor in accordance with the Election Commission's binding opinion, a jurisdiction the Seshan Commission exercised on the record and on time.

### Q: What did Seshan argue in The Degeneration of India?

The Degeneration of India, published in 1995 while Seshan still held office, is his own statement of the philosophy behind his tenure. Its diagnosis is that India's institutions had not failed for want of constitutional design but had been hollowed out by the occupants who declined to operate the design at full strength, and that corruption and criminality in public life advanced wherever office-holders treated their powers as negotiable. Applied to his own office, the argument held that Article 324 had always contained the authority his Commission exercised, and that the missing element before 1990 had been the will of the office-holder rather than the text of the Constitution.

### Q: What did David Gilmartin argue in One Day's Sultan?

David Gilmartin, in "One Day's Sultan: T.N. Seshan and Indian Democracy", published in Contributions to Indian Sociology in 2009, reads the Seshan tenure as a public argument about popular sovereignty rather than as a story of one official's temperament. His title takes the popular image of the voter as sultan for a day, sovereign at the moment of voting in the presence of politicians who ordinarily command him. Gilmartin argues that Seshan's enforcement, and the furious public debate it generated, dramatised that reversal and forced India to argue openly about what elections mean, about the tension between law and democracy in defining the people's voice.

### Q: How did election observers work during the Seshan years?

Observers are senior officers of the all-India services deputed from states other than the one going to polls, appointed by the Commission to watch the conduct of the election in assigned constituencies and districts and to report directly to the Commission. The Seshan years multiplied their deployment, sharpened their mandates and, decisively, made their reports actionable: an observer's adverse finding could ground a repoll, the transfer of a local officer or the postponement of polling, and expenditure observers were added to shadow the money side of the campaign. The system converted the Commission's distant superintendence into eyes on the ground whose testimony carried institutional consequences.

### Q: What was the Dhanoa case of 1991 about?

The Dhanoa case arose from the first attempt to make the Election Commission multi-member. In October 1989 the outgoing Rajiv Gandhi government appointed S.S. Dhanoa and V.S. Seigell as Election Commissioners; the incoming V.P. Singh government amended the service rules and the two stood removed on 1 January 1990. Dhanoa challenged the removal, and the Supreme Court held in S.S. Dhanoa v Union of India, reported at (1991) 3 SCC 567, that the appointments and their termination were both lawful, since the President could fix the number of commissioners and their conditions of service under the constitutional scheme. The case established, before Seshan's tenure began, that the Commission's size was a lever governments could pull.

### Q: Did the assertive pattern survive Seshan's retirement in 1996?

It did, and the survival is the central evidence for reading the tenure architecturally. M.S. Gill administered the elections of 1998 and 1999 inside the rebuilt framework, J.M. Lyngdoh used the Commission's control of the calendar to refuse a hurried Gujarat election in 2002 and was sustained on the presidential reference that followed, and their successors through S.Y. Quraishi, Sunil Arora, Sushil Chandra and Rajiv Kumar enforced the Model Code, extended the photographed roll and applied the Symbols Order as standing practice. Assertiveness has varied by tenure, as E. Sridharan's essays document, but no Commission has returned to the deference that preceded 1990.

### Q: What was the Dinesh Goswami Committee on electoral reform?

The Goswami Committee was the committee on electoral reforms appointed in January 1990 by the V.P. Singh government under its Law Minister, Dinesh Goswami, drawing members from across the political parties. Its report of May 1990 recommended, among a wide agenda, that the Election Commission be converted into a multi-member body, that photo identity cards be issued to all electors, and that a range of changes be made to disqualification, election expenditure and the conduct of polls. The report was not implemented by the governments that received it, but its two most famous recommendations were carried into practice within three years, one by Seshan's Commission and the other against him.
