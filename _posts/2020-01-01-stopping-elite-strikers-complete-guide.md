Stopping elite strikers is a classification problem before it is a defending problem, and almost every plan that fails on a Saturday failed on the Thursday it was written, when somebody in the meeting said the words "keep him quiet" and everybody nodded. That instruction contains no line height, no defender profile, no screening reference, no goalkeeper start position, and no decision about what the team is willing to give up. It is a wish. The forward on the other side has spent a decade building a specific way of hurting a back four, and he does not get worse because eleven people agreed in advance to concentrate.

The useful question is narrower and much harder: which of this forward's inputs can we actually remove, and what does removing it cost us somewhere else on the pitch? Every plan against a top centre-forward is a trade. A team that pushes its line to twenty-five yards from its own goal to deny a depth runner the grass behind is choosing to defend a large space with two centre-backs and a goalkeeper. A team that drops to the edge of its own box to deny that same space is choosing to defend fourteen crosses and a rebound economy. Neither choice is wrong. Making the choice by accident, in the ninth minute, because the first ball over the top frightened somebody, is wrong every time.

![Stopping elite strikers, a complete defensive guide to forward threat types and counter-plans - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-14.webp)

This guide gives you a taxonomy and a template set. It argues that elite forwards divide into four threat types, that each type is beaten by a different combination of line height, defender profile, second-defender behaviour, midfield screen, and goalkeeper positioning, and that applying the template for one type against another type does not merely fail, it actively improves the forward you were trying to reduce. Six heavily studied modern forwards are used as worked examples throughout: Erling Haaland, Kylian Mbappe, Harry Kane, Robert Lewandowski, Victor Osimhen, and Julian Alvarez. Each of them has his own full counter blueprint elsewhere in this series, and this page routes you to the right one rather than repeating it.

## What Stopping Elite Strikers Actually Means

A goal by a centre-forward is the last event in a chain, and the chain is short. Somebody supplied a ball into an area from which scoring is realistic. The forward had enough space, in the right place, at the right moment, to make contact on his terms. Or the first two conditions failed and a rebound, a deflection, a scramble, or a dead ball handed him a second attempt at close range. Supply, space, second chances. That is the whole list. There are no goals from anywhere else.

This matters because it converts a vague defensive objective into three concrete ones, and because it exposes immediately why the vague version keeps failing. A back line that concentrates hard on the forward has addressed exactly none of the three. It has not reduced the number of entries into the final third, it has not decided where the space he wants will be, and it has not organized who arrives second when the goalkeeper parries. Concentration is a prerequisite, not a plan.

### What is a striker plan actually trying to reduce?

A plan against a forward is not trying to produce a clean sheet, which is a result rather than an objective. It is trying to reduce the number and quality of the chances he personally reaches, by removing one or two of his three inputs, and to do so without opening a larger hole for the players around him.

That distinction between the objective and the result is the beginning of professional thinking about this problem. A team can execute an excellent plan and concede, because a forward at this level converts the two moments he gets. A team can execute a poor plan and keep a clean sheet because the goalkeeper had an outstanding evening and one shot hit the post. Judging the plan by the scoreline teaches the coaching staff nothing and frequently teaches them the opposite of the truth. Judge the plan by the count and location of the touches he took inside the box, the number of times he received facing goal between the lines, the number of first contacts he won in the channel, and the number of second-ball situations he was allowed to attack. Those numbers move when the plan is good, and they move whether or not the ball went in.

### The supply, space, second rule

Here is the governing principle of this entire guide, stated as plainly as it can be stated. A defending team cannot remove all three inputs at once. The shapes that kill supply concede space, and the shapes that kill space concede second balls. This is not a coaching preference or a stylistic argument; it is a structural fact about where eleven players can stand. Call it the supply, space, second rule: a real plan names the two inputs it denies and manages the third on purpose, rather than discovering in the second half which one it accidentally surrendered.

Work through why the three cannot all be closed. To cut supply you have to defend forward, which means pressing the players who deliver into the box, occupying passing lanes high up the pitch, and committing bodies away from your own penalty area. The moment you do that, the distance between your last defender and your goalkeeper becomes the largest single area on the pitch, and it sits directly behind the players you have just committed. You have removed supply and you have created space.

To cut space, invert everything. Drop the line, compress the area in front of your goalkeeper to something a forward cannot run into, and defend a box that is fifteen yards deep. Now there is no grass behind, and the forward's runs take him into the arms of a centre-back who is already goalside with cover. But you have surrendered the entire pitch in front of you. The opposition can deliver from anywhere at any tempo, unpressured, forty times, and each of those deliveries generates blocks, headers, parries, and rebounds inside a crowded area. You have removed space and you have created second chances.

To cut second chances you need bodies inside the box, disciplined marking on rebounds, a goalkeeper who catches rather than parries into danger, and defenders who clear away from the middle rather than into it. That is a full-time job for four or five players. Those four or five players are not pressing the supply, and they are not high enough to compress the space. You have removed second chances and you have conceded the other two.

The rule is not a counsel of despair. It is what makes planning possible. Once a coaching staff accepts that one input is going to be conceded, they can choose which one, choose it based on the specific forward in front of them and the specific players in their own squad, rehearse the concession so it is defended rather than merely suffered, and stop the mid-match panic that turns a considered plan into an improvised one. A team that has decided in advance that it will concede crosses to a forward who is poor in the air is calm when crosses arrive. A team that has not decided anything drops ten yards after the first cross, concedes the second input as well, and loses.

The second half of the rule is the harder half: manage the conceded input on purpose. Conceding supply does not mean allowing supply from anywhere. It means allowing supply from the areas where this forward is weakest, which for most centre-forwards means from wide and deep rather than from the half-space at the byline, and from the side of his weaker foot rather than his stronger one. Conceding space does not mean allowing space anywhere. It means allowing it in the wide channel with a full-back and a covering centre-back arriving, rather than centrally with the goalkeeper exposed. Conceding second balls does not mean abandoning the box. It means accepting that rebounds will occur and deciding, in advance, who occupies the two zones where they most often land, which are the top of the six-yard box and the arc twelve to eighteen yards out.

## The Dimension That Separates Elite Forwards

Fans separate strikers by quality, and quality is real, but it is not the dimension that determines the plan. Two forwards of identical quality can require opposite defensive templates, and two forwards of very different quality can require the same one. What determines the plan is where a forward creates his value, which is a question about geography and timing rather than about how good he is.

Think of the pitch as three zones relative to your last defender. There is the space behind that line, which only exists if the line is high or if it can be turned. There is the box itself, the fourteen yards where finishing happens and where the forward's job is occupation rather than travel. And there is the area in front of the line, between the defensive line and the midfield, where a forward who receives facing his own goal can turn the whole structure around by playing a single pass. A fourth dimension cuts across all three: the contested first contact, in the channel or in the air, where the value is created by winning a physical duel that was not going to be won by positioning alone.

Every elite centre-forward has a primary home among those four, and the honest ones will tell you so. Almost all of them can operate in a second, and the very best operate credibly in three. But the primary is what the plan must address, because the primary is what he does when the match is even and nobody has been forced into anything. Under fatigue, under pressure, and in the moments that decide matches, forwards revert to their primary. The classification is therefore not an insult to a complete player's range. It is a statement about where his value concentrates when everything is normal.

### How do you classify a forward from ten minutes of video?

Watch only his movements without the ball and ignore the goals entirely. Count where he starts his runs, whether they travel away from the ball or toward it, whether he arrives at the near post or holds the penalty spot, and whether he seeks contact or avoids it. The pattern that repeats five times in ten minutes is his type.

The reason to ignore goals is that goals are the output of the chain, not the mechanism of it, and a highlight reel systematically misleads a coach. Every forward in world football looks like a finisher in a compilation, because the compilation is made of finishes. What the compilation removes is the seventy off-ball actions that produced each one, and those actions are the only part a defensive plan can interact with. You cannot defend a finish. You can defend the run, the reception, the contact, and the arrival.

Watch a phase in which his team builds slowly against a set defense, and watch a phase in which they win the ball in their own half. Those two phases separate the types more cleanly than anything else. The depth runner is invisible in slow build-up and lethal in transition. The box occupier is invisible until the ball reaches the wide areas and then becomes the entire problem. The drop-and-link forward is busiest in slow build-up, because that is when he comes to find the ball. The duel forward looks the same in both phases, because his contribution is contact and contact is available everywhere.

## The Four Threat Types

### Type one: the depth runner

The depth runner creates value in the space behind the last defender, and everything else in his game exists to make that space available or to punish it once it appears. His mechanism has four component actions, and a defender who understands all four defends him differently from a defender who has only been told that he is fast.

The first component is the start position. A depth runner does not stand on the shoulder of a centre-back for ninety minutes, because a defender who can see him has already solved half the problem. He drifts a yard or two into the blind side, usually toward the far centre-back rather than the near one, so that the defender tracking him has to choose between watching the ball and watching him. That choice is the entire duel. The moment the centre-back's head turns to the ball, the forward's start position is behind his eye line, and the two yards he steals in that instant are worth more than any burst of pace later.

The second component is the double movement. The elite depth runner rarely runs straight from a standing start, because a straight run from a standing start is easy to track and easy to time. He steps toward the ball first, drawing the defender forward half a yard, then turns and runs. That half yard is the whole margin. A defender who has stepped forward has his weight on the wrong foot and his hips square to the wrong direction, and a forward with genuine acceleration converts that into three or four yards within fifteen metres.

The third component is the run's angle. Straight runs down the middle are the easiest to defend, because they keep the forward in front of the goalkeeper and inside the covering defender's shadow. Elite depth runners bend their runs across the last defender, starting from a wide-ish position and finishing centrally, or starting central and peeling into the channel. The bend does two things: it takes the forward across the defender's body, forcing a turn that costs a full stride, and it puts the ball on the diagonal where it can be played into space rather than at a player.

The fourth component is what happens on arrival. A forward who arrives behind the line with the goalkeeper coming has approximately one and a half seconds, and the difference between a good depth runner and a great one is entirely in that window. The great ones take the first touch away from the goalkeeper's near side and across their own body, which opens the whole goal, or they take no touch at all and finish first time. This is why the goalkeeper's start position, discussed at length below, is a plan-level decision rather than an individual one.

The structural weakness of the depth runner is that his threat is conditional on a space existing. Remove the space and you have not merely reduced him, you have removed the mechanism entirely. That is why a depth runner is, counterintuitively, the most solvable of the four types for a well-drilled defensive side, and why the very best depth runners spend years adding a second string to their game. The cost of removing the space is enormous, and it is paid in a different currency, which is the subject of the template section below.

### Type two: the box occupier

The box occupier creates value inside twelve yards of goal, in a space so small that running into it is not the point. Occupation is the point. His mechanism is not travel but timing, position, and the ability to generate a shot from a body position that other forwards need a touch to reach.

The first component is the starting relationship with the near-post defender. Box occupiers live on the shoulder of the second centre-back or on the blind side of a full-back who has tucked in, and their movement is measured in yards, not tens of yards. Three yards to the near post takes a defender's eyes off the ball. Three yards back toward the penalty spot creates the separation for a header. The whole duel happens inside a rectangle the size of a small living room.

The second component is the timing of the final movement relative to the delivery. Box occupiers move as the crosser's hips open, not when the ball is struck, and certainly not before. Move early and the marker recovers. Move late and the ball is past you. The elite ones read the delivery from the crosser's body, which is why they are so much more dangerous against a team that allows unpressured crossers: a crosser under no pressure telegraphs less and delivers more accurately, and both help the forward.

The third component is contact technique in a crowd. This is the least visible and the most decisive part. A box occupier generates power and direction from a standing jump with a defender attached, from a half-turn on a ball at knee height, from a toe-poke in a scramble, from a redirected header across the goalkeeper rather than back at him. He does not need clean possession. He needs contact, and he needs the contact to be aimed.

The fourth component is the rebound instinct. Box occupiers are the type most likely to score the second-chance goal, because they are already standing in the second-chance zones when the first attempt is blocked or parried. This is why the second-ball input, which sounds like a footnote in most defensive discussions, is a headline consideration against this type specifically.

The structural weakness of the box occupier is that he cannot manufacture his own service. He is dependent on the ball arriving in a narrow band of areas at a usable height and pace, and a team that removes the crossing platform removes him. Deny the byline, deny the cutback, force the delivery from deep and from wide angles, and a box occupier can go eighty minutes without a meaningful action. That is a genuinely achievable objective, and it is the reason this type rewards a specific kind of hard, unglamorous work from full-backs and wide midfielders rather than from centre-backs.

### Type three: the drop-and-link forward

The drop-and-link forward creates value between the lines, and the value is frequently in the pass he plays rather than the one he receives. He is the type most consistently mishandled by defensive plans, because the instinctive response to a forward is to defend him goalside, and defending this forward goalside hands him exactly what he wants.

The first component is the drop itself, and specifically its timing. He does not drop when his team is under pressure or when the ball is going backward. He drops on the second pass of a settled possession, at the moment the opposition's midfield line has committed a body forward, into the seam between that line and the defensive line. The seam is not a fixed place. It moves with the ball, and the elite versions of this type find it repeatedly because they are reading the opposition's midfield shape rather than looking for a spot on the grass.

The second component is the reception body shape. A forward receiving with his back to goal has done nothing yet. A forward receiving on the half-turn, with his hips open to the far side of the pitch and his first touch already taking the ball away from the pressure, has turned a defensive structure that was facing the right way into one that is facing the wrong way. Watch the shoulders in the two seconds before he receives; the elite ones check across both shoulders and set their hips before the ball arrives, so the touch is a continuation of a decision already made.

The third component is the pass. This is the part that separates a good link forward from a genuinely destructive one. Having turned, he plays the pass that the defensive line cannot see: the ball in behind for a runner, the diagonal to a winger attacking a full-back who has stepped up, the reverse pass into the half-space vacated by the centre-back who came to meet him. The forward has now created a chance without ever entering the box, and the defensive plan that was built around denying him space in the box has been irrelevant for the whole sequence.

The fourth component is the late arrival. Having dragged a centre-back out and released the ball wide, he does not stand and watch. He turns and attacks the box he just vacated, arriving from deep at the edge of the area where nobody is marking, because his marker followed him out and then had to make a decision about following him back.

The structural weakness of the drop-and-link forward is that his effectiveness collapses when he is denied the turn. Everything downstream depends on him receiving and facing forward. A defensive structure that lets him receive but never lets him turn has removed the mechanism, and it has done so without dropping deep and without man-marking him thirty yards from goal. The way to achieve that involves the screening midfielder rather than the centre-back, which is why this type is the strongest argument in football for taking a holding midfielder's positional discipline seriously.

### Type four: the duel forward

The duel forward creates value by winning contested first contacts, in the air and in the channels, and by converting the chaos that follows into a chance for himself or for somebody else. He is the type most often described in physical language and least often analyzed properly.

The first component is the attack on the ball rather than the space. Where the depth runner runs to arrive before the ball, the duel forward runs to arrive at the same time as the ball and at the same time as the defender, because contact is the point. He wants the aerial duel, the shoulder-to-shoulder in the channel, the ball into his chest with a centre-back behind him.

The second component is body positioning in the moment before contact. He gets his arm across, gets his body between defender and ball, and creates the half-yard by feel rather than by looking. A defender who tries to win these duels by strength alone loses to a forward who wins them by timing and position, which is why physical size is a poor predictor of who wins them.

The third component is the second action. Winning the first contact is worth nothing on its own; a flick-on that goes nowhere is a turnover. The elite duel forward wins the contact and then attacks the space it created, or plays the knockdown deliberately into the zone a runner is entering. His teammates rehearse this, which is why the duel forward's team looks disorganized and is not.

The fourth component is the psychological pressure of repeated contact. Facing this type is physically expensive across ninety minutes. Centre-backs who are winning duels comfortably in the first half start losing them in the last twenty minutes, not because the forward improved but because the defender's legs and concentration decayed. Substitution planning against this type is therefore a real tactical decision rather than a courtesy.

The structural weakness of the duel forward is that a duel he does not contest is a duel he cannot win. Deny the long ball, deny the channel ball, control the second phase, and he spends the match chasing shadows in areas where the game is not happening. That requires a team comfortable in possession or comfortable defending high, and it is the reason this type is far more dangerous against a side that goes long under pressure than against a side that plays out.

## The Striker Threat-Type Matrix

The matrix below is the operational core of this guide. It crosses the four threat types with the six decisions a defensive plan actually has to make. Read it as a starting template, not as a script: the personnel available modify every row, and the final column is the one most coaches skip and most plans fail on.

| Threat type | Line height | Primary defender profile | Second-defender behaviour | Midfield screen job | Goalkeeper start | Input conceded on purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Depth runner | Low to mid, last line no higher than thirty yards from own goal, compress behind | Recovery pace first, aggression second; comfortable defending facing his own goal | Cover behind and central, never flat alongside; stays goalside of the forward at all times | Screen the through-ball lane, deny the forward pass that starts the run, not the ball carrier | High, ten to fourteen yards off the line, sweeping actively behind | Supply from wide and deep, crosses and long shots |
| Box occupier | Mid to high, defend the crossing platform not the box | Aerial dominance and body positioning; a marker who is comfortable being touched-tight | Front-post attacker or zonal anchor depending on scheme; never both jobs on one player | Protect the cutback lane and the top of the box; occupy the rebound arc | Middle of the six-yard box, commanding, defending the cross line | Space in front of the block, entries into wide areas |
| Drop-and-link forward | High to mid, never deep; the block must not invite him into the seam | Front-foot centre-back who steps in early, reads the pass, and is comfortable defending in midfield | Slides across immediately to cover the vacated central lane, back three logic even from a four | Man-oriented on the forward's receiving zone; deny the turn, not the reception | Higher than normal, ready for the ball played over the stepping centre-back | Second-chance situations and set pieces from the pressure this creates |
| Duel forward | High, deny the long ball and the channel entry at source | Strong, front-foot defender who competes early and does not let him settle | Attacks the knockdown zone, not the forward; the second ball is his responsibility | Occupy the flick-on landing area; win the second phase | Standard to slightly high, dominant on anything into the box | Space behind if the first contact is lost, and direct supply into wide channels |

Two features of this matrix deserve emphasis before you use it. First, the line-height column and the goalkeeper column are locked together and can never be planned separately. A high line with a goalkeeper who stays on his line does not exist as a defensive structure; it is a very long invitation. A low line with a goalkeeper fourteen yards off it is a chipped goal waiting to be scored. The distance between the last defender and the goalkeeper is a single variable that both must know, and it should be spoken aloud in the meeting as a number in yards.

Second, the final column is not a disclaimer. It is an instruction. Every row says what the plan gives up, and the giving up has to be rehearsed. If your plan against a depth runner concedes crosses, then your week includes crossing defense, your near-post marking is drilled, your goalkeeper's cross-claiming is discussed, and your full-backs know they are defending the delivery rather than the byline. If the concession appears in the plan but never appears in the training week, you have not conceded it deliberately. You have just written down where you are going to lose.

## The Six Forwards, Read Through the Matrix

Each of the six forwards below is treated at hub depth: enough to classify him, enough to see which template applies, and enough to understand why the classification is not obvious. The full plan for each one lives in his own article, and this page deliberately does not duplicate it.

### Erling Haaland: a depth runner who became a box occupier as well

Haaland is a left-footed Norwegian centre-forward who came through Bryne and Molde before Salzburg, Borussia Dortmund, and Manchester City, and the arc of that journey is visible in his game. The early version was almost purely a depth runner: enormous stride length, a start position on the blind shoulder, and a repeated attack on the space behind a high line. The later version added something that made him substantially harder to plan against, which is genuine penalty-box occupation. He now generates a large share of his output from inside twelve yards, from cutbacks and low crosses, without needing the space behind to exist at all.

That combination is what makes him a two-template problem. A team that plans only for the depth runner drops its line, kills the space behind, and then spends the match defending crosses against a forward who is excellent at attacking crosses. A team that plans only for the box occupier defends the crossing platform, holds a higher line to squeeze the wide areas, and gets run through by the first diagonal ball. The resolution is not to split the difference, which produces a mid-block that does neither job properly, but to choose the concession explicitly based on the wide players you have and the state of the match. The detail of that decision, the defender profiles that fit each version, and the sequenced plan belong to the full breakdown of [how a defensive plan is built against Haaland specifically](/2020/01/15/how-to-stop-erling-haaland/), which is the canonical owner of that query.

For classification purposes the point is this: his left foot is the finishing foot and the near-post attack is the recurring pattern, so the geometry of the plan is not symmetrical. Forcing a delivery to one side of the box is a different proposition from forcing it to the other, and a plan that treats the box as symmetrical against an asymmetrical finisher has thrown away a free advantage.

### Kylian Mbappe: a depth runner whose starting position moves the whole problem wide

Mbappe is a right-footed French forward developed at Monaco before Paris Saint-Germain and Real Madrid, who has played extensively from the left and through the middle, and whose defining physical quality is acceleration over the first fifteen metres rather than top speed over sixty. That distinction is the single most useful thing a defender can know about him, and it is almost always stated wrongly. A forward whose weapon is top-end speed can be handled by starting deep enough that the race never begins. A forward whose weapon is acceleration beats you in the first three strides, from a standing start, in a space the size of a penalty area, and starting deeper does not solve it.

His classification is depth runner, but with a critical modifier: the depth he attacks is frequently the space behind a full-back rather than behind a centre-back, because his start position from the left turns the duel into a one-versus-one in the wide channel with the whole pitch to run into. That changes the primary defender profile from centre-back to full-back, changes the second-defender question from cover to double-up, and changes the screening job from protecting a central lane to protecting a wide one.

Against the central version he presents a more familiar depth-running problem, and against the wide version he presents an isolation problem, which is a genuinely different article. The comparison of the two profiles, and the verdict on which of the two most-discussed modern forwards actually sets the harder defensive question, is resolved in the dedicated head-to-head on [whether Haaland or Mbappe is harder to defend](/2020/05/15/haaland-vs-mbappe-harder-to-defend/), and the full sequenced plan sits in the counter blueprint on [how teams have structured a defense against Mbappe](/2020/02/15/how-to-stop-kylian-mbappe/).

### Harry Kane: the drop-and-link forward, and the type most plans get wrong

Kane is a right-footed English centre-forward formed at Tottenham before Bayern Munich, whose game combines finishing with a passing range from deep positions that very few forwards in the history of the position have possessed. He is the cleanest available example of the drop-and-link type, and he is the reason that type gets its own template rather than being folded into a general category.

The mechanism is the one described above, executed at an unusual level: he drops into the seam between the opposition's midfield and defensive lines, receives on the half-turn, and plays a forward pass that a midfielder in that position would be praised for. The defensive problem this creates is a genuine dilemma rather than a difficulty. Follow him and your defensive line has a hole in it, into which a winger or a midfielder runs. Do not follow him and he turns unopposed with the pitch in front of him. Both options are bad; the plan has to decide which is less bad against the specific runners in the opposition's team, and then commit.

The commonly attempted third option, which is to drop the whole line so that the seam closes, is the single worst response available and it deserves to be named as such. Dropping deep does not close the seam; it relocates the seam to the edge of your own penalty area, where a forward turning with the ball is thirty yards closer to your goal and where the pass he plays arrives in a finishing position rather than a build-up one. Against this type, depth is the enemy. The correct answer involves a front-foot centre-back who steps in early and a screening midfielder who denies the turn, and the sequenced version of that plan is set out in the counter blueprint on [how a team is organized to stop Kane receiving and turning](/2020/03/15/how-to-stop-harry-kane/).

### Robert Lewandowski: the box occupier as a complete technical proposition

Lewandowski is a right-footed Polish striker with a long career arc through Lech Poznan, Borussia Dortmund, Bayern Munich, and Barcelona, and he is the reference example for the box occupier type. What makes him instructive is that his box work is not primarily physical. It is technical and geometric: a set of small movements that arrive at the right yard at the right instant, and a contact technique that generates an accurate shot from body positions where most forwards need a touch to set themselves.

Watch him against a deep block and the pattern repeats. He starts between the two centre-backs, drifts to the blind side of the far one, and then makes a three-yard movement in the last half-second of the delivery, either across the near-post defender or checking back to the penalty spot as the cross clears the first man. The finish is frequently one touch, frequently on the half-volley, frequently with a defender in contact. None of that requires space in the conventional sense; it requires the ball to arrive.

That is why the template against this type is a crossing-platform template rather than a box template, and why the work is done by full-backs and wide midfielders forty yards from goal rather than by centre-backs in the six-yard box. The full breakdown of the marking scheme, the near-post responsibilities, the rebound zones, and the concession that comes with all of it belongs to the article on [how the delivery platform is closed against Lewandowski](/2020/04/01/how-to-stop-robert-lewandowski/).

### Victor Osimhen: the duel forward with a depth-running second gear

Osimhen is a Nigerian forward developed through Charleroi and Lille before Napoli, who pairs pace in the channels with a genuine aerial threat, and he sits primarily in the duel forward category with a substantial depth-running component. The aerial threat is not the standing header of a traditional target man; it is a running, attacking jump, frequently at the ball rather than at a position, and frequently in the channel rather than in the middle.

The classification consequence is that the two obvious counters cancel each other. A high line denies the channel run and hands him the long ball into a duel he wants. A deep line kills the run and invites the cross into the aerial contest he also wants. The template that actually reduces him works on the supply side rather than the duel side: deny the long diagonal at the source, force the opposition to build through midfield where the duel is not available, and make the first contact happen forty yards from goal rather than twenty. The sequencing of that plan and the defender profiles it requires sit in [the counter blueprint built around Osimhen's first-contact threat](/2020/04/15/how-to-stop-victor-osimhen/).

### Julian Alvarez: the hybrid, and why hybrids are planned for differently

Alvarez is an Argentine forward and a World Cup winner with Argentina who came through River Plate before Manchester City and Atletico Madrid, and who has operated both as a lone nine and as a second striker. He is the example that proves the taxonomy has to be applied to a role rather than to a person, because his threat type genuinely changes with the role he is given.

As a lone nine in a possession side he behaves closest to the drop-and-link type with a heavy pressing contribution, coming toward the ball, linking, and arriving late into the box. As a second striker alongside a target he becomes a depth runner, attacking the space that the first forward's duel creates, and the plan against him has to change accordingly. Against a hybrid, the classification exercise is done twice, and the plan carries a trigger: if he starts on the shoulder, template one applies; if he starts between the lines, template three applies; and if the opposition change his role at half time, the trigger has already been discussed and the response does not require a new meeting. The detail of that conditional plan is set out in [the counter blueprint that handles Alvarez in both of his roles](/2020/05/01/how-to-stop-julian-alvarez/).

### Why the mirror image matters

Coaches who spend a season studying how to reduce forwards eventually discover that the same map read backward is a coaching manual for their own attackers. Every conceded input in the matrix above is an invitation to a forward who knows how to accept it, and the forward's version of this guide, which treats the centre-back as the subject and asks where his structure breaks, is the companion piece on [attacking elite centre-backs from the forward's side of the duel](/2023/10/01/attacking-elite-centre-backs-guide/). Reading both is how a coach stops seeing defending and attacking as separate subjects.

## The Four Templates, Phase by Phase

The matrix gives the shape of each plan. This section gives the plan itself, narrated in sequence, because a defensive plan is a series of decisions in time rather than a diagram.

### Template one: defending the depth runner

The plan in one sentence: compress the space behind by defending in a mid-to-low block with an actively sweeping goalkeeper, and accept crosses and long shots as the price.

Phase one is the setting of the reference. The last line does not defend a fixed line on the grass; it defends a distance from its own goal, and that distance is roughly thirty yards when the ball is in the opposition half and considerably less when the ball reaches the final third. The number matters more than the principle, because "drop off a bit" produces four defenders at four different depths and a forward who only needs one of them to be a yard high. Give the back line one reference point, usually the near centre-back, and require the other three to relate to him rather than to the ball.

Phase two is the denial of the pass rather than the run. This is the part most teams get wrong, and it is the reason so many well-drilled back lines still get run through. A depth runner cannot be stopped by defenders once the ball is travelling; the physics do not allow it. He is stopped by preventing the ball from being played, which is a midfield job. The screening midfielder's reference is not the ball carrier and not the forward, but the lane between them. He positions so that the direct forward pass requires a lofted, slower, more readable delivery, and he accepts that the sideways pass is available. A holding midfielder who spends the match pressing the ball carrier has abandoned his actual function.

Phase three is the individual duel when the pass does arrive. The primary defender's profile is recovery pace before aggression, because against this type a defender who steps and misses has ended the sequence badly. His body shape at the moment of the pass should already be half-turned toward his own goal, hips open to the side the ball is coming from, so that his first movement is a run rather than a turn. The single most common technical error here is a defender standing square, watching the pass, and then having to turn a hundred and eighty degrees while a forward with a running start goes past him.

Phase four is the second defender. He is behind and inside, never flat, and his job is not to double the forward but to be the reason the forward's first touch has to be a controlled one rather than a shooting one. A covering defender in the right position converts a clear run on goal into a run into a funnel, and the funnel points away from the middle of the pitch.

Phase five is the goalkeeper. Against this type the goalkeeper is a defender, and his start position should be ten to fourteen yards from his line when the ball is in the middle third. That is not a preference; it is what makes the whole structure work, because it converts every ball played into the channel behind into a race the goalkeeper can win. Goalkeepers who are uncomfortable with this should be given a modified plan rather than an instruction they will not execute, and the modification is a deeper line, which costs more elsewhere.

Phase six is the concession, managed. Crosses will come, and they will come from unpressured wide players, so the near-post marking and the rebound zones need to be organized before the match rather than discovered during it. Long shots will come, so the block's second line has to close the shooting lanes from twenty to twenty-five yards rather than dropping to the edge of the six-yard box and watching.

The fallback, when the first plan is beaten twice, is not to drop deeper. Dropping deeper against a depth runner who has already got behind you means you are now defending the same runs with less distance to recover into and a goalkeeper who can no longer sweep. The correct fallback is to add a body to the screening job, usually by shifting from two central midfielders to three, which reduces the number of forward passes attempted rather than the space they are played into.

### Template two: defending the box occupier

The plan in one sentence: destroy the delivery platform in the wide areas and defend the two rebound zones religiously, accepting that the opposition will have the ball in front of you all evening.

Phase one is the wide defensive assignment, which is where this plan is won and lost. The objective is not to stop the winger; it is to stop the delivery from the two areas that produce quality service, which are the byline cutback zone and the half-space pocket between full-back and centre-back. Everything else is a manageable cross. That means the full-back defends the inside shoulder and shows the winger down the line, the wide midfielder tracks back to double rather than to press, and the near centre-back's first job is to prevent the ball from reaching the cutback zone at all.

Phase two is the marking scheme inside the box, and the choice between man, zonal, and hybrid should be made on the basis of the players available rather than on ideology. What matters far more than the scheme is that every player knows which of two jobs he has: attacking the ball or attacking the space. Players who are trying to do both do neither. Against a box occupier of this quality, the recommended hybrid is a zonal core of three across the six-yard box with a man-marker on the forward whose only job is to be touch-tight and to occupy his run, plus a front-post attacker whose only job is the flick-on.

Phase three is the timing rule for the marker. The marker's reference is the forward's hips and not the ball, because this type initiates his final movement from a static position and the first indication is a hip rotation. A marker watching the ball is always half a second late, and half a second is the whole duel. He should be physically in contact, side-on, with an arm across, from the moment the ball reaches the wide area.

Phase four is the rebound economy, and this is the phase that separates teams who defend crosses well from teams who defend crosses and then concede anyway. Two zones matter: the area immediately in front of the goalkeeper at the top of the six-yard box, and the arc twelve to eighteen yards out where blocked shots and cleared headers land. Assign both explicitly, usually to the screening midfielder for the arc and to a centre-back or full-back for the near zone, and require clearances to go wide rather than central. A cleared header that lands on the penalty spot has not been cleared.

Phase five is the goalkeeper's role, which against this type is a cross-claiming role rather than a sweeping one. His start position is central in the six-yard box and slightly toward the near post on wide deliveries, and the plan should state clearly whether he is a keeper who comes for crosses or one who holds his line, because the defenders in front of him need to know which. A goalkeeper who sometimes comes is worse than either alternative.

Phase six is the concession, managed. This plan hands the opposition possession and territory in front of the block, so the concession is space in the middle third and shooting from distance. Manage it by keeping the block's second line high enough to contest shots from twenty yards, and by accepting that the opposition's centre-backs will have the ball. They are welcome to it.

The fallback, when the crosses are arriving anyway, is to add a body to the wide area rather than to the box. Six players in the box against a good crosser is not safer than five; it is more congested, and congestion favors the attacking side because the attacker knows where he is going and the defenders are reacting.

### Template three: defending the drop-and-link forward

The plan in one sentence: let him receive, never let him turn, and defend the seam with a midfielder rather than with a centre-back, accepting the set-piece and second-chance cost that the resulting pressure creates.

Phase one is the recognition of the receiving zone. Before the match, identify the two or three yards of pitch where this forward habitually receives, which is almost always the seam between the opposition's own midfield and the defensive line, offset toward one side. That zone is the assignment. It is not the player, and the distinction is important: assigning a man to follow him everywhere produces a defender in odd positions all over the pitch, whereas assigning a man to own the zone produces a structure that holds.

Phase two is the screening midfielder's positioning and it is the heart of this plan. He plays goalside of the receiving zone, not touch-tight but within a stride, with his body angled so that he can see both the ball and the forward's shoulders. When the pass is played into the forward, he steps into the forward's back at the moment of the touch. Not before, which is a foul or a bypass, and not after, which is too late. The objective is not to win the ball. It is to make the turn impossible, so the forward's only option is to lay the ball back the way it came, at which point the entire sequence has cost the attacking side two passes and gained them nothing.

Phase three is the centre-back's decision, which follows from phase two rather than preceding it. Because the midfielder owns the seam, the centre-back is not obliged to follow the forward out of the line, and that is the point of the whole design. He steps only when the midfielder is beaten or absent, and when he steps he steps early and aggressively, arriving as the ball arrives rather than two seconds afterward. A centre-back who steps late has produced the worst of both worlds: he has left the line and he has not prevented the turn.

Phase four is the covering slide. The instant the centre-back does step, the remaining defenders shift into a three-across-the-back relationship, with the far centre-back taking the central lane and the near full-back tucking in. This has to be automatic, because the pass that punishes a stepping centre-back is played within a second of him stepping. If your back line needs to be told, it is already too late.

Phase five is the line height, which is high to mid and never deep, for the reason given in the section on Kane above. A deep line against this type relocates the seam into your penalty area. If you are losing the match and instinct says drop, resist it; the correct response is to add a second screening body, not to add depth.

Phase six is the concession, managed. This plan is aggressive and produces fouls in dangerous areas, stepping defenders who occasionally get caught, and a higher volume of set pieces against. Rehearse the set-piece defense properly, because that is the price of this plan and it is a price worth paying against this type.

The fallback, when he is turning anyway, is a temporary man-marking assignment on the forward by the screening midfielder for a fifteen-minute period, accepting the shape distortion, usually while the team is protecting a lead and can afford a more conservative midfield.

### Template four: defending the duel forward

The plan in one sentence: attack the supply so the duel never happens, compete for the first contact early rather than deep, and own the second ball, accepting that the space behind is exposed when the first contact is lost.

Phase one is the pressure on the source. Long balls and channel diagonals require a player with time and a clear head, so the objective is to make every long delivery a hurried one. Against this type the press does not need to win the ball; it needs to make the delivery inaccurate. An inaccurate long ball is a duel the forward starts from a bad position, and the whole plan is a series of those.

Phase two is the first-contact assignment. The primary defender competes early, meeting the ball at the highest point he can reach, and he goes for the ball rather than for the man. Passive defending against this type is punished immediately, because a forward allowed to control the ball with his back to goal and a defender behind him has won the phase before it started.

Phase three, and this is the phase teams neglect, is the second ball. The first contact against an elite duel forward is genuinely a coin flip, and a plan that assumes the defender wins it is not a plan. Assign the flick-on landing area, which is typically ten to fifteen yards behind and to the side of the duel, to a specific midfielder, and require the far-side midfielder to compress toward the duel rather than holding width. Teams that win second balls consistently look like they are defending a different match from the one their opponents are playing.

Phase four is the channel-run coverage. This type attacks the channel between full-back and centre-back with a running duel rather than a pure sprint, so the full-back's job is to stay tight to his inside shoulder and force the run outside, and the covering centre-back's job is to arrive at the same time. A duel forward who is forced wide and met by two defenders has been reduced to a crosser.

Phase five is the fatigue plan. Against this type, the defenders' physical decay is a tactical variable rather than a medical one. Plan the substitutions around it, consider a fresh central defender or a fresh screening midfielder in the last twenty-five minutes, and be aware that the last quarter of the match is where this type's output concentrates.

Phase six is the concession, managed. This plan is played high and it concedes the space behind when the first contact is lost, so the goalkeeper is sweeping and the covering defender is genuinely deep. That is the trade, and it is the right trade against a forward whose primary value is contact rather than pure running.

The fallback, when the first contacts are being lost repeatedly, is to change the defender rather than the plan, because this is the one type where the individual matchup dominates the structural one.

### Which input should a plan concede on purpose?

Concede the input the specific forward is worst at converting, and the one your own personnel are best equipped to defend. Against a depth runner with a modest aerial record, concede crosses. Against a box occupier with no acceleration, concede the space in front. Name it in the meeting, rehearse it in the week, and the concession stops being a weakness.

## The Principles That Transfer Across All Four Types

Four templates is four things to remember, which is three too many for a player under pressure in the seventy-eighth minute. Underneath them sit six principles that apply regardless of type, and a team that has genuinely installed these six will defend a forward it has never seen better than a team that memorized a template for the one it prepared for.

### First-defender body orientation

The most consequential technical detail in defending forwards is the angle of the first defender's hips at the moment the pass is played, and it is barely coached at amateur level. Square hips mean the defender can move in any direction slowly. Open hips, angled toward the side the ball is coming from and half-turned toward his own goal, mean he can move in one direction very quickly and in the other direction adequately. Almost every duel against a forward is decided by which of those two states the defender was in half a second before the ball arrived.

The rule that makes this teachable is that the defender's orientation is set by where the danger is, not by where the ball is. Against a depth runner the danger is behind, so the hips open toward his own goal. Against a drop-and-link forward the danger is the turn, so the hips are square and the weight is forward, ready to step. Against a box occupier the danger is the near-post movement, so the body is side-on with contact established. Against a duel forward the danger is the first contact, so the body is front-foot and the defender is already moving toward the flight of the ball. Same principle, four applications.

### The distance between the last line and the goalkeeper

This is the single number that a defensive plan most often leaves undefined, and leaving it undefined is how teams end up with a high line and a goalkeeper on his six-yard line, which is not a structure but a gap. Decide the number, say it out loud, and let both the back line and the goalkeeper repeat it back. Twelve yards is a common working figure for a team defending a high line with an active goalkeeper; six or seven for a deeper block with a keeper who stays.

What makes it a shared variable rather than two separate ones is that the space between them is nobody's by default and therefore everybody's by accident. A ball played into that space is defended by whoever decides to defend it, and if two players decide simultaneously or neither does, the outcome is the same. The distance should also change with the ball's position: it shrinks when the opposition's carrier is under pressure and cannot play the pass, and it grows when he has time, because time means a chip or a threaded ball is coming.

### The screening midfielder's reference point

A holding midfielder in front of a back four has three possible references, and only one of them is right. He can reference the ball, in which case he presses and leaves the space behind him. He can reference the opposition's most advanced midfielder, in which case he man-marks and gets dragged around. Or he can reference the lane between the ball and the most dangerous receiver, which is the correct answer against every forward type in this guide.

Practically, that means his position is determined by drawing a line from the ball to the forward's feet and standing on it, at a depth that lets him also step into a receiver in front. He is not trying to intercept, though interceptions happen. He is trying to make the forward pass slow, lofted, or impossible, which is a much lower bar and a much more reliable one. The failure mode to watch for is ball-watching, which is what happens when a screening midfielder loses his reference and starts responding to whatever looks urgent.

### Delay before commitment

Every one of the four templates contains a moment where a defender is tempted to commit, and in almost every case delay is worth more than the tackle. A forward with the ball and his back to goal, twenty-five yards out, is not dangerous. He becomes dangerous when a defender commits and is beaten. The instruction is to show, delay, and force the play sideways or backward until support arrives, and to make the tackle only when the ball is loose or the touch is heavy.

This is harder to coach than it sounds, because a delayed defender looks passive and a committed one looks brave, and the crowd, the bench, and the player's own instinct all reward the wrong thing. The way to install it is to define the trigger for committing rather than the prohibition on it: commit when the forward's touch takes the ball more than a stride from his foot, when he has been forced onto his weaker side, or when a second defender is within two yards. Otherwise, delay.

### Clearance direction and the second-chance economy

Every clearance is either the end of an attack or the start of the next one, and the difference is direction. A ball cleared into the central arc twelve to eighteen yards out is returned with interest against every one of these forward types, because that is where the box occupier is standing and where the drop-and-link forward has arrived late. A ball cleared wide and high buys three seconds and forces a rebuild.

Install two habits. Defenders clear away from the middle, toward the touchline, and upward rather than flat. And a designated player occupies the central arc when the ball enters the box, whose entire job is to be standing where the rebounds land. That player is usually the screening midfielder, and against the box occupier type he is more valuable there than anywhere else on the pitch.

### Communication that carries information

Defensive talking is mostly noise, and noise is not communication. "Man on" tells a player nothing he does not already suspect. Useful defensive communication contains a direction, a number, or a decision: "he is behind you left", "step, I have cover", "he is dropping, I have him", "keeper's ball". Against a forward whose movement is designed to break the visual link between two defenders, the voice is the only thing that maintains it, which is why the sequences these forwards score from so often begin with one defender who could see the run and did not say anything.

## Why the Wrong Template Makes a Forward Better

The strongest claim in this guide is that mismatching the template is worse than having no template at all, and it deserves to be defended rather than asserted.

Take the most common error, which is applying the depth-runner template to a drop-and-link forward. The depth-runner template drops the line. Dropping the line against a forward whose value is created between the lines does three things simultaneously, all of them helpful to him. It moves the seam he receives in closer to your goal, so the pass he plays after turning is a final pass rather than a build-up pass. It removes the space behind, which he did not want, in exchange for space in front of your defenders, which he did. And it hands his teammates the entire middle third, so the number of times he receives goes up. You have not reduced his output; you have changed the location of his contributions and increased their value.

Take the reverse error, which is applying a high, aggressive template to a pure depth runner because somebody in the meeting said the best form of defense is to press. Now the space behind is at its maximum, the forward's start position is on the shoulder of a defender who is thirty-five yards from his own goal, and every turnover in midfield is a foot race that only ends one way. A single misplaced press trigger produces a chance, and the forward does not need many.

Take the third error, which is applying the box-occupier template, with its emphasis on defending the delivery and packing the area, to a duel forward. Packing the box against a forward whose value is the first contact gives him more bodies to make contact against and more knockdowns to attack, in a crowd where his teammates' late runs are unmarkable. And the wide defensive assignment that the box-occupier template requires pulls the very players who should have been controlling the second ball into positions where they cannot.

The general form of the claim is that every one of these templates gives something up, and each forward type is specifically excellent at converting one of those concessions. The depth-runner template concedes crosses, which is what the box occupier wants. The box-occupier template concedes space in front, which is what the drop-and-link forward wants. The drop-and-link template concedes set pieces and second chances, which is what the box occupier and the duel forward want. The duel template concedes the space behind, which is what the depth runner wants. Mismatch the template and you have not simply failed to solve the problem; you have handed the forward the exact input he is best in the league at converting.

### Why does the wrong template make a forward better?

Because each template concedes a specific input, and each forward type is defined by his ability to convert a specific input. Match them wrongly and the concession you chose is the one he is best at punishing. This is why classification precedes planning, and why a plan built on reputation rather than threat type is worse than no plan.

## Transitions, Set Pieces, and the Inputs Nobody Rehearses

A defensive plan against a forward that only covers settled play covers roughly half of the match, and the wrong half. The moments when elite forwards produce their highest-value chances cluster heavily in transition and at dead balls, and both are governed by preparation rather than by defending ability.

### Rest defense and the transition problem

Every attack a team mounts is also a defensive position, and the shape of the players who did not go forward determines whether a turnover becomes a counter-attack. Against a depth runner or a duel forward, the rest-defense structure has to be planned as carefully as the block, because a turnover in the opposition half with a forward on the shoulder is the highest-quality chance available in football.

The practical version is a decision about numbers and positions behind the ball during attacks. Two centre-backs plus a screening midfielder, with at least one full-back holding, is the conservative default against these types, and it means accepting fewer bodies in the opposition box. The second element is the counter-press rule: after a turnover, the nearest two players press immediately to delay the first pass, not to win the ball, because the depth runner needs the first pass to be forward and quick. Delay it by two seconds and the recovering defenders have arrived.

The third element, which is frequently forgotten, is the goalkeeper's role during your own attacks. Against a forward whose threat is the ball over the top from a turnover, the goalkeeper's start position during your possession phases should be much higher than most goalkeepers naturally take, because his sweeping range is the difference between a counter-attack and a clearance.

### The set-piece dimension

All four forward types are dangerous at attacking set pieces, but the box occupier and the duel forward are dangerous in a different way from the others, and the marking scheme should reflect it. The box occupier attacks the space between two markers and arrives late, which argues for a man-marker plus a zonal core. The duel forward attacks the ball at its highest point and wants contact, which argues for a physical marker who competes rather than one who tracks.

The overlooked half is defending set pieces against these forwards when your team is defending a lead, and the overlooked detail is the near-post zone. Most goals conceded from corners at every level below the elite come from the near post, either from a flick-on or from a ball that beats the first man. Assign it to a player who attacks the ball rather than to the shortest player available, and require him to attack from a running start rather than a standing one.

The other set-piece consideration is your own, because a forward of this type is the primary counter-attacking outlet when your corner is cleared. The rest-defense principle applies to attacking set pieces exactly as it applies to open play, and the two players who stay back exist specifically to make sure the cleared corner does not become a three-versus-two.

### The second-chance input in practice

The rebound economy has been mentioned in several templates and deserves consolidation, because it is the input teams most consistently concede by accident. Second chances come from four sources: parried shots, blocked shots, cleared headers, and loose balls from challenges. Three of the four are within your control.

Goalkeeper technique determines where parries go, and a goalkeeper who parries wide rather than straight has removed a category of chance. Blocking technique determines where blocked shots go, and defenders who block with a closed body shape send the ball out rather than up. Clearance direction, already covered, determines where headers land. And the occupation of the central arc determines whether any of those loose balls are contested. A team that addresses all four has meaningfully reduced the third input without changing its shape at all, which makes this the cheapest defensive improvement available to most sides.

## Who Each Type Is Hardest For

The same forward presents a different level of difficulty to different teams, and the difference is structural rather than a question of talent. Knowing which type is hardest for your own side is how a coach decides where to spend a limited preparation week.

### The high-line possession side

A team that defends high and wants the ball is close to invulnerable against a box occupier and severely exposed to a depth runner. The box occupier needs a delivery platform, and a side that presses the ball high and wins it back in the opposition half never allows the platform to form. The depth runner needs the space this side creates by definition, and no amount of collective quality removes it, because the space is a consequence of the identity.

The honest options are narrow. Either accept a lower line for one match, which the players will find uncomfortable and execute imperfectly, or keep the identity and buy insurance in two places: a centre-back with genuine recovery pace and a goalkeeper who sweeps aggressively. The second option is usually the right one, because a possession side that abandons its structure for a single fixture tends to be worse at both things than it was at one.

The drop-and-link forward is a middling problem for this side, because the high line closes the seam naturally, but the duel forward can be surprisingly awkward: a team that presses high invites the direct ball over the press, and the direct ball over the press is exactly the delivery a duel forward wants.

### The mid-block side

A mid-block is the most balanced structure against these four types and the least dominant against any of them. It gives the depth runner less space than a high line, gives the box occupier less crossing time than a low block, and covers the seam adequately without stepping. That balance is genuinely useful for a side that does not know which forward it will face until the team sheet arrives.

The mid-block's specific vulnerability is the drop-and-link forward, because a mid-block by design leaves a seam between its two banks and this forward's whole game is finding it. The fix is the screening discipline described above rather than a structural change, and a mid-block side that installs one excellent screening midfielder has closed its main weakness.

### The low-block side

A low block is the strongest structure against a depth runner, a reasonable structure against a duel forward whose runs need space, and a poor structure against both the box occupier and the drop-and-link forward. That combination is the opposite of what most low-block sides believe about themselves.

Against the box occupier the low block is bad because it concedes unlimited crossing time to unpressured wide players, and unlimited crossing time is the box occupier's oxygen. Against the drop-and-link forward it is bad for the reasons already given at length: depth relocates the seam into the danger area. A low-block side facing either type has to modify, and the modification is usually a slightly higher second line rather than a higher back line, so that the crosser is pressed and the seam is occupied without exposing the space behind.

### The amateur or school side facing a much better forward

This is the most common situation of all and the one least served by tactical writing. A side with one training session a week and no video is not going to install four templates, and should not try. The simplified version is a two-type classification, which can be made from ten minutes of watching without any equipment: does he mostly run away from the ball, or does he mostly come toward it?

If he runs away from the ball, drop the line, keep a spare defender behind the one who marks him, tell the goalkeeper to start further off his line than feels comfortable, and accept crosses. If he comes toward the ball, keep the line where it is, put your most disciplined midfielder behind him, and tell that midfielder that his only job for the whole match is to be in the forward's back when the ball arrives. That is the entire plan, it is achievable in one session, and it is dramatically better than concentration and hope.

The other adjustment that works at this level is the deliberate use of a spare man. A back four against a lone forward already contains one, and the mistake is letting all four defenders mark space equally instead of nominating who is free. Nominate him, put him behind, and the two most common amateur concessions, the ball over the top and the missed first contact, both become recoverable.

### The youth side

Age changes what can be installed, and pretending otherwise produces sessions that look like coaching and teach nothing. Individual defending fundamentals, the body orientation and the delay principle, land early and are worth more than anything else at that stage. Collective ideas such as a shared line reference and a screening job land in mid-adolescence. Concession planning, which requires a player to deliberately allow something, is a mature idea and lands late, because a young defender who is told to allow crosses will hear it as permission to stop working.

There is also a duty of care consideration specific to this cluster. Youth defenders facing a physically dominant forward are frequently asked to compete in duels that are unfair on the day, and the coaching response should be structural rather than exhortative: change the matchup, add cover, adjust the line so the duel happens further from goal. Telling a fourteen-year-old to be stronger is not coaching. The session work that builds these behaviours safely, with progressions and small-squad versions, belongs to the article on [how a week is structured to prepare a team for a dominant individual opponent](/2020/10/15/training-to-contain-a-superstar/).

## The Debates Worth Settling

Six positions recur constantly in discussion of this subject, in coaching rooms and in match threads. Each contains something true, and each has a verdict.

### "Elite strikers cannot be stopped, only limited"

The true part is that no plan drives the probability of a goal to zero, and any coach who promises otherwise is selling something. A forward of this quality who gets three half-chances converts one often enough to decide matches, and a plan that produces three half-chances instead of eight has done excellent work and may still lose.

The false part, and the reason the phrase is worth dismantling, is the use it gets put to. In practice it functions as advance permission to arrive without a plan, because if he cannot be stopped then nothing you did wrong actually mattered. The measurable objective was never a clean sheet. It is the count and quality of the chances he personally reaches, and that number demonstrably moves. Verdict: true as a statement about probability, useless as a statement about preparation, and it should never be allowed to end a conversation about how to defend.

### "Modern centre-backs are worse than previous generations"

The true part is that the job has changed, and some of the skills that defined the position in earlier eras are less trained now, because the game asks for different ones. A defender who spends most of his match in possession forty yards from his own goal is being selected and coached for a different profile than one whose match consisted of ninety minutes of duels in his own third.

The false part is the conclusion. Defending is a collective output and it has been reorganized, not degraded. Offside lines are held more precisely, distances between lines are managed more deliberately, and goalkeepers cover more ground behind the last defender than they used to. What has genuinely declined in some contexts is individual duel exposure, simply because fewer situations reach the individual duel. Verdict: the profile has shifted and specific old skills are less rehearsed, but the claim that defending as a discipline has got worse does not survive contact with how few clear chances the best defensive structures now concede.

### "Man-marking is dead"

The true part is that full-pitch man-marking as a system is rare at the top level, for good reasons: it can be broken by rotation, it hands the initiative to the attacking side, and it is physically expensive.

The false part is the leap from "rare as a system" to "dead as a tool". Every plan in this guide contains man-oriented elements, and the drop-and-link template is built on one. What is dead is the reflex of assigning a man to follow a forward everywhere without asking what that achieves; what is very much alive is the assignment of a specific player to a specific opponent in a specific zone, for a specific purpose. Verdict: the system is largely gone, the tool is essential, and coaches who conflate the two end up with no answer to a forward who needs to be denied a turn.

### "Just foul him early and let him know you are there"

The true part is that a forward who is met physically and legally in the first ten minutes has received information, and the first-contact principle in the duel template is a legitimate version of this idea.

The false part is everything else. A tactical foul in a safe area to stop a transition is a real and defensible tool. An early deliberate foul as a message is a poor trade at every level: it risks a card that ruins the plan for eighty minutes, it hands the opposition a set piece, and against a forward of this calibre it does not achieve the intimidation it imagines. The players it most reliably rattles are the ones who were not going to hurt you anyway. Verdict: compete early and physically within the laws, and drop the message-sending, which costs more than it returns.

### "A low block always concedes eventually"

The true part is arithmetic. Sustained pressure produces chances, chances produce goals, and a team that defends its own box for eighty minutes will concede some proportion of the time. Nobody sensible disputes it.

The false part is treating that as an argument against the low block, which is a comparison against a fantasy alternative rather than against the real one. The question is never whether a low block concedes eventually; it is whether it concedes less than the alternative available to this specific squad against this specific forward. Against a depth runner with a weak squad in front of him, a low block concedes far less than a mid-block would. The genuine caution is the one already given: against a box occupier or a drop-and-link forward, the low block is the wrong tool and the "eventually" arrives much sooner. Verdict: true and largely irrelevant, except as a reminder that a block needs an out-ball and a plan to relieve pressure.

### "Finishing overperformance proves a forward is due to decline"

The true part is that finishing output does fluctuate around a player's underlying chance quality, and a forward who is converting far above the expected value of his chances over a short run is likely to convert closer to it later. That is a real statistical property and it is worth understanding.

The false part is the inference about a specific elite forward. Sustained overperformance across a long career is evidence of a genuine finishing skill rather than evidence of luck waiting to correct, and the models that generate the expectation do not fully capture shot technique, contact quality, or the ability to shoot from body positions others cannot. Treating a forward as due to decline because a model says so is exactly the kind of reasoning that gets a defensive plan written for the wrong player. Verdict: use the underlying numbers to understand the chances a forward reaches, which is what they measure well, and do not use them to predict that a proven finisher will suddenly stop finishing. The wider question of which metrics carry signal for a named forward is a subject in its own right and belongs to the data articles in this series rather than here.

## Building the Classification Without a Budget

Everything in this guide assumes a coach can classify a forward, and classification is the step most likely to be skipped by anyone without an analysis department. It does not require one.

The minimum viable process is two matches and forty minutes of attention. Watch one match in which the opponent has the ball for long periods against a set defense, and one in which they are chasing the game or countering. In each, watch the forward and not the ball for ten-minute blocks, and record four things by tally rather than by note: how often he moves away from the ball, how often he moves toward it, how often he seeks contact, and where he is standing when a cross comes in. Those four tallies produce the classification with more reliability than an hour of highlights, because they measure the mechanism rather than the output.

The second artifact worth building is a one-page duel sheet for the defender who will face him, containing his stronger foot, the side he prefers to attack, his habitual first movement, and the two or three situations in which he is least effective. The defender reads it, the goalkeeper reads it, and the screening midfielder reads it, and nobody else needs to. A plan that lives in the coach's head is a plan the players do not have.

The third is the concession statement, written as one sentence and said aloud in the team meeting: we are conceding crosses from deep and wide, we have rehearsed it, and nobody is to drop the line when the first one comes in. That sentence prevents more goals than any diagram, because the mid-match collapse of a good plan almost always begins with one player unilaterally deciding the plan is not working.

## The Defender's Own Checklist, Type by Type

Coaches plan structures; defenders play duels. This section is written for the player who is going to spend ninety minutes within two yards of one of these forwards, and it translates each template into what one individual should actually be thinking.

Against a depth runner, your first thought before every opposition pass is where he is relative to your shoulder, and your second is where your cover is. You do not need to see him constantly; you need to know his position at the moment the ball can be played forward, which is a different and much easier task. Set your hips half open before the pass, not after. When he steps toward the ball, do not follow him more than a stride, because the step toward is the setup for the run away. If he does get beyond you, do not aim for the ball; aim for the line between him and the goal, and let the goalkeeper and the covering defender do their jobs. The duel you must win is the start of the run, not the end of it.

Against a box occupier, your entire match happens inside a small rectangle and you win it with contact and eyes. Establish physical contact early, side-on, with an arm across so you can feel his movement rather than watch it. Watch his hips, not the ball, from the moment the ball enters the wide areas. Expect the near-post movement and expect the check back toward the penalty spot, and know which of the two he prefers before kickoff. When the delivery comes, your job is to occupy the yard he wants rather than to attack the ball, unless you are certain you win it. And when the shot is blocked, do not watch it; find him again immediately, because the second chance is where he lives.

Against a drop-and-link forward, you are frequently not the primary defender at all, which is the hardest thing to accept. Your job is to hold the line while the screening midfielder does the work, and to step only when you are told or when he is clearly unsupported. When you do step, step early and arrive with the ball, and make the contact a shoulder into his back at the instant of his touch. Never step late, never step half-heartedly, and never step and then stop. If he receives and you have not stepped, drop and delay; the pass he wants to play requires him to be facing forward with his head up, and a defender backing off in a straight line in front of him denies the picture he is looking for.

Against a duel forward, compete for everything at the highest point and the earliest moment. Meet the ball rather than the man, get to the flight early, and accept that you will lose some. Do not let him settle with the ball under control and his back into your chest, because that phase is his and there is no good outcome from it. Between duels, recover your position rather than your breath, because his second action is the one that hurts. And know that the last twenty minutes are the danger period for you specifically, so manage your effort in the first hour rather than winning the first three duels spectacularly.

The common thread across all four is that the defender's job is defined before the ball arrives, not after. Every one of these duels is decided by a decision made half a second early, and the players who look calm against elite forwards are not calmer people. They have simply decided sooner.

## The Goalkeeper's Job Against Each Type

Goalkeeping against elite forwards is frequently discussed as shot-stopping, which is the smallest part of it. The larger part is positioning, decision-making, and the effect the goalkeeper's starting position has on the eight players in front of him.

Against a depth runner the goalkeeper is the last line's insurance policy and his starting position is the plan. Ten to fourteen yards off the line with the ball in the middle third, adjusting constantly with the ball, and a clear decision rule for when to come: any ball played into the space behind that he can reach before the forward is his, and he goes with commitment and takes the ball or the ball's path, not the player. The plan should state whether he clears or controls, and the honest answer for most goalkeepers is clear first and be brave later.

Against a box occupier the goalkeeper's job is the cross line. His start position is central and slightly toward the near post, his decision to come or stay must be consistent, and his communication should be a single early call rather than a running commentary. The most valuable thing he can do is catch, because a caught cross ends an attack while a parried one restarts it in the most dangerous zone on the pitch. Where catching is not available, punch high, wide, and long, and never soft into the arc.

Against a drop-and-link forward the goalkeeper starts higher than instinct suggests, because the plan involves a stepping centre-back and the pass over that stepping centre-back is the counter to it. He is also the team's release valve when the pressure comes, and against this type the pressure will come, so his distribution under pressure is a defensive skill rather than an attacking one.

Against a duel forward the goalkeeper's dominance of his own area is the difference between a knockdown becoming a chance and becoming a clearance. He should claim aggressively anything in the six-yard box, communicate the second-ball assignment loudly, and be prepared to sweep when a first contact is lost and the forward's teammate is running through.

Across all four types, the goalkeeper is the only player who can see the whole picture, which makes his voice the most valuable defensive tool the team owns and the most consistently underused. A goalkeeper who calls one instruction, early and specifically, before every opposition entry into the final third is worth more than a goalkeeper who makes an extra save.

## The Mistakes That Repeat

Five errors account for the majority of well-intentioned plans that fail against forwards of this quality, and every one of them is preventable in the meeting rather than on the pitch.

The first is planning for the reputation rather than the threat type. A famous forward attracts a plan built from what everybody knows about him, which is usually his highlight profile rather than his mechanism. The correction is the classification process: watch the off-ball movement, tally the four behaviours, and let the tally rather than the reputation choose the template.

The second is dropping deeper as a reflex. The line drops when the first ball goes over the top, then drops again when a cross is nearly converted, and by the hour mark a team that planned a mid-block is defending its own six-yard box with no out-ball. The correction is the concession statement said aloud before kickoff and repeated by the captain during the match, plus a nominated player, usually the deepest midfielder, whose secondary job is to hold the line's reference point.

The third is the screening midfielder ball-watching. He drifts toward the ball because the ball is where the action looks like it is, the lane behind him opens, and the forward receives in the seam. The correction is to give him a reference he can check constantly, which is the line between the ball and the forward, and to have the goalkeeper or a centre-back call him back into it rather than assuming he will notice.

The fourth is asking one player to do two jobs in the box. A defender who is both marking the forward and attacking the near post does neither, and a midfielder who is both screening the cutback and occupying the rebound arc will be in the wrong one of the two every time. The correction is to assign single jobs and accept that this means fewer bodies doing more useful things.

The fifth is treating the plan as a promise rather than a set of conditional decisions. Plans fail in matches; that is a normal event rather than a crisis. What separates a good staff from a poor one is having decided in advance what the second plan is and what triggers it, so that the response to trouble is a rehearsed adjustment rather than an improvisation delivered by a coach shouting across forty yards of touchline.

## When a Forward Changes Type Mid-Match

The taxonomy describes roles, not identities, and good opposition coaches change roles. A plan that cannot survive the forward being asked to do something different at half time is a plan with one life, and one life is rarely enough against a staff that studies you as carefully as you study them.

The change usually arrives in one of three forms. The first is a role swap within the front line: a forward who started on the shoulder is asked to drop between the lines, or a wide starter is moved central. The second is a structural change behind him: the opposition adds a second striker or an advanced midfielder, which changes what the forward is being fed and from where. The third is a state-driven change: a team chasing a goal pushes its forward higher and more directly, and a team protecting one asks him to hold the ball and relieve pressure, which turns almost any forward temporarily into a duel forward.

The response is not to re-plan at half time under time pressure. It is to write the triggers into the original plan, in the form of an explicit conditional. If he starts dropping into the seam, the screening midfielder takes him and the line does not drop. If a second forward arrives, the covering centre-back stops covering and starts marking, and the full-backs tuck. If they go direct with twenty minutes left, the line steps up five yards and the deepest midfielder occupies the flick-on zone. Three sentences, agreed before kickoff, said again in the dressing room at half time, and the team adapts without needing a coach to reorganize it from the touchline.

The captain or the deepest defender should own the recognition rather than the bench, because the players see the change first. The recognition cue for each conditional should be something visible and unambiguous: not "if they change their approach", but "if he receives with his back to goal twice in five minutes", or "if there are two of them in the box when a cross comes". Vague triggers produce late adaptation, and against forwards of this quality the interval between a role change and a chance is measured in minutes.

There is a nastier version of this problem, which is the forward who genuinely operates in two types at once rather than switching between them. The two-template problem described in the section on Haaland above is the clearest case: a forward who threatens the space behind and occupies the box is asking you to concede one input from each of two different templates, which cannot be done cleanly. The honest answer is to pick the template that suits your personnel, execute it properly, and accept that the other half of his game will get chances. Trying to half-defend both produces a structure that is not committed to either and gives him both.

## Defending a Front Two

Most of this guide has assumed a lone forward, because that is the most common structure and the clearest teaching case. A front two changes the arithmetic in ways that deserve their own treatment, and the changes are not intuitive.

The first change is that the spare defender disappears. A back four against one forward has two defenders free; against two forwards it has one, and that one is the whole margin. Every decision that pulls a centre-back out of the line now leaves a one against one behind, which means the stepping behaviour that makes the drop-and-link template work becomes considerably more expensive. Against a front two containing a link forward and a runner, the covering slide behind a stepping defender has to be immediate and it usually has to come from a full-back, which then opens the wide area.

The second change is that the two forwards are almost never the same type, and that is the point of playing two. The standard construction pairs a forward who occupies defenders with one who attacks the space that occupation creates. Recognizing which is which decides the plan: mark the occupier tightly with your strongest duel defender, and use the spare defender as cover against the runner rather than as a second marker on the man who already has one.

The third change is that the screening midfielder cannot cover both seams. Against a lone forward, one disciplined holding player can own the receiving zone. Against a front two, that zone splits, and a single screen will be pulled to one side and beaten on the other. The structural answer is a second holding player, which means sacrificing a body further forward, and that sacrifice should be made explicitly rather than discovered at half time.

The fourth change is in the counter-press. A team defending against two forwards loses the ball into a situation where two players are already positioned to attack, so the rest-defense numbers rise: three behind the ball is the minimum, and four is common. Sides that keep attacking with the same commitment against a front two as against a lone striker concede the highest-quality chances in football, which are transitions into a two against two.

None of this argues for a specific formation, and the instinct to switch to a back three purely because the opposition plays two forwards is worth examining rather than obeying. A back three restores the spare defender and simplifies the marking, which is a real benefit. It also commits a third centre-back who may be more useful elsewhere, and it can make the wide areas harder to defend against a team that then attacks the space beside the wing-backs. Decide it on the personnel you have and on which of the two forwards you consider the primary problem, rather than on a rule about matching shapes.

## The Verdict

The instruction to keep an elite forward quiet is not a plan, and the people who give it are not being lazy so much as skipping a step that nobody taught them. The step is classification. Watch where his value is created rather than how good he is: behind the last line, inside the box, in the seam between the lines, or in the contested first contact. That answer selects a template, and the template names a line height, a defender profile, a second-defender behaviour, a screening job, a goalkeeper start position, and, most importantly, the input you are choosing to give up.

The supply, space, second rule is the reason the last item cannot be avoided. You cannot deny supply, space, and second chances simultaneously, because the shapes required contradict each other. So name the two you are denying, name the one you are conceding, rehearse the concession as seriously as the denial, and stop losing matches to inputs you surrendered without noticing. A team that has made that choice deliberately is calm when the concession arrives, and calm is worth more than any diagram.

None of this makes a great forward harmless, and no honest analysis would claim otherwise. It makes him predictable, which is the only thing a defending team ever actually gets. The chances he reaches go down, their quality goes down, and the ones he does reach come from the areas you chose rather than the areas he chose. That is the whole job, it is achievable at every level of the game, and it starts with ten minutes of watching a forward move without the ball.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What makes an elite striker so hard to defend?

The difficulty is not speed or strength on their own; it is that an elite forward has a repeatable mechanism that converts one specific input into a chance, and that he executes it under fatigue and pressure when defenders stop executing. He needs one of three things: supply into a dangerous area, space to attack, or a second chance from a rebound or dead ball. A defending team cannot remove all three at once, because the shape that kills supply concedes space and the shape that kills space concedes second balls. So there is always something available to him, and his skill is recognizing which one you have left open, usually faster than you recognize it yourself.

### Q: How do you defend against a fast striker?

Reduce the situations in which his pace is usable rather than trying to match it. Pace only matters when there is space to run into, so set your last line at a distance from your own goal that makes the space behind small, and push your goalkeeper ten to fourteen yards off his line so that anything played into the remaining space becomes a race he can win. The more important work is in midfield: a fast forward cannot run onto a pass that is never played, so your screening midfielder occupies the lane between the ball and the forward and makes the forward pass slow or lofted. Accept crosses and long shots as the price, and rehearse defending them.

### Q: Which defender profile handles a physical number nine best?

Not the biggest defender, but the one who competes earliest. Against a forward whose value comes from winning contested first contacts, the profile you want is a front-foot defender who attacks the flight of the ball at its highest point and refuses to let the forward settle with the ball under control and his back to goal. Strength matters less than timing and body positioning, because these duels are won by getting there first rather than by pushing hardest. Add a second requirement that is easy to overlook: he must recover his position quickly rather than admiring the duel he just won, because the forward's second action after the first contact is where most of the damage happens.

### Q: Should a defender mark tight or drop off against a top forward?

It depends entirely on where the forward creates value, and getting it wrong actively helps him. Mark tight against a forward whose value is created inside the box through small movements and contact, because separation of even a yard is what he is trying to buy. Drop off against a forward whose value is created by running into space behind you, because tight marking high up the pitch hands him a start position on your shoulder with grass to attack. Against a forward who drops between the lines, do neither in the conventional sense: let him receive, hold your line, and have a midfielder step into his back so that he cannot turn.

### Q: How high should a defensive line sit against a striker who runs in behind?

Lower than you would against most forwards, and the useful way to set it is as a distance from your own goal rather than as a position on the pitch. Around thirty yards from your own goal when the ball is in the opposition half is a workable starting reference, compressing further as the ball advances. The number matters less than the fact that it is a single agreed reference every defender relates to, because a back line at four slightly different depths gives a depth runner an offside line he can beat by targeting the highest defender. Pair the line with a goalkeeper positioned well off his line, since the two decisions are one decision.

### Q: Is double marking a striker ever worth it?

Rarely as a permanent assignment and often as a situational one. Permanently committing two players to one forward means you are outnumbered everywhere else, and elite teams are extremely good at punishing that within a few passes. The situational version is worth a great deal: doubling in the wide channel when a forward is isolated one against one, doubling on the first contact when a long ball is coming, or doubling for a fifteen-minute period when you are protecting a lead. Define the trigger precisely, so the second defender arrives because a condition was met rather than because somebody felt nervous, and make sure the player who leaves his position knows who covers it.

### Q: How do you stop a striker who drops into midfield?

Let him receive and stop him turning, which is a completely different objective from stopping him receiving. Assign a midfielder to the zone he drops into rather than to the man, position that midfielder goalside and within a stride, and have him step into the forward's back at the moment of the first touch so that the only available option is a pass back the way the ball came. Keep your defensive line where it is; do not drop it. Dropping deep against this type moves the area he receives in closer to your goal, so the pass he plays after turning becomes a final ball rather than a build-up pass, which makes him more dangerous rather than less.

### Q: What is the safest way to defend crosses against a tall forward?

Defend the delivery rather than the header. The largest gains come forty yards from goal, by denying the two areas that produce quality service, which are the byline cutback zone and the pocket between full-back and centre-back, and by ensuring the crosser is always under some pressure so the delivery is hurried. Inside the box, use a zonal core across the six-yard box with one dedicated man-marker whose only job is the forward, plus a designated front-post attacker. Give nobody two jobs. Then organize the rebound zones, because a large share of goals from crossing situations arrive from the second contact rather than the first.

### Q: How do you stop a striker on the counter-attack?

Win the two seconds immediately after the turnover, because that is the only window in which a counter can be stopped cheaply. The nearest two players press the ball to delay the first forward pass rather than to win possession, which buys time for the recovering players to get goalside. Behind that, your rest defense during your own attacks decides everything: keep two centre-backs plus a screening midfielder behind the ball, and hold at least one full-back. Your goalkeeper should also start much higher during your possession phases than most goalkeepers naturally do, because his sweeping range converts a through ball into a clearance.

### Q: Can a low block shut down a world class forward?

Against a forward whose threat is running into space behind a defensive line, yes, and it is often the best available answer for a weaker side. Against a forward whose value is created inside the box from crosses, or one who drops between the lines and plays forward passes, a low block is the wrong tool and tends to make him more effective. The reason is that a deep block concedes unlimited crossing time to unpressured wide players, and it moves the receiving seam between your lines into your own penalty area. Choose the block based on the forward's mechanism rather than on your general preference for defending deep.

### Q: How does a goalkeeper help defend against elite strikers?

More through positioning and communication than through saves. His starting distance from his line determines how much of the space behind the defense is actually defended, and that distance should be an agreed number the back line also knows. His decision rule on crosses, come or stay, must be consistent, because defenders in front of an unpredictable goalkeeper hedge and lose their own duels. He controls where parries and punches go, which decides whether a blocked shot becomes a second chance. And he is the only player who sees everything, so one specific early instruction before each entry into the final third is worth more than an extra save.

### Q: What should an amateur side do against a much better striker?

Simplify to a two-way classification and one clear decision. Watch him for ten minutes and ask whether he mostly moves away from the ball or toward it. If he moves away, drop your line, keep one defender spare behind the one marking him, push the goalkeeper further off his line than feels natural, and accept crosses. If he moves toward the ball, hold your line and give your most disciplined midfielder one job for the whole match, which is to be in the forward's back whenever he receives. Then nominate a spare defender explicitly, because an unnominated spare man is the most commonly wasted resource in amateur defending.

### Q: How do you coach young defenders to face a strong striker?

Coach the individual fundamentals first and the collective ideas later, because that is the order in which they land. Body orientation before the pass arrives, delay before committing, and the trigger for when a tackle is on are all teachable early and transfer to every opponent. Shared line references and screening responsibilities land in mid-adolescence. The response to a physical mismatch should always be structural rather than exhortative: change the matchup, add a covering defender, or move the line so the duel happens further from goal. Telling a young defender to be stronger is not coaching, and it puts a player in a contest that is unfair on the day.

### Q: Does man to man marking still work against modern forwards?

As a whole-pitch system it is rare at the top level, because it can be unpicked by rotation and it is physically expensive across a season. As a tool inside a zonal structure it is essential, and almost every plan in this guide contains a man-oriented element. The version that works is a specific player assigned to a specific opponent in a specific zone for a specific purpose: denying the turn between the lines, occupying a forward's movement inside the box, or competing for the first contact on a long ball. The version that fails is a defender told to follow a forward everywhere without any statement of what following him is supposed to achieve.

### Q: How do you cut the supply to a dangerous number nine?

Identify the two or three entry routes his team actually uses to find him, which is a much shorter list than it appears, and close those rather than defending everywhere. For most sides the routes are the direct pass from a deep midfielder, the cutback from the byline, and the diagonal into the channel. Each has a different answer: the direct pass is closed by a screening midfielder occupying the lane, the cutback is closed by wide defenders protecting the inside shoulder and the near centre-back covering the low zone, and the diagonal is closed by pressing the deep carrier so the delivery is hurried. Closing supply always costs you space behind, so decide that trade before kickoff.

### Q: What mistakes do defenders repeat against top forwards?

Four errors recur at every level. Standing square when the pass is played, which forces a full turn against a forward who already has momentum. Following a forward more than a stride when he steps toward the ball, which is exactly the movement designed to draw that step. Watching the ball instead of the forward's hips inside the box, which puts the marker half a second late on every movement. And losing the forward after the first action, whether that is a blocked shot, a won duel, or a clearance, because the second contact is where a large share of these goals actually come from. All four are decisions made half a second too late rather than physical failures.
