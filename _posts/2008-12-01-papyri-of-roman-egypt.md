Almost everything known about the ancient world comes from two kinds of source: monuments, which record what powerful people wanted remembered, and literature, which records what a tiny educated minority thought worth writing down and what later generations chose to keep copying. Both are severe filters. The result is a picture of antiquity in which emperors, generals, philosophers, and architects are visible and the overwhelming majority of human beings who actually lived are not.

Egypt is the exception, and the reason is rubbish. The argument this article makes is the rubbish-heap-archive thesis: the discarded paperwork of Roman Egypt, thrown onto village mounds, stuffed into mummy casings, and abandoned in collapsing houses, and preserved by nothing more than dryness, constitutes the richest surviving record of ordinary ancient life anywhere in the Roman world. It survived because nobody valued it, and it tells us things no monument was ever going to record.

This article covers the material, the finds, the discipline that reads them, and the limits of the whole body of evidence. The province they document is set out in the [complete guide to Roman Egypt](/2008/05/01/roman-egypt-complete-guide/), and what they show about ordinary lives in the article on [daily life in Roman Egypt](/2008/07/01/daily-life-in-roman-egypt/). The wider question of how any period without written sources can be known at all is treated in the article on [how we know about predynastic Egypt](/2000/09/01/how-we-know-about-predynastic-egypt/), which is the same problem approached from the opposite end.

## What Papyrus Is

Understanding the material explains both why so much was written and why so much was thrown away.

Papyrus is made from a tall sedge that grew in the marshes of the Nile Delta and along the river. The stem is triangular in section, and the working part is the pith inside it. The pith was cut into thin strips, and the strips were laid side by side in a layer, with a second layer laid across them at right angles. The whole was then pressed, which released natural adhesive substances that bonded the layers as they dried, and the resulting sheet was smoothed with a stone or shell.

Individual sheets were joined edge to edge with a slight overlap to form a roll, and a standard roll consisted of a substantial number of sheets. The side on which the horizontal fibres ran was smoother and easier to write on, and was normally used first; the other side was used later, for reuse or for a second text.

### What is papyrus made of?

The pith of a tall sedge that grew in Egyptian marshes. Strips of pith were laid side by side in one layer, a second layer was laid across at right angles, and the two were pressed together so natural substances bonded them as they dried, producing a sheet that was then smoothed.

Egypt held a near-monopoly on production, and papyrus was exported across the Mediterranean, supplying the writing material for the entire literate ancient world. That trade is one of the province's significant exports, treated in the article on [Egypt as the breadbasket of Rome](/2008/06/01/egypt-breadbasket-of-rome/).

Cost is the point that explains the archaeology. Papyrus was not cheap. It was not so expensive that ordinary people could never use it, since the documentary record shows villagers writing letters and keeping receipts routinely, and it was expensive enough that people economized. Sheets were reused on the back. Old documents were washed or scraped. Above all, once a document had served its purpose it was discarded, and discarding meant throwing it on the village rubbish heap along with everything else.

That habit is the foundation of the entire discipline. A tax receipt for a payment made twenty years ago is worthless to its owner and priceless to a historian, and the reason historians have it is that its owner threw it away in a place where it did not rot.

## Why Egypt and Nowhere Else

The preservation question deserves precision, because the usual answer, that Egypt is dry, is correct and incomplete.

Organic materials decay through the action of water and the microorganisms that require it. In a genuinely arid environment with no groundwater reaching the material, decay effectively stops, and organic objects can survive for thousands of years. Egypt supplies that environment in specific places: above the flood plain, in the desert margins, in abandoned settlements that were never rebuilt, and in tombs cut into dry rock.

It does not supply it everywhere. The Nile valley floor floods annually and holds groundwater, so papyrus buried there rots. The Delta is wetter still. Alexandria sits on the coast with a high water table and has been continuously occupied and rebuilt for two thousand years.

### Why did papyri survive in Egypt?

Because parts of Egypt are arid enough that the water needed for organic decay never reaches buried material. Papyrus survived above the flood plain, in desert margins, in abandoned settlements, and in tombs cut into dry rock. It did not survive in the flooded valley floor, the Delta, or coastal Alexandria.

The consequence is the single most important qualification in this whole field, and it should be stated before anything else about what papyri show. The surviving record comes from the Fayum and from Middle and Upper Egypt. It does not come from Alexandria, the province's capital, largest city, and administrative centre, or from the Delta, its most populous agricultural region. The best-documented ancient society is documented for the places where documents survive, which are not the places where most of the action was.

Two smaller preservation contexts round out the picture. Carbonization, in which material is charred rather than burned and thereby rendered inert, preserves papyri elsewhere, most famously in a buried Italian villa, and the resulting texts are extraordinarily difficult to read. Waterlogging in an oxygen-free environment can also preserve organic material, though it has not produced significant papyrus finds.

The general lesson generalizes beyond Egypt and it is worth carrying into any historical subject. The evidence that survives is not a sample of what existed. It is a sample of what happened to be in conditions that permitted survival, and those conditions are never randomly distributed.

## Where Papyri Are Actually Found

Four contexts produce the bulk of the material, and each shapes what kind of text comes out of it.

Rubbish mounds are the largest source and the most characteristic. Ancient settlements accumulated waste in heaps at their edges, and those heaps contain everything a town discarded: broken pottery, food waste, textile scraps, and paper. Where the mound sat above the water table and was never disturbed, the papyrus survived. This is where the great documentary finds come from, and the material is by definition what somebody threw away.

Abandoned settlements supply the second context and it is the most valuable archaeologically. A town that was deserted rather than rebuilt leaves its houses standing with their contents in place, and papyri found on a house floor or in a storage jar can be connected to a specific room, a specific building, and sometimes a specific family.

### Where are papyri found in Egypt?

In four main contexts: ancient rubbish mounds at the edges of settlements, abandoned towns whose houses survive with contents in place, tombs and burial equipment, and mummy cartonnage, which is casing made from layers of discarded papyrus stiffened with plaster.

Tombs and funerary contexts supply a third. Literary and religious texts were sometimes buried with the dead, and burial equipment could incorporate papyrus in various ways.

The fourth is cartonnage, and it deserves its own section below because of what it produces and the problems it raises.

A fifth context should be mentioned because it destroyed as much as it revealed. Through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, local farmers dug ancient mounds for the decayed organic material they contained, which made excellent fertilizer. That activity churned enormous quantities of ancient settlement debris, destroyed stratigraphy, and brought papyri to light in ways that produced a supply for the antiquities market without any record of where anything came from.

## Cartonnage and the Crocodiles

One preservation context is strange enough to deserve full treatment, and it accounts for a substantial share of the earlier material.

Cartonnage is a composite made by layering scrap material with plaster to produce a light, rigid shell that could be moulded and painted. Mummy masks, chest covers, and foot cases were made this way. The scrap material used was whatever was available and worthless, and in Egypt that frequently meant used papyrus: old contracts, receipts, accounts, official correspondence, and occasionally literature, glued together in layers.

The result is that a painted funerary object may contain, inside its structure, dozens of documents from the decades before the burial. Separating them destroys the object. Preserving the object leaves the texts unread. That conflict is a live ethical and methodological problem in the field and it is examined below.

### What is cartonnage and why does it matter for papyri?

Cartonnage is mummy casing made by layering scrap material with plaster to form a rigid shell. In Egypt the scrap was often used papyrus, so a painted mask or foot case can contain dozens of discarded documents. Extracting them destroys the funerary object.

The crocodiles are the other case and they are genuinely remarkable. At a Fayum site excavated at the end of the nineteenth century, the excavators were disappointed to find a cemetery of mummified crocodiles rather than the human burials they wanted. According to the account that has come down, a workman broke one open in frustration and revealed that it was packed with papyrus. The crocodiles, sacred animals given ritual burial, had been stuffed and wrapped with discarded documents.

That site produced a large body of Ptolemaic-period material as a direct result, and it is the most memorable illustration of the principle behind this whole article. Documents survive because they were treated as worthless. A tax roll considered valuable would have been kept in an archive in a damp city and would have rotted. A tax roll considered rubbish was used to stuff a crocodile in a desert cemetery and survived for two thousand years.

## Oxyrhynchus

One site dominates the subject, and its story is the founding episode of the discipline.

Oxyrhynchus was a nome capital in Middle Egypt, a provincial town of moderate importance with the usual apparatus of a Roman Egyptian metropolis: archives, a gymnasium, temples, and later churches. It sat beside a canal off the Nile, above the flood plain, and its rubbish mounds accumulated for centuries and were then abandoned.

Two young Oxford scholars, Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt, working for an English excavation society, began digging those mounds at the very end of the nineteenth century. They had chosen the site on reasoning rather than luck: it had been a Greek-speaking administrative centre for a long period, it was above the water table, and nobody had systematically worked it.

### What was the Oxyrhynchus papyri find?

The excavation of a provincial Egyptian town's ancient rubbish mounds by Grenfell and Hunt from 1896, which produced papyri in quantities nobody had anticipated. The mounds yielded an estimated several hundred thousand fragments, documentary and literary, and publication of them has continued for over a century.

What they found exceeded anything expected. The mounds produced papyrus in bulk, layer upon layer, across seasons of digging that continued into the early twentieth century. Estimates of the total run to several hundred thousand fragments, and the fragments range from complete documents to scraps of a few letters.

The scale created a problem that persists. Every papyrus requires an expert to read it, and reading involves transcription, reconstruction of damaged passages, identification of the text or document type, dating, and placement in context. That work takes time proportional to difficulty rather than to quantity, and a single literary fragment can occupy a specialist for months. Publication has proceeded in volumes for well over a century and a substantial majority of the material remains unpublished.

The finds divide into two categories that require different skills and answer different questions. Documentary papyri record the business of a town: taxes, contracts, letters, petitions, accounts. Literary papyri preserve copies of texts, both works known from the medieval manuscript tradition and works otherwise lost.

## What Oxyrhynchus Gave Back

The literary recoveries are the part that reaches the general public, and they are genuinely extraordinary because they represent works that had been lost for well over a thousand years.

Greek poetry is the largest gain. Substantial fragments of lyric poets known previously only through brief quotations in later writers have come out of the mounds, restoring at least the shape and something of the voice of poets whose reputations rested on other people's admiration. Fragments of choral poetry, of early epic outside the Homeric poems, and of Hellenistic verse have all been recovered.

Drama has been recovered too. Fragments of Athenian tragedy from plays lost entirely, including a substantial portion of a satyr play, and a considerable body of Greek New Comedy have emerged, changing what can be said about the development of the form.

### What lost literature has been found in the papyri?

Substantial fragments of Greek lyric poets previously known only through quotation, portions of lost Athenian tragedies including a satyr play, a considerable body of Greek New Comedy, historical writing lost from the manuscript tradition, and early Christian texts including a collection of sayings attributed to Jesus.

Historical writing has been recovered in fragments, including an anonymous Greek history covering a period for which the surviving accounts are thin, which has become an independent check on the narrative historians previously had to accept.

Early Christian texts appear in quantity, including fragments of gospels and other works, and among the most significant was a group of sayings attributed to Jesus that puzzled their first editors and was only understood decades later when a complete version in Coptic turned up in a separate discovery in Upper Egypt.

The recoveries have limits worth stating. Fragments are fragments: a column of text with the beginnings or ends of lines missing, or a scrap of ten words. Reconstruction is inference. And the works recovered are those that a provincial town happened to own copies of and happened to discard, which is a different selection from what a great library held.

## The Other Great Finds

Oxyrhynchus dominates the subject and it is not alone, and knowing the other major discoveries prevents the impression that one site produced everything.

A Fayum site excavated at the turn of the twentieth century produced the crocodile mummies described above and, with them, a large body of Ptolemaic-period documents from a temple and village context. That material is the backbone of what is known about the earlier administration, and it complements the Roman-period material from elsewhere.

An estate manager's papers from the third century BCE, recovered from another Fayum town, form the single richest archive from the Ptolemaic period: correspondence, accounts, instructions, and complaints relating to the running of a large landholding, covering several decades in the life of one busy official. It is the closest thing antiquity offers to a corporate filing cabinet.

### What other major papyrus finds are there besides Oxyrhynchus?

A Fayum site whose sacred crocodile mummies were stuffed with Ptolemaic documents, a third-century BCE estate manager's archive covering decades of correspondence and accounts, Aramaic papyri from a Persian-period Jewish garrison at Elephantine, and major collections of early Christian biblical manuscripts.

Aramaic documents from a garrison community on an island at the first cataract, dating to the Persian period, preserve the legal and personal affairs of a Jewish military colony centuries before the Roman period, including marriage contracts, property disputes, and correspondence with authorities about their temple.

Collections of early Christian biblical manuscripts, assembled in the twentieth century from Egyptian sources, include some of the oldest substantial copies of New Testament books and have become foundational for textual scholarship on those writings.

A separate Upper Egyptian discovery of Coptic codices, examined in the article on [how Christianity came to Egypt](/2008/08/01/how-christianity-came-to-egypt/), preserved a body of Christian texts that the eventually dominant church rejected, and it transformed what could be said about early Christian diversity.

Two important comparisons lie outside Egypt. A library of carbonized rolls buried by a volcanic eruption in Italy preserves a philosophical collection in a form so damaged that reading it has occupied specialists for centuries and is only now yielding to advanced imaging. And a cache of scrolls found in desert caves in the Levant demonstrates that arid conditions elsewhere can preserve ancient writing, though nothing outside Egypt approaches the Egyptian volume.

## The Documentary Papyri

The documents are the larger body and the more historically transformative, and describing what they actually contain conveys the texture better than any generalization.

Fiscal material dominates because the Roman state generated it relentlessly. Tax receipts naming a payer, a sum, a date, and a category. Registers listing landholders and assessments. Census returns declaring every person in a household with age, relationship, and status. Land surveys recording what was flooded and what was sown.

Legal and commercial documents come next in volume. Sales of land, houses, animals, and slaves. Leases of agricultural land with the share arrangements specified. Loans with interest, security, and repayment terms. Wills. Marriage contracts recording what each party brought and what happened on dissolution. Divorce agreements. Apprenticeship contracts placing a boy with a weaver for a term of years.

### Which document types dominate the record?

Fiscal material dominates, because the Roman state generated it relentlessly: receipts, registers, census returns, and land surveys. Legal and commercial documents follow in volume, including sales, leases, loans, wills, and marriage contracts. Petitions, private letters, accounts, and school exercises make up most of the remainder.

Petitions are the category that most often startles readers who expect ancient documents to be dry. Somebody has been beaten at a festival. A neighbour has diverted irrigation water. A tenant has absconded owing rent. An official has demanded more than was owed. A woman has been abandoned by her husband. A man's donkey has been stolen. Each is written to an official, opens with a formulaic address, states the injury, and requests redress.

Private letters are the most immediate. A soldier writes to his mother from a posting. A son apologizes for not writing. A brother sends greetings to named relatives and asks about the harvest. A merchant gives instructions about a shipment. The formulae of opening and closing are conventional and what sits between them is not.

Accounts, lists, and memoranda fill out the record: what a household bought, what a workshop produced, what a festival cost, what was owed to whom.

## What a Single Document Looks Like

Descriptions of categories can obscure the texture, so it is worth walking through what three individual documents actually contain.

A census return opens with a formulaic address to the officials responsible for the registration, gives the year, and names the declarant with his father's name and his legal status. It then states the property being declared, usually a house or a share of one, in a named village or quarter. Then it lists the people: the declarant himself with his age, his wife with hers and her father's name, their children by name and age, any other relatives resident, and any slaves. It closes with a declaration that the return is truthful and a date. In a few dozen lines it supplies a household, a dwelling, and a set of ages.

A petition opens with an address to the official, gives the petitioner's name, village, and often status, and then narrates the grievance in a plain sequence: what was owed or owned, what the other party did, what damage resulted. It typically closes by asking the official to summon the offender, to compel restitution, or to record the complaint against future need, and it is dated.

### What does an actual papyrus document contain?

A census return names the declarant, his status, his property, and every household member with their age and relationship. A petition names the parties, narrates the grievance, and requests action. A private letter carries formulaic greetings around news, instructions, and requests.

A private letter begins with a greeting formula naming sender and recipient and wishing health, frequently invoking divine protection, and the wording of that invocation is what allows religious change to be tracked. The body then proceeds without preamble to whatever the letter is about: an instruction to send something, news of an illness, a complaint at not hearing from the recipient, arrangements for a journey, or a request for money. It closes with greetings to named third parties and a farewell formula.

What these have in common is that each assumes context the reader already possessed and the historian does not. The letter writer knows what the recipient knows, so nothing is explained. The petitioner knows the local circumstances, so they are compressed. The census declarant knows what the official wants, so the format carries the information.

That gap is the standing frustration and the standing discipline of the field. A papyrus is one side of a conversation whose other side is lost, and reading it well means reconstructing the assumed context from everything else known about the place and period, and marking clearly where that reconstruction begins.

## Archives

The single most valuable configuration in papyrology is not an individual document but a group of related ones, and these are called archives.

An archive is a set of papyri that belonged together in antiquity: the papers of one person, one family, one office, or one estate, deposited or discarded together and recovered together. The value is that documents illuminate each other. A single loan contract tells you a loan was made. Forty documents belonging to the same man across thirty years tell you how his property grew, whom he dealt with, what disputes he had, how his family was structured, and what happened when he died.

### Why do archives matter more than single documents?

Because documents from one person, family, or office across many years illuminate each other. A single contract records one transaction; an archive shows a life, with property changing hands, disputes running their course, family structure shifting, and the same names recurring in different roles.

Several archives have become central to the study of Roman Egypt. A village scribe's papers preserve the working life of a minor official, including practice sheets on which he copied out his own signature formula repeatedly, which is direct evidence for a man who could write his name and little else while holding a post that required literacy.

Family archives from Fayum towns follow households across generations, showing land accumulated and divided, marriages contracted, tax obligations met, and petitions lodged. Estate archives from the later period document large landholdings with their own administrative staff, granaries, and workforce.

The problem with archives is that many were dispersed. Material recovered by dealers rather than excavators was sold piece by piece, so documents that belonged together are now in different collections on different continents, and reconstructing an archive requires recognizing the same names and hands across catalogues. That reconstruction work is one of the quieter achievements of modern papyrology.

## Who Actually Wrote These Documents

The literacy question runs underneath the entire corpus, and the papyri answer it about themselves in a way that no other source can.

Documents record their own production. A contract or petition frequently ends with a subscription in a different hand, and where the party could not write, the formula states explicitly that another person wrote on his or her behalf because they did not know letters. Counting those formulae across large numbers of documents produces an estimate of writing ability among the people who appear in them.

The results indicate that full literacy was a minority accomplishment, that it was more common among men than women, more common in the metropoleis than the villages, and more common among the propertied. Estimates for the population as a whole fall in a low range, and the article on [daily life in Roman Egypt](/2008/07/01/daily-life-in-roman-egypt/) discusses the figures.

### Who wrote the papyrus documents?

Professional scribes for most formal documents, with parties signing or having someone sign for them. Documents state explicitly when a party could not write, and counting those formulae shows full literacy was a minority accomplishment, more common among men, in towns, and among the propertied.

Professional scribes handled formal documents. Villages had scribes, nome capitals had more, and the writing of contracts, petitions, and official paperwork was a trade with its own standards and formulae. The uniformity of documentary language across the province is the product of that professional culture.

A striking piece of evidence for the limits of official literacy is a village scribe's practice sheet on which the holder of a writing-based post copied out his own signature formula repeatedly, and reproduced an error each time, which indicates a man who had learned to reproduce a fixed sequence of letters without reading. Signature literacy of that kind was evidently sufficient to hold a post that required documents to be produced, because the actual writing could be delegated.

Between full literacy and none lay gradations that the documents capture. People described as slow writers appear, indicating partial ability. Others could read but not write. Others could sign but not compose. The papyri show a spectrum rather than a binary, and that finding has changed how ancient literacy is discussed generally.

Private letters complicate the picture usefully. A letter dictated to a scribe carries the sender's words in someone else's hand, which means the voice may be authentic even when the writing is not. Some letters show a change of hand for a closing greeting, where the sender added a few words personally, and those additions are frequently the most direct thing in the document.

## Karanis: The Excavated Town

If Oxyrhynchus demonstrates what quantity can produce, a second Fayum site demonstrates what context can produce, and the contrast between them is the most instructive methodological lesson in the field.

Karanis was a village on the northern edge of the Fayum, founded under the Ptolemies, occupied through the Roman period, and gradually abandoned as the irrigation that sustained it failed. Because it was abandoned rather than rebuilt, its houses survived to a considerable height, filled with wind-blown sand, with their contents in place.

An American university expedition excavated it over more than a decade in the early twentieth century, and the approach was different in kind from the mound digging that had preceded it. Rooms were recorded. Objects were plotted. Papyri were noted where they were found rather than merely collected. Houses were identified as units, and the material from each was kept together.

### Why does the excavation of Karanis matter?

Because it recovered papyri in context. The town was abandoned rather than rebuilt, so houses survived with contents in place, and an early twentieth-century expedition recorded where each document was found. That allows a text to be connected to a specific room, building, and household.

The result is a body of material that can be read spatially. A document found in a particular room of a particular house, alongside pottery, tools, textiles, and food remains from the same room, is a different kind of evidence from the same document extracted from a mound. It becomes possible to ask what a household owned, where it kept its papers, and how the papers relate to the objects around them.

Karanis also supplies something a rubbish mound cannot: negative evidence. If a house has no papyri in it, that is a fact about the house. In a mound, absence means nothing.

The general principle is one that applies far beyond Egypt. Objects without provenance can be described; objects with recorded context can be interrogated. Nineteenth-century collecting produced enormous quantities of the first kind, and the shift toward the second is the single most important methodological development in the archaeology of the period.

## What the Papyri Say About Writing Itself

A small and revealing category consists of documents that discuss documents, and they open a window onto how ancient people related to their own paperwork.

Complaints about lost or withheld documents appear regularly. Somebody's deed cannot be produced. A guardian will not hand over papers relating to an estate. A creditor claims a receipt was never issued. Those petitions establish that ordinary people understood documents as things that protected them and reacted with alarm when they went missing.

Registration and archiving appear as a routine concern. Roman Egypt maintained property registers and record offices, and documents refer to depositing copies, obtaining certified extracts, and consulting the archives to establish title or status. A society that argues about what is in the archive is a society with a functioning archive.

### Do the papyri discuss writing and record-keeping themselves?

Frequently, and revealingly. Petitions complain about documents lost, withheld, or never issued in the first place. Others refer to registering deeds, obtaining certified copies, and consulting record offices to establish title or status. Letters mention sending, copying, and buying books, sometimes requesting specific titles by name.

Letters mention books. Requests to have a work copied, notes about sending a text to someone, and references to buying and borrowing appear, which is direct evidence for how literature circulated outside the great collections. A provincial reader asking a correspondent to obtain a copy of something is the ancient book trade seen from below.

Instructions about writing appear too: directions to a scribe, requests to write back promptly, complaints about not receiving replies, and notes on where a letter should be sent. Communication over distance was slow and unreliable, and the anxiety about it in the letters is one of the more human features of the corpus.

School exercises complete the picture from the other end. Alphabets written out repeatedly, syllables drilled, maxims copied, and passages of poetry laboriously transcribed by children learning to form letters. Those exercises are the origin of the whole system: somebody at some point sat down and learned to do this, and the evidence of the learning survived alongside the evidence of the doing.

## The Physical Book

The papyri are evidence about the history of the book itself, and that evidence exists nowhere else in comparable quantity.

The standard ancient book was a roll. Sheets joined edge to edge produced a long strip, written in columns across it, read by unrolling with one hand while rolling up with the other. A roll had a practical maximum length, which is why long works were divided into books, and the division of ancient literary works into books reflects the physical constraint of the medium rather than any authorial plan.

The layout is recoverable from surviving pieces. Column width, margins, line spacing, the presence or absence of word division, the use of marks to indicate speakers in drama or to flag noteworthy passages, and the placement of titles at the end rather than the beginning of a work are all visible.

### What do papyri show about the ancient book?

That the standard form was the roll, read by unrolling column by column, with titles at the end and no word division. They also record the transition to the codex, the bound stack of leaves, which Christian texts adopted early and which had displaced the roll for most purposes by late antiquity.

The codex is the great change and the papyri document it. A codex is a stack of leaves folded and bound at one edge, which is the format of every modern book. It uses both sides of a sheet, which halves the material cost. It is more compact, more durable, and far easier to consult, since a reader can open it at a chosen point instead of unrolling to it.

The Egyptian evidence shows the transition happening over several centuries, with the roll dominant in the earlier period and the codex progressively displacing it. It also shows a striking distributional fact: Christian texts appear overwhelmingly in codex form from very early, at a time when literary texts generally were still rolls. Explanations for that preference are debated and the pattern itself is clear.

The consequences of the transition run far beyond format. As the article on [the Library and Lighthouse of Alexandria](/2007/10/01/library-lighthouse-of-alexandria/) argues, the change acted as a filter on ancient literature, because a work survived only if somebody chose to copy it into the new format, and copying was expensive. What was still being taught crossed over; what had fallen out of use did not.

## Languages and Scripts

The papyri are multilingual and the distribution of languages is itself historical evidence.

Greek dominates overwhelmingly. It was the language of government, law, and commerce throughout the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, and the great bulk of documentary material is in it. Anyone working on this material needs Greek before anything else, and the Greek in question is the common form used across the eastern Mediterranean rather than the classical Attic of the Athenian authors.

Egyptian appears in two written forms. Demotic, the cursive script used for legal and administrative purposes, is well represented in the Ptolemaic and early Roman material and declines through the Roman period as Greek legal forms displace it and the temple scribal tradition that maintained it contracts. Hieratic, the older cursive script, appears in religious contexts.

### What languages are Egyptian papyri written in?

Greek overwhelmingly, since it was the language of government, law, and commerce. Egyptian appears as demotic and, in religious contexts, hieratic, and later as Coptic. Latin is confined largely to military and citizenship documents. Aramaic, Hebrew, and later Arabic also occur.

Coptic, the Egyptian language written in a Greek-derived alphabet with additional characters, appears from around the third century and grows steadily, carrying Christian scripture, liturgy, monastic correspondence, and eventually ordinary documents. Its emergence is covered in the article on [the Coptic language and its history](/2009/07/01/coptic-language-and-its-history/), and its appearance in the papyri is one of the ways the Christianization of the country can be tracked.

Latin occupies a narrow band: military documents, citizenship records, certain legal instruments, and communication with the imperial centre. Its scarcity is itself a finding, since it shows Rome governed Egypt in Greek rather than imposing its own language.

Aramaic appears in earlier material, notably from a Jewish garrison community of the Persian period, and Hebrew occurs. Arabic appears after the conquest of the 640s, initially alongside Greek in a bilingual administration.

Bilingual and archival mixtures are common. A single archive may contain Greek contracts, demotic receipts, and later Coptic letters, which tells us directly about the linguistic world its owners moved in.

## The Later Papyri

Attention concentrates on the Roman period and the later material is substantial, historically important, and frequently overlooked.

Byzantine-period documents, from the fourth century onward, are abundant and they show a changed society. Christian formulae are standard in letters. Church and monastic institutions appear as landowners, employers, and parties to contracts. Large estates with their own administration dominate parts of the rural economy, and the archives of such estates document their operations in detail: leases, wage payments, transport arrangements, and accounts.

Coptic material grows steadily across the same period. Monastic correspondence, rules, letters between religious communities, and eventually ordinary legal documents in Coptic supply a body of evidence in the Egyptian language for a population that had previously left almost no written trace in its own tongue.

### What do the later papyri show?

A Christianized society with church and monastic institutions as major landowners and employers, large estates with their own administrations, Coptic in growing use for monastic and then ordinary documents, and after the Arab conquest a bilingual administration operating in Greek and Arabic.

The conquest period is documented in a way that no other transition in Egyptian history is. Documents from the years immediately after the Arab arrival in the 640s show the administration continuing to operate, tax demands being issued, and Greek remaining in use alongside Arabic. Bilingual documents from this period, with the same content in both languages, are among the most direct evidence anywhere for how a change of empire was actually managed at ground level.

That continuity is one of the recurring findings of this series. The article on [Egypt as the breadbasket of Rome](/2008/06/01/egypt-breadbasket-of-rome/) traces the survival of the assessment and collection machinery across four conquests, and the later papyri are the proof: the same kinds of document, produced by the same kinds of official, for a new government.

Arabic papyri continue the record for centuries afterward, which means Egypt's documentary sequence runs from the Ptolemaic period into the Islamic centuries with no fundamental break. Very few places anywhere can offer that.

## Papyrology as a Discipline

The field that grew up around this material has its own methods, institutions, and habits, and knowing them helps a reader assess what they encounter.

It emerged as a distinct discipline at the end of the nineteenth century, when the volume of material coming out of Egypt made ad hoc treatment impossible. Editions began appearing in numbered series, each volume presenting texts with transcription, translation, and commentary, and those series have continued for over a century.

Training combines several skills that rarely sit together. A papyrologist needs Greek to a high standard, including the documentary formulae that classical training does not cover. Palaeography, the reading of hands, is a separate competence acquired by practice. Knowledge of Roman administration, law, and metrology is required to interpret what documents say. Demotic or Coptic is needed for parts of the corpus. And physical handling and conservation awareness matter for anyone working with originals.

### What is papyrology?

The discipline of recovering, conserving, reading, editing, and interpreting texts written on papyrus. It combines Greek and other ancient languages, palaeography for reading damaged hands, knowledge of ancient law and administration for interpretation, and conservation science for handling the physical objects.

The community works collectively to an unusual degree. Because material is scattered across institutions and archives were dispersed by dealers, progress depends on scholars recognizing connections between texts held in different countries. International congresses, shared catalogues, and collaborative projects have been features of the field since early in its history.

Digital resources have changed working practice substantially. Databases aggregating published texts allow searching across the entire published corpus for a word, a formula, a name, or a place, which turns questions that would once have required a lifetime of reading into queries answerable in an afternoon. High-resolution images of originals allow readings to be checked without travel. Collaborative platforms permit corrections and improved readings to be recorded against published texts.

Prosopography, the study of individuals across documents, has benefited most from this. Following one name through tax registers, contracts, and petitions was previously feasible only for the most famous archives; searchable databases make it possible generally, and reconstructing a person's affairs from scattered mentions is now a standard method.

## How Papyrologists Read Them

The discipline that turns a brown fragment into a historical statement is worth describing, because the process is invisible in the finished edition and it determines how much confidence any statement deserves.

Conservation comes first. A papyrus recovered from the ground may be rolled, folded, crumpled, encrusted with salt or plaster, torn, or fused into a block with others. Unrolling a brittle roll without destroying it requires humidification and patience. Cartonnage requires separating layers glued with plaster. Fragments are cleaned, flattened, and mounted between glass or in inert supports.

Imaging follows and has transformed what can be read. Photography under different wavelengths, particularly in the infrared, can recover ink that is invisible to the eye against a darkened background, and carbonized material that appears uniformly black can yield text under the right imaging.

### How do historians read ancient papyri?

Through a sequence: conservation and flattening, imaging including infrared to recover faded ink, transcription letter by letter using standard conventions to mark damage and uncertainty, identification of the text or document type, dating by handwriting and internal evidence, and publication with commentary.

Transcription is the core skill and it is harder than it sounds. Ancient documentary hands vary from careful book scripts to rapid administrative cursive that runs letters together, abbreviates freely, and assumes the reader knows the formulae. Words are not separated. Damage removes letters, words, and lines. The transcriber works out what is there, what is probably there, and what cannot be recovered.

Identification follows. A documentary text has to be recognized as a lease, a receipt, a petition, or whatever it is, which is possible because the formulae are standardized. A literary text has to be matched against known works, and if it matches nothing, it has to be characterized by metre, dialect, subject, and style.

Reconstruction fills gaps where the formulae permit. If a contract's opening is damaged but the surviving portion is a standard lease, the missing elements can often be supplied with confidence, and honest editions mark exactly which letters are read, which are inferred, and which are guessed.

Publication puts the text into circulation with a transcription, a translation, notes on readings, and a commentary placing it in context. That is the point at which a fragment becomes usable evidence for anyone other than its editor.

## The Conventions of an Edition

A short guide to the notation repays the effort, because published papyri are unreadable without it and the notation encodes exactly how much the editor is claiming.

Square brackets mark a gap in the papyrus where the material is lost. Letters printed inside them are the editor's restoration, which means an informed guess; letters absent inside them mean the gap could not be filled. A reader can therefore see instantly how much of a quoted passage is actually on the papyrus.

Dots beneath letters mark uncertainty: the traces are consistent with the letter printed but do not establish it. A word with several dotted letters is a proposal rather than a reading.

### What do the brackets and dots in papyrus editions mean?

Square brackets mark where the papyrus is lost, with any letters inside them supplied by the editor as a restoration. Dots beneath letters mark uncertain readings where the surviving traces are consistent with the letter but do not prove it. The notation shows exactly how much is evidence and how much is inference.

Angle brackets mark letters the editor has added because the scribe omitted them in error. Braces mark letters the scribe wrote that the editor thinks should be deleted. Parentheses expand abbreviations that the scribe wrote in short form.

Numbers in the margin count the lines, and a horizontal rule or note marks where the text changes hand, which matters because a document signed by a different writer, or a subscription added by an official, is evidence about who was present and who could write.

That system is one of the quiet triumphs of nineteenth and twentieth-century scholarship. It allows an editor to publish a damaged text while showing the reader precisely where the evidence stops and the reconstruction begins, and any historical argument built on a papyrus should be checked against it. A sensational claim about an ancient document frequently turns out, on inspection of the edition, to rest on heavily restored text.

## Dating a Papyrus

Establishing when a text was written is prior to almost every historical use of it, and the methods differ sharply between documentary and literary material.

Documentary papyri frequently date themselves. Roman Egyptian documents were commonly dated by the regnal year of the reigning emperor, the Egyptian month, and the day, and a text carrying that formula can be placed to a specific day. Where the emperor's name is damaged, the regnal year alone may narrow the range, and consular dating, indiction cycles in later periods, and references to datable officials all help.

That self-dating is one of the great advantages of the Egyptian material over almost any other ancient evidence. A historian working with Roman inscriptions or literary texts usually has approximate dates; a papyrologist frequently has an exact one.

### How are papyri dated?

Documentary papyri often date themselves by the reigning emperor's regnal year, month, and day, which gives an exact date. Literary papyri have no internal date and are dated by handwriting, compared against the securely dated documentary hands, which gives a range of decades rather than a year.

Literary papyri carry no such information, and they are dated palaeographically: by comparing the handwriting with hands whose date is known. This is where the documentary material repays the debt, because dated documents supply a securely anchored sequence of scripts against which undated literary hands can be placed.

The method's precision should not be overstated, and this is a point where popular accounts frequently go wrong. Palaeographic dating gives a range, typically of several decades and sometimes of a century, because individual scribes were trained at a particular moment and wrote in the manner of their training for the rest of their lives. A confident single-year date for a literary fragment is not something the method can deliver.

Physical evidence supplements both approaches. The reuse of a sheet gives a relative sequence, since the text on the back is later than the text on the front. Archaeological context, where it survives, provides an outside limit. Radiocarbon dating of the papyrus establishes when the plant was harvested rather than when the text was written, which is useful for detecting forgery and blunt for dating composition.

## What the Papyri Changed

The historiographical impact is the reason this material matters beyond Egypt, and it can be stated field by field.

Economic history was transformed. Prices for wheat, wine, land, animals, and labour appear in thousands of ordinary transactions across centuries, which allows the construction of price series of a kind that exists for no other ancient society. Interest rates, rents, wages, and transport costs can all be studied from documents rather than inferred from literary complaints about the cost of living.

Demography was created rather than transformed. Census returns record households with every member's age, sex, relationship, and status, and enough survive to support statistical analysis. Ancient demography as a quantitative discipline rests very largely on this material.

### Why are Egyptian papyri so important to historians?

Because they are the only body of evidence that lets an ancient society be studied at the level of individual households and transactions. They created ancient demography, transformed economic history through price and wage series, and supplied the first quantitative evidence on literacy, women's legal capacity, and religious change.

Social history was rewritten. Women appear as property owners, litigants, creditors, and correspondents in numbers that overturned assumptions drawn from prescriptive literary sources. Slavery, apprenticeship, tenancy, and wage labour can be studied through the arrangements people actually made. Literacy can be estimated rather than guessed, using the formula by which a document records that someone else wrote on behalf of a party who did not know letters.

Religious history acquired a measure. The proportion of Christian names and Christian letter formulae in dated documents allows the pace of conversion to be counted rather than described, as the article on [how Christianity came to Egypt](/2008/08/01/how-christianity-came-to-egypt/) sets out.

Administrative history became concrete. The machinery of assessment, collection, liturgy nomination, and appeal can be followed through the documents it generated, which turns the study of Roman provincial government from a reading of imperial legislation into an examination of what actually happened at village level.

Literary history gained works that had been lost since antiquity, and it also gained something less obvious: evidence about the ancient book itself, including format, layout, scribal practice, and the transition from roll to codex.

## Three Things the Papyri Overturned

Abstract claims about historiographical impact are less persuasive than specific reversals, and three are worth setting out because in each case the papyri contradicted what the literary sources had established.

The first concerns women. Greek and Roman prescriptive literature describes women as legally dependent, confined to the household, and requiring male authorization for transactions, and for centuries that description was taken as a picture of ancient reality. The Egyptian documents show women owning property in their own names, buying and selling, lending money, appearing in court, initiating divorce, and managing estates, in numbers too large to be exceptional. The explanation lies partly in Egyptian legal tradition, which granted women rights that Greek law did not, and the finding forced a general reconsideration of how far prescriptive texts describe practice anywhere.

The second concerns the ancient economy. An older model held that ancient economies were overwhelmingly subsistence-based, barely monetized, with markets marginal and credit rare. The papyri show coin circulating in villages, wages paid in money, banks handling deposits and transfers, loans at recorded interest rates, an active land market with property changing hands repeatedly, and price series responsive to supply. That evidence has not settled the debate about how sophisticated ancient economies were, and it has made the minimalist position much harder to hold.

### What did the papyri prove that literary sources had got wrong?

That women held and used substantial legal and economic rights despite prescriptive literature describing them as dependent; that villages were monetized with banks, credit, and active land markets rather than being purely subsistence economies; and that ordinary people used written documents and the legal system constantly rather than rarely.

The third concerns literacy and legal culture. Literary sources give the impression that ordinary provincials had little contact with writing or with formal law. The documents show villagers drawing contracts for small transactions, keeping receipts as protection, submitting written petitions in enormous numbers, and expecting officials to respond. Whether or not they could write personally, they operated inside a documentary culture, using scribes when necessary.

Each reversal followed the same pattern, which is worth noticing because it generalizes. A body of prescriptive or literary evidence produced a confident picture. A body of documentary evidence produced a different one. Where the two conflict, the documents describe what people did and the literature describes what educated writers thought or wished, and the first is better evidence for behaviour.

The corollary is uncomfortable for the rest of ancient history. If the literary picture was wrong about Egypt in these three areas, there is no particular reason to assume it is right about provinces where no documents survive to check it.

## The Biases

An honest account of any body of evidence spends real space on what it distorts, and the distortions here are systematic rather than random.

Geographic bias is the largest and it has been stated already: the material comes from the Fayum and Middle and Upper Egypt, not from Alexandria or the Delta. Any statement about Roman Egyptian society is really a statement about provincial towns and villages in specific dry regions.

Chronological bias follows the same logic. The Roman and Byzantine periods are best represented because the sites that produced material were occupied then, and Ptolemaic and earlier material is thinner.

### What are the limits of papyrus evidence?

Geographic bias toward dry regions and away from Alexandria and the Delta, chronological bias toward the Roman and later periods, social bias toward those who generated documents, survival bias toward what was discarded or reused, and the fundamental limit that documents record transactions rather than thoughts.

Social bias is real and smaller than in any other ancient source. Documents were generated by people with property to record, obligations to prove, and disputes to pursue, which skews toward the propertied. Against that, the poll tax and census reached everyone, so the poorest appear as names in registers even when they appear in no other capacity, and letters written on behalf of the illiterate preserve their words.

Survival bias is the subtlest. What survives is what was thrown away, buried, or reused. Documents that mattered enough to be kept carefully in an archive in a city rotted. That means the record over-represents the ephemeral and under-represents whatever was considered worth preserving, which is close to the opposite of the bias affecting literary sources.

Fragmentation is a practical limit. Most papyri are incomplete, and reconstruction, however careful, introduces uncertainty that the notation records and that summaries of the notation frequently lose.

The deepest limit is one no amount of material solves. Documents record transactions, obligations, complaints, and instructions. They do not record what anyone believed, felt, or intended. A tax receipt proves a payment; it says nothing about whether the payer thought the tax just. Inferring attitudes from documentary evidence is inference, and the best work in the field is careful to say so.

## The Sebakh Diggers

A part of the story that rarely appears in popular accounts destroyed more than any excavator recovered, and it deserves stating.

Ancient settlement mounds contain decayed organic material of high fertilizer value, known locally as sebakh, and through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries farmers dug the mounds extensively to obtain it. The activity was legal, economically rational, and enormously destructive. Whole ancient towns were carried away in baskets to be spread on fields.

Papyri came up in the process, and that is where much of the material that reached the antiquities market originated. A digger who found paper in the sebakh could sell it, which created a supply chain from ancient mound to European collection with no record at any point of where anything came from.

### How much was lost before papyri were valued?

An enormous and unquantifiable amount. Ancient settlement mounds were dug extensively for their fertilizer value through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, destroying stratigraphy and whole sites. Papyri surfaced in the process and entered the antiquities market without any record of origin.

The losses run in several directions. Documents were destroyed outright, torn or crumbled in digging. Context was destroyed even where documents survived, so texts that could have been connected to a house, a room, or an archive arrived as isolated fragments. Archives were split among buyers. And sites were removed entirely, so that later excavation had nothing to work with.

The excavators who came to these mounds were often working in areas already partly dug, and their accounts describe racing the sebakh diggers to reach material first. That framing is itself worth noting: the scientific recovery of this material was in competition with its commercial destruction, and the competition was not always won.

Assessing the scale is impossible by definition, since what was destroyed left no record. What can be said is that the surviving corpus, enormous as it is, is a remainder rather than a total, and that the loss fell most heavily on exactly the contextual information that later scholarship came to value most.

## What Remains Unread

A fact that startles most readers is that the excavation of Egyptian papyri produced far more material than has ever been read, and the backlog shapes what can be said about the whole subject.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Publication requires a trained specialist to conserve, read, identify, date, and annotate each text, and difficult fragments can occupy months. The number of people qualified to do this work at any time is small. The number of texts is very large. A century of steady publication has therefore covered a minority of what has been recovered.

That backlog means every general statement about what the papyri show is a statement about the published sample. Whether the unpublished remainder would change the picture is unknowable in principle, though the sheer volume of routine documentary material suggests most of it confirms rather than overturns.

### Why does publishing papyri take so long?

Because each text requires a trained specialist to conserve, transcribe damaged handwriting, identify the document type or literary work, date it, and annotate it, and a difficult fragment can take months. The number of qualified people is small and the number of fragments is very large.

Imaging technology is changing the position at the difficult end of the spectrum. Multispectral photography recovers ink that has faded below visibility. Techniques capable of reading writing inside objects without opening them are developing, with the most dramatic applications on carbonized material that cannot be unrolled at all.

Machine assistance has begun to affect the routine end. Automated approaches to matching fragments, suggesting readings for damaged letters, and searching for parallels in the published corpus reduce some of the labour, without removing the need for expert judgment on any individual text.

The realistic assessment is that the backlog will not be cleared, and that this matters less than it sounds. Documentary papyri are highly repetitive by nature, since receipts and contracts follow standard forms, and the marginal historical return on the thousandth tax receipt is small. The material that would most repay attention is the literary and the unusual, and identifying it within the mass is itself a task that improved searching and imaging assist.

## A Note on Numbers

Quantities appear constantly in discussions of this material and most of them are estimates, so a short warning is useful.

Totals for how many papyri exist are approximations. Counting depends on what is counted: a fragment the size of a fingernail and a complete roll are both one item in some tallies and not in others. Material is held in many institutions with varying cataloguing standards, some of it unopened and unassessed. Estimates for a single major site run to several hundred thousand fragments, and that figure is an order of magnitude rather than a count.

Publication figures are firmer, since published texts are enumerable, and they establish the proportion problem: the published corpus is large in absolute terms and small relative to what has been recovered.

### How many papyri have been found?

No exact figure exists. Estimates for major sites run to several hundred thousand fragments each, and totals depend on whether a fingernail-sized scrap and a complete roll count equally. What is firmer is that the published corpus, though large, represents a minority of what has been excavated.

Percentages derived from the corpus need their own caution. A statement that a given proportion of documents show some feature is a statement about the published, surviving, and regionally skewed sample, and each of those three filters is severe. That does not make such statements useless, and it does mean they describe a sample whose relationship to the original population is unknown.

The most defensible quantitative claims in the field are comparative and internal: this feature is more common in the second century than the fourth, this formula appears in Middle Egypt and not the Fayum, this price rose against that one. Comparisons within the corpus are protected against the shared biases, because the same filters apply to both sides of the comparison.

Absolute claims about the ancient world derived from the corpus are the ones to scrutinize, and the discipline this article recommends throughout applies: ask what the sample is, ask what filtered it, and treat the number as an indication rather than a measurement.

## Ethics, Provenance, and Destruction

The field carries several live ethical problems and a responsible account states them rather than leaving them to specialist discussion.

The first concerns how material left Egypt. A substantial part of the corpus was acquired through purchase from dealers rather than through recorded excavation, particularly in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and that material arrived in European and North American collections stripped of context. The relationship between source countries and holding institutions over such acquisitions is an ongoing discussion, and stating the history plainly is the minimum an account should do.

The second concerns the market. Papyri have commercial value, and a market in antiquities creates incentives for undocumented digging, for the dispersal of archives across buyers, and for forgery. Fragments continue to surface without provenance, and the scholarly community has moved toward stricter standards about publishing material whose history cannot be established, on the reasoning that publication confers value and thereby funds further looting.

### What ethical problems does papyrology face?

Four principal ones: the acquisition of much material through dealers rather than recorded excavation, an antiquities market that funds undocumented digging and disperses archives among buyers, forgeries entering collections and publication, and the destruction of painted funerary objects when cartonnage is dissolved to extract the papyri inside it.

The third is the cartonnage problem and it is genuinely difficult, because both sides of it are defensible. Mummy masks and cases made from layered waste papyrus contain documents that may be historically important. Extracting them requires dissolving the plaster, which destroys the painted object. One side argues that the texts are irreplaceable evidence and the object is a mass-produced funerary item; the other argues that the object is itself an artefact with archaeological and artistic value, that destroying artefacts to extract texts is exactly what the discipline has spent a century learning not to do, and that improved imaging may eventually read the layers without separation.

Non-destructive imaging is the direction of travel and it is not yet a complete solution. Techniques capable of reading ink through layers without dismantling an object exist and are developing, and where they can be applied they remove the dilemma entirely.

The fourth problem is publication itself. Enormous quantities of excavated material remain unpublished, in some cases for a century or more, which means evidence exists that no historian can use. That backlog is a resource problem rather than an ethical failing, and it has consequences: statements about what the papyri show are statements about the published fraction.

## Using a Papyrus in an Argument

For a student or teacher building an argument on this material, four habits separate a strong treatment from a weak one.

The first is to check the edition before quoting. Square brackets show where the papyrus is lost and any letters inside them are the editor's restoration; dots beneath letters show uncertain readings. A striking phrase may be mostly reconstruction, and an argument resting on it is resting on a scholar's inference rather than on ancient evidence.

The second is to establish the date and place. Documentary papyri usually date themselves, and the place of writing is often stated or inferable. An argument that treats a document from a Fayum village in the second century as evidence for Egypt generally, or for the Roman world generally, has skipped a step that a reader will notice.

The third is to ask what kind of document it is and what that kind can support. A petition is a partisan account of a grievance and it proves that a complaint was made in a certain form. A contract proves an agreement was drafted. A tax receipt proves a payment. None of them proves what the parties thought about any of it.

### How should a papyrus be used as evidence?

Check the edition to see how much is restored, establish the date and place rather than generalizing from one village, match the claim to what that document type can actually support, and treat a single text as an instance rather than a pattern unless many parallels exist.

The fourth is to distinguish an instance from a pattern. A single vivid document is memorable and proves only that something happened once. The strength of this material lies in numbers: hundreds of leases showing standard terms, thousands of receipts producing a price series, dozens of census returns supporting a demographic claim. An argument built on one document should say so; an argument built on a corpus should cite the corpus.

A fifth habit is worth adding for anyone writing at length. Say where the evidence comes from geographically, because the whole field rests on material from dry regions, and a sentence acknowledging that is the clearest signal that a writer understands what they are working with.

## The People Who Appear in Them

The final thing worth saying about the documents before the questions is what they contain that no summary conveys, which is people.

The papyri are populated. A soldier posted far from home writes to his mother, complains that nobody has written back, and reports that he is well. A man writes to his brother about a legal case and asks him to hurry. A woman petitions about a husband who has left and taken her property. A father arranges an apprenticeship for his son with a weaver, specifying the term, the training, the clothing allowance, and the penalties on both sides. A village official reports that people have fled and he cannot meet the assessment for which he is liable.

None of these people is important. None of them appears in any literary source. Almost none of them would be recoverable in any other province of the Roman empire, where the same lives were lived and the paper rotted.

### What do papyri reveal about ordinary people?

Their names, ages, households, property, debts, work, disputes, and family relationships, and in letters something of their voices. They record soldiers writing home, women petitioning over property, fathers apprenticing sons, and officials reporting that villagers have fled to escape obligations.

What emerges is not a flattering or unflattering picture but a recognizable one. People worried about money, disputed with neighbours, complained about officials, arranged marriages, worried about absent relatives, chased debts, avoided obligations where they could, and wrote to each other about all of it.

The letters carry the most. Formulae open and close them and what sits between is unguarded, because a letter to a brother is not a performance. Requests, reproaches, jokes, anxieties, and instructions appear without the shaping that literary composition imposes.

There is a specific value in the ordinariness. Ancient history read through its literature is a history of crisis, achievement, and argument, because those are what writers wrote about. Read through its paperwork, it becomes a history of maintenance: crops sown, taxes paid, contracts drawn, disputes settled, children apprenticed, letters sent. Most of human experience is maintenance, and Egypt is the one place in the ancient world where it left a record.

## Five Claims Tested

Because this material attracts confident description, five common claims deserve grading.

The first claim is that the papyri give us ordinary ancient life. This is right and needs a qualifier. They give ordinary life in provincial Egyptian towns and villages in dry regions during particular centuries, which is far closer to ordinary life than any other ancient source reaches, and is not a general portrait of the Roman world.

The second claim is that Egypt's papyri survived because Egyptians were unusually literate or bureaucratic. This fails as an explanation. Other provinces generated paperwork too; theirs rotted. The Egyptian record reflects climate and geology, not exceptional record-keeping, though the Roman administration in Egypt was genuinely thorough.

The third claim is that Oxyrhynchus has been published. This fails. Estimates put the material recovered in the hundreds of thousands of fragments, publication has proceeded in volumes for well over a century, and a substantial majority remains unpublished because each text requires expert work that cannot be scaled.

The fourth claim is that a papyrus gives direct unmediated access to the past. This overstates. Editions involve transcription of damaged text, restoration of gaps, and interpretation of formulae, and the notation exists precisely to show where evidence ends and inference begins. A quoted passage may be substantially the editor's.

The fifth claim is that the papyri tell us what ancient people thought. This fails. They record what people did, paid, owned, claimed, and instructed. Belief and attitude are inferences from behaviour, and the strongest work in the field marks them as such.

### Which claims about the papyri are securely evidenced?

The preservation mechanism, the find contexts, the range of document types, the self-dating of documentary texts, the transformation of economic and demographic history, and the systematic biases are all firm. Total quantities, the representativeness of the sample, and any inference about belief are not.

## Why This Matters Beyond Egypt

The largest question in the field is whether Egyptian evidence describes Egypt or describes the Roman world, and it deserves setting out because it determines how much of ancient history depends on this material.

The case for treating Egypt as typical rests on structural similarity. Roman provincial administration used comparable instruments across the empire: censuses, land taxes, liturgies, petitions to governors, and standard legal forms. The economic environment was broadly shared, with agricultural production, limited transport technology, and a common currency system after the third century. If the framework was similar, evidence from one province may illuminate the others.

The case against rests on the peculiarities established throughout this series. Egypt was governed by an equestrian prefect with senators barred from entering. It ran a poll tax assessed by inherited legal category. It operated a closed currency zone for three centuries. Its agriculture depended on an annual flood rather than rainfall. Its administrative machinery was inherited from a pharaonic and Ptolemaic tradition that no other province had.

### Can Egyptian papyri be used as evidence for the Roman world generally?

Cautiously, and only with the differences stated openly. Roman provinces shared administrative instruments and broad economic conditions, which supports careful comparison, but Egypt alone had an equestrian governor, a status-based poll tax, a closed currency zone, flood-based agriculture, and administrative machinery inherited from the pharaohs.

The pragmatic position most working historians adopt is comparison rather than extrapolation. Egyptian material establishes what was possible, what forms documents took, how procedures worked in practice, and what orders of magnitude apply to prices, yields, and household sizes. Applying a specific Egyptian figure to another province is unsafe; using the Egyptian evidence to check whether a general claim is plausible is reasonable.

There is a subtler argument worth registering. Some of what looks distinctively Egyptian may simply be what any Roman province looked like from close up, and the appearance of exceptionalism may be an artefact of being the only province visible at that resolution. Nobody can test that, because the comparative material does not exist.

What is not in doubt is the dependence. Ancient demography, ancient price history, the study of literacy, the study of women's legal position, and the study of provincial administration all rest substantially on Egyptian papyri. Remove them and large areas of ancient social and economic history become guesswork again.

## What Would Change the Picture

A field this dependent on chance survival should say what discoveries or developments would alter its conclusions, and three would.

The first would be documents from Alexandria or the Delta. Everything in this article is qualified by the absence of evidence from the province's capital and its most populous region, and material from either would test whether provincial patterns hold at the centre. Waterlogged deposits, carbonized material from a fire, or an unusually dry deposit could in principle supply it, and nothing of the kind has appeared at scale.

The second would be a substantial reduction in the publication backlog. Statements about proportions, about how common a practice was, and about regional variation all rest on the published sample, and a large increase in published material could shift them. Improved imaging and automated assistance make that more plausible than it was.

### What discoveries would change what the papyri show?

Documents from Alexandria or the Delta, which would test whether provincial patterns held at the centre; a large reduction in the unpublished backlog, which could shift claims about how common practices were; and comparable documentary finds from another Roman province, which would settle how far Egypt was typical.

The third would be comparable documentary material from another Roman province, which would settle the representativeness question that currently cannot be tested. Nothing suggests it is coming, since the climates required do not exist in the relevant places, and its absence is why the debate about Egyptian exceptionalism remains open.

A fourth possibility is technical rather than archaeological. Techniques capable of reading text inside sealed or fused objects without opening them would unlock cartonnage without destroying it, would allow rolls too brittle to open to be read, and would resolve the ethical dilemma described above. Progress in that direction is real and its eventual reach is not yet known.

Stating this matters for the same reason it matters in any evidential field. A body of evidence whose limits are named can be used confidently within them. A body of evidence presented as complete invites conclusions it cannot support, and the papyri are large enough to tempt exactly that mistake.

## The Roll, the Mound, and the Museum

Following a single hypothetical document from manufacture to publication draws the whole article together and shows how many accidents lie between an ancient act of writing and a modern historical statement.

A sheet is cut from marsh reed pith in the Delta, pressed, dried, and joined into a roll. The roll is sold, transported, and cut into sheets by a dealer or a scribe. A village scribe in a Fayum town uses one sheet to write a lease between two cultivators, in Greek, dated by the emperor's regnal year, witnessed and signed.

The lease is kept by one party for as long as it matters. When the term expires and the obligations are discharged, it becomes waste. It is thrown onto the town's rubbish mound, where it is trampled, compressed, and buried under successive layers of household refuse. The town is eventually abandoned as irrigation fails, and the mound sits above the water table, dry, for seventeen centuries.

### What has to go right for a papyrus to reach a historian?

A great deal: the document must be discarded rather than carefully stored, buried somewhere permanently dry, left undisturbed for centuries, recovered by an excavator rather than a fertilizer digger, conserved, transcribed by a specialist, and finally published in an edition that shows what is read and what is restored.

In the late nineteenth century the mound is dug, either by farmers seeking fertilizer, in which case the lease is probably destroyed or sold without record, or by excavators, in which case it is collected with a note of where it was found. Assume the second. It is packed, shipped, and deposited in an institution.

There it waits, sometimes for decades, until a specialist has time. It is cleaned, flattened, and mounted. It is photographed, possibly in infrared to recover faded ink. A papyrologist transcribes it letter by letter, works out that it is a lease from the standard formulae, restores the damaged opening from parallels, reads the date, and identifies the parties.

It is published with a transcription marking every restored letter, a translation, and a commentary. It enters a database. A historian studying agricultural tenancy searches for lease terms, finds it among two hundred others, and includes it in a statistical claim about rent shares in second-century Middle Egypt.

Every step in that chain could have failed and most documents ever written did fail at one of them. What survives is the residue of an enormous filtering process, and remembering the chain is the best possible protection against treating the surviving evidence as though it were the ancient world itself rather than the small dry corner of it that happened to last.

## Papyri and the Rest of Egyptian History

A final placement is useful, because this article covers the Roman period and the papyrological record runs far beyond it in both directions.

Earlier Egyptian writing survives on papyrus too, in hieratic and demotic, including literary works, medical and mathematical texts, administrative records, and religious compositions. Those materials are the basis for what is known about pharaonic administration and thought, and they survive by the same mechanism of dry burial. The Roman-period corpus is larger and more documentary in character, but it is a phase in a much longer sequence rather than a separate phenomenon.

Later material continues past the Arab conquest, in Greek, Coptic, and Arabic, documenting the early Islamic administration and the long linguistic transition. Egypt therefore has a documentary record running with interruptions from the pharaonic period into the medieval centuries, which no other region approaches.

### How far back and forward does the papyrus record run?

From the pharaonic period, with hieratic and demotic literary, medical, mathematical, and administrative texts, through the Ptolemaic and Roman centuries when Greek documentary material dominates, and forward past the Arab conquest into the Islamic period in Greek, Coptic, and Arabic.

What changes across that span is not the survival mechanism but the character of what was written and by whom. Pharaonic material is weighted toward temple and state institutions and toward literary and religious composition. Ptolemaic and Roman material is weighted toward the documentary paperwork of a bureaucratized province. Later material shifts toward monastic and ecclesiastical concerns alongside the continuing administration.

That progression is itself a historical finding. It tracks the spread of writing from a restricted priestly and official function to a routine instrument of ordinary life, and back toward institutional concentration in the monastic period, and it can be observed because the same climate preserved all three phases.

The article on [how we know about predynastic Egypt](/2000/09/01/how-we-know-about-predynastic-egypt/) approaches the same question from before writing existed, where everything must be inferred from material remains. Setting the two together defines the range of the problem: at one end a society reconstructed entirely from pottery and burials, at the other a society whose villagers left their tax receipts, and the same country in both cases.

## The Honest Verdict

The papyri of Roman Egypt are the closest thing the ancient world offers to a record of itself written by people who were not trying to be remembered.

That is the whole value and it is worth stating precisely. Monuments are arguments. Literature is selection, twice over: what an educated minority chose to write, and what later generations chose to keep copying. Inscriptions are public statements. All three were produced with an audience in mind, and all three tell us what somebody wanted known.

A tax receipt has no audience beyond the man who might otherwise be asked to pay twice. A private letter has an audience of one. A petition wants an official's attention and nothing else. A school exercise wants to be finished. None of this material was composed for posterity, and that absence of intention is exactly what makes it trustworthy in a way that no monument can be.

The accident that preserved it should be kept in view because it is the argument's foundation and its limit. These documents survived because they were thrown away in places where nothing rots. Their survival has nothing to do with their importance, which means they are a genuinely random sample of the paperwork of certain places at certain times, and a completely non-random sample of the ancient world as a whole. Alexandria generated more documents than every Fayum village combined and left almost none.

What that accident purchased is an entire discipline and, through it, whole fields of history. Ancient demography exists because of census returns from dry Egyptian towns. Ancient price history exists because villagers kept receipts. What is known about literacy, about women's legal capacity, about how a Roman province was actually administered rather than how the law said it should be, and about how a country changed religion, all rests substantially on documents that their owners considered worthless.

One further reflection belongs before that lesson, because it concerns the strange relationship between value and survival that runs through this whole subject. The Ptolemies built the greatest library of the ancient world, funded scribes to copy its holdings, catalogued them, and housed them in a palace quarter, and the collection is gone without a single roll from it surviving. A cultivator in a Fayum village threw a used receipt onto a rubbish heap and it is in a museum. The library was destroyed by the withdrawal of the funding that kept it alive, as the article on [the Library and Lighthouse of Alexandria](/2007/10/01/library-lighthouse-of-alexandria/) argues, while the receipt survived precisely because nobody spent anything on it at all. Care, in the ancient Mediterranean, meant keeping something in a building in a city, and buildings burn and cities are damp. Neglect meant leaving it in the desert, and the desert keeps things.

The lesson generalizes and it is the one to carry into any historical subject. The evidence that survives is not the evidence that mattered. It is the evidence that happened to be somewhere dry, or deep, or forgotten, and a historian's first question about any body of material should be why this survived rather than what it says. In Egypt the answer is that nobody cared enough about it to keep it safe, and it is difficult to think of a more useful accident.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What do papyri tell us about Roman Egypt?

Almost everything that is known in detail. They supply the fiscal system in operation through tax receipts and registers, household composition through census returns, land tenure through leases and sales, credit through loan contracts, and grievance through petitions about assault, theft, water disputes, and official extortion. They record prices, wages, rents, and interest rates across centuries, which allows economic series that exist for no other ancient society. They document the spread of Christianity through the changing formulae in private letters, the extent of literacy through the clauses recording that someone else wrote on a party's behalf, and the operation of provincial government at the level where it touched ordinary people rather than as imperial legislation described it.

### Q: What information do papyri contain?

An enormous range, most of it mundane and therefore valuable. Tax receipts, registers, and census returns. Land surveys recording what the flood reached. Sales of land, houses, animals, and slaves. Leases with share arrangements specified. Loans with interest and security. Wills, marriage contracts, divorce agreements, and apprenticeship contracts. Petitions to officials over injuries, thefts, diverted irrigation, and abandonment. Official correspondence and prefects' edicts. Private letters carrying news, instructions, and greetings. Accounts and inventories. School exercises with alphabets and copied maxims. Magical texts and oracle questions. And literary papyri preserving copies of known works and of texts otherwise lost since antiquity.

### Q: How were papyri preserved in Egypt?

By aridity. Organic material decays through water and the microorganisms that need it, and in genuinely dry conditions where no groundwater reaches buried material, decay effectively stops. Papyrus survived above the flood plain, in desert margins, in settlements abandoned rather than rebuilt, and in tombs cut into dry rock. It did not survive in the annually flooded valley floor, in the Delta, or in coastal Alexandria with its high water table and two thousand years of continuous rebuilding. That distribution is the single most important qualification in the field: the best-documented ancient society is documented for the places where documents survive rather than for the places where most people lived.

### Q: What was written on Egyptian papyri?

Two broad categories. Documentary texts are the paperwork of a functioning province: receipts, contracts, registers, petitions, letters, accounts, and official correspondence, overwhelmingly in Greek, with Egyptian in demotic, later Coptic, some Latin for military and citizenship matters, and Arabic after the conquest. Literary texts are copies of works, including plays, poetry, history, philosophy, and religious writings, some known from the medieval manuscript tradition and some lost until the papyri restored them. Alongside both sit school exercises, magical formulae, horoscopes, medical recipes, and the miscellaneous writing any literate society produces without expecting it to last.

### Q: How do historians read ancient papyri?

Through a sequence of specialist steps. Conservation comes first, since a papyrus may be rolled, folded, encrusted, torn, or fused with others, and unrolling brittle material without destroying it requires humidification and patience. Imaging follows, with infrared photography recovering ink invisible against darkened backgrounds. Transcription is the core skill, working letter by letter through documentary hands that run letters together, abbreviate freely, and leave no spaces between words. Identification matches the text against known document types or literary works. Dating uses internal date formulae for documents and handwriting comparison for literature. Publication then presents transcription, translation, and commentary using conventions that mark exactly what is read and what is restored.

### Q: What was the Oxyrhynchus papyri find?

The excavation of the ancient rubbish mounds of a provincial town in Middle Egypt by two Oxford scholars, Grenfell and Hunt, beginning in 1896 and continuing for years. They chose the site by reasoning rather than luck: a long-occupied Greek-speaking administrative centre, above the water table, not previously worked systematically. The mounds produced papyri in quantities nobody had anticipated, with estimates running to several hundred thousand fragments ranging from complete documents to scraps of a few letters. The material is both documentary, recording the business of a town across centuries, and literary, including fragments of works lost since antiquity. Publication began immediately, has continued in volumes for over a century, and remains substantially incomplete.

### Q: What do papyri reveal about ordinary people?

Their names, ages, households, property, work, debts, disputes, and something of their voices. Census returns list every member of a household with age and relationship. Contracts record what people owned and what they agreed. Petitions describe assaults at festivals, diverted irrigation water, absconding tenants, stolen animals, and officials demanding more than was owed. Private letters carry news, reproaches, instructions, and anxieties between family members separated by distance. Apprenticeship contracts show fathers placing sons with craftsmen on specified terms. None of these people appears in any literary source, and in almost any other Roman province their equivalents left no trace at all.

### Q: Why are Egyptian papyri so important to historians?

Because they are the only body of evidence that allows an ancient society to be studied at the level of individual households and transactions. Ancient demography exists as a quantitative discipline largely because Egyptian census returns survive. Ancient price and wage history rests on receipts and contracts from Egyptian villages. Literacy can be estimated rather than guessed, using the clauses recording who wrote for whom. Women's legal capacity, slavery, tenancy, and apprenticeship can be studied through the arrangements people actually made. Religious change can be counted through names and letter formulae. Remove the papyri and large areas of ancient social and economic history revert to inference from literature written by and for a tiny elite.

### Q: What is papyrus made of?

The pith of a tall sedge that grew in the marshes of the Nile Delta and along the river. The stem is triangular in section, and the pith inside it was cut into thin strips. Strips were laid side by side in one layer, a second layer was laid across them at right angles, and the whole was pressed, which released natural substances that bonded the layers as they dried. The resulting sheet was smoothed with a stone or shell. Individual sheets were joined edge to edge with a slight overlap to form rolls. The side with horizontal fibres was smoother and normally used first, with the other side available for reuse or a second text.

### Q: What lost literature has been found in the papyri?

A remarkable amount, mostly Greek. Substantial fragments of lyric poets previously known only through brief quotations in later writers have been recovered, restoring the shape and something of the voice of poets whose reputations rested entirely on other people's admiration. Fragments of Athenian tragedies lost from the manuscript tradition have appeared, including a large portion of a satyr play, along with a considerable body of Greek New Comedy that changed what could be said about the form. An anonymous Greek history covering a thinly documented period has become an independent check on the surviving narratives. Early Christian texts include fragments of gospels and a collection of sayings attributed to Jesus.

### Q: What languages are Egyptian papyri written in?

Greek overwhelmingly, since it was the language of government, law, education, and commerce throughout the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, and the form used is the common variety spoken across the eastern Mediterranean rather than classical Attic. Egyptian appears as demotic, the cursive script used for legal and administrative purposes, which declines through the Roman period, and as hieratic in religious contexts. Coptic, the Egyptian language in a Greek-derived alphabet, appears from around the third century and grows steadily. Latin is confined largely to military documents, citizenship records, and communication with the imperial centre. Aramaic occurs in earlier material and Arabic after the conquest of the 640s.

### Q: How are papyri dated?

Documentary papyri usually date themselves. Roman Egyptian documents were commonly dated by the reigning emperor's regnal year, the Egyptian month, and the day, which places a text to a specific day, and consular dating, indiction cycles, and references to datable officials help where the formula is damaged. That self-dating is a major advantage over most ancient evidence. Literary papyri carry no internal date and are dated palaeographically, by comparing the handwriting with hands whose date is known from documents, which produces a range of several decades rather than a year, because scribes wrote in the style of their training throughout their lives. Physical evidence and archaeological context supply supporting limits.

### Q: What is papyrology?

The discipline of recovering, conserving, reading, editing, and interpreting texts written on papyrus. It emerged as a distinct field at the end of the nineteenth century when the volume of Egyptian material made ad hoc treatment impossible, and it combines skills that rarely sit together: Greek to a high standard including documentary formulae that classical training does not cover, palaeography for reading damaged and cursive hands, knowledge of ancient law, administration, and measurement for interpretation, often demotic or Coptic, and awareness of conservation for handling originals. Because material is scattered across institutions and archives were dispersed by dealers, the field has always depended heavily on international collaboration and shared catalogues.

### Q: Why are papyri so fragmentary?

Because of how they survived and how they were recovered. Most came from rubbish mounds, where documents were discarded, trampled, and compressed among other waste for centuries. Papyrus becomes brittle with age, so folded or rolled material cracks along its creases. Ancient reuse involved cutting and tearing sheets. Cartonnage manufacture involved cutting documents into pieces to build up layers. Nineteenth and early twentieth-century digging for fertilizer churned mounds destructively. Excavation and transport add further damage to brittle material. The result is that complete documents are the exception, and most texts are partial, with the missing portions marked in editions by square brackets.

### Q: Why does publishing papyri take so long?

Because each text requires a trained specialist working slowly. Publication involves conservation, imaging, transcription of damaged and abbreviated handwriting with no word division, identification of the document type or literary work, reconstruction of gaps where the formulae permit, dating, and annotation placing the text in context. A difficult literary fragment can occupy a scholar for months. The number of people qualified to do this at any time is small and the number of fragments is very large, so a century of steady publication has covered only a minority of what has been recovered. Every general statement about the papyri is therefore a statement about the published sample.

### Q: What is cartonnage and why does it matter for papyri?

Cartonnage is a composite material made by layering scrap with plaster to produce a light rigid shell that could be moulded and painted, used in Egypt for mummy masks, chest covers, and foot cases. The scrap used was whatever was worthless, and in Egypt that frequently meant used papyrus, so a painted funerary object can contain dozens of discarded documents from the decades before the burial. That creates a genuine dilemma: extracting the texts requires dissolving the plaster, which destroys the object. One side values the irreplaceable documents, the other objects to destroying artefacts to obtain texts. Non-destructive imaging capable of reading layers without separation is developing and would resolve it.

### Q: What are the limits of papyrus evidence?

Five systematic biases. Geographic bias is the largest: the material comes from dry regions, not from Alexandria or the Delta, so it describes provincial towns and villages rather than the province's centre. Chronological bias favours the Roman and later periods over the Ptolemaic and earlier. Social bias favours those with property to record and obligations to prove, though the poll tax and census reached everyone. Survival bias means what remains is what was discarded, buried, or reused, which is close to the opposite of the bias affecting literary sources. And the fundamental limit is that documents record transactions, obligations, and instructions rather than beliefs, so any statement about what people thought is inference.

### Q: Are papyri found only in Egypt?

Almost only, and for reasons of climate rather than culture. Papyrus was used across the entire Greek and Roman world, since Egypt exported it as the standard writing material, but it rots wherever water reaches it, which is nearly everywhere else. Two significant exceptions exist. A library of rolls in an Italian villa was carbonized by volcanic eruption, which rendered the material inert and preserved it in a state so damaged that reading it has occupied specialists for centuries and is only now yielding to advanced imaging. Scrolls found in arid Levantine caves show that dry conditions elsewhere can preserve ancient writing. Neither approaches the Egyptian volume, and no other region supplies a documentary record.
