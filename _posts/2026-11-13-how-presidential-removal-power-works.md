## The Answer Begins With a Silence in the Text

A President may dismiss purely executive officers at will, without proving misconduct and without obtaining permission from any other institution. Congress may not reserve removal to itself, whether by requiring its own consent or by vesting dismissal in an officer answerable only to Congress. A statutory for-cause protection for an officer who wields executive power was held unconstitutional in Trump v. Slaughter, decided June 29, 2026, because a condition that allows dismissal only for stated grounds withdraws the control that Article II places in the President over the execution of federal law.

Removal power is the mechanism that decides whether a federal agency answers to an elected President or to the Congress that created it, and American law has answered by asking what kind of power the officer wields rather than what the office is called. An officer who enforces statutes, brings cases in the name of the United States, issues binding rules, or directs the spending of appropriated funds exercises executive power in substance. Tenure protection for such an officer transfers practical control away from the President even when formal appointment remains in presidential hands, because direction without dismissal is advice rather than command.

Congress retains wide authority at the creation stage. Congress establishes offices by statute, defines their duties, fixes their jurisdiction, sets qualifications, appropriates their funds, and may abolish an office altogether through legislation presented to the President. None of those powers answers the separate question of tenure after appointment. A statute that creates an office and then insulates its holder from presidential dismissal does two different things, and courts have judged the second act under Article II rather than under the power to create the office in the first place.

The distinction explains why labels in a statute carry limited weight. Congress has at different times described the same regulatory functions as independent, quasi-legislative, or quasi-judicial, and courts in earlier periods accepted some of those descriptions as a basis for protection. The functional inquiry discards the label and examines the authority actually granted. If the officer can bind private parties, select enforcement targets, or set policy that the President is charged with executing, the power is executive however the statute styles it.

That functional answer also defines the boundary of presidential control. At-will dismissal does not give a President power to direct the outcome of a particular adjudication where a statute forbids it, to spend funds Congress has not appropriated, or to retain an officer whom the Senate has removed after impeachment. Removal concerns who holds office, not a license to exceed the substantive limits Congress may lawfully impose on what the office may do. The contest in the case law has centred on whether Congress may use tenure as the instrument by which an executive officer is made answerable to someone other than the President.

## A Constitution That Names Appointment and Falls Silent on Removal

Article II opens with a vesting sentence that places the executive power in a President of the United States of America. The phrasing differs from the legislative vesting language in Article I, which grants Congress the legislative powers enumerated in the Constitution. From that difference, advocates of broad removal authority infer that executive power arrives as a general grant, limited by specific qualifications elsewhere in the text. The inference depends on whether removal is part of executive power at all, rather than a separate incident of officeholding the text leaves unassigned.

The Take Care Clause supplies a second textual anchor. Article II, Section 3 provides that the President shall take care that the laws be faithfully executed. Faithful execution is an obligation, and obligations in constitutional argument often generate implied means. If a subordinate charged with executing a statute refuses a lawful presidential direction or performs the work with neglect, the President bears constitutional responsibility for the failure while lacking, on a restrictive reading, any means to replace the subordinate. Supporters of at-will removal treat dismissal as the necessary means attached to the duty. Opponents answer that the clause imposes a duty of supervision without specifying dismissal as its instrument, leaving Congress free to choose other instruments such as reporting duties or appropriations controls.

The Appointments Clause in Article II, Section 2 describes how officers arrive without describing how they leave. Principal officers are nominated by the President and appointed with the advice and consent of the Senate. Congress may vest the appointment of inferior officers in the President alone, in the courts of law, or in the heads of departments. The clause allocates the front end of tenure in careful detail and says nothing about its end. One reading treats that silence as deliberate exclusion, on the ground that a text so precise about appointment would have stated a shared removal power if sharing had been intended. Another treats the silence as a gap open to congressional provision under the Necessary and Proper Clause.

### What does for cause removal mean in a federal statute?

Statutory phrases such as inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office confine dismissal to proven misconduct or failure, judged after notice, rather than policy disagreement. The protection replaces at-will tenure with a defined cause, a stated ground, and a process for testing whether the ground is supported.

Impeachment is the only removal mechanism the Constitution names in express terms. Article II, Section 4 provides for removal from office on impeachment for and conviction of treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanours, and Article I gives the House the sole power of impeachment and the Senate the sole power to try impeachments. Impeachment cannot serve as the ordinary method of administration, since it requires charges by one chamber, trial by the other, and a two-thirds vote to convict. A restrictive reader argues that the framers specified one removal route and thereby excluded an unqualified presidential route. A broad reader argues that impeachment addresses offences against the state, distinct from administrative dismissal for loss of confidence. Because the sparse words underdetermine the answer, courts have turned to structure and to the practice of the First Congress.

## The Decision of 1789 and the First Congress

The First Congress confronted removal in 1789 while creating a Department of Foreign Affairs, the predecessor of the Department of State. The bill had to specify, or deliberately avoid specifying, what would happen if the President sought to dismiss the secretary who headed the new department. A clause referring to the secretary being removable by the President could be read as Congress granting a power, which implied that the power came from Congress and could be conditioned by Congress. The debate therefore became an argument over the source of the power as much as over its existence.

James Madison led the argument in the House of Representatives for the proposition that removal is executive in nature. Madison contended that the executive power vested by Article II included the power to dismiss those through whom the President executed the laws, and that joining the Senate in removal would divide the responsibility that the Constitution concentrated in one person. A President answerable for faithful execution required subordinates answerable to the President, and a Senate share in dismissal would give the Senate a continuing hold over administration after appointment had ended. Madison also warned that a removable-only-with-consent rule would allow an officer to retain office by cultivating senatorial support against the President, weakening the chain that connected enforcement decisions to a single elected official. The argument rested on structure and accountability rather than on any clause naming removal.

Opponents in the debate offered positions that later litigation would revive. Some members maintained that the power to appoint jointly implied a power to remove jointly, so that Senate consent to appointment carried Senate consent to dismissal as its counterpart. Others argued that Congress, having created the office under its legislative authority, could attach the tenure conditions it judged suitable, including protection against dismissal without cause. A third position treated impeachment as the intended check on officers and resisted a presidential power that might be used to displace an officer for refusing an unlawful order. Several theories thus advanced toward the same legislative result for different reasons, a fact that complicates later descriptions of 1789 as if a single theory had prevailed.

The House adopted language that recognised presidential removal without granting it as a legislative concession. In the Senate the vote divided equally, and Vice President John Adams cast the tie-breaking vote in favour of the version that preserved presidential removal. A decision reached by one vote in one chamber, on a bill about one department, became the foundation for a general proposition about all executive officers.

Later courts treated the Decision of 1789 as weighty but not conclusive. Chief Justice William Howard Taft, writing in Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52 (1926), read the debates as a contemporaneous construction of the Constitution by many of its framers, entitled to special respect. Other judges have noted limits that keep the decision short of a binding precedent. A legislature does not adjudicate, its members vote for mixed motives, and an inference from the wording of a statute differs from a judgment entered after argument in a case. The Decision of 1789 therefore functions as evidence of original meaning, strong enough to shift burdens of persuasion, yet still open to contest.

## What the Early Republic Practised

Practice in the early republic treated presidential removal as a routine incident of administration rather than as an exceptional constitutional event. George Washington dismissed officers who had lost his confidence and expected department heads to serve in alignment with the administration that appointed them, and no established procedure required a statement of charges or a hearing before dismissal took effect. The absence of litigation over those dismissals leaves the record in a particular shape. Practice shows what Presidents did and what Congress tolerated, while the silence of the courts shows only that displaced officers seldom obtained a judicial forum in which to contest the power, not that a court had approved every exercise of it.

Thomas Jefferson gave the practice its most systematic early demonstration after taking office in 1801. Jefferson regarded many Federalist officeholders appointed in the final period of John Adams administration as opponents of the government they served, and his administration replaced substantial numbers of marshals, attorneys, customs officials, and other federal officers with supporters of the new administration. The removals were defended as necessary to give effect to an electoral verdict, on the theory that an election changing the party in power changes little if every subordinate charged with execution remains committed to the displaced programme. The episode established a durable expectation that tenure in executive office follows the administration, an expectation that operated politically even without a judicial decision defining its constitutional limits.

The federal establishment grew within that expectation without settling tenure by general law. Congress created customs districts, land offices, post offices, territorial governments, and revenue services office by office, specifying duties, salaries, bonds, and in many statutes a term of years for the appointment itself. A fixed term answered the question of when an appointment expired. It did not, without more, answer whether the President could end the appointment early, and statutes varied in whether they addressed early removal at all. The resulting pattern was a patchwork in which presidential practice supplied the default and statutory language occasionally displaced it, leaving later interpreters to argue over whether a term provision alone implied protection against dismissal before the term expired.

Patronage formed the practical background against which those arguments developed. Federal offices carried income, local standing, and party value, and appointments rewarded political service in a spoils system that both political tendencies used while in power and criticised while out of it. Dismissal secured administrative alignment, and dismissal also distributed jobs to loyalists and punished opponents. Andrew Jackson defended rotation in office as democratic, arguing that long tenure converted public office into a species of property. Critics made the inverse argument decades before general civil service protection, namely that administration required experienced officers insulated from the electoral cycle. By the middle of the nineteenth century, practice untested in court remained vulnerable to a determined congressional challenge. That challenge arrived when a President and a Congress of opposing parties fought for control of Reconstruction policy and of the Cabinet officer charged with executing it.

## The Tenure of Office Act and an Impeachment Built on a Firing

Congress enacted the Tenure of Office Act in 1867 over the veto of President Andrew Johnson. The statute provided that civil officers appointed with the advice and consent of the Senate would hold office until a successor had been appointed and confirmed, and that Cabinet secretaries would hold office for the term of the President by whom they were appointed, subject to removal with Senate consent. In substance the Act converted dismissal into a legislative transaction. A President could suspend an officer during a Senate recess, but the Senate could refuse to concur and restore the officer. The measure formed part of the contest between Johnson and the Republican majority in Congress over Reconstruction, and its purpose was to protect officeholders who supported congressional Reconstruction against a President committed to a more lenient settlement.

Secretary of War Edwin Stanton stood at the centre of the design. Stanton, a holdover from Abraham Lincoln administration, administered the War Department through which federal authority reached the military districts governing the South, and he aligned with congressional Republicans against Johnson policy. Protecting Stanton protected a policy channel, not merely a person. Johnson suspended Stanton during the recess in August 1867 and appointed General Ulysses S. Grant as secretary ad interim. When the Senate refused to concur in January 1868, Stanton resumed the office under the statute. Presidential action alone could not complete a removal against Senate opposition.

Johnson forced the confrontation in February 1868 by removing Stanton outright and appointing Lorenzo Thomas in his place, in asserted defiance of the Act. Stanton refused to surrender the office, and Thomas was arrested for violating the statute before the charge was dropped. The House of Representatives responded by impeaching Johnson, and the articles placed the Stanton removal at the heart of the case, charging that the dismissal violated the Tenure of Office Act and the Constitution. Whether Johnson had committed an impeachable offence depended in part on whether the statute he defied was itself constitutional, and senators voting on guilt had to judge the validity of a law enacted by the institution in which they sat.

The Senate acquitted Johnson by one vote, with thirty-five senators voting guilty and nineteen voting not guilty, one short of the two-thirds required for conviction. Senators gave differing reasons, and some who voted to acquit doubted that violating a statute of doubtful constitutionality supplied grounds for removing a President. An acquittal entered for mixed reasons cannot be read as a judicial holding that the Tenure of Office Act was invalid, any more than a conviction would have established its validity. Stanton left office after the acquittal, and the underlying constitutional question remained argued rather than decided.

Congress modified the Act in 1869 and repealed it in 1887 during the administration of President Grover Cleveland, who had asserted presidential authority over removal in a message to the Senate in 1886. Repeal removed the statutory obstacle without supplying a judicial determination. Each political resolution reversed or expired with the balance of power that produced it, so politics could not settle the constitutional question. A durable answer required a court with jurisdiction over a claim in which removal was decisive. That claim took another four decades to reach the Supreme Court, arriving not through a Cabinet secretary but through a postmaster seeking back pay.

## Myers and the Postmaster From Oregon

Frank L. Myers held appointment as a first-class postmaster at Portland, Oregon, an office graded by the receipts of the post office it served and placed within the postal establishment administered through the Postmaster General. Postmasters of the first class handled substantial public funds, supervised staff, and stood as the visible representative of the federal government in a commercial city. The modesty of the office by comparison with a Cabinet secretary gave the case a particular usefulness. If presidential removal prevailed even for an officer several layers below the President, the principle would rest on the nature of the power rather than on the dignity of the post.

A statute enacted in 1876 governed the tenure at issue. Congress subjected the appointment and the removal of postmasters in the first three classes to the advice and consent of the Senate, and set a term of four years subject to earlier removal through that shared process. Appointment and removal were joined in a single sentence and subjected to the same senatorial participation, embodying the symmetry argument that members of the First Congress had advanced and lost in 1789. The dispute turned on whether the middle of a four-year term could be cut short by presidential action alone.

President Woodrow Wilson removed Myers in 1920 before the four-year term had expired, without Senate consent. Myers protested the dismissal, and after his death the claim was pursued by his widow as administratrix of his estate for the salary of the unexpired portion of the term. A suit for back pay in the Court of Claims could proceed only if the removal had been unlawful, because a lawful dismissal ended any entitlement to salary for a period in which no service would be rendered. The constitutional question thus reached the courts attached to a money claim, without any need for a court to order reinstatement or to direct a sitting President.

The case reached the Supreme Court as Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52 (1926), as the vehicle for settling the question the Stanton crisis had left open. The United States defended the removal on the ground that Article II vested dismissal of executive officers in the President alone. The claimant defended the 1876 statute on the ground that Congress, which created the office and fixed its term, could condition the ending of that term on the same consent required at its beginning. No dispute about Myers conduct drove the case. The record presented a pure conflict between a statute requiring shared removal and a President asserting an exclusive power, with salary as the only remedy.

Chief Justice William Howard Taft wrote the opinion of the Court. Taft had served as President of the United States from 1909 to 1913, the only person to have held both that office and the chief justiceship, and he wrote about the practical necessities of the presidency from direct experience. A former President judging the powers of the presidency gave the opinion an authority its critics could also characterise as the perspective of an interested institution. The opinion was correspondingly large in scope, running well beyond one salary claim into a general history of removal from 1789 forward.

## The Reasoning Taft Built in Myers

Taft began from the vesting sentence of Article II and from the Take Care duty. Executive power, in the opinion, is vested in the President as a grant of the whole power of execution, subject to the express qualifications the Constitution states elsewhere, such as Senate participation in appointments and treaties. Faithful execution requires subordinates through whom the President acts, and subordinates who cannot be dismissed become independent of the official charged with answering for their performance. Taft treated control over personnel as the practical content of the Take Care obligation. A President who must retain an officer whose judgment the President distrusts remains formally responsible while being substantively displaced, a separation of responsibility from control that the opinion regarded as forbidden by the constitutional structure.

The Decision of 1789 supplied the historical proof for that structural claim. Taft read the First Congress debates as a considered construction of the Constitution by the legislature closest to its framing, concluding that Congress in 1789 had recognised a removal power belonging to the President under the Constitution rather than conferring a power of its own creation. The classification mattered as much as the conclusion. A granted power can be conditioned by the grantor, while a recognised constitutional power cannot be withdrawn by the body that acknowledged it. Senate consent to removal thus became an encroachment on a power already vested, rather than a permissible condition on an office Congress had created.

Senate consent to removal failed for a reason distinct from Senate consent to appointment. Advice and consent at appointment selects the person who will hold an office, an act completed at a single moment and expressly assigned by the Appointments Clause. Consent to removal gives the Senate a continuing share in administration, allowing it to keep in office an officer the President seeks to dismiss. Taft also rejected the position that a protected officer could still be controlled by direction or by impeachment. Direction without dismissal depends on obedience the President cannot compel, and impeachment lies with Congress and addresses offences rather than loss of confidence.

The Court accordingly held the removal provision of the 1876 statute unconstitutional and the dismissal of Myers effective without Senate consent, so no salary was owed for the unexpired term. The disposition needed no more than a postmaster, a four-year term, and a consent requirement, yet the reasoning ranged across federal administration. Taft discussed Cabinet officers, department heads, and subordinate officials within a single framework, and passages addressed officers whose tenure protections were not before the Court. That range created the dicta problem. A holding binds through the reasoning necessary to the judgment, while observations beyond the facts are dicta, open to limitation by later courts. Later judges therefore had to decide whether the broad language controlled officers who adjudicated cases or served on multi-member commissions, or whether the holding stopped at the purely executive officer whose claim the Court had decided. The breadth was deliberate, meant to prevent Congress from recovering through tenure drafting the influence the Decision of 1789 had denied it. Its cost became clear within a decade, when the Court faced an officer whose duties did not fit the purely executive description and declined to follow Myers to the end of its logic.

## The Dissents That Kept the Other Reading Alive

The majority opinion in Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52 (1926), spoke as if history and constitutional structure pointed in one direction. The dissents showed that the same materials could support the opposite direction, and they did so from three different grounds. Their value to later law was not merely disagreement. Each dissent isolated a premise in Chief Justice Taft's reasoning, denied that premise, and left behind a developed alternative that Congress and later courts could use without starting again from first principles.

Justice Brandeis rejected the idea that separation of powers existed to produce administrative efficiency. In his dissent, the doctrine served to divide power so that its exercise would encounter friction, deliberation, and resistance from institutions with independent standing. A department or commission that a president could empty at pleasure would, on that reading, cease to check executive energy and would instead transmit it. Brandeis treated independence not as an accident that Congress had smuggled into the structure but as one of the purposes the structure was designed to secure. The point also carried a practical claim about corruption and haste.

Justice Holmes dissented separately and briefly, and the brevity carried the argument. He doubted that the presidential duty to take care that the laws be faithfully executed converted every federal employee into the President's personal officer. A postmaster performed duties laid down by Congress, in offices Congress created, under appropriations Congress voted. Calling such an officer an extension of the President, Holmes suggested, mistook a chain of accountability for a relationship of personal service. The distinction matters because Myers depended on scale. If faithful execution requires unrestricted removal of a first class postmaster in Portland, Oregon, the same logic reaches thousands of minor offices whose holders make no national policy at all. Holmes refused to build so large a constitutional rule out of the execution clause when the clause itself says only that the President shall see that enacted law is carried out, not that every hand carrying it out must be a hand the President may sever without reason.

Justice McReynolds wrote at length on history and precedent, contesting Taft's account of the Decision of 1789 and drawing support from practice in which Congress had conditioned tenure without the Republic collapsing into legislative rule. His dissent treated the early debates as inconclusive rather than as a settlement, and it preserved a reading of cases that had sustained civil service protections and other statutory limits. Together, the three dissents kept two propositions available. Separation could mean mutual independence as well as presidential control, and congressional power to create an office could include some power to define the security with which the office would be held. When Congress defended the Federal Trade Commission Act in the following decade, and when the Supreme Court reconsidered the question in 1935, those propositions did not need to be invented. They stood in the United States Reports, reasoned and ready.

## Humphrey's Executor and the Commissioner a President Could Not Dismiss

William E. Humphrey was a member of the Federal Trade Commission whom President Herbert Hoover had reappointed in 1931 for a term scheduled to run until 1938. President Franklin Roosevelt, who took office in March 1933, wanted commissioners committed to his own view of the Commission's work. On July 25, 1933, Roosevelt wrote to Humphrey raising doubts about their compatibility, and in a further letter on August 31, 1933, he asked for Humphrey's resignation on the ground that their minds did not meet on the policies that should govern the Commission. The letter was candid about motive. Roosevelt alleged no inefficiency, no neglect of duty, and no malfeasance. He wanted a different policy direction and a commissioner of his own choosing.

Humphrey refused to resign. He maintained that the Federal Trade Commission Act permitted removal only for cause and that disagreement over policy was not a cause the statute named. Roosevelt then removed him in October 1933. Humphrey continued to assert title to the office until his death on February 14, 1935. His executor, Rathbun, sued in the Court of Claims for the salary due between removal and death, and the case reached the Supreme Court as Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602 (1935). No injunction could restore a deceased commissioner. The only question was whether the removal had been legally effective, because Humphrey's right to salary depended entirely on whether he had remained commissioner in law after Roosevelt declared him removed.

The governing text was the removal sentence in 15 U.S.C. section 41, the provision of the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914 creating the Commission. Commissioners served staggered seven year terms, and the President could remove a commissioner for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. Justice Sutherland delivered the opinion for a unanimous Court in May 1935. The Court held the removal ineffective. Roosevelt had stated a ground outside the statutory list, and a ground outside the list could not do the work the statute reserved to the three causes it named. The more consequential holding was constitutional. Congress, the Court concluded, had power to enact that restriction without violating Article II, even after Myers. The decision did not overrule Myers. It confined Myers to officers who were purely executive in character, and it placed the Federal Trade Commission outside that category.

### Why did the Supreme Court protect the Federal Trade Commission in 1935?

The Court described the Federal Trade Commission as a nonpartisan expert body exercising quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial duties and no purely executive power, so presidential control was not constitutionally required to preserve the separation of powers, and Congress could condition removal on stated cause.

That description did substantial work. A body exercising no purely executive power, Sutherland reasoned, could be made independent of executive control in a way that a postmaster or a cabinet officer could not. Congress intended the Commission to be nonpartisan, to be composed of persons with experience and judgment about trade practices, and to act on evidence rather than on instructions from the White House. Unrestricted presidential removal would have defeated that design at its root, because a commissioner who served at pleasure could not give the independent judgment the statute presupposed. Sutherland also distinguished the constitutional stakes. The unanimous judgment left Myers intact for the officers Myers actually resembled.

## Quasi-Legislative and Quasi-Judicial: the Distinction That Carried a Century

The labels quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial sounded technical, but in 1935 they described a recognizable way of working. An agency acted in a quasi-legislative manner when it filled in the details of a broad statute through rules of general application, much as a legislature enacts prospective policy, though under a delegation and within limits Congress set. The Federal Trade Commission identified unfair methods of competition, gathered industry information, and reported to Congress with recommendations. An agency acted in a quasi-judicial manner when it decided particular cases on a record, with notice, evidence, findings, and an order directed to named parties, much as a court decides, though without the constitutional status of an Article III tribunal. The Commission heard complaints, weighed testimony, and entered cease and desist orders reviewable in the federal courts of appeals. Justice Sutherland's opinion in Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602 (1935), attached constitutional significance to that double character. A body that made rules for an industry and judged disputes under them, the reasoning ran, resembled a legislature and a court enough that complete presidential dominion was not required.

The institutional design matched the labels. The Commission had five members, not one director answering alone. No more than three commissioners could belong to a single political party, a party balance requirement that prevented any president from treating the whole body as an instrument of one party even after several appointments. Terms lasted seven years and were staggered, so vacancies opened gradually and a president elected for four years ordinarily inherited commissioners chosen by a predecessor. Members were expected to develop expertise in the economics and practices of American trade, and continuity in office was the mechanism by which expertise accumulated. Each feature weakened the claim that the Commission was simply the President's arm. A collegial body, divided by statute between parties, renewed in fractions, and charged with deciding on a record is poorly described as a personal agent, whatever theory of Article II a court brings to the question.

The distinction always contained a weakness, and later litigants pressed it with increasing force. The Federal Trade Commission did more than recommend and adjudicate. It investigated suspected violations on its own initiative, selected targets for enforcement, filed complaints in its own name, and sought penalties and orders against businesses. Investigation, prosecution, and the imposition of sanctions are executive functions in any ordinary understanding of the term, close to the core of what taking care that the laws be faithfully executed has meant since the eighteenth century. Describing enforcement as merely incidental to quasi-judicial work required a court to rank an agency's functions and to treat some as defining and others as secondary. An agency could look legislative when it wrote a rule in the morning, judicial when it heard a case at noon, and executive when it sent investigators in the afternoon. Later courts inherited both the distinction and the difficulty, and the twentieth century argument over independent agencies became, in large part, an argument over whether that difficulty could be managed or whether it proved the distinction unsound.

## Wiener and the Protection a Statute Never Wrote

Myron Wiener was a member of the War Claims Commission, a temporary body created by the War Claims Act of 1948 to adjudicate claims arising out of the Second World War. President Harry Truman appointed him, and President Dwight Eisenhower removed him in December 1953, before his term had run, in order to appoint a replacement of Eisenhower's own selection. Wiener sued for salary, arguing that Eisenhower had no lawful power to remove him. The difficulty was textual and obvious. The statute creating the Commission fixed the members' tenure by reference to the life of the Commission, but it said nothing at all about removal. It contained no clause comparable to the for cause language in the Federal Trade Commission Act, no list of grounds, and no statement that members served at the President's pleasure. A court faithful only to enacted words had nothing to apply.

The Supreme Court decided the case as Wiener v. United States, 357 U.S. 349 (1958). Justice Frankfurter wrote for a unanimous Court and held the removal unlawful. His reasoning moved from the character of the body to the intent that such a body implies. The War Claims Commission did no enforcement work and set no policy for an industry. Its members heard claims, evaluated losses, and decided what individual claimants were owed, under standards Congress had supplied. That function was adjudicatory through and through, and adjudication of that kind presupposes decision makers who are not afraid that an unwelcome award will cost them their seats. Frankfurter concluded that Congress, in creating a tribunal of that nature, must have intended its members to be free from presidential removal except as the law allowed, even though Congress had not written a removal clause into the statute. Silence, on those facts, was read as protection rather than as an omission leaving the Myers default in place.

The holding extended Humphrey's Executor in a direction its critics found hard to contain. In 1935 Congress had at least supplied words for a court to enforce. In 1958 the Court supplied the restriction itself, inferring for cause protection from function alone. That step gave agency designers a new lesson and gave presidents a new grievance. A legislature could secure independence without paying the political price of writing a removal restriction into a bill, provided the body's duties looked sufficiently adjudicatory, and a president could lose control over an officer without any legislator ever voting for language that said so. Frankfurter's opinion rested on a confident judgment about what fair adjudication requires, a judgment rooted in his long study of administrative law. Whether a later court, less confident that function dictates tenure, would draw the same inference from silence remained an open vulnerability in the doctrine. Wiener nonetheless stood as decided law, and for bodies built to judge, it made independence the presumption.

## The Administrative State Grows Around the Distinction

After 1935, Congress drafted new regulatory bodies with Humphrey's Executor as a usable precedent rather than as a narrow exception. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 had already created the Securities and Exchange Commission as a multimember body with staggered terms, and the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 placed labor disputes before the National Labor Relations Board, whose members the President could remove only upon notice and hearing for neglect of duty or malfeasance in office. The Communications Act of 1934 followed a comparable pattern for the Federal Communications Commission. Each statute assumed that some distance between a regulator and the White House was constitutionally available, and each used that distance for a different reason: credibility with investors who feared political manipulation of markets, impartiality between unions and employers, or continuity in licensing decisions that businesses planned around for years.

The Federal Reserve supplied the extreme form of the design. Under the Federal Reserve Act as amended by the Banking Act of 1935, governors of the Federal Reserve Board served fourteen year terms, arranged in stagger so that one term expired every two years, and a governor who had served a full term could not be reappointed. The President could remove a governor only for cause. Fourteen years spans parts of four presidential terms, which means a board majority ordinarily reflects appointments made under several administrations, including administrations of opposing parties. Monetary policy was the field in which Congress had the strongest reason to fear short term political pressure, because an administration facing an election benefits from cheaper credit in ways that can impose inflationary costs after the votes are counted. Length of term did the protective work there more than any statement about quasi-legislative character. No doctrine about the nature of central banking was required once the tenure itself outlasted the electoral cycle by so wide a margin.

Tenure became one tool among several rather than a solitary device. Congress also shaped administration through the conditions it attached to federal money, a lever distinct from tenure, as explained in [how federal grant conditions work](/2026/11/05/how-federal-grant-conditions-work/). Appropriations could be granted, withheld, earmarked, or conditioned, and jurisdiction could be widened or narrowed as an agency gained or lost congressional confidence. Party balance requirements limited the partisan range of appointments, staggered terms limited the speed of change, Senate confirmation limited the pool from which presidents chose, and fixed terms limited the reward a president could offer for compliance. None of these tools gave Congress power to direct a particular decision, and Humphrey's Executor had not held that they could. Together they produced administrations inside the executive branch whose responsiveness to a sitting president varied by design from statute to statute. Removal doctrine supplied the constitutional boundary for that variation. So long as the body resembled the Commission of 1935 closely enough, the variation stood. The resemblance question, deferred rather than answered, awaited a case in which Congress tried to keep a hand on the officer directly.

## Bowsher and the Door Closed to Congress Itself

Bowsher v. Synar, 478 U.S. 714 (1986), arose from a deficit statute rather than from a regulatory commission. The Balanced Budget and Emergency Deficit Control Act of 1985, widely called the Gramm-Rudman-Hollings Act after its Senate sponsors, set maximum deficit amounts for successive fiscal years and provided automatic spending reductions if the projected deficit exceeded the target. The statute assigned a central calculation to the Comptroller General, head of the General Accounting Office, who received estimates from the Office of Management and Budget and the Congressional Budget Office and reported the reductions the President was then required to order. Representative Mike Synar and others challenged the assignment. The difficulty lay in how the Comptroller General held office. Under the Budget and Accounting Act of 1921, Congress could remove the Comptroller General by joint resolution for stated causes including inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance, as well as by impeachment.

Chief Justice Burger delivered the opinion of the Court. The majority held the assignment unconstitutional because the Comptroller General, in performing the deficit calculation, executed the laws, and an officer who executes the laws may not be subject to removal by Congress. The reasoning turned on the direction of control. Humphrey's Executor had limited the President. Bowsher limited Congress. If legislators retained power to remove the officer who determined the cuts, the officer would in practice answer to the institution that could end his tenure, and Congress would thereby acquire a share in execution itself. Burger described that result as legislative aggrandizement, an accumulation by one branch of functions the Constitution assigns to another. The separation of powers, in the majority's hands, policed both sides of the line. Presidents could not be stripped of all control over purely executive officers, and Congress could not reserve control over any officer performing executive duties merely because Congress had created the office and funded it.

### What made the Comptroller General different from other federal officers?

The Comptroller General headed the General Accounting Office, an arm of Congress, yet the deficit statute gave that officer the final word on binding budget cuts. Execution by a congressional agent was the defect: the officer answered to the legislature while performing an executive act, joining two powers the Constitution keeps apart.

The remedy followed the reasoning directly. The Court invalidated the automatic reduction mechanism that depended on the Comptroller General's report, leaving the statute's fallback procedure, under which Congress itself had to enact the reductions by joint resolution. What Congress could not do was combine those legislative support functions with executive authority in a single officeholder while retaining a removal string. That combination, not the existence of an accounting office, was the constitutional defect. Bowsher also clarified the standing of the earlier cases by treating the question institutionally. The issue was not whether a particular officer deserved protection or whether a particular president deserved control. The issue was which branch held the removal power over execution, and on that question the Court allowed no sharing arrangement of the kind the 1985 Act had attempted.

## Morrison v. Olson and the Independent Counsel Test

The Ethics in Government Act of 1978 created a mechanism for investigating senior executive officials that deliberately placed part of a prosecution outside ordinary presidential control. When the Attorney General received specific information about possible federal crimes by covered officials and could not resolve the matter through preliminary investigation, application went to a special division of the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, which appointed an independent counsel and defined the counsel's jurisdiction. The counsel could be removed by the Attorney General only for good cause, physical or mental disability, or another condition substantially impairing performance. Alexia Morrison was appointed under the Act to investigate whether Assistant Attorney General Theodore Olson had given false testimony to a House subcommittee concerning the Environmental Protection Agency's Superfund program. Olson and others subpoenaed in the investigation challenged the Act, and Morrison litigated in her own name to enforce the subpoenas, producing Morrison v. Olson, 487 U.S. 654 (1988).

Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for a majority of seven. The Court first held that an independent counsel was an inferior officer rather than a principal officer under the Appointments Clause of Article II. Morrison had limited duties, no policymaking authority, a jurisdiction defined by the appointing court, and tenure tied to the completion of a particular investigation. That classification placed the counsel lower in the constitutional hierarchy than a commissioner of the Federal Trade Commission, and it made the remaining question narrower: whether a good cause restriction on removing such an officer so interfered with the President that Article II forbade it.

Rehnquist answered that question with a functional test rather than with the categories of 1935. The Court asked whether a removal restriction impeded the President's ability to perform the constitutional duty of faithful execution, considering the office and the restriction together. Earlier language about quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions was treated as less decisive than the practical burden on executive control. Applying that test, the Court upheld the good cause provision. The Attorney General, a presidential appointee removable at pleasure, retained removal authority. The counsel's work was limited in scope and duration, and the executive branch kept enough oversight through the Attorney General's standards and through the requirement that an independent counsel comply with Department of Justice policy where possible. Congress had not, the majority concluded, aggrandized itself or weakened the President enough to upset the balance Article II requires. Bowsher was distinguished on the decisive ground that Congress had reserved no removal power to itself in the 1978 Act.

Only Justice Scalia dissented. He argued that prosecuting crime is executive power in its purest form, that Article II vests all of that power in the President, and that a prosecutor a president cannot control is a constitutional contradiction the majority's balancing had disguised rather than answered. The dissent restated the Myers position with a precision the majority had avoided adopting, and it identified the cost of the functional test: a standard asking how much interference is too much gives no firm answer before litigation, and it can sustain each new restriction by pointing to the controls that remain. For the time of its decision, however, Morrison represented the high point of judicial tolerance for protected tenure inside the executive branch. A prosecutor investigating the administration itself could hold office under good cause protection because a court had weighed the burden on the presidency and found it bearable.

## One Justice Alone: the Scalia Dissent in Morrison

Justice Antonin Scalia filed the sole dissent in Morrison v. Olson, 487 U.S. 654 (1988), and the opinion differed from the majority in method as well as result. Chief Justice William Rehnquist, writing for seven colleagues, had asked whether the good cause removal restriction impeded the President in the performance of a constitutional duty, and had answered that it did not. Scalia rejected the question itself. Article II vested all executive power in a single President, without qualification, and a court had no authority to weigh how much of that power Congress might withdraw before the withdrawal became unconstitutional. The vesting clause was, for Scalia, a complete allocation rather than an opening statement to be balanced against legislative convenience.

From that premise the classification of the independent counsel followed without difficulty. A prosecutor investigates, decides whether evidence warrants an indictment, presents a case to a grand jury, negotiates pleas, and represents the United States in court. Scalia treated each function as executive power in its purest form, because no legislature makes the charging decision and no court originates it. If the officer exercising that power could be removed only for good cause, and only by an Attorney General answerable upward, then a portion of executive power had been placed outside presidential direction. The defect was structural and did not depend on proof that a particular President had been frustrated in a particular investigation.

The dissent gave that structural point a memorable image, a wolf arriving in the clothing of a wolf rather than in disguise, to convey that the statute presented its separation of powers problem openly. Earlier disputes, in his account, had at least dressed a transfer of power in ambiguous language or in the character of an office. The Ethics in Government Act provisions at issue in Morrison did the transfer plainly, by creating a prosecutor and then shielding the prosecutor from at will removal, and candor about the design did not cure the design.

For a generation the dissent attracted admiration without attracting votes. Lower courts applied the Morrison majority test because it was governing precedent, and the independent counsel statute lapsed in 1999 after bipartisan dissatisfaction with its operation, removing the most visible occasion for revisiting the question. A lone dissent that reframes a settled field needs either a change in personnel or a change in the questions reaching the court before it can move from commentary into doctrine.

The reframing came through scholarship that converted the dissent into a research program. Steven G. Calabresi and Christopher S. Yoo, in The Unitary Executive: Presidential Power from Washington to Bush (2008), examined presidential practice administration by administration and argued that a consistent understanding of presidential control over execution, including removal, ran through American history despite periodic congressional resistance. The book supplied what a dissent cannot supply for itself, a detailed historical record organized around a single claim. The dissent became the intellectual seed of a majority position because its central move, treating the vesting of executive power as a rule rather than a factor, proved capable of extension to permanent regulatory agencies.

## Free Enterprise Fund and the Rule Against Two Layers

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board to register accounting firms, set standards for audits of public companies, inspect firms, and bring disciplinary proceedings. Congress placed the Board inside the orbit of the Securities and Exchange Commission without making it a conventional bureau of the Commission. The Commission appointed the five members of the Board and could remove a member only for good cause shown through a formal process. The commissioners themselves were understood to enjoy for-cause protection against presidential removal. A member firm and a nonprofit organization challenged that arrangement in Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, 561 U.S. 477 (2010).

Chief Justice John Roberts, writing for the majority, accepted one layer of for-cause protection as a premise supplied by precedent and attacked the second layer as the point at which accountability broke. A President unable to remove a commissioner at will could at least judge whether the commissioner had applied a removal standard faithfully, provided the commissioner held direct authority over the subordinate officer. Where the Board member was protected from the Commission by a good cause standard, and the commissioner was protected from the President by a comparable standard, neither superior could ensure a removal that the superior believed the law required. Responsibility for Board action therefore rested with no elected official in a form the public could trace.

The reasoning turned on accountability as a chain rather than on the importance of accounting regulation. Roberts treated the power to oversee an officer as hollow if the overseer lacked a credible removal sanction, and he treated a sanction filtered through two independent findings of cause as too attenuated to discipline discretion. The opinion did not hold that the Commission structure was invalid, and it did not hold that Board members had to become removable by the President directly. Its narrower proposition was that the combination was impermissible, because the second layer changed the character of the first. A single layer left a superior who could be pressed and criticized. Two layers left each superior able to attribute inaction to the protection enjoyed by the other.

The remedy displayed the same preference for the smallest structural correction that would restore the chain. The Court severed the removal restriction protecting Board members from the Commission, leaving the rest of the Sarbanes-Oxley scheme in place. Board members became removable at will by the Commission, the Board continued to operate, and its past actions were not undone. Severance separated the constitutional defect, which concerned who could remove and under what standard, from the regulatory program, which Congress remained free to maintain under a corrected structure.

The decision also placed removal doctrine inside a larger contest over financial regulators, and the removal thread inside the wider litigation over financial regulators can be followed through [the long litigation history of financial regulation in the courts](/2014/01/15/financial-regulation-court-cases/) without losing sight of the narrower holding. Free Enterprise Fund did not decide whether a single for-cause layer protecting the head of a financial agency was valid, because the Commission was a multimember body and the Board sat beneath it. It announced that layering would receive skeptical review, and it supplied a vocabulary of presidential accountability that later challengers could direct at a single layer when the protected officer was one person rather than a board.

## Seila Law and the Single Director Problem

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau within the Federal Reserve System and placed it under a single Director. Congress gave the Director a term of five years and provided that the President could remove the Director only for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. Unlike a commission, the Bureau had no bipartisan membership requirement and no collegial vote through which a dissenting member might slow or expose a decision. The Director supervised enforcement and issued rules governing consumer financial products across the national economy.

The constitutional question arrived through an ordinary enforcement instrument. The Bureau issued a civil investigative demand to Seila Law LLC, a California law firm that provided debt related services. Seila Law declined to comply and argued that the Bureau was structured in violation of the separation of powers because its Director was insufficiently accountable to the President. The lower courts had to decide whether a defect in the removal protection of the issuing officer defeated the demand itself.

In Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 591 U.S. 197 (2020), Chief Justice John Roberts, writing for the majority, described the Constitution as granting the President removal authority subject to two historical exceptions. The first covered a multimember commission of experts, balanced along partisan lines, that exercised no executive power in the relevant sense. The second covered an inferior officer with limited duties and no policymaking authority, of the kind sustained in Morrison v. Olson. Roberts presented those exceptions as bounded readings of Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602 (1935), and Morrison, rather than as illustrations of a general congressional power to insulate administrators whenever insulation seemed useful.

### Can the President fire the head of a consumer bureau at will?

Yes. After Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in 2020, a single director who exercises executive power serves at the pleasure of the President, because that structure fits neither the multimember commission exception nor the inferior officer exception recognized in earlier removal decisions.

A single Director fit neither exception under that framework. The Director was not one member of a collegial body, and the Bureau did not merely resolve a narrow class of claims. The Director was a principal officer who formulated policy, brought enforcement actions, and executed federal law against private parties, with a fixed term that could extend into the administration of a successor President. Concentration in one person removed the internal checks that a commission supplies through deliberation, while the for-cause clause removed the external check that presidential removal supplies.

Justice Elena Kagan, joined by Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Justice Stephen Breyer, and Justice Sonia Sotomayor, dissented and defended congressional flexibility in agency design. The dissent argued that the Constitution did not prescribe a single template for administration and that Congress could choose a structure designed to reduce swings driven by electoral change. Kagan emphasized function over formal classification and found no sufficient showing that a good cause standard disabled presidential oversight in practice. Roberts and the formalist majority argued that control follows executive power, while Kagan argued from congressional design flexibility and function.

The remedy again preserved the institution while changing its tenure rule. The Court severed the removal restriction, so the Director became removable at will and the Bureau continued its work. The structural holding and its practical consequences moved on different tracks, and the second track became the subject of the next major decision.

## Collins and the Harder Question of the Remedy

The Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008 created the Federal Housing Finance Agency to supervise the housing finance system, including the Federal National Mortgage Association, known as Fannie Mae, and the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation, known as Freddie Mac. After the financial crisis of 2008, the agency placed both enterprises into conservatorship. The Act provided for a single Director, appointed for a term of five years and removable by the President only for cause. Shareholders of the two enterprises challenged a 2012 amendment to the government's preferred stock purchase agreements, under which the enterprises paid to the Treasury a dividend tied to net worth, arguing that the agency had acted through a Director insulated by an unconstitutional removal restriction.

Collins v. Yellen, 594 U.S. 220 (2021), reached the Supreme Court after Seila Law had invalidated a materially similar protection for the single Director of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Justice Samuel Alito, writing for the majority, treated the removal question as largely governed by that reasoning. The housing agency differed from the consumer bureau in mission, but its Director exercised executive authority as a single principal officer, and the for-cause clause limited presidential control in the same constitutional manner. The Court rejected distinctions based on the conservatorship role. The restriction therefore could not stand.

The harder part of Collins concerned what followed from the defect. The shareholders sought relief that would have unwound the 2012 amendment or compensated them for it, on a theory that treated action taken under an unconstitutional structure as action that should be set aside. Alito declined to draw that consequence from the removal holding alone. An unconstitutional restriction on removal did not by itself void the actions the officer took, because the officer had been appointed through a valid process and the constitutional injury concerned presidential control rather than the existence of the office. A plaintiff who wanted a court to disturb a completed action had to show that the restriction caused compensable harm.

That causation requirement changed the value of a removal victory. A litigant might establish the governing constitutional rule, obtain a declaration that a tenure clause was invalid, and secure at will removal for successors, yet leave the contested transaction intact. Retrospective relief required evidence that an unprotected director would have acted differently, or that presidential pressure made possible by at will tenure would have altered the decision. Such proof is difficult where the record shows an appointed director pursuing a policy that the administration of the day supported, and speculative where the counterfactual depends on a removal that never occurred and on a replacement who was never named.

The separation has a logic that extends beyond housing finance. Severance preserves statutes, causation limits retrospective disruption, and removal doctrine allocates future control. Combined, those tools mean that the most important effect of a removal decision may be prospective and institutional rather than compensatory for the parties who financed the litigation. Why remedy doctrine decides how much a removal victory is worth in practice is the lesson Collins added to the line, and it taught litigants to plead and prove the remedial link from the beginning.

## Firings, Stays and Certiorari Before Judgment

In March 2025, President Donald Trump removed Federal Trade Commission Commissioners Rebecca Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya without identifying a statutory cause. The Federal Trade Commission Act permitted removal for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. Slaughter sued and contended that the removal violated that restriction and that Humphrey's Executor had sustained the restriction against a presidential challenge in 1935. The District Court for the District of Columbia declared the removal unlawful and enjoined interference with her duties, treating the governing precedent as controlling unless the Supreme Court displaced it. A divided panel of the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit denied a stay of that order, leaving the district court judgment in effect while appellate proceedings continued.

On September 22, 2025, the Supreme Court stayed the district court order and granted certiorari before judgment in the case. Argument followed on December 8, 2025. Each action performs a different procedural function. A stay suspends the legal effect of a lower court order until further disposition. It does not reverse the judgment and does not by itself establish the final rights of the parties. In practical terms, however, a stay in a removal case determines who occupies the office while the merits are pending, because the person removed remains out of the seat and the person who would return under the injunction does not return.

Certiorari before judgment governs the route and timing of review. Ordinarily, the Supreme Court reviews a case after a court of appeals has entered a final judgment. The Court may instead grant a petition for a writ of certiorari before judgment when a case pending in a court of appeals is of such imperative public importance that deviation from the normal order of review is justified. The grant moves the case to the Supreme Court for briefing and argument without waiting for the court of appeals to complete its work. It signals that the justices consider the question ready and consequential, but it is not a merits holding. The merits arrive only through the opinion that follows argument.

Before the Commission case was argued, the Court had acted on emergency applications in disputes involving the National Labor Relations Board and the Consumer Product Safety Commission. In the Wilcox matter, concerning Board member Gwynne Wilcox, and in the Boyle matter, concerning Commission members including Mary Boyle, the Court allowed removals to remain in effect while litigation proceeded. Those orders resolved requests for interim relief under standards that weigh likelihood of success, irreparable harm, and the balance of equities. They did not supply a full merits opinion overruling precedent, and orders in emergency posture do not bind the Court in the manner of a reasoned judgment after argument.

Direction and decision are therefore different categories. The emergency orders signalled that a majority was prepared, at the interim stage, to treat the government's likelihood of success as sufficient to justify leaving removed commissioners out of office. Yet the signal left open the reasoning by which a final majority might define executive power, handle the Federal Reserve, or frame a remedy. The December 8, 2025 argument in the Slaughter case was the first occasion in that sequence on which the full merits apparatus was brought to bear on the survival of Humphrey's Executor itself.

## Trump v. Slaughter and the Bright Line

Trump v. Slaughter, No. 25-332, decided June 29, 2026, resolved the merits by a vote of six to three. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the majority and held that the for-cause provision of the Federal Trade Commission Act was contrary to the separation of powers. The holding did not rest on a finding that Rebecca Slaughter had performed inadequately or that a stated statutory cause existed. It rested on classification of the agency and of the power the agency wielded. An agency that exercises executive power, including executing a congressional mandate against private parties, is headed by principal officers removable at will by the President. Once the Commission was placed in that category, a clause permitting removal only for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office could not be reconciled with the presidential control the majority derived from Article II.

The opinion confronted the precedent that had protected the Commission since 1935 directly rather than distinguishing it. The majority stated that Humphrey's Executor "has not withstood the test of time" and held that whatever remained of the decision was overruled. That step mattered because earlier modern cases had narrowed Humphrey's Executor by describing its office narrowly or by limiting it to multimember bodies. A narrowing approach leaves litigants to argue over the boundaries of the surviving island in every new agency design. Overruling removed the island as a source of protection for an agency exercising executive power, and the clarity of that move is inseparable from an account of [how the Supreme Court overrules one of its own precedents](/2026/11/08/how-supreme-court-overrules-precedent/) when it chooses replacement over further distinction.

Justice Clarence Thomas joined all but Part III-B of the majority opinion, a partial joinder that preserved a six justice majority for the judgment and for the central holding while withholding his assent from one portion of the reasoning. Justice Neil Gorsuch concurred. Justice Sonia Sotomayor dissented, joined by Justice Elena Kagan and Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson. The dissent defended the capacity of Congress to structure commissions with a measure of independence and warned that the majority rule subordinated that design choice to a theory of presidential control that the dissent did not find compelled by text or by the long practice of independent regulation. The alignment preserved the familiar division in the field. Roberts and the formalist majority argued that control follows executive power, while Sotomayor and Kagan argued from congressional design flexibility and function.

The bright line announced in Slaughter is best understood by what it refuses to balance. The majority did not ask whether a particular President had been impeded in supervising the Commission, whether the Commission had acted wisely, or whether competition policy benefited from insulation. Those inquiries belonged to the functional method associated with Morrison. Under Slaughter, the threshold classification controls the result. If the agency executes the law against private parties through rulemaking, enforcement, or adjudication backed by federal authority, its principal officers are executive officers for removal purposes, and Congress may not convert their tenure into for-cause tenure by statute.

That boundary explains both the reach and the limit of the judgment. The decision settled the tenure of commissioners at an agency long treated as the model of the independent commission, and by its reasoning it placed other multimember agencies exercising executive power under the same at will rule. It did not answer every question about inferior officers after Morrison or about an institution whose history and function the Court treats as distinct from ordinary administration. The table below settles how each authority in the line changed the rule.

| Authority | Question before the court | Rule the decision left |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decision of 1789 | May Congress structure a department whose head the President alone dismisses | The First Congress treated removal as an executive act and legislated on that basis |
| Myers v. United States (1926) | Must the Senate consent before a postmaster is removed | A purely executive officer is removable at will and Senate consent to removal is void |
| Humphrey's Executor (1935) | May Congress protect a trade commissioner from policy dismissal | A multimember expert body with no purely executive power may be given for-cause protection |
| Wiener v. United States (1958) | Does silence in a statute leave a claims commissioner removable at will | An adjudicatory function implies protection even where the statute says nothing |
| Morrison v. Olson (1988) | May a prosecutor be shielded by a good-cause removal clause | Protection stands where it does not impede faithful execution and the officer is inferior |
| Free Enterprise Fund (2010) | May two layers of for-cause protection stand between President and board | Two layers are one too many and the extra layer is severed |
| Seila Law (2020) | May a single bureau director hold for-cause protection | A single director exercising executive power fits neither recognised exception |
| Collins v. Yellen (2021) | Does an unlawful removal clause void the actions taken under it | The clause falls but past actions stand unless the clause caused the harm claimed |
| Trump v. Slaughter (2026) | Does any of Humphrey's Executor survive for executive agencies | An agency exercising executive power is headed by officers removable at will |

## Trump v. Cook and the Federal Reserve Exception

On the same day the Court decided Trump v. Slaughter, June 29, 2026, it decided Trump v. Cook, No. 25A312, by a vote of five to four. The posture differed in a way that shaped the result. The government asked the Court to allow removal of Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook to take effect while her challenge continued in the lower courts. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the majority in denying that request. Cook therefore remained in office during the litigation, not because the Court entered a final judgment that her removal was unlawful, but because the government did not obtain the interim relief it sought at that stage.

The majority treated the Federal Reserve as an institution whose place in removal doctrine could not be settled by simple transfer of the Slaughter classification. The Federal Reserve sets monetary policy through the Federal Open Market Committee, supervises banks, and operates under a structure built across the twentieth century to distance interest rate decisions from direct electoral pressure. Governors serve long staggered terms, and the Board sits within a system that includes 12 regional Federal Reserve Banks. Roberts treated that singular role in monetary policy and that distinctive history as reasons the general rule in Slaughter did not dispose of the question presented by the emergency application.

That treatment was bounded by stage and by question. The Court did not hold in Cook that the for-cause protection in the Federal Reserve Act was fully constitutional against a final merits challenge, and it did not create a freestanding exemption for every agency able to claim economic importance. It held that the government had not justified the requested interim removal. A governor removed during litigation and later vindicated could not be restored to the identical sequence of votes and deliberations that the removal had interrupted, because monetary policy decisions are time specific and a seat left vacant for a particular meeting cannot be recreated after the meeting has passed.

The contrast between the two decisions of June 29, 2026 is itself the map of where the doctrine stops. In Slaughter, after full argument, a multimember commission exercising executive power against private parties was held to be headed by officers removable at will, and the precedent that had protected that commission was overruled. In Cook, on an application concerning interim status, a Federal Reserve governor remained in office while her case continued, and the Court declined to let the general rule answer the Federal Reserve question by implication. One decision drew a bright line for executive agencies. The other marked a boundary at the central bank and postponed the merits analysis that a final judgment would require.

That pairing also disciplines predictions. A litigant cannot reason from Slaughter alone that every for-cause clause in federal law fell on June 29, 2026, because Cook distinguishes a merits judgment about an executive agency from an interim application about the central bank. Nor can a litigant reason from Cook that independence survived generally, because Slaughter overruled the precedent on which the general defense of independent commissions rested. The doctrine after the two decisions is a rule of at will removal for principal officers who exercise executive power, accompanied by a reserved Federal Reserve question whose resolution will depend on argument addressed to that institution in particular.

## What Counts as Executive Power After Slaughter

The majority opinion in Trump v. Slaughter, decided on June 29, 2026, did not define executive power by naming a single act. The opinion assembled a working test from the functions an agency performs in the world. An officer exercises executive power, in the majority formulation, when the officer makes binding rules under a mandate granted by Congress, brings enforcement proceedings against private parties, imposes penalties for violation of federal law, or grants, denies and revokes licenses that determine whether a private person may lawfully operate. Each element shares a common character. Each changes the legal position of someone outside the government, and each does so in the name of the United States under a statute the President is charged with executing. The test looks past an agency label, a commission structure and a statutory declaration of independence, and it asks instead what the office does on an ordinary working day.

Measured against that test, nearly every operating agency in the federal establishment qualifies without difficulty. The Securities and Exchange Commission writes rules for markets, investigates issuers and brokers, and seeks civil penalties in federal court and in its own proceedings. The Federal Trade Commission, the agency at issue in Slaughter itself, challenges mergers, polices deceptive practices and obtains orders that bind firms. The National Labor Relations Board prosecutes unfair labor practice complaints through its general counsel and issues remedial orders against employers and unions. The Environmental Protection Agency sets emission standards, issues permits and assesses penalties. The Slaughter majority treated any substantial share of such work as enough to bring the whole office within the removal rule.

The same test leaves a narrower territory outside its reach, and the majority identified the boundaries by example rather than by exhaustive definition. Pure adjudication of private claims stands in a different position. An officer who hears a dispute between two private parties, finds facts under an existing standard and enters a judgment that resolves only that dispute performs a task closer to the work of a court than to the work of a prosecutor or a regulator. Advice without binding effect stands outside for a related reason. A body that studies a problem, gathers information and reports recommendations to Congress or to the President changes no private legal position by its own act, because another actor must still decide whether to act on the recommendation. The boundary matters because it preserves a class of federal work that Slaughter does not reach, even though the class is far smaller than the independent establishment built during the twentieth century.

The removal question also belongs to a wider family of instruments through which Presidents direct administration, a family that includes [the history of executive orders and what their counts mean](/2007/08/17/executive-orders-count-per-president/) alongside proclamations, memoranda and signing statements. An executive order directs officers who already serve; removal replaces officers who will not follow direction. The Slaughter majority treated the second instrument as the foundation of the first, on the reasoning that a direction carries weight only when the person directed knows that refusal may end the appointment. Where no binding function exists, neither direction nor removal has executive work to do.

## Inferior Officers, Administrative Judges and the Open Edges

The category of inferior officers survived Slaughter intact, and Morrison v. Olson remains the governing authority for offices within that category. An inferior officer, under the analysis the Court applied in Morrison in 1988 and refined in Edmond v. United States in 1997, is an officer whose work is directed and supervised by a principal officer, whose duties, jurisdiction and tenure are limited in scope, and who holds no authority to set final policy for the government. Congress may provide a measure of removal protection for such an officer when the protection does not impede the President in the performance of the constitutional duty to execute the laws. The Slaughter majority distinguished Morrison rather than displacing it, and the distinction preserves space for protected service in subordinate positions even after protection has ended for the heads of executive agencies.

Administrative law judges occupy the most contested ground along that line. In Lucia v. SEC in 2018, the Supreme Court held that administrative law judges of the Securities and Exchange Commission are officers of the United States under the Appointments Clause, and that such judges must therefore be appointed by the President, by a court of law or by a head of department, rather than selected through ordinary staff hiring. Lucia decided an appointment question and left the removal question open. Administrative law judges hear enforcement cases, take testimony, rule on evidence and issue initial decisions that an agency may affirm, modify or reverse. Slaughter supplied arguments for both readings and resolved neither.

A related reservation appeared in the structure of the majority coalition itself. The Slaughter majority expressly left open the status of judges of non-Article III courts, naming the United States Tax Court as an example, in a portion of the opinion that Justice Thomas did not join. Tax Court judges decide disputes between taxpayers and the Internal Revenue Service, serve for terms fixed by statute and exercise a form of judicial power that Congress placed outside Article III. At least one member of the coalition that ended protection for executive agencies was unwilling to extend the reasoning to officers whose work consists of deciding cases, and no majority formed for a rule governing such judges at all.

Members of multimember bodies whose work is adjudicatory only present the same problem in commission form. A board that reviews claims, hears appeals from initial decisions and enters orders resolving disputes between a claimant and the government, or between private parties, performs few of the functions in the Slaughter working test. Whether the removal rule follows the institution or the function determines the outcome for those bodies, and the opinion points in both directions, toward function in its test and toward institution in its treatment of the Federal Trade Commission. The edges matter more than the centre for the agencies that remain because the centre has been settled. Litigation after Slaughter will concern claims boards, review panels, administrative judges and specialized courts, and the answers given for those bodies will define how much of the older independent design survives in practice.

## The Case Against the Bright Line, Given Its Best Form

The strongest case against the rule announced in Slaughter begins with silence. Justice Kagan, dissenting in Seila Law v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in 2020, argued that the Constitution says nothing about removal at all. The text grants appointment, structures impeachment and charges the President with faithful execution, and the dissent maintained that the absence of a removal clause is itself significant in a document that specifies so much else about officeholding. From that silence, Justice Kagan drew a conclusion opposite to the one drawn by Chief Justice Roberts for the Seila Law majority. Where the majority inferred a general removal power from the vesting of executive power in the President, the dissent inferred congressional latitude, on the ground that a power the text never grants in words is a poor foundation for striking down arrangements Congress enacted through the ordinary legislative process. The dissent also defended congressional design across two centuries as evidence in its own right.

Justice Sotomayor, dissenting in Slaughter in 2026, carried the argument from text and history into institutional consequence. Expert multimember bodies, in that dissent, were designed to decide technical questions through deliberation among members appointed at different times, from different backgrounds and, under many statutes, from both political parties. Justice Sotomayor argued that removal at will converts every commission into an arm of the White House at each change of administration, because a President entering office may empty a commission on the first day and refill it with members chosen for loyalty to a program rather than for knowledge of a field. The dissent treated stability across administrations not as an accident of the older design but as the purpose for which Congress built it.

A third strand of the opposing case concerns reliance. Humphrey's Executor v. United States was decided by a unanimous Court in 1935, and the ruling was not an isolated holding left undisturbed by neglect. Congress relied on the decision while enacting and amending the organic statutes of dozens of agencies over the following nine decades, structuring terms, quorum rules, party balance requirements and succession provisions on the assumption that for-cause protection was constitutionally available. Supporters of the older rule argue that a redesign of that system, if redesign is warranted, belongs to Congress, which may rewrite terms and protections statute by statute with attention to each agency function. Stare decisis, in this argument, protects not a single precedent but a settled framework on which both branches organized a large part of the government.

The formalist account survives part of that case and narrows under the rest. The account survives on the question of a single director wielding enforcement and rulemaking authority, where the Seila Law and Collins v. Yellen holdings rest on the direct connection between execution and accountability, and where even many defenders of Humphrey's Executor accepted that a sole director differs from a multimember commission. The account must narrow at two points that the opposing case identifies with precision. The Federal Reserve, treated separately in Trump v. Cook, resists the bright line because monetary policy was designed for insulation and because the Slaughter majority itself declined to force the question. Adjudicatory bodies resist the line for a different reason, because deciding cases between parties is not execution in the ordinary sense, and a removal power aimed at judges of claims reaches judgment rather than policy. A thesis that holds for enforcement and rulemaking and yields at adjudication and central banking is narrower than the rule Slaughter announced, and the narrowing is the measure of the dissenting case.

## What Removal Control Changes Inside an Agency

Removal doctrine appears in reports as a question about named officials and Senate votes, but the practical effect of the doctrine registers inside an agency long before any dismissal occurs. Enforcement priorities change first. A commission or bureau whose leadership serves at the pleasure of the President receives direction on which industries to examine, which practices to challenge and which matters to close without action, and career staff adjust case selection to the priorities of leadership that the White House may replace. Rulemaking agendas move in the same way. A rule proposed under one administration may be withdrawn, delayed or rewritten under the next, and the notice and comment process, designed as a record of evidence and argument, also becomes a record that a successor leadership reviews for reasons to change course. Regulated parties read personnel announcements as regulatory information, because the identity of a chair or a director predicts the content of rules and the selection of cases more reliably than any statement of general policy.

Quorum loss presents a sharper operational problem. Many multimember agencies may act only through a quorum fixed by statute, and a board with vacancies below that number cannot issue orders, adopt rules or decide appeals, however many career employees remain at work. Dismissals that create vacancies, resignations that follow a change in administration and slow replacements combine to produce periods in which an agency holds authority on paper and cannot exercise the authority in fact. Removal power therefore includes a negative dimension.

### Can the President fire an FBI director at will?
Yes. The director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation serves a statutory ten-year term established by a law enacted in 1976, but that statute sets a term length rather than a for-cause shield, and Presidents have dismissed directors during the term, in 1993 and in 2017, without a court invalidating the dismissal.

The Federal Vacancies Reform Act of 1998 supplies the adjacent mechanism that Presidents use while nominations wait. When a Senate-confirmed office falls vacant, the Act designates the first assistant to the office as the acting officer by default, and it also permits the President to direct a confirmed officer from another agency, or a senior employee of the same agency meeting service requirements, to perform the duties of the vacant office for a period generally limited to 210 days, with extensions tied to the pendency of a nomination. Acting officers may exercise nearly the full authority of the office, which makes the choice of an acting officer a governing decision in its own right.

Senate confirmation remains the counterweight that removal doctrine does not touch. The Appointments Clause gives the Senate a share in placing principal officers, even though Slaughter gives the President the whole power of displacing such officers afterward. That division explains why regulated parties plan around personnel rather than around rules. A rule may stand unchanged in the Code of Federal Regulations while the officials who interpret the rule, select cases under the rule and decide whether to defend the rule in court have all been replaced. Planning follows the people because enforcement discretion, interpretive choice and litigation position all travel with the officeholder. ## The Same Problem in Other Constitutional Systems

The United Kingdom lodges the corresponding choices at nearly the opposite point. Senior British officials form a permanent civil service whose members serve successive governments of different parties, and impartiality in that service is a statutory value rather than a professional custom alone. The Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010 placed the civil service on a statutory footing, affirmed recruitment on merit through fair and open competition, and treated dismissal of an individual official as an employment matter governed by the terms and procedures of the service rather than as an instrument of ministerial policy. Ministers themselves hold office at the pleasure of the Prime Minister, who appoints, moves and dismisses members of the government at will and without a legislative vote. Political direction in the British system therefore runs through a small layer of removable ministers who answer to Parliament, while administration below that layer continues across changes of government as a matter of design.

Canada arranges the same elements in a pattern closer to the British model than to the American model, with a distinctive position at the top of each department. Deputy ministers, the senior public servants who direct federal departments, serve at pleasure and may be moved or released by the government of the day acting through the Governor in Council on the advice of the Prime Minister. Below the deputy minister level, a professional public service staffed on merit principles administers programs across administrations, and individual dismissal is governed by public service employment law rather than by the policy preferences of a minister. The Canadian arrangement concedes that the official who works most closely with a minister must hold the confidence of that minister, and it protects continuity everywhere below that point of contact. Accountability runs upward through the minister to Parliament, and a failure of administration is charged politically to the minister whether the failure originated with an official whom the minister inherited or with one whom the minister selected.

The contrast clarifies the American question by removing any claim of necessity. Government on a large scale functions under a system in which almost no administrator is removable for policy reasons, and government also functions under a system in which the heads of major agencies serve at presidential pleasure. Each arrangement purchases a different good with a different cost. The British and Canadian arrangements purchase continuity, institutional memory and a measure of neutrality in administration, and each pays with a weaker direct connection between an election result and the conduct of departments. The American arrangement after Slaughter purchases a direct connection between the electorate, the President and administration, and pays with discontinuity at each change of administration and with exposure of expert bodies to political direction. Removal doctrine, seen comparatively, is a choice about where democratic accountability is lodged, in a removable political layer above a permanent service or in presidential control over the service itself, and no feature of administration as such dictates the answer.

## What Is Settled and What Remains Open

Several propositions in removal law stand settled beyond serious litigation. Congress may not reserve a removal role to itself, a proposition established by Bowsher v. Synar in 1986 for officers who execute the laws and reinforced by the structure of every later decision in the line. A principal officer who exercises purely executive authority, including the single director of an agency that makes rules and brings enforcement actions, is removable by the President at will after Seila Law, Collins v. Yellen and Slaughter. Two layers of for-cause protection fail together, as Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board held in 2010, because insulation compounded through two protected offices leaves the President unable to reach the officer who executes the law. An unlawful removal clause does not by itself void the past acts of an agency, a remedial limit that Collins established when the Court declined to unwind actions taken by a director who had served under an unconstitutional protection. A reader returning to the doctrine table set out earlier will see those holdings in sequence, arranged as a doctrine that moved by narrowing exceptions rather than by announcing a final rule in any single case.

The open questions concern the institutions that the settled propositions do not cleanly describe. The Federal Reserve holds the first position on that list. Trump v. Cook, decided on the same day as Slaughter, treated the Board of Governors differently from the heads of executive agencies, and the reasoning in Cook leaves the final position of the central bank unresolved in important respects. A litigant challenging a future removal from another financial regulator will test whether the Cook reasoning travels beyond the Federal Reserve, and a litigant defending the Board will argue that the central bank stands alone.

Adjudicatory multimember bodies present the second open question, for the reasons developed in the discussion of administrative judges and claims boards. A body that decides cases and performs no rulemaking or enforcement function fits the Slaughter working test poorly, and the Court has given no holding that squarely governs such a body after the overruling of Humphrey's Executor for executive agencies. Non-Article III judges present the third question, expressly reserved in Slaughter and left without majority support for any extension of the new rule. The Tax Court, the Court of Federal Claims and the bankruptcy and magistrate systems each combine judicial method with a statutory home outside Article III, and removal rules for those judges implicate the fairness of proceedings between private parties and the government. The fourth question concerns reach. Bodies that only advise, study and report exercise no binding authority, and a removal test built on binding authority may leave such bodies available for congressional design even after Slaughter. The answers will arrive agency by agency, and the map of independent government that emerges will be drawn by those answers rather than by the rule for enforcement agencies, which has already been drawn.

## Why a Power the Text Never Grants Decides So Much

A statute means, in practice, what the officials who administer the statute take the statute to mean. Congress writes prohibitions, permissions, standards and deadlines, and an agency gives those words operational content through rules, guidance, case selection and adjudication. Control over dismissal is therefore control over the meaning of every statute an agency administers, because the official who fears removal for an interpretation will choose a different interpretation, and the official who expects removal for inaction will act. The power governs through anticipation, in the memorandum drafted with a known reader in mind, in the enforcement recommendation shaped for a known decision maker, and in the rule preamble written to survive review by the administration that appointed the author. The Constitution grants appointment in words, provides impeachment in words and says nothing in words about the ordinary removal of an executive officer, and the silence has been argued in every generation since the First Congress divided over the question in 1789 because the practical government of the United States turns on the answer.

Each generation has answered the silence with the materials of its own constitutional method. The First Congress answered through legislative debate and a vote that later Courts treated as interpretation. The nineteenth century answered through practice, through the Tenure of Office Act and through an impeachment that turned in part on a removal. The twentieth century answered through Myers, Humphrey's Executor and Morrison, decisions that divided the administrative state into categories and assigned a removal rule to each category. The twenty-first century answered by dissolving most of the categories and returning to a single inquiry about executive power, first for single directors, then for a financial regulator and then, in Slaughter, for the multimember commissions that had defined the older settlement. A companion [notebook reference](https://vaultbook.net) is available for readers who keep their own record of the argument.

The structural implication reaches beyond any administration that benefits from the rule or loses under the rule. A constitution that lodges execution in one President and legislation in a Congress of two houses must locate the administrators who stand between statute and citizen, and the location chosen determines whose judgment prevails when a statute admits two readings, whose priorities govern when resources permit only some enforcement, and whose accountability an electorate may enforce when administration disappoints. Removal is the legal form of that location decision. The decision will be revisited whenever the administrative state changes shape, because new agencies pose the old question in new institutional dress, and because a power inferred from structure rather than granted in text remains open to reconsideration by the same structural reasoning that produced the inference. The question endures for the reason the silence endures. A written constitution that creates a government must still decide, in practice, who may end the service of the people who run that government, and every answer rearranges the balance among the branches that the text established.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is presidential removal power in United States law?
Presidential removal power is the authority claimed for the President to dismiss executive officers of the United States without obtaining permission from Congress or from a court. The power concerns officers appointed to administer federal statutes, including heads of departments, members of commissions and directors of bureaus. Doctrine distinguishes between removal at will, which requires no stated reason and permits no review of the reason given, and removal for cause, which a statute limits to specified grounds such as neglect or misconduct. The scope of the power has been defined mainly by Supreme Court decisions rather than by any single constitutional clause, and the answer varies with the kind of officer, the functions performed and the number of protective layers a statute creates. Military command, judicial tenure and ordinary federal employment each follow separate rules outside the central doctrine.

### Q: Where does the Constitution mention removing federal officers?
The Constitution contains no clause that expressly grants a general power to remove executive officers. Impeachment appears in Article I and Article II as the method for removing a President, a Vice President and civil officers after trial in the Senate following charges brought by the House of Representatives. Article II vests executive power in the President, grants authority to appoint officers with Senate consent for principal offices, and requires the President to take care that the laws be faithfully executed. Article III protects judges through tenure during good behaviour. Supporters of a broad presidential power infer removal from the vesting and take care provisions combined together. Defenders of congressional authority answer that the Appointments Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause permit Congress to structure offices, including the terms on which officers leave those offices.

### Q: What was the Decision of 1789 about?
The Decision of 1789 was the conclusion reached by the First Congress during debate over the bill creating the Department of Foreign Affairs, the predecessor of the Department of State. Members debated whether the Secretary would leave office through impeachment alone, through action by the Senate, through a rule stated in the statute itself, or through dismissal by the President under the Constitution. The House and Senate ultimately enacted language that treated the Secretary as removable by the President, and James Madison argued during the debate that the executive power and the duty of faithful execution supported that result. Later Supreme Court opinions, most prominently Myers v. United States, treated the legislative outcome as an early interpretation of the Constitution by many participants in the founding generation. Historians and judges have disputed how clear or divided that early understanding actually was.

### Q: What did Myers v. United States decide?
Myers v. United States, decided in 1926, held unconstitutional a statute that required Senate consent before the President could remove a postmaster of the first class. Frank Myers had been appointed postmaster in Portland, Oregon, and President Woodrow Wilson dismissed him before the end of his term without seeking Senate approval. Chief Justice William Howard Taft wrote an opinion grounding dismissal authority in Article II and in the Decision of 1789, reasoning that a President unable to dismiss subordinates could not control execution of the laws. The Court treated postmasters as executive officers and rejected a legislative role in their departure beyond impeachment and the original appointment process. The opinion spoke in broad terms about presidential control, and later decisions limited parts of that breadth while preserving the central holding for officers performing executive work under direct presidential supervision.

### Q: What did Humphrey's Executor v. United States decide?
Humphrey's Executor v. United States, decided in 1935, upheld a statute that permitted dismissal of a Federal Trade Commissioner only for specified causes. The Court distinguished Myers v. United States by characterizing the Federal Trade Commission as a body created to exercise quasi-legislative and quasi-judicial functions, including investigation, reporting and adjudication, rather than purely executive functions. Justice George Sutherland wrote for a unanimous Court that Congress could fix a term for commissioners and protect members against dismissal based on policy disagreement, because the commission was intended to operate free from presidential coercion in its expert and deliberative work. The holding allowed multimember independent commissions to persist across administrations with staggered membership. The decision stood as the principal authority for for-cause protection of commissioners until Trump v. Slaughter overruled the ruling for agencies exercising executive power in 2026.

### Q: What do the words inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office mean in a removal statute?
The phrase inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office appears in several statutes that limit dismissal of commissioners to cause. Inefficiency generally refers to failure to perform the work of an office with competence and diligence over a meaningful period, rather than a single error of judgment. Neglect of duty refers to failure to carry out obligations assigned by law, including persistent absence, refusal to act on matters requiring action, or disregard of statutory procedures. Malfeasance in office refers to wrongful conduct in the performance of official duties, such as corruption, abuse of authority, or intentional violation of law. Courts have rarely defined the phrase in removal litigation because disputes have usually turned on whether any cause standard may constitutionally apply to the office at all, rather than on whether particular conduct satisfied the standard in a given case.

### Q: Who was William Humphrey and why did President Roosevelt remove him?
William Humphrey was a member of the Federal Trade Commission appointed by President Calvin Coolidge and reappointed by President Herbert Hoover for a term scheduled to run until 1938. Humphrey held conservative views on trade regulation and opposed several policies of President Franklin D. Roosevelt, whose administration sought a commission more receptive to expanded federal economic regulation after 1933. Roosevelt asked Humphrey to resign, stating in correspondence that the aims and purposes of the administration respecting the commission could not be carried out effectively with Humphrey in place, and Humphrey refused to leave. Roosevelt then dismissed him in October 1933 even though the Federal Trade Commission Act allowed dismissal only for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. Humphrey challenged the dismissal, died in 1934, and his executor pursued back pay, producing the Supreme Court case that bears the executor name.

### Q: What is the difference between a principal officer and an inferior officer in removal law?
A principal officer exercises significant authority pursuant to the laws of the United States, is appointed by the President with Senate consent, and answers to no higher officer apart from the President. Heads of departments and members of major commissions ordinarily fall in that class. An inferior officer performs work directed and supervised by a principal officer, holds duties limited in scope, jurisdiction or tenure, and lacks authority to make final policy decisions binding on the government. Congress may provide for appointment of inferior officers by the President alone, by department heads, or by courts of law. Removal doctrine treats the two classes differently because protection for a subordinate officer interferes less directly with presidential control when a removable superior retains authority to direct, review, or reverse the subordinate work. Morrison v. Olson turned largely on classification of the independent counsel as an inferior officer.

### Q: What did Morrison v. Olson decide about independent counsel?
Morrison v. Olson, decided in 1988, upheld provisions of the Ethics in Government Act that created an independent counsel to investigate and prosecute certain high officials. The counsel was appointed by a special division of judges, performed investigative and prosecutorial work, and could be dismissed by the Attorney General only for good cause. Ted Olson challenged the arrangement after counsel Alexia Morrison sought documents in an investigation involving the Environmental Protection Agency. Chief Justice William Rehnquist wrote for the Court that the counsel qualified as an inferior officer, that judicial appointment of such an officer was permissible, and that the good cause limit did not impermissibly burden presidential control of law execution given the limited duties and tenure involved. Justice Antonin Scalia dissented alone, arguing that prosecution is executive power and must remain subject to full presidential direction and dismissal authority.

### Q: Why did the Supreme Court strike down two layers of removal protection in the Free Enterprise Fund case?
Free Enterprise Fund v. Public Company Accounting Oversight Board, decided in 2010, involved members of an accounting oversight board created by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act after major corporate accounting scandals. Board members could be dismissed by the Securities and Exchange Commission only for cause, and commissioners themselves enjoyed protection against presidential dismissal except for cause. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that two layers of protection placed board members beyond meaningful presidential oversight, because the President could neither dismiss board members directly nor dismiss commissioners for declining to dismiss board members on policy grounds. The Court held that compounded insulation contradicted Article II accountability for execution of securities laws. The remedy severed the protection applying to board members, leaving the board in existence and leaving commissioners with the protection that a single layer provided under governing precedent at that time.

### Q: What did Seila Law v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau decide?
Seila Law v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, decided in 2020, held unconstitutional a statute protecting the single director of that bureau against dismissal except for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. The bureau, created after the financial crisis of the first decade of the twenty-first century, possessed authority to write consumer finance rules, supervise institutions, and bring enforcement actions, all directed by one person serving a five-year term. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that a sole director wielding substantial executive authority and insulated from presidential dismissal lacked the accountability that Article II requires, and distinguished multimember commissions under Humphrey's Executor. Justice Elena Kagan dissented, defending congressional authority to design agencies and disputing a textual basis for the majority rule. The Court severed the protection clause and left the bureau operating with a director removable at will.

### Q: What did Collins v. Yellen decide?
Collins v. Yellen, decided in 2021, concerned the Federal Housing Finance Agency, which regulates Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac and acted as conservator for both enterprises after the financial crisis. Shareholders challenged agency action and argued that the single director was unconstitutionally protected by a statute permitting dismissal only for cause. The Supreme Court agreed that the structure violated the separation of powers under the reasoning of Seila Law, because the director exercised broad authority over housing finance while answering to no elected official through dismissal. The Court nevertheless declined to invalidate the agency actions under challenge, holding that an unconstitutional protection clause does not by itself void decisions made by an officer who was validly appointed. Collins therefore extended the at-will rule for single directors and simultaneously limited the remedy available to parties injured by action taken under the invalid structure.

### Q: What did Trump v. Slaughter decide?
Trump v. Slaughter, decided on June 29, 2026, held that for-cause protection for members of the Federal Trade Commission violated the separation of powers and overruled Humphrey's Executor v. United States for agencies exercising executive power. The case arose after dismissal of a commissioner whose statutory term had not expired and who had committed no act alleged to constitute inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office. The majority reasoned that the commission makes binding rules, investigates firms, brings enforcement proceedings, and seeks penalties, and that officers performing such functions must remain subject to presidential dismissal at will. The ruling removed the doctrinal basis for protection of commissioners at executive agencies generally, while leaving open questions concerning adjudicatory bodies, judges of non-Article III courts, inferior officers, and the Federal Reserve, which the Court addressed separately in Trump v. Cook on the same date.

### Q: Why was the Federal Reserve treated differently in Trump v. Cook?
Trump v. Cook, decided on June 29, 2026, treated the Federal Reserve Board of Governors as distinct from the executive agencies governed by Trump v. Slaughter. The Court pointed to the structure and history of central banking in the United States, including earlier national banks and the design adopted in the Federal Reserve Act, in which governors serve long staggered terms intended to separate monetary policy decisions from the electoral cycle. Interest rate policy also differs from ordinary enforcement because a decision to raise or lower rates binds through markets rather than through orders directed at named private parties. Governors combine rulemaking and supervisory functions with that monetary role, which complicated any single classification. The outcome preserved protection for governors while the broader commission rule changed, and later cases must determine whether the distinction rests on history, function, or a category limited to the central bank alone.

### Q: Can Congress remove an executive officer by its own vote?
Congress may not remove an executive officer by its own vote, resolution, or statute directed at an individual officeholder, apart from impeachment. In Bowsher v. Synar in 1986, the Supreme Court invalidated a budget statute that gave the Comptroller General a role in executive decisions while making that officer removable by a joint resolution of Congress for specified causes. Chief Justice Warren Burger wrote that control over dismissal gives control over execution, and that a legislative role in dismissal of an officer performing executive functions violates the separation of powers. Congress retains other instruments, including appropriations, oversight hearings, restructuring of offices by general legislation, abolition of an office created by statute, and impeachment of civil officers by the House followed by trial in the Senate. A general law changing an office differs legally from a legislative decision dismissing the person who holds that office.

### Q: What happens to agency actions taken while an officer was unlawfully protected from removal?
Agency actions taken while an officer served under an unconstitutional removal protection ordinarily remain valid. In Collins v. Yellen, the Supreme Court refused to unwind actions of the Federal Housing Finance Agency even after holding that the director protection clause violated the separation of powers, because the director had been appointed through a lawful process and the protection defect did not show that specific actions would have differed under a removable director. Courts applying Collins generally require a challenger to show a connection between the constitutional defect and harm in the challenged action, rather than treating every act of the agency as void. The appointment defect in cases such as Lucia v. SEC produces a different remedy because the officer lacked lawful authority to hold the position at all. Removal protection, by contrast, concerns the terms of service of an officer whose appointment itself was valid.

### Q: Can the President remove a federal judge?
The President may not remove a federal judge holding office under Article III. Judges of the Supreme Court, the courts of appeals and the district courts hold their offices during good behaviour under Article III, and that tenure continues until death, resignation, retirement, or removal through impeachment. The House of Representatives may bring articles of impeachment, and the Senate may convict by a two-thirds vote, after which the judge leaves office and may also be disqualified from future federal office by a separate Senate vote. Salary protection reinforces the same independence, because compensation for an Article III judge may not be diminished during service. Discipline short of removal belongs to judicial councils within limits set by statute, and criminal prosecution remains available for conduct that violates federal law. Presidential disagreement with a ruling provides no ground for dismissal of any kind.

### Q: What is the unitary executive theory?
The unitary executive theory holds that Article II vests all executive power in the President personally, and that every officer who exercises executive authority must therefore remain subject to presidential direction and dismissal sufficient to preserve presidential control. Supporters ground the theory in the vesting clause of Article II, the duty to take care that laws be faithfully executed, and the Decision of 1789. Justice Antonin Scalia expressed a strong version of the argument in a dissent in Morrison v. Olson, maintaining that prosecution and other core executive functions may not be placed beyond full presidential authority. Critics of the theory, including Justice Elena Kagan in a dissent in Seila Law, answer that the Constitution grants Congress broad authority to structure government offices and that historical practice includes many arrangements limiting presidential control. Modern Supreme Court majorities have adopted major elements of the theory in removal cases without always using the label.

### Q: How does removal work for commissioned military officers?
Commissioned military officers hold office under statutes enacted by Congress, and separation from service follows procedures established in Title 10 of the United States Code and in service regulations issued under that authority. The President acts as Commander in Chief under Article II, and that constitutional role interacts with statutory provisions governing appointment, promotion, retirement and dismissal. An officer may leave service through resignation accepted under applicable rules, through retirement after qualifying service, through administrative separation proceedings, or through sentence of a court-martial convened under the Uniform Code of Military Justice. Each route carries its own procedural requirements, including notice and an opportunity to respond in many administrative contexts. Removal doctrine developed for civilian agency heads does not govern military separation directly, because the statutory framework for the armed forces, the command structure and court-martial jurisdiction create a distinct system for ending an officer commission.

### Q: Why do supporters of independent agencies say removal protection improves decisions?
Supporters of independent agencies argue that protection against dismissal for policy disagreement improves decisions by allowing officials to apply expertise, evidence and long-term analysis without adjusting each judgment to the preferences of an administration that may replace the officials at any moment. Justice Sotomayor made a version of that argument in a dissent in Trump v. Slaughter, emphasizing expert multimember bodies and the value of deliberation among members appointed under different administrations. Supporters also point to credibility in fields such as monetary policy, where markets and foreign governments respond to expectations that policy will remain steady across political transitions, and to fairness in adjudication, where parties require confidence that a decision maker faces no penalty for ruling against the government. A further argument concerns error correction over time, because staggered terms preserve institutional knowledge that a wholesale replacement of leadership at each transition would discard.
