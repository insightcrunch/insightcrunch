A broken slab of dark grey granodiorite, about a metre high and weighing something over three quarters of a tonne, carries on one face a tax-and-temple decree issued by a committee of priests in 196 BCE on behalf of a thirteen year old king. The text is administrative, formulaic, and in substance rather dull: a list of honours voted to the monarch, a schedule of concessions he had granted, and an instruction that copies be set up in temples across the country. Several hundred similar decrees were issued in Egypt over the Ptolemaic centuries and almost none of them attract any attention at all.

![The Rosetta Stone and how the hieroglyphs of ancient Egypt were deciphered - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-110.webp)

This one reopened three thousand years of Egyptian writing. The reason has nothing to do with what it says and everything to do with how it says it: the same text appears three times, in hieroglyphic, in a cursive Egyptian script called Demotic, and in Greek, and Greek was a language European scholars had never stopped being able to read. Somewhere in the relationship between those three panels lay the possibility of working backward from a known language to an unknown one, and in 1822 a French scholar who had never set foot in Egypt did exactly that.

The framework this article advances can be called the key-that-opened-a-civilization claim, and it needs stating carefully because both the loose version and the debunking version are wrong. The loose version holds that the Rosetta Stone was deciphered and hieroglyphs were thereby read, as though the object contained a solution that could be extracted. The debunking version, popular among people who enjoy correcting the loose version, holds that the stone was incidental and that decipherment would have happened anyway from other material. Neither is right. The stone was necessary and insufficient. It supplied the one thing no other source of the period could supply, a substantial Egyptian text whose meaning was already known, and it supplied it in a form so damaged and so partial that reading it required twenty three years, a competition between scholars in several countries, a dead language preserved in the liturgy of Egyptian Christians, and an obelisk from an island at the First Cataract.

What that effort recovered is the point of the whole exercise. Before 1822, ancient Egypt was mute. Its monuments could be measured, its tombs entered, its statues admired, and nothing it had written about itself could be read. Everything Europe believed about Egyptian religion, history, and thought came from Greek and Roman authors describing the place from outside and late, and from a tradition of mystical speculation that had been confidently wrong for a thousand years. After 1822 the Egyptians could speak for themselves: their king lists, their letters, their accounts, their poetry, their medical texts, their complaints about workmen's wages. That is what a decipherment is for, and it is why an unremarkable priestly decree is one of the most consequential objects ever recovered from the ground.

## What the stone physically is

Getting the object itself right matters, because a great deal of loose description circulates about it.

It is a stele, an upright inscribed slab of the kind Egyptian temples used for public proclamations, cut from granodiorite, a dark speckled igneous stone frequently misdescribed as basalt in older accounts. It stands roughly one hundred and twelve centimetres high, about seventy five wide, and close to twenty eight thick, and weighs in the region of seven hundred and sixty kilograms.

It is broken. The top is gone, including whatever winged disc and lunette scene the original carried, and the lower right corner is missing as well. The surviving fragment preserves fourteen lines of hieroglyphic text, all of them incomplete at one end or the other, thirty two lines of Demotic with some damage at the right-hand ends, and fifty four lines of Greek with the bottom right lost. The complete stele would have been considerably taller, and estimates of its original height are reconstructions rather than measurements.

The proportions of the three panels are worth noticing because they explain the shape of the problem. The hieroglyphic section, the one everybody wanted to read, is the smallest and the most damaged. The Demotic, which nobody in Europe could read either, is the largest and best preserved. The Greek, the key, is complete enough to be translated in full.

The surface carries two later additions in white paint on the sides, recording in English that the stone was captured in Egypt by the British army in 1801 and presented by King George III. Those words are not part of the monument. They are a nineteenth century museum label applied directly to the object, and they have become part of the argument about it.

### Why had hieroglyphs become unreadable at all?

Because the script died with the culture that used it. Hieroglyphic writing fell out of use as temples closed under Christian Roman rule, the last dated inscription belongs to the late fourth century CE, and within a few generations nobody alive could read it. The Egyptian language survived in Coptic, written in Greek letters, and the old script did not.

## The thousand years of silence

To understand what the stone made possible, you have to understand how completely the knowledge had been lost, and for how long.

Hieroglyphic writing had been in continuous use for something over three thousand years. Its last securely dated inscription was cut at the temple on the island of Philae near the First Cataract late in the fourth century CE, by which point the script was the preserve of a shrinking priesthood in a country that had largely become Christian. When the temples closed, the training stopped, and a writing system that had to be taught died within a generation or two of its last teachers.

What survived was the language, not the script. Egyptian continued to be spoken, and in its final stage it was written down in Greek letters with a handful of extra signs borrowed from Demotic for sounds Greek lacked. That written form is Coptic, and it became the liturgical language of the Egyptian church. Coptic was still being used in church services when the Rosetta Stone was found, which turns out to be the single most important fact in the entire decipherment.

What replaced knowledge was a theory, and the theory was wrong in a specific and paralysing way. Greek and Roman writers who described Egypt, including the late author Horapollo whose treatise on hieroglyphs circulated widely in Europe from the fifteenth century, presented the signs as symbolic rather than phonetic: a picture of a goose meant son because geese love their offspring, and so on. That account contains a grain of truth about certain determinative signs and is catastrophically misleading as a general principle.

European scholarship then built on it for centuries. The seventeenth century Jesuit polymath Athanasius Kircher, a genuinely formidable linguist who did important work on Coptic, produced translations of hieroglyphic inscriptions that were entirely imaginary, rendering short royal names as elaborate mystical sentences about the divine order. His errors were not careless; they were the logical output of the symbolic assumption applied with great learning.

The assumption was so entrenched that even after phonetic evidence appeared, serious scholars resisted it. If the signs were picture-symbols standing for ideas, no amount of comparison with a Greek text could yield a reading, because there would be no grammar and no sound values to recover. The first thing the decipherment had to overcome was not the difficulty of the script but a confident misdescription of what kind of thing the script was.

## The decree and why it was carved

The text on the stone is a specific document from a specific political moment, and knowing the moment explains why three scripts appear together.

Egypt in 196 BCE was ruled by a Greek-speaking Macedonian dynasty founded after Alexander's conquest, the subject of the article on [Ptolemaic Egypt](/2007/07/01/ptolemaic-egypt-complete-guide/). The reigning king was Ptolemy V Epiphanes, who had come to the throne as a small child after the deaths of both parents, and whose minority had been a period of severe instability: court murders, the loss of most of the dynasty's overseas possessions to Seleucid and Macedonian rivals, and a serious native Egyptian revolt in the south that had produced rival indigenous kings at Thebes for the better part of two decades.

In 197 BCE the young king was formally crowned at Memphis according to Egyptian rite, which was itself a political gesture toward the priesthood and the native population. The decree on the stone was issued by an assembly of priests meeting at Memphis in the following year, on the anniversary of that coronation.

Its content is an exchange. The priests record that the king has remitted certain taxes and arrears, made grants to the temples, released prisoners, and provided for the sacred animals, and in return they vote him a package of honours: cult statues in every temple, a festival on his birthday and accession day, a special title, and the establishment of his image in the shrines. The final clause orders that the decree be inscribed on hard stone in the sacred writing, the native writing, and the Greek writing, and set up in temples throughout the kingdom.

That last clause is the reason the object exists in the form it does. The three scripts are not a helpful accident. They are a deliberate instruction reflecting the three constituencies the decree addressed: the temple establishment that used hieroglyphs, the Egyptian administrative and commercial world that used Demotic, and the Greek-speaking government that ran the country.

It is worth registering what this implies about the Ptolemaic state. A regime that must publish the same text in three forms to be understood by its own subjects is a regime managing a linguistic and political division it has not resolved, and the decree's generous concessions to the priesthood read as the settlement of a king who had just spent twenty years unable to control Upper Egypt.

## Who could actually read the three panels

A question the stone raises and rarely gets asked is who, in 196 BCE, could read any of it, and the answer changes how the object should be understood.

Literacy in Egypt at any period was a minority skill confined largely to a professional scribal class, and estimates for the proportion of the population who could read run low by every reckoning. The three panels therefore did not address three reading publics in any straightforward sense.

Hieroglyphic writing by the Ptolemaic period was the preserve of a shrinking temple elite. It had become more elaborate rather than less, with the number of signs in use expanding considerably as priests developed increasingly learned and cryptographic spellings, which is one reason Ptolemaic hieroglyphic texts are harder for modern scholars than earlier ones. Very few people could read the top panel when it was carved.

Demotic was the working script of the Egyptian administrative and commercial world, so its readership was wider, though still confined to those with scribal training. The middle panel is the one an Egyptian professional might actually have read.

Greek was the language of government, of the army, of the Greek settler population, and of Egyptians who had learned it for advancement. It was widely readable among those who mattered politically and not at all among the peasantry.

So the trilingual format is best understood as symbolic and jurisdictional rather than practical. Publishing in the sacred writing asserted that the decree had temple authority; publishing in the native writing asserted that it was an Egyptian document; publishing in Greek made it an act of the government. Very few individuals could have checked one panel against another, which is the exact operation the decree's format later made possible for people two thousand years away.

There is something worth sitting with in that. The instruction that made the decipherment possible was issued for reasons of political display, by a priestly committee flattering a teenage king, with no conception that the redundancy they were building into the monument would one day be the only reason any of it could be read.

## The find

The stone came out of the ground in July 1799 during military engineering work, which is an unglamorous origin that has been steadily romanticized over two centuries.

French troops were rebuilding an old Ottoman fort at el-Rashid, the town Europeans called Rosetta, on the western branch of the Nile in the Delta, as part of the defensive preparations of an occupation that had already lost its fleet. The full setting is described in the article on [Napoleon's invasion of Egypt](/2013/10/01/napoleon-invasion-of-egypt/). Demolition and reconstruction of this kind routinely turned up ancient stone, because Egyptian monuments had been quarried for building material for centuries and Delta forts were full of reused blocks.

An officer of engineers, Pierre-Francois Bouchard, is credited with recognizing that this particular block was different, and the recognition rather than the digging is what mattered. A slab carrying three distinct scripts including Greek was obviously significant to anyone with a classical education, and the expedition's officer corps had one.

The stone was sent to Cairo and placed at the disposal of the scientific commission whose work is described in the article on [how Napoleon's scholars revealed Egypt](/2013/12/01/napoleon-scholars-rediscovered-egypt/). The scholars grasped the implication immediately and their first priority was reproduction rather than study, which was exactly the right instinct.

They made copies by inking the incised surface and pressing paper onto it, producing reversed impressions, and also by taking sulphur casts. Those copies went to Paris and circulated from 1800 onward. This detail deserves emphasis because it determined everything that followed: the decipherment was carried out from reproductions, by people who mostly never saw the object, and the physical stone's subsequent travels had no bearing on the scholarship at all.

The Greek text was translated promptly, since translating Greek was a routine skill. Within a short time European scholars knew precisely what the stone said. What they did not know was how any of it worked in the other two scripts.

## How it came to London

The transfer of the object to Britain is a clause in a surrender document and is the origin of everything contested about it since.

When the French capitulated at Alexandria on 2 September 1801, the terms included provision for the antiquities and collections assembled during the occupation. The British commanders demanded them as the property of a defeated state; the scholars resisted furiously and threatened destruction; a compromise let the commission keep its notes, drawings, and natural history material while the large antiquities passed to Britain.

The Rosetta Stone was the most significant object in that transfer. It was shipped to England, arrived in early 1802, was presented to the British Museum by George III, and has been displayed there since, apart from wartime removals for safekeeping.

The painted inscriptions on its sides were applied at that time and record the British army's capture and the royal presentation. A museum of the early nineteenth century saw nothing odd in writing on an ancient monument to record how it had been acquired, and the words have since become the most quoted evidence in the argument over whether it should stay.

Three things about this sequence are worth fixing clearly, because the popular account blurs them.

First, no Egyptian party was present at the negotiation. The stone passed from one European army to another under a treaty between France, Britain, and the Ottoman Empire, at a moment when Egypt had no government of its own and its population had been consulted about nothing.

Second, the Ottoman Empire was the legal sovereign and a party to the arrangement, which is the basis of the claim that the acquisition was lawful. Whether the consent of a distant imperial sovereign, whose actual control over Egypt had been negligible for a century as the article on [Ottoman Egypt](/2013/05/01/ottoman-egypt-complete-guide/) sets out, settles a modern ownership question is exactly what is in dispute.

Third, and most importantly for the history of scholarship, none of it mattered to the decipherment. Copies were already in Paris. Champollion worked from paper.

## Three scripts, two languages

The most common error about the stone is to say it carries three languages. It carries three scripts and two languages, and the distinction is the whole basis of the decipherment.

The top panel is hieroglyphic, the monumental script of temple and tomb, in use since the beginning of Egyptian writing and treated by the culture itself as sacred, a subject taken up in the article on [the origins of Egyptian hieroglyphs](/2000/05/01/origins-of-egyptian-hieroglyphs/). It is Egyptian language written in the formal script.

The middle panel is Demotic, which is also Egyptian language, written in a fast cursive script that had developed out of earlier cursive forms and was the everyday writing of contracts, letters, and administration by the Ptolemaic period. Demotic looks nothing like hieroglyphs to an untrained eye, and the relationship between them was not obvious to the people trying to work it out.

The bottom panel is Greek, a different language in a familiar alphabet.

So the stone offers two routes at once. Greek to Demotic is a bilingual comparison between two languages. Demotic to hieroglyphic is a comparison between two scripts recording the same language. Both routes were attempted, and the successful path ran through both: scholars worked from Greek into Demotic first, because Demotic was better preserved, and only then across into the hieroglyphic panel.

A further complication made the hieroglyphic panel harder than the raw damage suggests. The hieroglyphic version is not a word-for-word transcription of the Greek. It is a translation into a formal register with its own conventions, so a scholar counting words and expecting one-to-one correspondence would go wrong immediately. The number of distinct hieroglyphic signs visible on the stone also vastly exceeded the number of Greek words, which was taken by some as proof that the signs could not be phonetic, since an alphabet would need far fewer characters.

That inference was reasonable and wrong. Egyptian writing uses several kinds of sign at once: signs standing for one consonant, signs standing for two or three consonants, signs standing for whole words, and signs that carry no sound at all but tell the reader what category of thing the preceding word belongs to. A system that mixes all of these will generate a large inventory of characters while still being fundamentally phonetic, and working that out was the hard part.

## The first attempts

Between 1802 and 1814 the work concentrated on Demotic and produced real but limited progress, mostly by two men.

Antoine-Isaac Silvestre de Sacy was the leading French orientalist of the period, a formidable Arabist and teacher, and he took up the Demotic panel early. He identified some proper names by matching their positions against the Greek and concluded, correctly, that the method could be extended. He also concluded, incorrectly, that the task was beyond reach, and eventually turned away from it while continuing to encourage others. His pupils included Champollion.

Johan David Akerblad, a Swedish diplomat and orientalist, went considerably further. Working on the Demotic in 1802, he identified the proper names occurring in the Greek and read them letter by letter in the Egyptian panel, producing a working alphabet of Demotic signs that was substantially correct for those names. He also recognized several Demotic words that corresponded to Coptic vocabulary he knew.

Akerblad's result was a genuine breakthrough and it stalled for a structural reason. His alphabet worked for foreign names and failed when applied to ordinary Demotic words, which produced gibberish. The conclusion drawn, by him and by others, was that Demotic was alphabetic only when transcribing foreign names and symbolic otherwise. The truth is that Demotic, like the hieroglyphic script it descends from, mixes phonetic signs with word signs and determinatives, so a purely alphabetic reading of running text was always going to break down.

That misdiagnosis held the field for a decade. It is the same error, in a different script, that the symbolic theory had been producing since Kircher.

## Thomas Young

The English polymath who took up the problem in 1814 made advances that were essential and that he could not follow through, and the reasons are as instructive as the results.

Young was a physician by training and one of the most versatile minds of the age, known for work on the wave theory of light, on elasticity, and on physiology. He came to the Egyptian material as an outsider with formidable analytical habits and no particular investment in the symbolic theory.

His contributions were substantial. He established that Demotic was not a separate invention but derived from the hieroglyphic script, which connected the two Egyptian panels for the first time. He identified the group of signs in the hieroglyphic text enclosed in an oval ring, the cartouche, as containing the name Ptolemy, following a suggestion that had been in circulation that cartouches marked royal names. He worked out approximate sound values for several of the signs in that name. He produced a substantial vocabulary of Demotic words matched to the Greek, a considerable proportion of which proved correct. And he recognized that the hieroglyphic text contained signs used phonetically.

His limitation was conceptual rather than technical. Young held that phonetic writing in hieroglyphs was a special device, adopted to render foreign names like Ptolemy and Berenice that the symbolic system could not accommodate, and that native Egyptian words were written symbolically. That is exactly the position Akerblad had reached with Demotic, and it is a natural inference if you believe the script is fundamentally pictographic and has bolted on an alphabet for imported words.

Holding that position meant he could read royal names and could not read a sentence, and he stopped where the position stopped him.

The priority dispute that followed between Young's supporters and Champollion's has generated more heat than it deserves. The fair account is that Young opened the door and could not walk through it, that Champollion used results Young had published and acknowledged them inconsistently, and that the decisive step was Champollion's alone. National feeling on both sides has kept the argument alive well beyond its interest.

### What exactly did the stone provide that earlier attempts lacked?

A substantial Egyptian text whose meaning was already known, with proper names occurring at identifiable points. Everything before it was guesswork against a symbolic theory with nothing to check against. The stone converted the problem from interpretation into comparison, and comparison can be tested and corrected.

## Champollion

The man who solved it had been preparing for it since childhood, and the preparation is the reason he succeeded where better-known scholars did not.

Jean-Francois Champollion was born in 1790 in southwestern France, showed an extraordinary aptitude for languages very early, and fixed on Egypt as a subject in his teens. By his twenties he had worked through Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, Aramaic, Persian, Chinese, and, decisively, Coptic. He studied Coptic seriously and at length, reading liturgical manuscripts and learning it to the point where he used it in private notes, and he did so on the explicit conviction that Coptic was the surviving form of the ancient Egyptian language.

That conviction was the key asset. If the hieroglyphic script records a language, and if that language survives in Coptic, then a scholar who recovers a sound value for a sign can test it by asking whether the resulting word exists in Coptic and means what the context requires. Without Coptic, a proposed sound value can be neither confirmed nor refuted. Young did not have Coptic at anything like this level, and that gap is the largest single reason he stopped short.

Champollion's career was unstable. He held a post at Grenoble, lost it for political reasons in the upheavals around the Bourbon restoration, was for a time under suspicion and in internal exile, and worked on the Egyptian material through periods of poverty and ill health. The decipherment was not produced by a well-funded institutional programme. It was produced by an obsessive private scholar working from published copies.

## The cross-check that gave the alphabet

The first decisive move was a comparison between two royal names, and it is worth following the logic because it is the cleanest piece of reasoning in the whole affair.

A cartouche containing the name Ptolemy was known from the Rosetta Stone. A second monument supplied a cartouche containing the name Cleopatra: an obelisk brought from the island of Philae by an English traveller, which carried hieroglyphic cartouches and stood on a base with a Greek inscription naming Ptolemy and Cleopatra.

Set the two names side by side in Greek and the overlap is obvious. Ptolemaios and Kleopatra share the letters P, T, O, and L, in different positions. If the hieroglyphic signs are phonetic, the sign standing for P in one cartouche must be the same sign standing for P in the other, and the same for the rest.

That is exactly what the comparison showed. The signs matched where the letters matched and differed where the letters differed, and the positions lined up. The test was falsifiable, it could have failed, and it did not. One or two apparent discrepancies turned out to reflect the existence of alternative signs for the same sound, a feature of the script rather than a flaw in the method.

From two names Champollion extracted a working set of sound values, and by applying them to other Greek and Roman royal cartouches available from monuments, names like Alexander, Berenice, Tiberius, and the Roman imperial titles, he extended and confirmed the alphabet. Each new name was another test.

At this stage he had gone further than Young in extent but not in principle. Everything so far was consistent with Young's view that phonetic writing was a device reserved for foreign names.

## The fourteenth of September 1822

The step that broke the problem open came from material that had nothing to do with the Rosetta Stone, and it is the single most important moment in the history of Egyptology.

Champollion received copies of reliefs from Abu Simbel in Nubia, monuments of a period long before any Greek or Roman had ruled Egypt. Among the cartouches was one beginning with a disc, followed by a sign he provisionally read as an S, repeated.

He knew the Coptic word for sun: ra, or re. If the disc was read for its meaning in Egyptian and pronounced as the Egyptian word for sun, then the cartouche read something like ra plus a consonant plus s plus s. Filling in the pattern gives the name known from Greek sources as Ramesses.

He then tested the method on a second cartouche beginning with the sign of an ibis, the bird associated with the god Thoth, giving a name of the form Thoth plus m plus s, which yields Thutmose, another name known from classical authors.

The implication was immediate and enormous. These were native Egyptian royal names, from monuments a thousand years older than the Ptolemies, and they were written phonetically. Phonetic writing was not a late device for foreign words. It was how the script worked, and had always worked.

The story of what happened next is well attested in family accounts: Champollion went to his brother's office, announced that he had it, and collapsed, remaining incapacitated for several days. The anecdote has the shape of legend and the sources are close to the event, so it is probably close to true.

On 27 September 1822 he read a paper to the Academy of Inscriptions in Paris, published as a letter addressed to its secretary, setting out the phonetic alphabet for Greek and Roman names. He was more cautious in print than in private about extending the principle to native Egyptian, and the full argument came two years later in a longer work that laid out the mixed system of phonetic signs, word signs, and determinatives.

### How did Champollion prove phonetic signs were used for Egyptian names?

By reading cartouches from Abu Simbel, monuments centuries older than any Greek presence. A sun disc read with the Coptic word for sun, followed by consonants he had already established, produced Ramesses; an ibis sign produced Thutmose. Native names were written phonetically, which destroyed the theory that phonetics were reserved for foreign words.

## The obelisk from Philae

The second object in the decipherment gets almost no public attention and was arguably as necessary as the first, so it deserves its own account.

An English traveller and collector, William John Bankes, acquired an obelisk from the island of Philae at the First Cataract in the second decade of the nineteenth century and had it shipped to his estate in Dorset, an operation of considerable difficulty that involved the obelisk being dropped into the Nile at one point and recovered.

The object carried hieroglyphic cartouches, and its pedestal carried a Greek inscription, a petition from the priests of the temple that names Ptolemy and Cleopatra. The pairing of a Greek text naming two rulers with hieroglyphic cartouches on the same monument made it a second bilingual anchor, smaller and less complete than the Rosetta Stone and, for the specific purpose of building a phonetic alphabet, more useful.

Bankes had lithographic copies made and distributed to scholars, and he formed a view about which cartouche contained Cleopatra's name and marked it on the copies. Champollion received one of these copies and used the Ptolemy and Cleopatra pairing for the cross-check that produced his sound values.

The episode adds a small coda to the priority disputes. Bankes felt his identification had gone unacknowledged, and there was some ill feeling about it, which is the third attribution quarrel this decipherment generated. Serious historians of the subject treat his contribution as real and preliminary, in the same category as Young's.

The larger point is structural. The Rosetta Stone alone gives one royal name. Two monuments give two names with overlapping letters, and overlap is what makes a cross-check possible. A single bilingual is a constraint; two bilinguals in the same language pair start to be a system.

## How the copies were made

The reproduction technique used in Cairo in 1799 is worth a short account because the entire decipherment depended on it.

The commission's printers took the stone, inked its incised face, and pressed paper onto the surface, producing an impression in which the carved characters appeared in reverse. Multiple pulls were taken. They also produced casts in sulphur and, later, plaster, which gave a three-dimensional record.

This was a printer's solution to a scholar's problem, and it worked because the inscription is incised rather than raised. The reversed impressions could be read directly by anyone willing to work in mirror image, or reversed again in reproduction.

The consequence is the most underrated fact in the whole story. Because good copies existed and circulated from 1800, the physical object became irrelevant to the scholarship within a year of its discovery. Silvestre de Sacy, Akerblad, Young, and Champollion all worked from reproductions. The stone's transfer to Britain, its display, its later cleaning, and its present location had no effect whatever on the decipherment, and the argument about where it should be held has never been an argument about scholarly access.

## Fourteen centuries of silence, in perspective

It helps to put the length of the gap into terms that make its scale felt.

The last dated hieroglyphic inscription was cut at the end of the fourth century CE. The decipherment was published in 1822. That is roughly fourteen hundred and thirty years, during which a writing system used continuously for over three thousand years was completely unreadable.

To calibrate: the gap between the last hieroglyphic inscription and Champollion is longer than the gap between Champollion and the founding of Rome would be if you ran it the other way. It is longer than the entire history of the English language. Whole civilizations rose and disappeared inside it.

During those centuries the monuments stood in plain sight, covered in text, in a country that people visited, wrote about, and lived in. Travellers copied inscriptions. Scholars speculated. Nobody could read a single word of the most heavily documented ancient civilization on earth, standing in front of documents.

That is the condition the stone ended, and stating the length of it is the quickest way to convey why an unremarkable tax decree is treated the way it is.

## How the system actually works

What Champollion recovered was not an alphabet but a mixed system, and understanding its structure explains why so many intelligent people had failed for so long.

Egyptian writing uses four kinds of sign at once. There are signs standing for a single consonant, roughly two dozen of them, which function like an alphabet. There are signs standing for two consonants and signs standing for three, a much larger group. There are logograms, signs standing for a whole word, often the thing pictured. And there are determinatives, signs written at the end of a word that are never pronounced and that tell the reader what category the word belongs to: a seated man after a word for a person, a pair of walking legs after a verb of motion, a roll of papyrus after an abstract noun.

Vowels are not written. Egyptian, like other Afroasiatic languages, writes the consonantal skeleton and leaves vocalization to the reader, which is why modern renderings of Egyptian names are conventional fillers rather than reconstructions of how the words sounded. Nobody knows how the name conventionally written Ramesses was actually pronounced.

Signs were frequently written redundantly. A word might be spelled with a three-consonant sign followed by single-consonant signs repeating two of those same consonants, a practice called phonetic complementing, which helps the reader disambiguate. A scholar expecting one sign per sound finds the extra signs baffling.

Direction varies. Text may run right to left, left to right, or in columns, and the reader determines direction by noticing which way the animate signs face, because they look toward the beginning of the line.

All of this together explains the failure of the symbolic theory. A system with this many sign types, redundant spelling, no vowels, and variable direction looks nothing like an alphabet and does look, superficially, like a code of pictures. Only comparison against a known text and a surviving descendant language could break it, and both of those arrived in the same generation.

## How we know the decipherment is right

A claim to have read a lost script is exactly the kind of claim that deserves scepticism, and the reasons for confidence here are worth setting out because they generalize to every decipherment.

The first test is internal consistency across an enormous corpus. The values Champollion established, refined by generations of scholars since, have been applied to hundreds of thousands of inscriptions, papyri, ostraca, and objects spanning three thousand years, in multiple stages of the language, without the system breaking down. A wrong decipherment fails as soon as it leaves the texts it was built on.

The second is independent bilingual confirmation. Other trilingual and bilingual decrees have been found since, most importantly the Decree of Canopus, an earlier Ptolemaic text discovered at Tanis in the 1860s in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek. Scholars could read the Egyptian panels using the established system and then check the result against the Greek, which had played no part in establishing that system. The readings matched. Additional copies of the Memphis decree itself have also been found, allowing the Rosetta Stone's damaged sections to be restored from parallel texts.

The third is the fit with Coptic. Words read from hieroglyphic texts turn out to correspond to Coptic vocabulary with regular sound correspondences, which is what you would expect if the two are stages of one language and is wildly improbable otherwise.

The fourth is predictive success. Readings of Egyptian texts have generated historical claims that were subsequently confirmed by independent evidence: king lists that match archaeological sequences, references to foreign rulers corroborated by Mesopotamian and Levantine records, accounts of building projects matched to the buildings.

A decipherment is never proved in the mathematical sense. This one is about as well confirmed as an empirical claim gets, which is why the occasional assertion that hieroglyphs remain unread belongs with the other things people assert about ancient Egypt without evidence.

## What became readable

The point of a decipherment is what you can read afterward, and the scale of what opened up in the decades following 1822 is difficult to overstate.

Egypt is one of the best documented ancient civilizations precisely because its climate preserves organic material and its people wrote constantly. Once the script could be read, the surviving corpus turned out to include king lists and annals that allowed a chronology to be built; temple inscriptions recording building programmes, endowments, and campaigns; tomb biographies in which officials described their own careers; religious texts including the funerary compositions that had been copied for millennia; and medical papyri setting out diagnoses, prognoses, and treatments in a recognizably clinical format.

It also included the ordinary. Letters between family members. Tax receipts. Court records. School exercises with the teacher's corrections. Work rosters from the village that housed the men who cut the royal tombs, recording who was absent and why, with reasons that include illness, a festival, and brewing beer. Complaints about late wages that escalated into what is usually described as the first recorded strike. Love poetry. Lists of laundry.

That last category is what changed the subject most fundamentally. Before decipherment, Egypt was monuments and mystery, and the people who built them were inaccessible. Afterward, Egyptian history could be written from the inside, by people who had names, jobs, disputes, and opinions.

Demotic and the later stages of the language opened a comparable body of legal and commercial material, and the study of papyri from Egypt, the subject of the article on [what papyri reveal about Roman Egypt](/2008/12/01/papyri-of-roman-egypt/), became a field in its own right.

The chronological effect was equally important. Classical authors had given Europe a garbled and partial king list through the summaries of Manetho, an Egyptian priest writing in Greek. Once inscriptions could be read, that framework could be tested against the Egyptians' own records, and the dynastic structure that organizes every account of Egyptian history, including this series, became a matter of evidence rather than of tradition.

## Champollion afterward

The man who did it had ten years left, and he used them.

He published the fuller exposition of the system in 1824, secured a position with the Egyptian collections in the French royal museums, and spent time in Italy studying the Egyptian material in Turin, which held one of the best collections in Europe including a fragmentary king list on papyrus that remains a central document of Egyptian chronology.

In 1828 he finally went to Egypt, at the head of a joint French and Tuscan expedition, and spent more than a year travelling from the Delta to the Second Cataract. This was the first survey of Egyptian monuments conducted by someone who could read the inscriptions, which changed what a survey could do: he could identify the kings who built the structures, date them, understand the scenes, and correct the attributions the earlier French commission had guessed at.

He also recorded, with visible distress, the destruction going on around him. Monuments were being quarried, reliefs cut out for sale, and sites stripped to supply the European collecting market described in the article on Napoleon's scholars. He petitioned the Egyptian authorities for protective measures, and the eventual establishment of an antiquities service in Egypt owes something to arguments he made.

He returned in 1830, was elected to the Academy of Inscriptions, was given a chair created for him, and died in March 1832 at forty one, worn out by overwork and by the after-effects of the expedition. His Egyptian grammar and dictionary were published posthumously by his brother.

The field he founded expanded rapidly afterward, with German, Italian, British, and French scholars building on the grammar, and the study of the Egyptian language became a technical discipline within a generation.

## The chronological consequence

One effect of the decipherment reached well beyond Egypt and is rarely credited to it, which is that it gave the ancient Near East a clock.

Before Egyptian texts could be read, the chronology of the whole region rested on classical authors, on biblical narrative, and on guesswork. Nothing could be fixed to a calendar year, and the sequence of Mesopotamian, Levantine, Anatolian, and Aegean cultures had to be assembled from stylistic comparison and fragmentary tradition.

Egyptian records changed that because the Egyptians kept unusually good ones. King lists give sequences of rulers with reign lengths. Inscriptions date events to a specific regnal year of a named king. Astronomical observations recorded in dated documents, particularly references to the heliacal rising of the star Sothis, allow certain reigns to be pinned to absolute dates within a range, which converts a relative sequence into a calendar.

Once Egyptian chronology was anchored, everything in contact with Egypt could be dated by association. A Mycenaean pot in an Egyptian tomb dates the pot. A letter from a Babylonian king in an Egyptian archive dates the king. A Levantine city destroyed in a campaign recorded on an Egyptian temple wall acquires a date for its destruction layer. Egyptian chronology became the backbone against which the second millennium BCE across the eastern Mediterranean and Near East is measured.

That framework is not free of dispute. Egyptologists argue about the high, middle, and low chronologies, the interpretation of the astronomical data is contested, and the margins of error widen considerably the further back one goes. Those arguments are technical and ongoing, and they concern the size of the uncertainty rather than the existence of the framework.

The point for this article is the leverage. A decipherment does not only open the texts it decodes. It dates everything those texts touch, which in Egypt's case meant a large proportion of the documented ancient world. That is a second-order consequence that nobody in 1822 was expecting and it may be the largest one.

## What the stone did not do

Correcting the overstatements is as useful as making the case, and there are four worth handling directly.

It did not contain the decipherment. A bilingual text is a constraint, not an answer. Plenty of bilinguals exist for scripts that remain unread, and plenty of scripts have been read without one. What the stone provided was a fixed point against which hypotheses could be tested, and testing them took twenty three years of work by several people in three countries.

It was not sufficient on its own. The decisive proof came from Abu Simbel cartouches, and the alphabet was built partly from a Philae obelisk. The stone's own hieroglyphic panel is small and damaged, and if it had been the only material available the decipherment would have stalled where Young stalled.

It did not make Egyptian instantly readable. The 1822 letter established sound values for royal names. A working grammar took years more, the understanding of verb forms developed across the nineteenth century, and Egyptian philology continued to be revised substantially into the twentieth. Reading a text and understanding its syntax are different achievements separated by decades.

And it did not reveal any secret. The stone's content is a tax and honours decree. The romantic expectation that a key to Egypt would unlock hidden wisdom was disappointed at the first translation of the Greek, in 1800, and the disappointment is instructive: what the object gave was method, not revelation.

## How this compares with other decipherments

Placing the Egyptian case beside its peers shows what was distinctive about it and what was ordinary.

Cuneiform was cracked over a similar period by a broadly similar route. A trilingual inscription cut into a cliff at Behistun in Persia, recording the same proclamation in Old Persian, Elamite, and Akkadian, served the function the Rosetta Stone served for Egypt, with Old Persian as the accessible entry point because it was alphabetic and related to known languages. The comparison is close enough that the two decipherments are frequently taught together.

Linear B, the script of Mycenaean Greece, was read in the 1950s with no bilingual at all, by statistical analysis of sign frequencies and patterns combined with a hypothesis about the underlying language that proved correct. That case demonstrates decipherment is possible without a key when the language turns out to be a known one.

The Maya script was read over the twentieth century through a long struggle against exactly the obstacle that held up Egyptian: a confident scholarly consensus that the signs were symbolic rather than phonetic, which had to be overturned before progress could be made. The parallel with Kircher and the symbolic theory is exact, and it recurred a century and a half later.

Linear A, the Indus Valley script, and several others remain unread, in some cases because no bilingual exists and the underlying language is unknown, which is the combination that defeats every method.

What these comparisons establish is that the Rosetta Stone was neither magic nor incidental. It belongs to a recognizable class of aid, the bilingual anchor, which makes a decipherment tractable without making it automatic, and Egyptian is the case where that anchor worked best because a descendant of the language had survived alongside it.

## The ownership question

The stone is among the most prominent objects in the international argument over cultural property, and this section sets out both cases at their strongest rather than resolving them by assertion.

A short history of the claim helps. Egypt's modern state took increasing interest in its antiquities across the twentieth century, established institutions to protect and study them, and progressively restricted export. Formal and informal requests concerning the Rosetta Stone have been raised on a number of occasions since the beginning of the present century, sometimes as requests for permanent return and sometimes as requests for a temporary loan to coincide with the opening of major Egyptian museum facilities. Egyptian archaeologists have also organized petitions pressing the case. The institution holding the object has consistently declined permanent transfer while remaining open in principle to discussion of loans.

Because this is a live dispute involving real institutions and governments, the treatment below gives each side's argument in the form its proponents make it, and the closing assessment separates what is legally settled from what is genuinely contested.

## The case for return

Those arguing that the stone belongs in Egypt make several distinct claims, and they should not be run together because they have different strengths.

The acquisition argument holds that the transfer in 1801 was a transaction between two invading European armies over an object taken from a country neither of them had any right to be in. No Egyptian representative was party to it. The consent that makes the transfer lawful, the Ottoman sultan's, was the consent of a distant imperial sovereign whose practical authority over Egypt had been negligible for a century, exercised at a moment when his territory had just been occupied by one European power and recovered for him by another. Treating that as valid consent, the argument runs, applies a formal legal test to circumstances the test was never designed for.

The context argument holds that objects belong with the culture that produced them and near the places they came from, where they can be understood in relation to other material from the same society. A decree from a Memphis priestly assembly means something different standing among Egyptian material in Egypt than it does standing in a general museum of world cultures.

The significance argument is specific to this object. The stone is not one item among many. It has become the emblem of Egyptian civilization's recovery, reproduced on everything from textbooks to souvenirs, and its absence from Egypt is read by many Egyptians as a standing symbol of a period when the country's heritage was disposed of by others. Symbolic objects are precisely the ones where retention causes the most damage to relations and where return would do the most good.

The access argument holds that Egypt now has the institutions, conservation capability, and display facilities to care for and present the object to international standards, which removes the practical objection that held for much of the twentieth century.

And the precedent argument holds that voluntary returns by other institutions in comparable cases have not produced the wholesale emptying of museums that was predicted, which suggests the floodgates concern is overstated.

## The case for keeping it where it is

Those defending the current arrangement also make several distinct arguments, and the strongest of them are not the ones usually quoted.

The legal argument is the narrowest and the firmest. The transfer took place under a treaty to which the recognized sovereign power was a party, it has been undisturbed for more than two centuries, and the governing national legislation for the institution that holds it restricts the disposal of objects from the collection, so a permanent transfer would require an act of the legislature rather than a decision by the museum. Whatever one thinks of the ethics, the institution is not free to hand it over on its own authority.

The universal museum argument holds that collections which display the achievements of many cultures side by side serve a distinct purpose that national collections cannot, allowing visitors to see Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Greek, and other material in relation to each other, and that dismantling such collections into their national components would be a loss to the public understanding of a shared human past.

The access argument runs the other way here. The object sits in a free-entry museum in a major transport hub visited by very large numbers of people from around the world, including many who would never travel to Egypt, and removing it would reduce rather than increase the number of people who encounter it.

The precedent argument likewise inverts. If the origin of an object two centuries ago determines where it must be held, the principle applies to an enormous proportion of the world's museum holdings, including Egyptian material in collections across Europe and North America and material from many countries held in Egypt itself.

And the practical argument notes that the stone's scholarly value was exhausted long ago: the text is fully published, casts and high-resolution reproductions exist everywhere, and no research question turns on its physical location.

### Why does the museum say it cannot simply return it?

Because the governing legislation restricts the institution from disposing of objects in its collection except in narrow circumstances, so a permanent transfer would require parliamentary action rather than a museum decision. That is a statement about legal capacity, not about the merits, and critics respond that legislation can be changed.

## Separating what is settled from what is contested

The dispute becomes much easier to think about once three different questions are pulled apart, because people arguing past each other are usually arguing about different ones.

The first question is legal title. This one is largely settled in the sense that matters practically. A treaty was concluded, the sovereign power of the day was a party, possession has been continuous and open for over two centuries, and no court is going to disturb that. Arguments that the acquisition was unlawful by the standards of its own time are weak; it was entirely ordinary practice, which is part of the problem rather than a defence.

The second question is ethical entitlement, and this one is genuinely open. Legality and rightness are not the same, and a great deal of what was lawful in 1801 is not defensible now, including most of what the two armies were doing in Egypt in the first place. Whether an object acquired under a settlement in which the people of the country had no voice should be held permanently on the strength of that settlement is a real moral question, and the answer does not follow from the legal one.

The third question is what would actually be best, which is separate again and depends on judgments about public access, scholarship, institutional relations, and symbolism that reasonable people weigh differently.

Treating the legal answer as settling the ethical question is the characteristic error of the retention case at its weakest. Treating the ethical question as settling the practical and legal ones is the characteristic error of the return case at its weakest. The serious versions of both positions acknowledge the distinction.

## The loan question

Between permanent transfer and permanent retention sits an option that has been raised repeatedly and never resolved, and it is worth examining because it exposes what each side actually wants.

A long-term loan would place the object in Egypt for an extended period without transferring title. It requires no legislation, it has precedents in international museum practice, and it would put the stone in front of Egyptian audiences and visitors to Egypt.

The objection from the return side is that a loan concedes the principle. Accepting the object on loan is an acknowledgment that it belongs to somebody else, and a claimant who has argued that an acquisition was improper cannot easily sign a document acknowledging the acquirer's title. Some Egyptian officials have framed requests as loans and others have rejected that framing for exactly this reason.

The objection from the retention side is symmetrical: a loan to a claimant who has publicly stated an intention to seek permanent return carries an obvious risk, and institutions are cautious about lending into a situation where recovery might become contentious.

So the middle path is blocked from both ends by the same feature, which is that the argument is about ownership rather than about location. If it were only about where the object should sit, a loan would have settled it long ago.

### What would a loan actually settle?

Where the object is displayed, and almost nothing else. It would not resolve title, which is what both sides are actually contesting, and accepting a loan can be read as conceding the other party's ownership. That is why an arrangement that looks like an obvious compromise has repeatedly failed to happen.

## The words painted on the sides

The English text applied to the stone's flanks in the early nineteenth century has acquired a significance nobody intended when it was painted.

It records that the object was captured in Egypt by the British army and presented by the king. At the time this was unremarkable museum practice, a provenance note written on the object because that was where a label would not get lost.

It now functions as the most concise possible statement of the case against retention. A monument that carries, in paint, a declaration that it was taken as a prize of war is an awkward exhibit for an institution arguing that its holding is uncontroversial. Visitors notice it, and the words do more rhetorical work for the return case than any argument.

There is a defence of leaving them, and it is a reasonable one. Removing the paint would erase evidence of how the object came to be where it is, which is itself historically significant and which a museum has an obligation to preserve rather than tidy away. An institution that scrubbed the inscription would be accused, correctly, of concealing its own history.

So the words stay, doing double duty as a record of nineteenth century practice and as an argument against the practice they record.

## The other copies

The decree ordered that the text be set up in temples throughout the kingdom, and that instruction was carried out, which has consequences for both scholarship and the ownership argument.

Further copies of the Memphis decree have been identified, some more complete than the Rosetta fragment and some preserving sections the Rosetta Stone has lost. Fragments and related stelae have turned up at Delta sites and in Upper Egypt, and comparison between copies allows the damaged hieroglyphic panel to be restored with confidence, because a scholar can see what the missing lines said on a parallel monument.

Related Ptolemaic decrees in the same trilingual format exist as well. The Decree of Canopus of 238 BCE, found at Tanis in the 1860s, is the most important, and its function as an independent check on the decipherment has already been described. Others, including a decree from the reign of Ptolemy IV, extend the corpus further.

Two conclusions follow. Scholarly ones first: the Rosetta Stone is not a unique document. Its text is known from multiple witnesses, its language is representative of a well attested genre of priestly decree, and nothing in it is irreplaceable as information.

The second conclusion bears on the dispute. If the argument for the stone's location rested on scholarly necessity, the existence of parallel copies would have settled it. It does not rest on that, and neither side claims it does. The argument is about a symbol, and symbols are not fungible with parallel copies.

## What the decree tells us about its own moment

Read as a historical source rather than as a key, the text is genuinely informative about the Ptolemaic state, and it repays attention.

It shows a government negotiating with its priesthood. The king grants tax remissions, cancels arrears, funds temple endowments, and confirms priestly privileges; the priests respond with cult honours that integrate him into Egyptian religious practice. This is the machinery of legitimacy in a state ruled by a foreign dynasty that needs the temples to make it Egyptian.

It shows a state under strain. Remitting arrears and releasing prisoners are the acts of a regime buying peace after disorder, and the decree belongs to the period of a prolonged native revolt in the south that the dynasty had struggled to suppress.

It shows the linguistic reality of the country. The order to publish in three forms is an admission that the government could not reach its own subjects in a single script.

And it shows the continuity of Egyptian institutional practice under Greek rule. The priestly assembly, the temple publication, the formulae of royal honours, and the monumental stele are all Egyptian forms, operating three centuries after the last native pharaoh, with a Macedonian king slotted into a pharaonic frame.

## The object's condition

A few words on the physical state of the stone, because its appearance has changed within living memory of the institution that holds it.

The surface was treated at various points with substances intended to make the inscription legible, including a white infill in the incised characters and a protective coating, which left the object with a dark, almost black appearance familiar from older photographs and reproductions. Cleaning work in the later twentieth century removed accumulated coatings and revealed the actual stone, a dark grey granodiorite with a pink vein and a lighter speckle.

This matters for two practical reasons. First, older reproductions and descriptions of the object as black basalt derive from the coated state and from the misidentification that went with it. Second, the removal of the white infill made the inscription harder to photograph and read in ordinary light, which is why high-quality published facsimiles and casts remain the working tools for anyone studying the text rather than the object itself.

Casts were made and distributed widely in the nineteenth century, and several institutions hold them. Anyone wanting to examine the inscription at first hand has options that do not involve the original.

## What the Greek panel revealed in 1800

A detail that gets skipped is that the content of the Rosetta Stone was known almost immediately, and the reaction to it tells you a great deal about European expectations.

The Greek text was translated within months of the copies reaching Europe. Translating Ptolemaic chancery Greek is not difficult for a classically trained scholar, and the result was published early.

What it said was a disappointment to anyone expecting revelation. No cosmology, no hidden wisdom, no lost history. A committee of priests voting statues and festivals to a teenage king in exchange for tax relief, with an administrative instruction about where copies should be posted.

That disappointment had a useful effect on the scholarship. It removed any prospect of a shortcut and focused attention where it belonged, on the structure of the writing rather than on the content. A text whose meaning was already fully known could only be interesting as a specimen of how Egyptian encoded meaning, which is precisely the right way to approach a decipherment.

It also set an expectation that the field has had to defend ever since. Popular interest in Egypt has always run toward mystery, and the founding document of Egyptology turned out to be a piece of tax administration. Most of what has been read since has been similarly ordinary, and the ordinariness is the value: a civilization is better understood through its receipts than through its legends.

## Demotic afterward

The middle panel, which did most of the early work, has had a quieter history than its importance deserves.

Demotic was the entry point for Akerblad and Young because it was the best preserved section and because its cursive forms suggested to some observers that it might be alphabetic. It turned out to be as mixed a system as the hieroglyphic script it descended from, and it required its own grammar, dictionary, and specialist training.

The material written in Demotic is enormous and largely administrative and legal: contracts, leases, loans, marriage settlements, tax records, and court documents from the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, alongside literary and religious texts. It is the documentary backbone of late Egyptian social and economic history, and it is written in a script so cursive and so variable that reading it remains a specialist skill acquired by a relatively small number of scholars in each generation.

The imbalance in attention is striking. Hieroglyphic writing is famous and carries mostly formal, monumental, and funerary content. Demotic is obscure and carries the records of how people actually lived, farmed, borrowed, married, and litigated. A student interested in Egyptian society rather than Egyptian kingship gets more from the middle panel's descendants than from the top one's.

## Where the object has been

The stone's own travels since 1802 are brief and worth recording, since the ownership argument is partly about continuity of possession.

It arrived in England in early 1802, was exhibited to the learned societies, and entered the British Museum's collection that year. It has been on public display there almost continuously since, which is over two centuries of open possession and forms part of the legal argument for retention.

The exceptions are wartime. During the First World War it was moved for safekeeping, along with other important objects, into an underground railway tunnel well below street level, and it spent roughly two years there. A comparable evacuation was carried out in the Second World War. Both removals were precautionary and both were reversed.

It has travelled abroad once, briefly, for an exhibition in Paris in the early 1970s marking the anniversary of Champollion's letter, which is the only occasion in its modern history when it has left the country holding it.

That single loan is frequently cited in the dispute, by the return side as evidence that lending is possible and by the retention side as evidence that lending is exceptional and carefully bounded. Both readings are fair.

## The rest of the 1801 transfer

The Rosetta Stone was not the only object that changed hands under the capitulation, and the others are worth a paragraph because they show the scale of what was involved.

The antiquities surrendered included a large sarcophagus, colossal statuary, architectural fragments, and a set of objects the French commission had assembled in Alexandria for shipment home. The bulk of them went into the same collection.

Two things follow. The first is that the stone was one item in a consignment, and its later fame is a product of the decipherment rather than of anything that happened in 1801. Nobody prioritized it at the time except the scholars who understood the inscription.

The second is that arguments about the stone apply with equal force to the rest of that consignment, and to the far larger quantity of Egyptian material that left the country in the following decades through excavation permits, purchase, and outright plunder. Focusing the ownership argument on one famous object makes it tractable as a public controversy and misrepresents its actual scope.

## The priority dispute, assessed

The question of who deserves credit has been argued for two centuries and mostly along national lines, so it is worth setting out what the evidence supports.

Young's claims are real. He established the derivation of Demotic from hieroglyphic. He identified the Ptolemy cartouche and assigned approximate values to several of its signs. He compiled a Demotic vocabulary from comparison with the Greek, much of it correct. He published this work, and Champollion read it.

Champollion's claims are also real, and they are of a different order. He established the phonetic principle for native Egyptian names, which Young explicitly denied. He built the alphabet systematically through falsifiable cross-checks across many cartouches. He identified the mixed nature of the system, with phonetic signs, word signs, and determinatives operating together. And he produced the grammar and dictionary that made the language readable rather than the names.

Champollion's conduct toward Young was less than generous. He acknowledged Young's contribution inconsistently and at times minimally, in a context where he was defending a fragile position against hostile critics and where national rivalry ran hot. Young's supporters had a legitimate complaint about attribution and converted it into a claim about the decipherment itself that the evidence does not support.

The proportionate judgment is that Young made important preliminary advances and held a theory that prevented him from going further, and that Champollion made the conceptual breakthrough, did the systematic work, and founded the field. Credit for a decipherment belongs to whoever can read the texts afterward, and after 1824 only one of them could.

There is a broader lesson here about how discoveries are made. Young had the better general reputation, the wider range, and the more prestigious institutional position. Champollion had one specialized asset, deep knowledge of Coptic, acquired over fifteen years in pursuit of exactly this problem. Specialized obsession beat general brilliance, which is the usual pattern in decipherments and is not the pattern the popular history of science tends to celebrate.

## The word as metaphor

The object's name has become a common noun in several languages, and the drift of the metaphor is worth noticing because it distorts the history.

To call something a rosetta stone is to say it is the key that makes an obscure body of material intelligible, and the phrase gets applied to everything from a decisive experiment to a useful reference document. That usage carries the loose version of the story with it: the idea that a key exists, that finding it is the hard part, and that comprehension follows automatically.

The actual history teaches the opposite. The key was found in 1799 and the lock opened in 1822, and the intervening twenty three years were the work. The object made the problem tractable and nothing more, and what closed the gap was a scholar's knowledge of a liturgical language preserved by Egyptian Christians, applied to a cartouche from a Nubian temple.

The metaphor is harmless in casual use and misleading in teaching, and a student who absorbs it will consistently underestimate how much labour any decipherment takes.

## What students get wrong

Since this article exists partly to serve people studying the subject, here are the errors that recur most reliably.

That the stone carries three languages. It carries three scripts and two languages, and confusing these makes the decipherment logic impossible to explain.

That Champollion translated the stone. He did not need to; the Greek had been translated in 1800 and the content was never in doubt. What he did was recover the writing system.

That the decipherment happened in a moment. The famous collapse in his brother's office is a real anecdote about a real breakthrough, and the breakthrough sat in the middle of two decades of work by several people.

That hieroglyphs are picture-writing. This is the error the entire field had to defeat, and it survives robustly in popular culture. The script is predominantly phonetic and records a language with grammar.

That the stone was deciphered. Scripts are deciphered; objects are read. The phrasing sounds pedantic and it encodes the conceptual mistake.

And that the stone is uniquely valuable as text. Parallel copies of the same decree exist. Its value is historical and symbolic, not informational.

## How Egyptology reckons with its origins

A field founded by a colonial expedition and built on an object taken as a prize of war has had to think about where it came from, and the thinking has gone through distinct phases.

For most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the origin was told as heroic prologue. European scholars had rescued a lost civilization from oblivion, and the countries that funded the excavations reasonably expected a share of what came out of the ground. The division of finds between excavator and host country was formalized and operated for decades, which is how a great deal of Egyptian material entered European and North American collections legally and with Egyptian government agreement.

The shift came from several directions at once. Egyptian scholars and officials pressed for control of the country's antiquities and increasingly staffed the institutions that managed them. The division of finds was ended. Broader scholarship on how European knowledge of the East had been produced forced the field to examine its own framing assumptions, including the habit of treating ancient Egypt as the property of universal civilization rather than of Egypt.

The field's current position is uneven and moving. Excavation in Egypt operates under Egyptian permit and Egyptian direction, publication increasingly involves Egyptian scholars as principals rather than as local support, and the question of what to do about objects acquired in earlier eras is live in every major collection.

Where the discomfort remains sharpest is in the relationship between the knowledge and its conditions of production. The decipherment is a genuine intellectual achievement and its results belong to everybody, including and especially Egyptians who can now read their own ancient records. The object that enabled it sits in another country under an arrangement made by armies. Neither of those facts cancels the other, and a mature field holds both rather than choosing the comfortable one.

## Reading Egypt in Egypt

One consequence of the decipherment that deserves more attention than it gets is what it eventually meant inside Egypt.

For the first half of the nineteenth century, the ability to read Egyptian belonged almost entirely to European scholars, and Egyptian engagement with the pharaonic past was mediated through them. That began to change as Egypt built its own educational and antiquities institutions, a process connected to the wider state-building programme described in the article on [Muhammad Ali and the birth of modern Egypt](/2014/03/01/muhammad-ali-and-modern-egypt/) and extended by his successors.

Egyptian scholars entered the field, learned the language, and eventually led excavations and directed institutions. Ahmed Kamal, working in the later nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, is the usual starting point: an Egyptian Egyptologist who taught the subject, published in Arabic, and campaigned for Egyptian training in a field that resisted it. Selim Hassan's excavations at Giza in the twentieth century established an Egyptian presence at the highest level of the discipline.

The political dimension followed. A country whose ancient past had been read and interpreted by foreigners acquired, through the same decipherment, the means to read it itself, and the pharaonic past became a resource in the formation of modern Egyptian national identity. That use of antiquity was contested inside Egypt too, between those who located Egyptian identity in the pharaonic inheritance and those who located it in the Arab and Islamic one.

The relevant point for this article is the symmetry. The script was made readable by a European working from copies of an object taken from Egypt. The readability, once achieved, was not confined to Europeans, and the recovery of Egyptian voices that the decipherment enabled has served Egyptian purposes as much as anyone else's.

## Why the object still draws crowds

Something should be said about the stone's peculiar status as an exhibit, because it is genuinely odd.

Visually it is unremarkable. A broken grey slab covered in small dense text, with no imagery, no colour, no gold, and no human figure. In a gallery containing colossal statuary, painted coffins, and mummies, it is the least immediately impressive thing on display, and it is routinely the most crowded.

The reason is that people come to see the idea rather than the object. The stone is understood as the moment a civilization stopped being silent, and standing in front of it is standing in front of that event. That is a legitimate reason to visit a museum and it has nothing to do with the aesthetic qualities of the thing.

It also explains why the ownership argument has the temperature it does. A dispute over an object that carries this much meaning is not really a dispute over a slab of granodiorite, and any resolution that treats it as one will fail to satisfy anybody.

## The verdict

The key-that-opened-a-civilization claim holds, in a form that needs three qualifications to be accurate and is still the largest claim that can be made for any single object recovered from the ground.

It holds because of what became possible. Before the decipherment, everything Europe and the wider world knew about ancient Egypt came from outside: Greek and Roman authors writing late and at second hand, a tradition of mystical speculation that had been confidently wrong for a millennium, and monuments that could be measured but not read. After it, the Egyptians could be heard directly across three thousand years, in king lists, letters, contracts, prayers, medical texts, court records, poetry, and complaints about late wages. No other object is responsible for a comparable expansion in the amount of the human past that can be heard rather than inferred.

The first qualification is that the stone did not contain the answer. It provided a fixed point, a known meaning against which hypotheses about an unknown script could be tested. That is what made the problem tractable and it is not the same as making it easy. Twenty three years, several scholars, three countries, and a bitter priority dispute separate the discovery from the reading.

The second is that it was not sufficient. The decisive proof came from cartouches at Abu Simbel, the alphabet was extended using an obelisk from Philae, and the whole enterprise rested on Coptic, a language preserved in Egyptian church liturgy by the descendants of the people who had written the original inscriptions. If credit is being distributed, the Egyptian Christians who kept their liturgical language alive for fourteen centuries have a claim nobody presses on their behalf.

The third is that its value now is symbolic rather than informational. Parallel copies of the same decree exist, the text is fully published, and casts and facsimiles are everywhere. Nothing turns on where the physical object is except meaning, and meaning is exactly what is being fought over.

On the ownership dispute this article offers a structural conclusion rather than a preferred outcome, because the question is contested and live. Legal title is settled and ethical entitlement is not, and the two are separate questions that both sides routinely collapse into each other. The legal answer rests on a treaty concluded between European armies and a distant imperial sovereign at a moment when Egyptians were party to nothing, which is a real answer to the legal question and is obviously unsatisfying as an answer to the moral one. The middle path of a long loan fails because both sides understand that the argument is about title rather than about location. Readers will reach their own view, and the useful thing this article can do is make sure the view is reached with the arguments separated rather than blurred.

What is not in dispute is the intellectual event. A twelve year old in provincial France decided he would read Egyptian, spent fifteen years learning a liturgical language almost nobody else thought relevant, and used it to recognize a sun disc in a Nubian cartouche. That is how a civilization stopped being silent, and it is a better story than the one about the magic slab.

## Using this for study and revision

The subject rewards being held as a chain of reasoning rather than as a list of facts, and five anchors carry it.

Fix the object: a granodiorite stele, found in 1799 during fortification work at Rosetta, carrying a priestly decree of 196 BCE from Memphis honouring Ptolemy V, in three scripts and two languages. Fix the problem: hieroglyphs had been unreadable since the fourth century CE, and European scholarship believed the signs were symbolic rather than phonetic. Fix the method: Greek to Demotic first, proper names matched by position, then cartouches cross-checked between Ptolemy and Cleopatra to yield sound values. Fix the proof: Abu Simbel cartouches read as Ramesses and Thutmose using Coptic, showing native names were phonetic. Fix the aftermath: the 1822 letter, the 1824 system, the grammar published after Champollion's death in 1832, and the confirmation of the whole structure by the later discovery of the Canopus decree.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is written on the Rosetta Stone?

A decree issued by an assembly of Egyptian priests meeting at Memphis in 196 BCE, on the first anniversary of the coronation of Ptolemy V Epiphanes. Its content is an exchange rather than a revelation. The priests record that the king has remitted taxes and arrears, made grants to the temples, released prisoners, and provided for the sacred animals; in return they vote him honours, including cult statues in every temple, festivals on his birthday and accession day, a special title, and the placing of his image in the shrines. The final clause orders that the decree be inscribed on hard stone in the sacred writing, the native writing, and the Greek writing, and set up in temples across the kingdom, which is why the object carries three panels. The Greek was translated within months of the find, so the content was never in doubt.

### Q: How was the Rosetta Stone discovered?

French soldiers uncovered it in July 1799 during engineering work on an old Ottoman fort at el-Rashid, the town Europeans called Rosetta, on the western branch of the Nile in the Delta. The occupation had already lost its fleet and was strengthening its defences, and demolition of this kind routinely turned up ancient stone because Egyptian monuments had been quarried for building material for centuries. An officer of engineers, Pierre-Francois Bouchard, is credited with recognizing that this block mattered, and the recognition rather than the digging is the achievement: a slab carrying three scripts including Greek was obviously significant to anyone with a classical education. It was sent to Cairo and given to the scientific commission, whose first act was to make ink impressions and casts and send copies to Europe.

### Q: How did the Rosetta Stone unlock ancient writing?

By supplying a substantial Egyptian text whose meaning was already known. Every earlier attempt on hieroglyphs was interpretation with nothing to check against, which is why a thousand years of confident guessing produced nothing. The stone converted the problem into comparison: scholars could locate where a Greek word must fall in the Egyptian panels and test a proposed sign value against that position. Work went from Greek into Demotic first, because the Demotic panel was better preserved, and only then across into the damaged hieroglyphic section. Proper names were the entry point, since a name must be spelled out phonetically in any script. The stone did not contain the solution and it made the problem tractable, which is a different and more useful thing.

### Q: Who cracked the code of the Rosetta Stone?

Jean-Francois Champollion, who announced his phonetic alphabet in a letter read to the Academy of Inscriptions in Paris in September 1822 and published the full system of the script in 1824. Important preliminary work was done by others. Johan David Akerblad produced a largely correct Demotic alphabet for proper names in 1802. Thomas Young established that Demotic derived from hieroglyphic, identified the Ptolemy cartouche, and assigned approximate values to several of its signs, but held that phonetic writing was a device reserved for foreign names, which is where he stopped. Champollion's decisive step was proving that native Egyptian names were also written phonetically, and he then built the grammar and dictionary that made the language readable rather than just the names.

### Q: What three scripts are on the Rosetta Stone?

Hieroglyphic at the top, Demotic in the middle, and Greek at the bottom. The common error is to call these three languages: they are three scripts and two languages, since both hieroglyphic and Demotic record Egyptian. Hieroglyphic is the formal monumental script of temple and tomb, and its panel is the smallest and most damaged, with fourteen incomplete lines. Demotic is a fast cursive script that had become the everyday writing of contracts, letters, and administration, and its thirty two lines are the best preserved section, which is why it was the entry point for the early work. The Greek panel runs to fifty four lines and is complete apart from the lower right corner. That structure gives two routes at once: a bilingual comparison between languages, and a comparison between two scripts recording the same language.

### Q: Where is the Rosetta Stone kept?

In the British Museum in London, where it has been on public display almost continuously since 1802, apart from precautionary removals during both world wars and a single brief loan to Paris in the early 1970s for an exhibition marking the anniversary of Champollion's letter. It came into British hands under the capitulation of Alexandria on 2 September 1801, when the French surrendered and the terms provided for the antiquities assembled during the occupation to pass to Britain. Painted inscriptions applied to the sides of the object in English record that it was captured in Egypt by the British army and presented by King George III. Those words are a nineteenth century museum label written directly on an ancient monument, and they have since become the most quoted evidence in the argument about where it should be.

### Q: Why is the Rosetta Stone so important?

Because of what became readable afterward, not because of what it says. Hieroglyphic writing had been unreadable since the late fourth century CE, a gap of roughly fourteen hundred years during which the most heavily documented ancient civilization on earth stood covered in text that nobody could read. Everything Europe believed about Egyptian religion and history came from Greek and Roman authors writing late and from outside, plus a mystical tradition that was confidently wrong. Once the script could be read, Egypt could speak for itself: king lists, temple records, tomb biographies, medical papyri, letters, contracts, court cases, school exercises, work rosters, love poetry, and complaints about late wages. No other single object is responsible for a comparable expansion in how much of the human past can be heard rather than inferred.

### Q: Should the Rosetta Stone be returned to Egypt?

This is genuinely contested, and the useful move is to separate three questions people routinely blur. Legal title is largely settled: a treaty was concluded, the Ottoman sovereign was a party, possession has been open for over two centuries, and the museum's governing legislation restricts disposal, so a transfer would need parliamentary action. Ethical entitlement is open: the 1801 settlement was made between two European armies over an object taken from a country whose people were party to nothing, and legality in 1801 is a weak moral argument. The practical question of what would be best depends on judgments about access, symbolism, and institutional relations that reasonable people weigh differently. A long-term loan looks like the obvious compromise and has repeatedly failed because both sides understand the dispute is about title rather than location.

### Q: Who was Jean-Francois Champollion?

A French scholar born in 1790 who fixed on Egypt as a subject in his teens and prepared for the decipherment for fifteen years before making it. He worked through Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, Aramaic, Persian, and Chinese, and, decisively, studied Coptic deeply on the conviction that it was the surviving form of the ancient Egyptian language. That conviction was his key asset: a proposed sound value could be tested by asking whether the resulting word existed in Coptic and meant what the context required. His career was unstable, disrupted by the political upheavals around the Bourbon restoration, and much of the work was done in poverty and ill health. He published the alphabet in 1822 and the full system in 1824, finally visited Egypt in 1828, and died in 1832 at forty one, with his grammar and dictionary published after his death.

### Q: What did Thomas Young contribute?

A great deal, and not the decisive step. Young was an English physician and polymath, better known for work on the wave theory of light, who took up the Egyptian material in 1814. He established that Demotic derived from the hieroglyphic script rather than being a separate invention, which connected the two Egyptian panels for the first time. He identified the cartouche on the stone as containing the name Ptolemy and worked out approximate sound values for several of its signs. He compiled a substantial Demotic vocabulary matched against the Greek, much of it correct. His limitation was conceptual: he believed phonetic writing was a special device adopted for foreign names like Ptolemy and Berenice, with native Egyptian words written symbolically, and that position let him read royal names and stopped him reading a sentence.

### Q: What is Demotic script?

A fast cursive form of Egyptian writing that developed out of earlier cursive scripts and became the everyday writing of contracts, letters, accounts, and administration from around the seventh century BCE onward. It records the Egyptian language, the same language as the hieroglyphic panel, in signs so abbreviated and run together that the connection to their hieroglyphic ancestors is invisible to an untrained eye. Like the parent script it is a mixed system rather than an alphabet, combining phonetic signs, word signs, and unpronounced determinatives, which is why Akerblad's alphabet worked for proper names and produced gibberish on running text. The material written in Demotic is enormous and largely legal and commercial, making it the documentary backbone of late Egyptian social and economic history, and reading it remains a specialist skill held by relatively few scholars in each generation.

### Q: What is a cartouche and why did it matter?

An oval ring drawn around a group of signs in Egyptian writing to mark a royal name. Its practical importance to the decipherment is enormous: it is a flag telling a reader who cannot read the script that the enclosed signs are a name, and names in any writing system must be spelled out phonetically because there is no symbol for an individual person. That made cartouches the one place in an Egyptian text where a scholar could be confident of finding sound values rather than ideas. The Rosetta Stone's hieroglyphic panel contained a cartouche that the Greek showed must be Ptolemy; an obelisk from Philae supplied a second pairing with Cleopatra. Comparing the two names, which share the letters P, T, O, and L in different positions, produced a falsifiable test that the signs passed.

### Q: How big is the stone and what is it made of?

It is granodiorite, a dark speckled igneous rock, frequently misdescribed as basalt in older accounts because coatings applied in the nineteenth century made it look almost black. Cleaning in the later twentieth century removed those layers and revealed the actual dark grey stone with a pink vein and lighter speckle. It stands roughly one hundred and twelve centimetres high, about seventy five wide, and close to twenty eight thick, weighing in the region of seven hundred and sixty kilograms. It is a broken fragment rather than a complete monument: the top is gone, including whatever scene the original carried above the text, and the lower right corner is missing as well. The complete stele would have been considerably taller, and figures given for its original height are reconstructions rather than measurements.

### Q: Why was the decree issued in 196 BCE?

To settle relations between a shaky Greek-speaking dynasty and the Egyptian priesthood after a long period of instability. Ptolemy V Epiphanes had come to the throne as a small child after both parents died, and his minority saw court murders, the loss of most of the dynasty's overseas possessions to Seleucid and Macedonian rivals, and a serious native revolt in the south that produced rival indigenous kings at Thebes for roughly two decades. He was crowned at Memphis according to Egyptian rite in 197 BCE, itself a gesture toward the priesthood and the native population, and the priestly assembly issued the decree on the anniversary of that coronation. Its tax remissions, cancelled arrears, and released prisoners are the acts of a regime buying peace, and the cult honours are what the temples gave in return.

### Q: Who was Ptolemy V?

The fifth ruler of the Macedonian dynasty that governed Egypt after Alexander's conquest, who came to the throne as a young child around 204 BCE after the deaths of both his parents and reigned until roughly 180 BCE. His minority was disastrous for the dynasty: regents and courtiers murdered each other, foreign rivals stripped away the overseas possessions in Syria and the Aegean, and a native Egyptian revolt held much of the south for years. He is remembered almost entirely because of the decree carved in his honour, which is a considerable historical irony, since the document exists to record concessions a weak government made to buy the support of its own priesthood. He is not a figure of consequence in Ptolemaic history and his name is one of the most frequently read in Egyptology.

### Q: Are there other copies of the same decree?

Yes, which follows directly from the decree's own instruction that copies be set up in temples throughout the kingdom. Further copies and fragments of the Memphis decree have been identified at Delta and Upper Egyptian sites, some preserving sections the Rosetta Stone has lost, which lets the damaged hieroglyphic panel be restored with confidence from parallel texts. Related trilingual decrees from the same dynasty exist too, most importantly the Decree of Canopus of 238 BCE found at Tanis in the 1860s. That one served as an independent check on the decipherment: scholars read its Egyptian panels using the established system, then compared the result with its Greek, which had played no part in building that system, and the readings matched.

### Q: Was Coptic important to the decipherment?

It was indispensable, and it is the most underrated element of the whole story. Coptic is the final stage of the Egyptian language, written in Greek letters with a few extra signs, and it survived as the liturgical language of the Egyptian church long after the old scripts died. That gave a scholar something no amount of comparison could otherwise supply: a way to test a proposed sound value by asking whether the resulting word exists in Egyptian and means what the context requires. Champollion had studied Coptic deeply for years on the conviction that it was ancient Egyptian in disguise, and it was Coptic that let him read a sun disc in an Abu Simbel cartouche as ra and recover the name Ramesses. Young lacked Coptic at that level, and the gap explains where he stopped.

### Q: Did anyone try to read hieroglyphs before?

Constantly, and for centuries, which is why the failure is so instructive. The obstacle was a theory rather than a lack of effort. Late classical writers, notably Horapollo, presented the signs as symbolic pictures standing for ideas rather than sounds, and that account circulated in Europe from the fifteenth century. Athanasius Kircher, a seventeenth century Jesuit and genuinely formidable linguist who did real work on Coptic, produced translations of hieroglyphic inscriptions that were entirely imaginary, rendering short royal names as elaborate mystical sentences. His errors were the logical output of the symbolic assumption applied with great learning. That assumption was so entrenched that phonetic evidence was resisted even after it appeared, and defeating it was the first thing any decipherment had to do.

### Q: How do we know the decipherment is correct?

Four independent lines of support. Internal consistency: the system has been applied to hundreds of thousands of inscriptions, papyri, and objects spanning three thousand years and multiple stages of the language without breaking down, whereas a wrong decipherment fails as soon as it leaves the texts it was built on. Independent bilinguals: later finds such as the Canopus decree let scholars read the Egyptian first and then check against a Greek text that played no part in establishing the system. Fit with Coptic: words read from hieroglyphic correspond to Coptic vocabulary with regular sound correspondences, which is wildly improbable if the readings were wrong. And predictive success: readings have generated historical claims later confirmed by archaeology and by Mesopotamian and Levantine records.

### Q: What are the white painted words on the sides?

English inscriptions applied to the flanks of the object in the early nineteenth century, recording that it was captured in Egypt by the British army in 1801 and presented by King George III. They are not part of the ancient monument. At the time this was unremarkable museum practice, a provenance note written directly on the object because that was where a label would not get lost. They now function as the most concise statement of the case against retention, since a monument carrying a painted declaration that it was taken as a prize of war is an awkward exhibit for an institution arguing its holding is uncontroversial. There is a reasonable defence for leaving them: removing the paint would erase evidence of how the object came to be where it is.
