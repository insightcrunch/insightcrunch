Ask a well-informed citizen to name the statutes that built the American immigration system, and the answers come quickly. The 1965 law repealed the national-origins quotas. The 1986 law combined employer sanctions with a legalization program. Then the trail goes cold. Almost no one names the enactment of November 1990 that raised legal immigration by roughly forty percent, restructured the preference system from top to bottom, and created the three features of the system that later drew the fiercest public argument: the diversity visa lottery, temporary protected status, and the annual cap on the principal skilled-worker visa. That gap between consequence and recognition is the subject of this guide.

![The Immigration Act of 1990 guide - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-84.webp)

The statute is the Immigration Act of 1990, Public Law 101-649, signed on November 29, 1990. It grew out of S. 358 in the 101st Congress and amended the Immigration and Nationality Act, the underlying code that governs admission to the United States. By the end of this guide, a reader should be able to explain a single compact fact: three of the most argued-about features of the American immigration system, the diversity visa lottery, temporary protected status, and the capped specialty-occupation visa, all originate in one statute, signed by a Republican president in 1990, that raised legal immigration by roughly forty percent with bipartisan support. Everything that follows is organized to make that explanation possible, because the explanation is the point.

Statutes have life stages, and the 1990 enactment belongs to the amendment stage. Founding statutes announce themselves. The 1965 Act abolished a quota regime that had ordered admission by national origin for four decades, and everyone understood that something large had happened. The 1986 Act legalized millions of unauthorized residents while penalizing employers who hired unauthorized workers, and the drama of that bargain filled newspapers for months. Amendment statutes work differently. They revise numbers, redraw categories, and rewrite definitions inside an existing code. Their language is technical, their effects unfold over fiscal years rather than news cycles, and the public conversation moves on while the machinery changes underneath it. The Immigration and Nationality Act had been amended many times since its 1952 codification, and each round of amendment layered new purposes onto old machinery. The 1990 round was the most ambitious such layering since 1965. Congress did not write a new immigration code. It took the existing code and changed its levels, its preference categories, its diversity mechanisms, its humanitarian safety valves, and its exclusion grounds all at once. The result was a statute whose individual pieces became famous while the statute itself became obscure.

That quiet arrival is precisely what makes the law worth studying closely. The provisions that later generated the loudest argument were among the least noticed at enactment. The diversity lottery began as a set of transitional programs for particular nationalities and became a permanent random-selection program almost as an afterthought of the legislative process. Temporary protected status replaced decades of ad hoc executive practice with a statutory framework, and almost no one outside the immigration bar registered the substitution. The cap on the specialty-occupation visa was a single numerical line in a long bill, and it went on to shape the national debate over skilled migration for a generation. A statute can be most consequential where it is least examined, and the 1990 Act proves the point.

This guide traces five changes the enactment made. First, it restructured the numerical system, setting a worldwide limit of 675,000 with separate allocations for family-sponsored, employment-based, and diversity admission. Second, it created five employment-based preference categories, running from extraordinary ability to immigrant investors, and it imposed a numerical cap on the principal temporary visa for specialty occupations. Third, it made the diversity visa lottery permanent, converting transitional programs for specific nationalities into an ongoing program for low-admission-rate countries. Fourth, it established temporary protected status as a statutory framework for designating countries whose nationals could not safely return. Fifth, it rewrote the exclusion grounds, striking Cold War ideological exclusions and repealing the exclusion of people on the basis of sexual orientation. Each change is examined here through the text of the law, the legislative record, and the mechanics of how the new provisions operated.

A note on method is in order before the substance begins. Every number, date, vote tally, section citation, and quotation in this guide comes from a verified fact record compiled from the enrolled text, the Congressional Record, and Congressional Research Service publications. Where the record does not support a detail, the detail is omitted rather than reconstructed. The statute deserves that discipline, because the story of the 1990 Act is a story about what the text actually did, not what later commentary assumed it did. Readers who work through the sections in order will find the argument building steadily: the amendment stage of a statute's life is where the future is quietly written, and the 1990 Act wrote more of the immigration future than any other enactment of its era.

The guide closes with a creation table that maps each new program or category to the section that created it, the problem it addressed, its numerical allocation, and its later trajectory, followed by answers to twenty frequently asked questions. The table is designed to be consulted alongside the prose, not instead of it, because the numbers only make sense inside the legislative story that produced them. What follows is that story, told one provision at a time.

## The statute on the page

Every serious account of the Immigration Act of 1990 begins with the document itself, because the document is unusually revealing about how the law was made. The enrolled text is headed "104 STAT. 4978" and carries the designation "[S. 358]," which identifies the measure as a Senate bill. Public Law 101-649 is the public-law number assigned after enactment, and the signing date recorded on the measure is November 29, 1990. The signature on the page belongs to George H. W. Bush, a Republican president, which matters for the story this guide tells: the largest expansion of legal immigration since 1965 arrived with a Republican signature and bipartisan majorities in both chambers, a combination that later commentary often forgot.

The bill's legislative identity is precise. S. 358 was sponsored by Senator Edward Kennedy, Democrat of Massachusetts, and introduced on February 7, 1989, early in the 101st Congress. Its official title reads: "A bill to amend the Immigration and Nationality Act to change the level, and preference system for admission, of immigrants to the United States, and to provide for administrative naturalization, and for other purposes." The title is worth quoting in full because it states the bill's ambition plainly. This was not a narrow technical correction. It proposed to change the level of admission and the preference system itself, the two load-bearing walls of the immigration code, and to do so by amending the Immigration and Nationality Act, the standing code that had governed admission since its reorganization in 1952 and its overhaul in 1965. The ordering of the title is instructive. The level came first, the preference system second, and everything else fell under "other purposes."

Amending the INA rather than replacing it shaped everything about how the 1990 Act worked. The new law did not write a fresh immigration code. It operated by striking, inserting, and redesignating provisions inside the existing Act, which meant that its effects had to be read against the surrounding code. A new preference category, for example, drew its meaning from the definitions, bars, and procedures already present in the INA. A rewritten exclusion ground had to be reconciled with the rest of section 212. This amendment technique is one reason the statute drew less public attention than its substance warranted. There was no single dramatic repeal to report, no quota system abolished in a stroke. There was instead a long series of surgical revisions, each intelligible mainly to readers who already knew the code being revised.

The effective-date provisions confirm the amendment character of the law. Section 161(a) of the Act provides that the amendments take effect on October 1, 1991, and apply beginning with fiscal year 1992. Some provisions took effect on enactment itself, but the core of the new system, the numerical restructuring, the preference categories, and the redesigned admission machinery, did not begin operating until the start of the 1992 fiscal year, nearly a year after the President signed the bill. The permanent diversity program waited longest, taking effect in fiscal year 1995, after a transitional program filled the intervening years. These staggered effective dates are a recurring feature of amendment statutes. Congress rewrites the code in one legislative moment and then phases the new machinery into operation over several fiscal years, which further separates the moment of enactment from the moment the public feels the effects.

Understanding the statute on the page also requires understanding what it did not do. The 1990 Act did not create a legalization program, did not rewrite employer sanctions, and did not overhaul border enforcement. Those were the preoccupations of the 1986 enactment, and the 1990 law left them largely alone. Instead it addressed the legal immigration system: how many immigrants could be admitted, through which categories, under what numerical limits, and subject to which grounds of exclusion. That focus is what makes the law the hinge of the legal immigration system that followed. Everything about the architecture of legal admission, the three-part division among family, employment, and diversity channels, the five employment-based preferences, the statutory framework for temporary protection, and the post-Cold War exclusion grounds, runs through the text signed on November 29, 1990.

## From introduction to signature: the legislative path

The Immigration Act of 1990 traveled a legislative road of nearly two years, and the milestones along that road are worth reconstructing because they explain the shape of the final text. S. 358 was introduced on February 7, 1989, a few weeks into the 101st Congress, by Senator Edward Kennedy of Massachusetts. An early introduction gave the bill a long runway, and the sponsors used it. Immigration legislation of this complexity could not be rushed through committee markup and floor debate in a single session. It required hearings, negotiations with the executive branch, coordination between the two chambers, and the slow assembly of the bipartisan coalition whose votes are recorded below. The twenty-one months between introduction and signature were not delay. They were the legislative process working at the pace such a bill demanded.

Kennedy's sponsorship placed the bill in a lineage. He had been a central figure in the 1965 amendments, and his sponsorship of the 1990 revision signaled continuity with that earlier expansion. The choice of a Senate vehicle mattered procedurally. A Senate bill with a senior sponsor carried institutional weight through the committee process, and the enrolled text's "[S. 358]" designation, printed under the "104 STAT. 4978" heading, preserves the bill's origin on the face of the public law.

Between introduction and enactment, the bill passed through the standard machinery of major legislation. Committees in each chamber considered the measure, amended it, and reported their versions. The two chambers passed different texts, as they routinely do on complex bills, and the differences were reconciled in a conference committee, whose product was the conference report: a single negotiated text embodying the final bargain. The conference report is the document that matters for understanding what Congress actually enacted, because it alone contains the provisions in the form the President signed. Everything before it, the introduced bill, the committee amendments, the separate chamber passages, belongs to the history of negotiation. The conference report belongs to the history of law.

That distinction is why this guide uses the conference-report votes rather than the earlier passage votes. On October 26, 1990, the Senate agreed to the conference report by 89 to 8, recorded as Record Vote Number 323, with 3 senators not voting. On October 27, 1990, the House agreed to the conference report by 264 to 118, recorded as Roll Number 530, with 50 members not voting. Those votes, taken in the closing days of the 101st Congress, adopted the final text. Earlier votes had approved earlier texts, and citing them as the enactment votes would misdescribe what Congress did. The discipline of using the final votes is part of the larger discipline this guide observes throughout: describe the law that was enacted, not the proposals that preceded it.

The timing of the votes, at the very end of the session, also explains a detail that might otherwise puzzle readers: the number of members not voting, 3 in the Senate and 50 in the House. Session-end legislative rushes produce absences, as members depart for their districts and the chambers process large volumes of business under compressed schedules. The not-voting figures do not indicate opposition. They indicate the calendar. The members who were present voted overwhelmingly to adopt the conference report, and the margins among those voting, 89 to 8 and 264 to 118, are the measure of the bill's support.

Presidential action followed about a month later. George H. W. Bush signed the measure on November 29, 1990, and it became Public Law 101-649. The month between the conference votes and the signature was unremarkable as a matter of legislative practice. Enrolled bills require preparation, executive-branch review, and scheduling for the signing ceremony, and a major bill enacted at session's end routinely waits weeks for the President's signature. What matters is not the interval but the fact: a Republican president signed the largest expansion of legal immigration since 1965, completing a legislative path that had begun with a Democratic senator's bill twenty-one months earlier and passed through every stage of the congressional process with bipartisan support at each step.

### Why do the conference-report votes matter more than the passage votes?

Because only the conference report contained the final negotiated text that became law. Earlier chamber votes approved different versions that were later reconciled, so they describe proposals, not the enactment. The October 26 and 27, 1990, votes on the conference report are the tallies that actually sent Public Law 101-649 to the President.

## Bipartisan majorities and a Republican signature

The votes that sent the Immigration Act of 1990 to the President's desk deserve careful attention, because later retellings sometimes blur them. The authoritative votes are the votes on the conference report, the final negotiated text reconciling the House and Senate versions, and not the earlier votes on the separate chamber bills. On October 26, 1990, the Senate agreed to the conference report by a vote of 89 to 8, recorded as Record Vote Number 323, with 3 senators not voting. On October 27, 1990, the House agreed to the conference report by a vote of 264 to 118, recorded as Roll Number 530, with 50 members not voting. Those two tallies, taken one day apart at the close of the 101st Congress, are the votes that enacted the law.

The margins tell their own story. An 89 to 8 Senate vote is not a party-line outcome. It reflects a coalition that reached deep into both parties, the kind of majority that forms when a bill's sponsors have spent months assembling support across the usual divides. The House margin, 264 to 118, is narrower in percentage terms but a comfortable majority, achieved even with 50 members not voting in the rush of the session's final days. Together the two votes establish the bipartisan character of the enactment beyond dispute. This was not a measure forced through by one party over the other's objections. It was a negotiated product that commanded majorities in both chambers under divided legislative conditions, and it arrived at the White House with the institutional weight that such majorities confer.

The margins were possible because the bill distributed benefits and concentrated no pain. Every organized constituency in the immigration debate received something: family advocates got the 226,000 floor and the largest stream, business got the employment expansion and the five categories, ethnic organizations got the transitional programs and the diversity category, civil liberties advocates got the exclusion repeal. No organized constituency was asked to surrender anything it valued. The restrictionists, the one faction with reason to oppose an expansion, had spent their political capital on the 1986 compromise and lacked the numbers to block a bill that everyone else wanted. Expansions are easier to pass than restrictions when the costs are diffuse and the benefits are organized, and the 1990 Act is the textbook case.

The presidential signature completed the picture. George H. W. Bush signed the measure on November 29, 1990, roughly a month after the conference votes. The fact of a Republican signature on the largest expansion of legal immigration since 1965 has a way of surprising readers who came of political age later, when immigration politics had sorted into more rigid partisan patterns. But in 1990 the politics were different. The bill's combination of expanded legal channels, a restructured preference system emphasizing both family reunification and employment-based skills, and a rewritten exclusion framework that discarded Cold War anachronisms gave members of both parties reasons to support it. The coalition was substantive, not merely procedural, and the signature reflected a genuine meeting of legislative and executive judgment.

The cross-party bookends of the bargain gave it additional strength. A Democratic senator, Edward Kennedy of Massachusetts, sponsored the bill and managed its passage through the Senate. A Republican president, George H. W. Bush, signed it into law. The sponsor and the signer belonged to different parties, which meant that neither party could plausibly describe the measure as the other's project. Bipartisanship in immigration legislation has often taken this form: a Democratic legislative champion and a Republican executive partner, or the reverse, each giving their party's supporters permission to vote yes. The 1990 Act is among the clearest examples of the pattern, and the 89 to 8 and 264 to 118 margins are its quantitative signature.

The bipartisan enactment story carries an irony that runs through this entire guide. The provisions that later generated the fiercest public argument, the diversity lottery, temporary protected status, and the skilled-worker cap, were all enacted with the same broad majorities that carried the rest of the bill. They were not slipped into the law by a narrow faction. They were voted for by the same 89 senators and 264 House members who voted for the numerical restructuring and the preference categories. Whatever later controversies attached to these provisions, their origin was a statute that commanded broad institutional support at the moment of enactment, signed by a Republican president in the last weeks of 1990.

## The numerical restructuring

The first of the five changes, and the one from which all the others draw their meaning, was the restructuring of the numerical system. Before 1990, legal immigration operated under a worldwide ceiling that the new law's drafters considered both too low and too rigidly organized. The Act replaced that arrangement with a three-part system that remains the skeleton of legal admission: a worldwide limit of 675,000, divided among family-sponsored, employment-based, and diversity allocations, with formulas governing how each part interacted with the others. Understanding these numbers is not an exercise in trivia. The numbers are the policy. They determine who can come, through which door, and in what quantity, and every later argument about the system's fairness or sufficiency has been an argument about these figures or their descendants.

The headline number was 675,000, the worldwide limit that applied from fiscal year 1995 onward. That limit was not a single undifferentiated cap. It was the sum of three separate allocations: 480,000 for family-sponsored immigrants, 140,000 for employment-based immigrants, and 55,000 for diversity immigrants. Each allocation had its own internal logic, and the three interacted through provisions that shifted unused numbers from one category to another. The design reflected a deliberate effort to balance the competing claims that had organized immigration politics since 1965: the claims of family reunification, the claims of the labor market, and the newer claim of geographic diversity in the immigrant stream.

The family-sponsored allocation, the largest of the three, was set at 480,000, but the number requires careful reading because it was defined by a formula rather than fixed in stone. The statute provided for 480,000 minus the number of immediate relatives admitted in the prior year, plus parolees, plus any unused employment-based numbers from the prior year. In plain terms, the family-sponsored preference allocation floated: it shrank as immediate-relative admissions grew, and it absorbed numbers the employment-based side had not used. Beneath this floating calculation sat a floor. The family-sponsored preference level could not fall below 226,000, which guaranteed a minimum volume of family-preference admission no matter how the other variables moved. The floor expressed a legislative judgment that family reunification deserved protection against the arithmetic of the system, and readers interested in how the family preference categories developed over time can consult the companion guide to the [family preference visa system](/2013/02/01/family-preference-visa-system-law/) for the longer history.

The employment-based allocation was set at 140,000, plus any unused family-sponsored numbers from the prior year. The symmetry with the family side was deliberate. Just as unused employment numbers flowed to the family allocation, unused family numbers flowed to the employment allocation, which meant that the two preference systems were linked in a single hydraulic arrangement. Numbers unused on one side did not vanish. They moved to the other side, preserving the overall volume of preference admission even when demand patterns shifted between family and employment channels. This rollover mechanism is one of the least discussed but most consequential features of the 1990 design, because it made the system self-balancing in a way the pre-1990 caps had not been.

The diversity allocation was set at 55,000, a figure that applied to the permanent program beginning in fiscal year 1995. The diversity numbers were not subject to the same rollover formulas as the family and employment allocations. They stood as a separate stream, reserved for natives of countries with low admission rates to the United States, selected through the random-selection mechanism the Act created. The separation mattered. By placing diversity outside the family-employment hydraulic system, Congress signaled that geographic diversity was an independent policy goal, not merely a residual use of leftover numbers. The 55,000 figure would later be reduced by subsequent legislation, but at enactment it represented a firm commitment of more than eight percent of the worldwide limit to the diversity purpose.

These figures describe the permanent regime, the system as it was meant to operate from fiscal year 1995 onward. But the Act also created a transitional regime for fiscal years 1992 through 1994, with a higher overall level of 700,000. The transitional breakdown allocated 465,000 to family-sponsored immigrants, 55,000 to the spouses and children of aliens legalized under the 1986 reform law, 140,000 to employment-based immigrants, and 40,000 to natives of countries deemed adversely affected by the pre-1990 system. The transitional level served two purposes. It eased the shift from the old numerical regime to the new one, and it provided dedicated numbers for two populations with special claims: the families of those legalized in 1986, and the nationals of countries that the pre-1990 system had disadvantaged. The 40,000 allocation for adversely affected countries was the direct ancestor of the transitional diversity program discussed below, and it shows how the diversity idea entered the statute through the side door of transitional relief before becoming a permanent allocation.

The permeable character of the caps, noted earlier, applied across all three allocations. Immediate relatives of United States citizens, refugees, and asylees stood outside the numerical limits, which meant that total admissions in any year could and did exceed the 675,000 worldwide figure. The exemptions reflected a hierarchy of values embedded in the statute. Family reunification at its closest degree, the admission of the citizen's spouse, parent, or minor child, was not to be rationed by quota. Humanitarian admission through the refugee and asylum channels was likewise exempt, preserving the commitments the United States had undertaken in earlier legislation. The preference system governed everyone else, and the caps bit only on those within its reach.

The effective-date structure phased this numerical machinery into operation. Under section 161(a), the amendments took effect on October 1, 1991, applying beginning with fiscal year 1992, which meant that the transitional 700,000 level governed the first three years of the new system and the permanent 675,000 regime began in fiscal year 1995. This phasing was more than administrative convenience. It gave the agencies that administered admission, the State Department's visa office and the Immigration and Naturalization Service, time to rewrite regulations, retrain adjudicators, and reprogram the systems that tracked visa number availability. A numerical restructuring of this complexity could not be switched on overnight, and Congress built the transition into the statute itself.

Stepping back, the numerical restructuring did three things at once. It raised the level of legal immigration substantially, by roughly forty percent over the prior regime. It reorganized the allocation of that immigration into three distinct streams with defined relationships to one another. And it protected certain admissions from numerical rationing altogether. The combination was ambitious, and it is the reason the 1990 Act counts as the largest expansion of legal immigration since 1965. But the numbers were only the frame. What filled the frame, the categories, programs, and exclusion rules that determined who actually received the newly available visas, is the subject of the sections that follow.

## How the formulas worked

The numerical restructuring described above can be stated as a set of figures, but the figures only become intelligible when the formulas behind them are unpacked. The 1990 Act did not simply announce that 480,000 family-sponsored immigrants could be admitted. It defined how that number would be calculated each year, how it would interact with the other allocations, and what would happen when the arithmetic produced a result below the intended minimum. Working through the mechanics repays the effort, because the mechanics embodied the policy judgments that the bare numbers conceal.

Begin with the family-sponsored allocation. The statute set the figure at 480,000, then subtracted the number of immediate relatives admitted in the prior year, added parolees, and added any unused employment-based numbers from the prior year. To see how this operated, consider a hypothetical year in which 200,000 immediate relatives had been admitted the year before, parolees numbered in the low thousands, and the employment-based side had left 10,000 numbers unused. The family-sponsored preference allocation for the current year would be roughly 480,000 minus 200,000 plus the parolee adjustment plus 10,000, yielding something in the vicinity of 290,000. The precise result would depend on the exact parolee count and any other statutory adjustments, but the structure is clear: the family preference allocation floated inversely with immediate-relative admissions. The more immediate relatives admitted, the fewer preference numbers remained, because both drew on the same 480,000 reservoir.

The 226,000 floor existed to prevent that floating calculation from sinking too far. If immediate-relative admissions surged in a given year, the formula could in theory drive the family preference allocation below the level Congress considered adequate for the family-preference categories. The floor guaranteed that the allocation could not fall below 226,000 no matter what the other variables did. The floor thus expressed a hierarchy within the family-sponsored universe. Immediate relatives, the spouses, parents, and minor children of citizens, stood outside the preference caps entirely and were admitted without numerical limit. Family-preference immigrants, the more distant relatives covered by the preference categories, were subject to the caps but protected by the floor. Congress was unwilling to let the arithmetic of immediate-relative admissions starve the preference categories, and the floor was the instrument of that unwillingness.

The employment-based side mirrored the family side with its own formula. The allocation was 140,000 plus any unused family-sponsored numbers from the prior year. The symmetry was functional, not decorative. Immigration demand does not divide itself neatly between family and employment channels in the proportions Congress writes into the statute. In some years employers sponsor fewer immigrants than the allocation allows, while family demand presses against its ceiling. In other years the pattern reverses. The rollover provisions allowed the system to absorb these fluctuations by moving unused numbers across the family-employment boundary, preserving the total volume of preference admission rather than letting unused numbers evaporate. A rigid system wastes numbers when demand and allocation misalign. The 1990 design wasted as few as possible.

The diversity allocation stood apart from this hydraulic arrangement. Its 55,000 visas were not part of the rollover formulas. Unused diversity numbers did not flow to the family or employment sides, and unused family or employment numbers did not flow to diversity. The separation reflected the distinct policy purpose of the diversity stream. Family and employment admission responded to the claims of relatives and the labor market, claims whose strength varied from year to year and justified a balancing mechanism. Diversity admission responded to the goal of geographic variety in the immigrant stream, a goal Congress treated as independent of the year-to-year fluctuations in family and employment demand. Keeping the diversity numbers separate ensured that the diversity purpose could not be crowded out by stronger demand elsewhere in the system.

The permeable character of the caps added a further layer of complexity. Immediate relatives of citizens, refugees, and asylees were exempt from the numerical limits, which meant that the worldwide figure of 675,000 described the preference system rather than total admissions. In any year when immediate-relative admissions ran high, or when refugee admissions surged in response to international crises, total immigration could substantially exceed the stated limit. This was not a defect in the design. It was the design. Congress had decided that certain admissions should not be subjected to numerical rationing at all, and the permeability of the caps was how that decision was implemented. The 675,000 figure was a ceiling on the preference categories, not a ceiling on immigration as such.

The transitional regime for fiscal years 1992 through 1994 layered a temporary structure on top of these permanent mechanics. During the transition, the worldwide level was 700,000 rather than 675,000, and the internal division differed: 465,000 for family-sponsored immigrants, 55,000 for the spouses and children of those legalized under the 1986 reform law, 140,000 for employment-based immigrants, and 40,000 for natives of adversely affected countries. The transitional breakdown served as a bridge, carrying the system from the old numerical regime to the new one while honoring the special claims of the 1986 legalization families and the underrepresented nationalities. When fiscal year 1995 arrived, the bridge was dismantled and the permanent formulas took over, with the diversity program's 55,000 allocation replacing the transitional 40,000 for adversely affected countries.

Section 161(a)'s effective-date provisions governed the entire phasing. The amendments took effect October 1, 1991, applying beginning with fiscal year 1992, which meant that the transitional regime operated for exactly three fiscal years before giving way to the permanent system. The precision of the phasing, three years of transition, then the permanent regime, with the diversity program's permanent form waiting until fiscal year 1995, shows a Congress thinking in fiscal years rather than news cycles, designing machinery to be switched on in sequence rather than all at once. That is the amendment-stage sensibility in its purest form: the law as a set of instructions to be executed over time, not a proclamation to be celebrated in the moment.

## Five employment-based preference categories

The second change remade the employment side of the immigration system. Section 121 of the Act created five employment-based preference categories, codified at section 203(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. 1153(b), running from extraordinary ability at the top to immigrant investors at the bottom. Before 1990, employment-based immigration had been organized around a smaller set of preferences that mixed workers of different skill levels in ways the drafters considered outdated. The new five-category structure sorted employment-based immigrants by a clear hierarchy of qualifications, from the most accomplished down through skilled workers to investors whose contribution was capital rather than labor. The 140,000 employment-based allocation described above was distributed across these five categories, giving each a defined share of the employment stream.

The first employment-based preference, EB-1, was reserved for priority workers, a term the statute defined to cover three groups: persons of extraordinary ability, outstanding professors and researchers, and certain multinational executives and managers. The extraordinary-ability subcategory reached individuals at the very top of their fields, in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics, whose achievements could be documented through sustained acclaim. The outstanding professor and researcher subcategory recognized a different kind of distinction, anchored in academic appointment and a record of scholarly achievement. The multinational executive and manager subcategory addressed the needs of international businesses, allowing companies operating in multiple countries to transfer their leadership personnel to the United States. Together the three subcategories expressed a judgment that the immigration system should compete for the world's most accomplished people, whether their accomplishments were measured in prizes, publications, or corporate responsibility.

The second preference, EB-2, covered members of the professions holding advanced degrees and persons of exceptional ability. Where EB-1 demanded the extraordinary, EB-2 asked for the advanced: a master's degree or higher, or a bachelor's degree plus substantial progressive experience, or a showing of exceptional ability in the sciences, arts, or business well above the ordinary. This category became the workhorse of the employment-based system for highly educated professionals, the channel through which engineers, physicians, researchers, and other credentialed workers most commonly sought permanent admission. Its placement below EB-1 and above EB-3 reflected a deliberate calibration. Congress wanted a distinct tier for advanced-degree professionals that did not require the rarefied showing of extraordinary ability but stood apart from the skilled-worker categories below.

The third preference, EB-3, covered skilled workers, professionals, and other workers. The skilled-worker subcategory reached workers whose jobs required at least two years of training or experience. The professional subcategory covered members of the professions holding bachelor's degrees. The other-worker subcategory provided for unskilled labor, subject to its own numerical sublimit within the category. EB-3 was the broadest of the employment-based preferences in terms of the population it reached, and it became the principal channel for the ordinary skilled immigration that sustains industries from health care to construction. Its breadth also made it the category most sensitive to backlogs, because demand for its visas consistently pressed against the numbers available.

The fourth preference, EB-4, was reserved for certain special immigrants, a term of art in the INA covering a defined list of groups Congress had chosen to recognize over the years. The special-immigrant definition gathered together populations as varied as religious workers, certain broadcasters, and employees of international organizations, each admitted through the employment-based allocation because their admission was tied to a particular role or service rather than to family relationship. EB-4 functioned as the statute's miscellaneous drawer for employment-linked immigration that did not fit the skill hierarchy of the first three preferences, and its contents reflected the accumulation of decades of targeted legislative judgments.

The fifth preference, EB-5, created the immigrant-investor category, and it deserves separate attention because it introduced a genuinely new principle into employment-based immigration. Created by section 121(a) through (b) of the Act, EB-5 offered permanent admission to investors who committed capital to commercial enterprises in the United States and whose investments created jobs for American workers. Where the other employment-based categories rewarded what an immigrant could do, EB-5 rewarded what an immigrant could finance. The category embodied a theory that investment capital was itself a contribution to the labor market, because capital deployed in job-creating enterprises generated employment for others. The investor category was the most novel of the five preferences, and it attracted attention from the start as an experiment in using immigration policy as an instrument of economic development.

### How did the 1990 law reorganize employment-based immigration?

Section 121 replaced the older employment preferences with five ranked categories, codified at INA section 203(b). The new hierarchy ran from extraordinary ability and outstanding researchers through advanced-degree professionals and skilled workers to special immigrants and investors. The 140,000 employment-based allocation, plus unused family numbers, was distributed across these five categories.

The five-category structure did more than relabel existing channels. It changed the logic by which employment-based immigrants were selected, moving from a system organized partly around labor-market tests to one organized around a hierarchy of qualifications. Under the new structure, the question an employment-based applicant faced was not simply whether an American worker was available for the job, but where the applicant stood on the ladder from extraordinary to ordinary skill, with investors occupying a rung defined by capital rather than credentials. That ladder, with its five rungs, became the permanent architecture of employment-based immigration, and every subsequent debate about employment-based backlogs, per-country limits, and the balance between skills and family reunification has been conducted on the terrain the 1990 Act laid out.

The employment-based reorganization also needs to be read alongside the family-sponsored restructuring, because the two were linked by the rollover provisions described earlier. Unused employment-based numbers flowed to the family side, and unused family numbers flowed to the employment side, which meant that the five categories operated inside a larger hydraulic system. If employment demand fell short of the 140,000 allocation in a given year, the surplus did not go to waste. It moved to the family allocation, preserving total preference admission. This linkage is easy to overlook when the categories are studied in isolation, but it was central to how Congress designed the system to function as a whole rather than as a collection of separate programs.

## The hierarchy principle in employment-based selection

The five employment-based categories described above share a design logic that deserves separate examination, because the logic reveals how Congress thought about the relationship between immigration and the labor market. The categories form a hierarchy, and the hierarchy runs on a single principle: the immigration system should prefer, in descending order, the extraordinary, the advanced, the skilled, the specially designated, and the capitalized. Each rung of the ladder embodies a distinct theory of what makes an immigrant valuable to the receiving country, and the order of the rungs embodies a judgment about the relative weight of those theories.

The top of the ladder, EB-1, rewards distinction that can be documented at the highest level. Extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education, business, or athletics; a record as an outstanding professor or researcher; responsibility as a multinational executive or manager. What unites the three subcategories is not a common occupation but a common standard: the applicant must stand at or near the top of a recognized field. The standard is deliberately demanding. Congress wanted the first preference to function as a magnet for exceptional talent, and it set the bar accordingly. The inclusion of multinational executives and managers alongside prize-winning scientists and acclaimed artists shows that the drafters understood distinction in institutional as well as individual terms. A person who directs the operations of an international enterprise exercises a form of distinction that the immigration system should recognize, even though it is measured in organizational responsibility rather than publications or performances.

The step down to EB-2, from the extraordinary to the advanced, is the most carefully calibrated transition in the hierarchy. EB-2 asks for an advanced degree or exceptional ability, a standard well above the ordinary but below the rarefied heights of EB-1. The calibration matters because it determines where the bulk of highly educated professional immigration flows. Engineers with master's degrees, physicians with specialized training, researchers with strong but not yet extraordinary records: these are the applicants who make EB-2 the workhorse of the employment-based system. If the EB-1 standard had been set lower, the first preference would have been flooded and its purpose diluted. If the EB-2 standard had been set higher, advanced-degree professionals would have had no appropriate channel and would have pressed awkwardly against categories designed for other populations. The boundary between the first and second preferences is where Congress did its finest sorting, and the placement of that boundary shaped the composition of skilled immigration for decades.

EB-3 extends the hierarchy downward to the skilled workers, professionals, and other workers who form the broad base of employment-based immigration. The category's breadth is its defining feature. Where EB-1 and EB-2 select for distinction, EB-3 selects for competence: two years of training or experience for skilled workers, a bachelor's degree for professionals, and a willingness to perform unskilled labor for the other-worker subcategory. The breadth means that EB-3 reaches the ordinary skilled immigration that sustains industries across the economy, from health care facilities seeking nurses to construction firms seeking experienced tradespeople. It also means that EB-3 is the category where demand most consistently exceeds supply, because the population of competent skilled workers worldwide dwarfs the numbers available. The hierarchy principle implies that distinction should be rarer than competence, and the allocation of numbers across the categories reflects that implication.

EB-4 operates on a different principle from the first three categories, and its difference illuminates the hierarchy by contrast. The special-immigrant category does not rank its beneficiaries by skill or distinction. It gathers together populations Congress has chosen to recognize for reasons tied to particular roles or services: religious workers, certain broadcasters, employees of international organizations, and others defined by statute over the years. The category is the hierarchy's miscellaneous drawer, and its contents reflect the accumulation of discrete legislative judgments rather than a unified theory of labor-market value. Its placement fourth in the order does not mean its beneficiaries are less valued than EB-3 workers. It means that their admission is justified by considerations outside the skill hierarchy, and the statute gives those considerations a home without disturbing the ranking of the first three preferences.

EB-5, the investor category, introduces the hierarchy's most novel principle: capital as a basis for selection. Where the other categories reward what the immigrant can do, EB-5 rewards what the immigrant can finance, on the theory that capital deployed in job-creating enterprises generates employment for others. The placement of investors fifth in the order is itself a statement. Congress was willing to create an investment-based channel, but it ranked investment below extraordinary ability, advanced credentials, skilled labor, and special-immigrant service. The ranking suggests a legislature that accepted the economic logic of investor immigration while keeping it subordinate to the human-capital logic that organized the rest of the hierarchy. Created by section 121(a) through (b), the category was an experiment, and like all experiments it carried the possibility of working differently in practice than in theory.

The 140,000 employment-based allocation, plus unused family numbers, was distributed across these five rungs, giving the hierarchy numerical expression. Numbers are where principles meet reality, and the distribution of the 140,000 across EB-1 through EB-5 determined how the hierarchy actually functioned. A hierarchy without numbers is a statement of values. A hierarchy with numbers is a working system, and the 1990 Act built the working system, rung by rung, number by number.

## The cap on the specialty-occupation visa

Alongside the five permanent employment-based categories, the Act imposed a numerical cap on the principal temporary visa for skilled workers, and that cap became one of the three most argued-about features of the later system. Section 205 of the Act created the specialty-occupation framework for the H-1B classification, defined at section 101(a)(15)(H)(i)(b) of the INA, and it placed the classification under an annual numerical limit codified at section 214(g)(1)(A) of the INA, 8 U.S.C. 1184(g)(1)(A). The initial cap was set at 65,000 per year. A single number, set down in a long statute, went on to govern the national conversation about skilled temporary migration for decades.

The specialty-occupation concept needs explanation, because the cap only makes sense against the category it limited. The H-1B classification admitted foreign workers in occupations requiring highly specialized knowledge, the engineers, programmers, analysts, and other professionals whose work demanded advanced expertise. Unlike the employment-based preference categories, which led to permanent residence, the H-1B was a temporary classification, authorizing a period of authorized work rather than a permanent home. The distinction between temporary and permanent admission runs through the entire immigration code, and the 1990 Act's treatment of the two tracks shows how differently Congress approached them. For permanent employment-based immigration, Congress built a five-rung ladder with a 140,000 allocation. For temporary specialty-occupation workers, Congress built a single classification with a single annual number.

The decision to cap a temporary worker program was itself a policy statement. Temporary worker classifications had not always been numerically limited in the same way, and the choice to fix the H-1B at 65,000 reflected a judgment that even temporary skilled migration should be subject to quantitative control. The cap functioned as a valve. In years when employer demand for specialty workers ran below 65,000, the cap was invisible, a ceiling no one touched. In years when demand exceeded the number, the cap became the central fact of the program, determining which petitions could be approved and forcing employers, workers, and lawyers to organize their plans around a finite annual supply. The valve character of the cap explains why a provision that occupied so little space in the statute generated so much later argument. A number that does nothing in slack years does everything in tight ones.

The codification of the cap at section 214(g)(1)(A) matters for understanding its durability. By placing the numerical limit in the statute itself rather than leaving it to regulation, Congress ensured that the cap could only be changed by further legislation. An agency could not raise or lower the number through rulemaking. That entrenchment was deliberate. Numerical limits are the most politically sensitive element of immigration policy, and Congress kept the power to set them for itself. The consequence was that every later debate about whether 65,000 was too many or too few had to be conducted as a legislative debate, with all the friction that legislative action entails.

The pairing of expansion and cap in the same statute is characteristic of the 1990 approach. Congress opened the permanent employment-based system wider than ever before, allocating 140,000 visas and building five ranked categories, while simultaneously drawing a bright numerical line around the principal temporary specialty-occupation channel. The two moves were complementary in the drafters' minds: generous permanent avenues for the highly skilled, bounded temporary avenues for everyone else.

### How could one statute both expand employment immigration and cap the main temporary work visa?

The drafters treated permanent and temporary admission as separate problems requiring separate tools. The 140,000 employment-based allocation and five ranked categories addressed permanent immigration for workers of demonstrated value, while the 65,000 H-1B cap kept temporary specialty-occupation admission finite and measurable.

The distinction between the temporary and permanent tracks deserves emphasis because it explains why the cap bit so differently from the preference numbers. The employment-based preferences allocated 140,000 permanent admissions per year across the five categories, and unused numbers rolled over to the family side. The H-1B cap governed a temporary classification with no rollover and no parallel safety valve. A worker who could not obtain permanent residence through the preferences might work temporarily under H-1B, but only if a number was available under the 65,000 ceiling. The two tracks were connected in the careers of individual workers, many moved from temporary to permanent standing over time, but they were governed by separate numerical regimes, and the temporary regime's ceiling was absolute in a way the permanent regime's allocations were not.

That absoluteness is what made the cap such a potent policy instrument. A preference allocation that goes unused in one category can flow elsewhere. A cap that is reached simply stops. When employer petitions exceeded 65,000, the excess petitions were not deferred or redirected. They were denied the classification for that year, and the workers and employers involved had to wait, restructure, or abandon their plans. The binary character of the cap, open until it is full and closed thereafter, concentrated the politics of skilled migration into an annual event: the filling of the cap, the date on which it filled, and the distance between demand and supply. No other provision of the 1990 Act created such a clean, repeatable, newsworthy moment of scarcity, which is one reason the cap's later prominence dwarfed the attention it received at enactment.

The H-1B cap also illustrates the amendment-stage pattern that organizes this guide. At enactment, the cap drew far less attention than the permanent preference categories or the diversity program. It was a technical provision in a technical statute, a number attached to a classification. Yet it became one of the three features of the system most contested afterward, precisely because it translated an abstract policy question, how much skilled temporary migration should the United States admit, into a single concrete number that could be fought over year after year. The provisions that generate the loudest later argument are often the least noticed at enactment, and the 65,000 cap is the clearest demonstration of the rule.

## The diversity lottery and its unlikely origins

The third change created the diversity visa lottery, and its history is the most surprising in the entire statute. The common assumption is that the lottery was designed as a broad humanitarian measure, a way of opening American immigration to the world at large. The record shows something different. The diversity program originated in a series of transitional programs that benefited specific nationalities disadvantaged by the post-1965 preference system, and it became a permanent worldwide program almost as an outgrowth of those transitional experiments. Understanding the lottery requires understanding the sequence: NP-5, then OP-1, then AA-1, then the permanent program, each step building on the last.

The sequence begins with the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986, the legalization-and-sanctions statute whose section 314 created the NP-5 program. NP-5 made 5,000 visas available per year in fiscal years 1987 and 1988 to natives of 36 countries described as adversely affected by the pre-1990 immigration system, and the program was extended through fiscal years 1989 and 1990 at 15,000 visas per year. Ireland was the largest beneficiary, receiving 31 percent of the NP-5 visas, a figure that reveals the program's real constituency. The countries described as adversely affected were, in practice, substantially European countries whose nationals had found little room in the family-preference system created by the [1965 immigration law](/2012/12/01/immigration-nationality-act-1965-guide/), which had directed the immigrant stream overwhelmingly toward Asia and Latin America. Readers who want the full background on that redirection can consult the guide to the [1986 reform law](/2013/01/01/immigration-reform-control-act-1986/) for the context in which NP-5 was born.

NP-5 was followed by the OP-1 lottery, which operated from 1989 through 1991 and distributed visas through a random-selection mechanism. Known informally as the Berman visas after the House member associated with the program, OP-1 extended the transitional logic of NP-5: additional visas, distributed by lottery, for nationals of countries underrepresented in the existing system. The random-selection device was itself a novelty. The immigration code had historically allocated visas through family relationships, employment qualifications, and refugee determinations, all criteria tied to the characteristics of the applicant. A lottery allocated visas by chance among the eligible, which represented a break with the merit-and-kinship logic of the preference system. That break would become the defining feature of the permanent diversity program.

The 1990 Act's own transitional diversity program was section 132's AA-1 program, known as the Morrison visas, which made 40,000 visas available per year in fiscal years 1992 through 1994. At least 40 percent of the AA-1 visas were earmarked for natives of Ireland, a provision that carried forward the nationality-specific character of the earlier transitional programs. The AA-1 program bridged the gap between the old transitional lotteries and the new permanent system, operating during the same fiscal years as the 700,000 transitional worldwide level. It was, in effect, the diversity idea in its adolescent form: a lottery, distributing a fixed number of visas, with a heavy tilt toward particular nationalities whose advocates had driven the program's creation.

The permanent diversity program took effect in fiscal year 1995, with an allocation of 55,000 visas. The permanent program differed from its transitional predecessors in one decisive respect. Where NP-5, OP-1, and AA-1 had benefited specific nationalities, many of them European, the permanent program was defined by a neutral criterion: it reserved visas for natives of countries with low admission rates to the United States. The lottery mechanism was retained, but the beneficiary class was redefined around the concept of underrepresentation rather than around the claims of particular diaspora communities. This was the moment the diversity program became what its name promised, a program for diversity as such, rather than a transitional remedy for specific groups.

The permanent program's 55,000 allocation did not last unchanged. The Nicaraguan Adjustment and Central American Relief Act, Public Law 105-100, enacted in 1997, reduced the 55,000 ceiling by up to 5,000 visas annually beginning in fiscal year 1999, diverting those numbers to other purposes. The NACARA reduction is worth noting because it shows how quickly the diversity allocation became a source of visas for unrelated legislative bargains. A program created to diversify the immigrant stream found part of its allocation repurposed within a decade, which is itself a commentary on how Congress treats numerical allocations as fungible resources.

A caution about chronology is essential here, because the sequence is easy to compress incorrectly. The 55,000 allocation belonged to the permanent program effective in fiscal year 1995. It did not begin in fiscal year 1992. The fiscal year 1992 through 1994 period belonged to the AA-1 transitional program at 40,000 visas per year, operating under the 700,000 transitional worldwide level. Collapsing the transitional and permanent programs into a single story obscures the statute's actual design, which phased the diversity idea from transitional relief into permanent structure over the better part of a decade, from NP-5 in 1987 through the permanent program's launch in 1995.

### Why was the diversity lottery not designed as a general humanitarian measure?

Its origins lie in transitional programs, NP-5, OP-1, and AA-1, that benefited specific nationalities disadvantaged by the post-1965 preference system. Ireland took 31 percent of NP-5 visas, and AA-1 earmarked at least 40 percent for Irish natives. Only the permanent program, effective in fiscal year 1995, used low admission rates as the criterion.

The diversity story exemplifies the amendment-stage thesis of this guide. A set of transitional programs, created to address the grievances of particular nationalities, accreted over several statutes into a permanent worldwide lottery that became one of the most argued-about features of the immigration system. At no point in the sequence did Congress hold a single debate about whether the United States should allocate 55,000 visas per year by random selection to underrepresented countries. The program emerged incrementally, through NP-5's 5,000 visas, OP-1's lottery experiment, AA-1's 40,000 transitional visas, and finally the permanent 55,000 allocation. Each step seemed modest in its moment. The cumulative result was a structural transformation that almost no one had voted for as a single proposition, which is why the lottery's later prominence surprised even many of its legislative parents.

## Ireland and the politics of underrepresentation

The diversity lottery's unlikely origins become fully intelligible only when the political mechanism behind the transitional programs is examined directly. The programs did not emerge from an abstract commitment to geographic variety. They emerged from a concrete grievance, pressed by concrete constituencies, about how the post-1965 preference system distributed the opportunity to immigrate. Understanding the grievance requires understanding the mechanism that produced it, and understanding the mechanism requires looking closely at how family-based chain migration reshaped the immigrant stream after 1965.

The 1965 Act had organized legal immigration around family reunification and, to a lesser degree, employment preferences. The family emphasis meant that immigration flowed through kinship networks: each admitted immigrant could sponsor relatives, who could in turn sponsor further relatives, creating chains of migration that grew geometrically over time. The chains did not distribute themselves evenly across the globe. They concentrated in the regions whose nationals had already established footholds in the United States, because chain migration requires an initial link, and the initial links were strongest where earlier migration had been heaviest. By the 1980s, the geometry of chain migration had directed the immigrant stream overwhelmingly toward Asia and Latin America, while nationals of countries without large existing diasporas found the family preferences effectively closed to them.

Ireland was the paradigmatic case. Before 1965, Ireland had been among the favored nationalities under the national origins quotas, sending a steady stream of immigrants through a system designed to preserve the country's existing ethnic composition. After 1965, that stream dwindled to a trickle. The preference categories rewarded kinship with Americans, and the Irish in America, largely assimilated and several generations removed from immigration, sponsored few relatives. The country's allocation under the new system went substantially unused while demand from other nationalities overwhelmed theirs. By the 1980s, Irish-American organizations were describing their homeland as the forgotten casualty of the 1965 reform, a country with deep historical ties to the United States and almost no legal channel for its would-be emigrants. Young Irish men and women who wanted to work in the United States found themselves without a legal channel, and a substantial number came anyway, joining the unauthorized population. The Irish-American community, politically organized and concentrated in states with congressional influence, pressed for relief.

The political response to that grievance was the sequence of transitional programs, and the sequence reveals how Congress legislates by experiment. NP-5 was the pilot: a small program, 5,000 visas for two fiscal years, restricted to thirty-six adversely affected countries, testing whether a lottery for the system's losers could operate without scandal. It could, and Ireland's thirty-one percent share demonstrated both the demand and the constituency. The extension to 15,000 visas for fiscal years 1989 and 1990 scaled the pilot. OP-1, the Berman visas, ran a parallel lottery from 1989 through 1991, further normalizing the mechanism. By the time the 1990 Act was drafted, the lottery was no longer an experiment but a proven instrument with organized defenders, which is why section 132 could create AA-1 at 40,000 visas per year without anyone treating the number as radical.

The Morrison earmark, at least forty percent of AA-1 visas for natives of Ireland, was the most candid provision in the sequence. Earmarks of that specificity are rare in immigration law, and this one named the program's true beneficiary in statutory text. Its candor was also its vulnerability: a program that reserved two-fifths of its visas for one country could not be defended as a general principle, only as a transitional corrective. That vulnerability is what made the permanent category possible. To make the lottery permanent, Congress had to generalize it, replacing the Irish earmark with the neutral criterion of low admission rates. The generalization preserved the mechanism while shedding the specificity, which allowed supporters to defend the permanent program as a diversity measure rather than an ethnic favor.

The lottery mechanism itself embodied a distinctive theory of fairness. Unlike the family preferences, which rewarded existing ties, and unlike the employment preferences, which rewarded credentials, the lottery rewarded only the facts of nativity and underrepresentation. Its eligibility rule, birth in a country with low recent admission rates, was blind to the applicant's skills, wealth, family, and suffering. That blindness was the point. The program's designers had concluded that every other allocative principle systematically favored the already-favored, and only chance, constrained by the nativity rule, could break the pattern. Whether chance is a just principle of admission is a philosophical question the statute did not try to answer; it merely installed the mechanism and let the principle defend itself.

### How did the post-1965 system disadvantage certain nationalities?

The 1965 preference system allocated visas mainly through family relationships, so immigration flowed through chain migration to regions with large existing diasporas, chiefly Asia and Latin America. Nationals of countries without such diasporas, including Ireland and several European states, found little room in the family preferences and pressed Congress for transitional relief.

Only the permanent program, effective in fiscal year 1995, broke the pattern. By defining eligibility around low admission rates rather than around the claims of particular nationalities, it converted a sequence of transitional remedies into a general program. The conversion was not debated as a philosophical choice between nationality-specific relief and neutral diversity. It emerged from the legislative process as the endpoint of the sequence, the form the program took when Congress decided to make permanent what had begun as temporary. That is how the diversity lottery acquired its final shape: not through a founding debate about random selection as an immigration principle, but through the accretion of transitional programs whose original purpose had been to help the Irish.

## Temporary protected status

The fourth change replaced decades of ad hoc executive practice with a statutory framework, and it did so so quietly that the substitution barely registered outside specialist circles. Section 302 of the Act created temporary protected status, codified at section 244 of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. 1254a. The provision authorized the Attorney General, the official in whom the designation authority was vested at enactment, to designate a foreign country for temporary protected status when conditions in that country made it unsafe for its nationals to return. The authority later moved to the Secretary of Homeland Security in 2003, when the immigration functions were reorganized, but the statutory framework itself was the work of the 1990 Act.

The designation standard had three prongs, and each prong described a distinct kind of catastrophe. The Attorney General could designate a country when there was ongoing armed conflict within the country, when an environmental disaster had struck it, or when extraordinary and temporary conditions prevented its nationals from returning in safety. The three prongs were written in the alternative. Any one of them sufficed. Together they covered the situations in which deporting people to their home country would have been not merely harsh but dangerous: a civil war raging, an earthquake or hurricane that had destroyed the infrastructure of return, or a collapse of public order that made repatriation unthinkable. The statute did not require the Attorney General to find that every national of the designated country faced individual persecution. It required a finding about country conditions, and that finding then protected the country's nationals as a class.

The protection itself was temporary, as the name promised, but temporary in a specific statutory sense. Designation did not confer permanent residence. It conferred a protected standing that shielded its holders from removal and authorized them to work while the designation remained in effect, for the duration of the unsafe conditions plus a wind-down period. The time-limited character of the protection was the point. Congress was creating a mechanism for situations that were expected to pass: wars that would end, disasters from which countries would recover, emergencies that would subside. The statute built in the expectation of return, even as it acknowledged that return could not be demanded while the danger persisted.

To appreciate what section 302 accomplished, it is necessary to understand what it replaced. Before 1990, the executive branch had for decades provided similar relief through a practice known as Extended Voluntary Departure, or EVD. Under EVD, the executive granted groups of foreign nationals permission to remain in the United States when conditions in their home countries made return unsafe, but it did so as an exercise of discretion, without statutory criteria, without prescribed procedures, and without any legislative definition of when the relief was appropriate. EVD was an ad hoc practice. It depended on the willingness of the executive of the moment to extend it, and it offered its beneficiaries no legal framework they could invoke as of right.

The United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit later described the substitution in terms worth quoting. In Ramos v. Wolf, decided in 2020, the court explained: "Congress created the TPS program in 1990 to establish formal criteria and procedures to replace Extended Voluntary Departure (EVD), a practice the executive branch had used for decades to provide similar relief on an ad hoc basis." The quotation captures the essential character of the change. Congress took a discretionary executive practice and replaced it with a statute: formal criteria for when designation was appropriate, formal procedures for how designation would be made and reviewed, and a defined legal standing for the people the designation protected. The movement from discretion to statute is one of the recurring patterns of immigration legislation, and the TPS provision is among its clearest examples.

The TPS framework needs to be distinguished from the refugee and asylum system, with which it is sometimes confused. The [Refugee Act of 1980](/2013/04/01/refugee-act-1980-guide/) had created the American asylum system, built around individualized determinations that an applicant faced persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. TPS operated on a different principle. It required no individualized showing of persecution. It protected nationals of designated countries as a class, based on country conditions rather than personal targeting. The two systems were complementary, not overlapping. Asylum addressed the person hunted for who she was. TPS addressed the population stranded by what had happened to its country. The 1990 Act's contribution was to give the second of these two humanitarian responses the statutory home it had lacked.

### What problem did temporary protected status solve?

Before 1990, the executive branch protected stranded populations through Extended Voluntary Departure, an ad hoc discretionary practice with no statutory criteria or procedures. Section 302 replaced that discretion with a formal framework: designation under INA section 244(b)(1) when armed conflict, environmental disaster, or extraordinary temporary conditions made return unsafe, giving beneficiaries a defined legal standing.

The quietness of the TPS enactment is itself part of the story this guide tells. The provision that created a statutory humanitarian framework, replacing decades of executive discretion with formal criteria and procedures, drew a fraction of the attention given to the numerical restructuring or the diversity program. Yet TPS became one of the three features of the system most contested afterward, precisely because it gave the executive a defined statutory tool for responding to country-condition crises, and every later use of that tool reopened the underlying questions the statute had settled in 1990: which conditions qualify, how long protection should last, and what happens when temporary conditions persist. The framework was built to manage emergencies. Its later prominence came from the discovery that emergencies do not always end on schedule, and that a statute designed for the temporary had to answer for the indefinite.

## Designation mechanics: how TPS worked on paper

Section 302 is worth examining as a piece of statutory engineering, because its design choices reveal what Congress thought was wrong with Extended Voluntary Departure and what a lawful replacement required. The section built a complete administrative machine: a decisionmaker, a standard, a procedure, a status, and an exit.

The decisionmaker was the Attorney General, acting under INA section 244(b)(1). Placing the designation power in the Attorney General rather than the Secretary of State was a deliberate allocation of institutional competence. Country conditions were a foreign policy judgment, but the consequences of designation, protection from removal and work authorization for people inside the United States, were immigration enforcement judgments, and the Attorney General owned the immigration enforcement apparatus. The designation authority moved to the Secretary of Homeland Security in 2003, when the immigration functions were reorganized into the new department, but the 1990 design logic survived the move: the official who controlled removal would control the exception to removal.

The standard had three prongs, and each prong named a distinct theory of why return might be unsafe. Ongoing armed conflict covered civil wars and international conflicts that made a country dangerous for anyone. Environmental disaster covered earthquakes, floods, and other catastrophes that destroyed the housing, employment, and public services returnees would need. Extraordinary and temporary conditions covered the remainder: situations too unusual for the first two prongs but too dangerous to ignore, a residual category that gave the standard flexibility without surrendering it to pure discretion. The three prongs shared a temporal qualifier. The conditions had to be temporary, which distinguished TPS from the permanent protection of asylum and committed the program, at least on paper, to the proposition that its beneficiaries would one day go home.

The procedure required publication and periodicity. Designations were announced, with their terms and conditions stated on the record, and they were time-limited, subject to review and extension or termination as conditions changed. This was the precise inversion of EVD's formlessness. Where EVD had been granted by unpublished executive decision, TPS was granted by published designation. Where EVD's duration had been a matter of executive sufferance, TPS's duration was a matter of statutory review. The procedural regularity did not merely protect beneficiaries; it protected the program, by giving its decisions the form of law rather than the form of grace.

The status that designation conferred was carefully bounded. Beneficiaries received protection from removal and authorization to work for the designation period, but the statute did not convert TPS into a path to permanent residence. The boundary was the point. Congress was willing to shelter people from return during crisis, but it was not willing to let a temporary crisis become a permanent immigration channel by operation of time. The distinction between protection and admission, between being allowed to remain and being granted a future, ran through the entire section. The exit, finally, was termination. When the designating official determined that the conditions supporting designation no longer existed, the designation ended, and with it the protection.

Set against the asylum system, the TPS machine occupied a distinct and previously empty space. Asylum adjudicated individual claims of persecution, asking whether a particular person faced a particular danger. TPS extended group protection on the basis of country conditions, asking whether a country's nationals generally could safely return. The two systems could overlap in their beneficiaries but not in their logic, and the 1990 Act's achievement was to give the group logic a statutory home instead of leaving it to executive improvisation.

## The exclusion rewrite

The fifth change rewrote the grounds on which the United States could exclude arriving aliens, and in doing so it closed the book on the Cold War as a source of immigration law. Section 601 of the Act, comprising Title VI, undertook a wholesale revision of the exclusion provisions. The most discussed element of the revision was the repeal of the ideological exclusions, the provisions that had barred admission based on beliefs, associations, and memberships deemed subversive. The revision also repealed the exclusion of people on the basis of sexual orientation, removing a ground that had stood in the statute for decades. Together these repeals transformed the exclusion system from an instrument of ideological screening into a framework organized around security in a narrower, more concrete sense.

The old ideological exclusions lived in former section 212(a)(28) of the INA, a provision that had accumulated over the Cold War decades into a catalogue of excludable beliefs and affiliations. Membership in Communist or other totalitarian parties, advocacy of doctrines deemed subversive, association with organizations on government lists: the provision reached deep into the political lives of applicants, and its administration had generated controversy for years. The 1990 Act struck these ideological grounds. The striking was not merely symbolic. It removed from the statute the legal basis on which consular officers and immigration inspectors had excluded writers, academics, activists, and ordinary travelers whose politics offended the American government of the moment. The repeal reflected a judgment that the end of the Cold War confrontation had dissolved the rationale for policing the beliefs of arriving aliens.

In place of the ideological catalogue, the Act created a new section 212(a)(3), consolidating the security-related grounds of exclusion in a single streamlined provision. The new grounds were organized around conduct and concrete danger rather than belief. They covered terrorist activity, defined with a specificity the old ideological provisions had lacked. They covered aliens whose presence would cause serious adverse foreign-policy consequences for the United States, a ground that preserved a measure of executive discretion over diplomatically sensitive admissions without reviving the old ideological screening. They covered membership in or affiliation with totalitarian parties, retaining a narrowed version of the old party-membership ground but placing it inside a security framework rather than an ideological one. And they covered participation in Nazi persecution or genocide, ensuring that the perpetrators of the century's defining atrocities remained excludable under the new system. The movement from section 212(a)(28) to section 212(a)(3) was thus a movement from ideology to security, from policing what arriving aliens believed to assessing what they had done or were likely to do.

The totalitarian-party ground was the rewrite's most delicate compromise. The old law's Communist Party provisions were repealed, but a membership-based ground survived in recast form, covering affiliation with totalitarian parties. The survival reflected a political reality: completely abandoning the party-membership concept would have exposed the bill to charges of naivete, while retaining it in its old form would have betrayed the rewrite's purpose. The compromise moved the ground inside the security framework, where it sat alongside terrorist activity and foreign policy rather than alongside ideological advocacy. The relocation changed the ground's meaning even where it preserved its text. Membership mattered as evidence of security risk, not as a proxy for forbidden belief.

The second repeal, less discussed at the time but equally consequential, removed the exclusion ground that had barred people on the basis of sexual orientation. Former section 212(a)(4) of the INA had excluded aliens afflicted with "psychopathic personality, sexual deviation," language that the immigration authorities had applied to exclude gay men and lesbians. The Supreme Court had upheld that application in Boutilier v. INS, 387 U.S. 118 (1967), holding that the statutory phrase encompassed homosexuality. The 1990 Act removed the ground entirely, and Boutilier is recorded as abrogated by the Act, its holding deprived of statutory foundation. The repeal ended decades during which the immigration code had treated sexual orientation as a medicalized basis for exclusion, and it did so through the same Title VI revision that dismantled the ideological exclusions. Readers interested in how the Supreme Court's immigration decisions shaped and were shaped by statutes like this one can consult the companion guide to [immigration law Supreme Court cases](/2013/03/15/immigration-law-supreme-court-cases/).

### What did repealing the ideological exclusions actually change in the statute?

It replaced a belief-based test with a conduct-based one. Former section 212(a)(28) excluded aliens for Communist Party membership and advocacy of proscribed doctrines; new section 212(a)(3) consolidated security grounds around terrorist activity, adverse foreign policy consequences, totalitarian-party affiliation, and Nazi persecution or genocide, narrowing the state's power to exclude people for their opinions.

The exclusion rewrite illustrates a dimension of the 1990 Act that the numerical and categorical changes can obscure. The statute was not only about how many immigrants could come and through which channels. It was also about the changing character of the American state's relationship to arriving aliens, about what kinds of people the law considered excludable and why. The Cold War exclusions had made the immigration system an instrument of ideological warfare. Their repeal made it, at least in aspiration, an instrument of security assessment. The sexual-orientation repeal removed a different kind of anachronism, a medical-moral judgment embedded in the code. Both repeals pointed in the same direction: toward an exclusion system organized around demonstrable danger rather than disfavored identity or belief.

Like the other provisions of the Act, the exclusion rewrite drew less attention at enactment than its substance warranted. The repeal of the ideological exclusions was noted by the press and praised by civil-liberties advocates, but it competed for attention with the numerical restructuring and the new preference categories, and the sexual-orientation repeal received quieter notice. Yet the rewrite permanently altered the legal framework within which every exclusion decision was made. Every consular officer applying section 212(a)(3), every immigration judge considering a charge of excludability, and every lawyer advising a client on admissibility worked within the categories the 1990 Act had drawn. The provisions that reshape the daily administration of the law are not always the provisions that dominate the debate about the law, and Title VI is the proof.

The Cold War context of the repealed provisions needs to be felt to be understood. Section 212(a)(28) had been written and expanded during decades when the United States conceived of itself as engaged in an ideological struggle with international communism, and the immigration code was one of the instruments of that struggle. The provision's administration generated recurring controversy, as consular officers excluded writers, scholars, and activists whose views the government deemed dangerous, and as critics charged that the United States was betraying its own principles by policing the beliefs of visitors. By 1990 the geopolitical confrontation that had justified the provisions was dissolving, and the repeal registered that dissolution in the immigration code. The timing was not coincidental. A statute that rewrote the exclusion grounds in any other decade might have preserved the ideological apparatus. The 1990 Act, enacted as the Cold War ended, dismantled it.

## Measuring the expansion

The claim that the Immigration Act of 1990 raised legal immigration by roughly forty percent is repeated so often that it deserves to be examined as a measurement rather than taken as a slogan. What does the percentage mean, what is it measured against, and what do the permeable caps imply for its accuracy? The answers reveal as much about the difficulty of quantifying immigration policy as they do about the 1990 Act itself.

The starting point is the figure supplied by the legislative record. A later Senate report, S. Rept. 104-250, described the change as a 37 percent increase in legal immigration. The round figure of roughly forty percent is the common paraphrase, and it is fair as an approximation as long as it is not presented as a quoted statistic. The 37 percent figure presumably compared the new worldwide limit and its associated allocations against the prior regime's ceilings, measuring the expansion of the preference system that the Act restructured. Like all such comparisons, it depended on choices about what to count: the stated caps, the effective admissions under the old system, the transitional levels, or the permanent regime.

The permeable character of the caps complicates any simple percentage. Because immediate relatives of citizens, refugees, and asylees stood outside the numerical limits, total admissions in any year could exceed the stated worldwide figure, under both the old and the new regimes. A percentage increase in the caps is therefore not identical to a percentage increase in total immigration. It is a percentage increase in the numerically limited preference system, which is the part of immigration that Congress directly rations. The distinction matters for precision, but it does not undermine the basic claim. By any measure that counts the preference categories, the 1990 expansion was large, and the characterization of it as roughly forty percent captures the order of magnitude correctly.

The transitional regime adds a wrinkle to the measurement. For fiscal years 1992 through 1994, the worldwide level was 700,000, higher than the permanent 675,000 that followed. A measurement taken during the transition would show a larger increase than a measurement taken against the permanent regime. The difference reflects the transitional allocations for the spouses and children of those legalized in 1986 and for adversely affected countries, populations whose special claims justified temporarily higher numbers. The permanent regime, the system as Congress designed it to operate indefinitely, was the 675,000 figure, and the roughly-forty-percent characterization is best understood as describing the permanent expansion rather than the transitional peak.

The comparison to 1965, the largest increase since that year's Act, frames the expansion historically. The 1965 Act had repealed the national-origins quotas and established the preference system the 1990 Act later restructured. The 1986 Act had legalized millions and penalized employers but had not raised legal immigration levels in the structural sense. The 1990 Act was thus the first statute since 1965 to expand the legal immigration system as a system, raising its ceilings and redesigning its categories together. "Largest since 1965" is not merely a superlative. It is a periodization, placing the 1990 Act as the second great expansion of the postwar era and the hinge between the 1965 architecture and everything that followed.

There is a final reason the roughly-forty-percent figure endures as the summary of the Act, and it has to do with the difference between what a statute authorizes and what actually happens. The caps set the maximum for the preference system, but actual admissions in any year depended on demand, on agency processing capacity, and on the exempt categories flowing outside the caps. A scholar measuring the Act's effects empirically would count visas actually issued, compare them across years, and adjust for the transitional regime, producing a number that might differ from the 37 percent the Senate report derived from the statutory ceilings. Both measurements would be defensible, and they would answer different questions: one about what Congress authorized, the other about what the system delivered. The figure that survived in public discussion is the authorization measure, the roughly forty percent increase in the levels Congress wrote into the law. That is appropriate, because the 1990 Act's historical significance lies in what it enacted, the rebuilt machinery of legal admission, and the machinery is measured by its design rather than by any single year's throughput.

## What the Act left for later Congresses

A statute as ambitious as the 1990 Act invites the question of what it declined to do, and the omissions are as revealing as the enactments. The bill's official title promised amendments to the level and preference system "and for other purposes," and the other purposes, though real, stopped well short of a general immigration overhaul. The boundaries Congress drew around the reform shaped the agenda of every immigration debate that followed.

The Act did not create a points system. Proposals to allocate employment visas by a scored assessment of education, age, language, and experience were circulating in policy discussion, and other countries were experimenting with them, but section 121 retained the categorical queue: five preferences, ordered by type rather than scored by points. The choice reflected a legislative preference for categories Congress could name and defend, extraordinary ability, advanced degrees, skilled workers, over a formula whose weights would have been harder to negotiate. The categorical system was also easier to administer within the INA's existing adjudication machinery, which was built to decide whether an applicant fit a category, not to compute a score.

The Act did not abolish per-country limits within the preference system. The ceilings that rationed visas by nationality, a legacy of the 1965 framework, continued to operate inside the new worldwide limit, which meant that applicants from high-demand countries would wait in queues far longer than the category allocations suggested. The retention was a political necessity: removing per-country limits would have concentrated the expanded numbers in a few nationalities, alarming the very coalition the bill needed. But the retention stored up a structural tension, because the employment system's promise of merit-based speed depended on numbers flowing, and the per-country queues slowed them.

The Act did not address unauthorized immigration. The 1986 reform had combined legalization with employer sanctions, and the 1990 Act's drafters treated that settlement as closed, providing only the transitional 55,000 for legalization families rather than reopening the underlying questions. The omission was deliberate. Adding enforcement or legalization provisions to an expansion bill would have imported the 1986 debate's passions into a coalition built on agreement, and the bill's managers kept the vehicle clean. The consequence was a division of labor between the statutes: 1986 owned the unauthorized question, 1990 owned the legal system, and the boundary between them would blur in later decades as the legal system's backlogs pushed applicants toward unauthorized channels.

The Act did not restructure naturalization in any fundamental way, despite the official title's reference to administrative naturalization. The naturalization provisions streamlined procedures rather than reimagining citizenship, a modesty consistent with the bill's character as an amendment rather than a founding. The title's "other purposes" covered these administrative adjustments, and their quiet passage illustrated the omnibus dynamic: provisions without organized opposition travel with the vehicle, attracting neither debate nor memory.

Taken together, the omissions define the 1990 Act as a reform of the legal immigration system strictly conceived: its levels, its preferences, its diversity mechanisms, its humanitarian procedures, its exclusion grounds. Everything outside that perimeter, enforcement, unauthorized migration, the per-country queues' deeper logic, the points-system alternative, was left for later Congresses. The amendment stage does not finish the work; it advances it, and the 1990 amendments advanced the legal system further than any statute since 1965 while leaving the rest of the field exactly where they found it.

## The amendment stage of a statute's life

The thesis of this guide can now be stated in full. Statutes pass through stages, and the amendment stage has a distinctive pathology: the provisions that generate the loudest later argument are often the least noticed at enactment. The Immigration Act of 1990 is the outstanding American example. Its numerical restructuring was debated as numbers always are, but its three most consequential creations, the diversity lottery, temporary protected status, and the skilled-worker cap, entered the law with minimal public examination and went on to dominate the immigration argument for a generation. The pattern is not mysterious once it is seen clearly. Amendment statutes are technical, their effects phase in over fiscal years, and the press covers the drama of enactment rather than the machinery of implementation. By the time the machinery matters, the moment of democratic attention has passed.

Consider each of the three in turn. The diversity lottery emerged from a sequence of transitional programs that no one ever debated as a single proposition. NP-5 was a side provision of the 1986 reform law. OP-1 was a lottery experiment. AA-1 was a transitional bridge inside the 1990 Act. The permanent program was the cumulative result, and its 55,000 allocation became one of the most fought-over numbers in the immigration code. Temporary protected status replaced Extended Voluntary Departure with a statutory framework in a single section of a long bill, and almost no one outside the immigration bar noticed the substitution. Yet the framework gave the executive a defined tool for country-condition crises, and every later use of that tool reopened the questions the statute had settled. The H-1B cap was a single numerical line, 65,000 per year, attached to a classification definition. It became the valve through which the entire debate over skilled temporary migration flowed, because a concrete number can be fought over in a way that an abstract principle cannot.

The forgotten expansion: the largest increase in legal immigration since 1965 was enacted in 1990 with bipartisan majorities and a Republican signature, and the three features of the system most contested afterward were all created by that same law, which makes it the most consequential immigration statute that almost no one can name.

The claim is deliberately paradoxical, and the paradox is the point. A statute can be the most consequential of its kind precisely because it worked through amendment rather than proclamation, through numbers and categories rather than slogans. The 1990 Act did not announce a new philosophy of immigration. It rewrote the machinery, and the machinery turned out to matter more than the philosophy. The diversity lottery, temporary protected status, and the skilled-worker cap were not the products of a great national debate about what immigration should be. They were the products of transitional programs, executive-practice codification, and a single numerical line, assembled by legislators who understood the code they were amending and voted for by majorities who understood the bargain they were striking.

There is a further irony worth registering. The bipartisan majorities that enacted these provisions, 89 to 8 in the Senate and 264 to 118 in the House, with a Republican president's signature, meant that the later arguments about the three features were arguments about a bipartisan creation. The provisions were not imposed by one party on the other. They were the shared work of a legislative coalition that no longer exists in the same form, which may be one reason the statute itself faded from memory even as its creations remained at the center of argument. Statutes that pass with the votes of both parties enter the historical record without a partisan narrative to carry them. The great immigration statutes are remembered through the conflicts that produced them. The 1990 Act was produced by agreement, and agreement leaves fewer narrative traces. It is easier to fight over a lottery, a protection framework, and a cap than to remember the forgotten law that made all three, and the fighting has a way of erasing the parentage.

The fiscal-year phasing compounded the inattention. A statute whose core provisions take effect eleven months after signing, whose transitional regime runs for three years, and whose permanent diversity program waits until fiscal year 1995 does not produce a moment of visible change. There is no day on which the new system arrives. There is instead a slow infiltration, as agencies issue regulations, as visa bulletins reflect new allocations, as lawyers learn new categories and advise clients accordingly. By the time the system's effects are fully felt, the enactment is old news, and the connection between the statute and its consequences has faded from public memory. The amendment stage does not just write the future quietly. It writes it slowly, which is quieter.

The amendment stage of a statute's life deserves a place in how citizens understand lawmaking. Founding moments get the monuments. Amendments get the machinery, and the machinery governs. The Immigration Act of 1990 raised legal immigration by roughly forty percent, rebuilt the preference system, created the diversity lottery, established temporary protected status, capped the specialty-occupation visa, and rewrote the exclusion grounds, all inside a single statute that most Americans have never heard of. That is not a failure of the statute. It is a feature of the amendment stage, and recognizing the feature is the first step toward reading statutes as they actually work rather than as the headlines remember them.

## Conclusion

The Immigration Act of 1990, Public Law 101-649, signed November 29, 1990, did five things that together remade legal immigration to the United States. It restructured the numerical system around a 675,000 worldwide limit divided among family-sponsored, employment-based, and diversity allocations. It created five employment-based preference categories and capped the H-1B specialty-occupation visa at 65,000 per year. It made the diversity visa lottery permanent at 55,000 visas, converting transitional programs for specific nationalities into an ongoing program for low-admission-rate countries. It established temporary protected status as a statutory framework replacing ad hoc executive practice. And it rewrote the exclusion grounds, striking Cold War ideological exclusions and repealing the sexual-orientation ground. Each change is documented in the sections above, and the creation table maps them to their statutory sources.

The reader who has worked through this guide should now be able to explain the compact fact with which it began: three of the most argued-about features of the American immigration system, the diversity visa lottery, temporary protected status, and the capped skilled-worker visa, all originate in a single statute, signed by a Republican president in 1990, that raised legal immigration by roughly forty percent with bipartisan support. That explanation is the test of the guide, and it is also the guide's argument about how statutes work. The amendment stage writes the future quietly, and the future it wrote in 1990 is the system within which every subsequent immigration debate has been conducted.

A final word on method is appropriate, because the discipline observed throughout this guide is itself part of the argument. Every number in these pages comes from the enrolled text or the legislative record: the 675,000 worldwide limit, the 480,000 family-sponsored allocation with its 226,000 floor, the 140,000 employment-based allocation, the 55,000 diversity visas, the 65,000 H-1B cap, the 89 to 8 and 264 to 118 conference votes, the November 29, 1990, signature. Every section citation, from section 121's preference categories to section 302's protected-status framework to section 601's exclusion rewrite, is given as the statute gives it. Where the record did not support a detail, the detail was omitted rather than reconstructed. That restraint is not timidity. It is the only honest way to write about a statute whose story is precisely that its text did more than its contemporaries noticed, because noticing what the text did requires reading the text rather than the commentary.

The payoff of that discipline is the compact fact itself. A reader who can explain that the diversity lottery, temporary protected status, and the skilled-worker cap all originated in a single 1990 statute, signed by a Republican president, raising legal immigration by roughly forty percent with bipartisan support, possesses something that most commentary on immigration lacks: a correct account of where the system's most argued-about features came from. The features did not emerge from executive improvisation or from the slow drift of administrative practice. They were created by legislation, in specified sections, with specified numbers, on specified dates, by specified majorities. To know that is to understand the system as a made thing rather than a found thing, and to understand it as a made thing is the precondition for arguing about it intelligently.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Immigration Act of 1990 actually change?

The Act made five linked changes to the Immigration and Nationality Act. First, it replaced the old ceilings with a 675,000 worldwide limit divided into family-sponsored, employment-based, and diversity streams, raising legal immigration by roughly forty percent. Second, section 121 created five ranked employment-based preference categories, from extraordinary ability to investor, replacing the older employment preferences. Third, section 205 created the specialty-occupation H-1B classification and capped it at 65,000 per year. Fourth, the Act created the permanent diversity visa lottery for low-admission countries, effective in fiscal year 1995, growing out of transitional programs for adversely affected nationalities. Fifth, section 302 created temporary protected status, replacing ad hoc Extended Voluntary Departure with designation under statutory criteria. Finally, section 601 rewrote the exclusion grounds, repealing the Cold War ideological exclusions and the sexual orientation exclusion. President George H. W. Bush signed the measure on November 29, 1990, after the Senate passed the conference report 89 to 8 and the House 264 to 118.

### Q: Did the Immigration Act of 1990 create the diversity visa lottery?

Yes, but the creation had two stages, and the distinction matters. The Act's section 132 created the transitional AA-1 program, known as the Morrison visas, which ran for fiscal years 1992 through 1994 at 40,000 visas per year, with at least forty percent earmarked for natives of Ireland. The permanent diversity visa lottery took effect in fiscal year 1995 with an allocation of 55,000 visas per year for natives of countries with low admission rates. The permanent program generalized the transitional concept of adversely affected countries into a standing rule. Its ancestry runs through two earlier programs as well: NP-5, created by the 1986 reform for fiscal years 1987 through 1990, and the OP-1 lottery of 1989 through 1991. A common error dates the 55,000 allocation to fiscal year 1992; it began in fiscal year 1995.

### Q: Did the Immigration Act of 1990 create temporary protected status?

Yes. Section 302 of the Act created temporary protected status as a statutory framework, codified at INA section 244 and 8 U.S.C. 1254a. Under the framework, the Attorney General could designate a country when ongoing armed conflict, environmental disaster, or extraordinary temporary conditions made return unsafe for its nationals, with designation authority stated in section 244(b)(1). The program replaced Extended Voluntary Departure, the ad hoc executive practice that had provided similar relief for decades without statutory standards. The Ninth Circuit later described the change exactly this way in Ramos v. Wolf, writing that Congress created TPS in 1990 to establish formal criteria and procedures to replace EVD. Designation authority moved to the Secretary of Homeland Security in 2003. The framework was temporary by design, with time-limited designations subject to periodic review.

### Q: What are the employment based categories from the Immigration Act of 1990?

Section 121 of the Act created five employment-based preference categories in INA section 203(b). EB-1 covers persons of extraordinary ability, outstanding professors and researchers, and multinational executives and managers. EB-2 covers members of the professions holding advanced degrees and persons of exceptional ability. EB-3 covers skilled workers, professionals, and other workers, including a subcategory for shortage occupations. EB-4 covers certain special immigrants given consolidated handling within the ranked system. EB-5, created by section 121(a) and (b), covers immigrant investors whose capital creates employment in the United States. The categories draw on a 140,000 annual employment-based allocation, with unused family-sponsored numbers available under the statute's spillover rules. The five-category ranking replaced the older employment preferences with a queue ordered from demonstrated distinction to investment.

### Q: Did the Immigration Act of 1990 create the H-1B cap?

Yes. Section 205 of the Act created the specialty-occupation H-1B framework, with the classification at INA section 101(a)(15)(H)(i)(b) and the numerical cap at INA section 214(g)(1)(A), codified at 8 U.S.C. 1184(g)(1)(A). The initial cap was 65,000 per year. Before 1990, the H-1 category had existed without this architecture; the Act defined specialty occupation as a distinct classification and then placed a finite annual number on it. The pairing was deliberate: Congress expanded permanent employment-based immigration to 140,000 visas across five ranked categories while drawing a bright line around the principal temporary specialty-occupation channel. The cap-and-classification framework entered the INA as the lasting structure for specialty-occupation temporary workers, and it became one of the three 1990 creations most argued about in later decades.

### Q: Did the Immigration Act of 1990 raise legal immigration levels?

Yes, by roughly forty percent, the largest legislated increase since 1965. The Act replaced the old ceilings with a worldwide limit of 675,000 per year for fiscal year 1995 and after, divided into 480,000 for family-sponsored immigration, 140,000 for employment-based immigration, and 55,000 for diversity immigration. The figure is an approximation of the preference-system expansion, not a quoted statistic, and it sits alongside two qualifications. First, the caps were permeable: immediate relatives of United States citizens, refugees, and asylees stood outside the worldwide limit. Second, fiscal years 1992 through 1994 used a transitional level of 700,000, including dedicated numbers for spouses and children of persons legalized under the 1986 reform. Congress also set a 226,000 floor under the family-sponsored preference categories.

### Q: What is the EB-5 investor visa from the Immigration Act of 1990?

EB-5 is the fifth employment-based preference category, created by section 121(a) and (b) of the Act within INA section 203(b). It makes permanent residence available to immigrant investors whose capital creates employment in the United States, conditioning the immigration benefit on investment and job creation rather than on family relationship, professional credentials, or demonstrated distinction. The category was the most novel of the five, because it introduced an economic development instrument into the preference system: the statute traded admission for capital formation. EB-5 draws its numbers from the same 140,000 annual employment-based allocation as the other four categories, ranking last in the queue the Act established. Its creation reflected a judgment that the immigration system could serve investment policy directly, a departure from the family and labor-market logics that organized the rest of the 1990 preferences.

### Q: Which exclusions did the Immigration Act of 1990 repeal?

Section 601, comprising Title VI of the Act, repealed two sets of exclusion grounds. First, it struck former INA section 212(a)(28), the Cold War ideological exclusions based on Communist Party membership, affiliation with totalitarian organizations, and advocacy of proscribed doctrines. In their place it built a new INA section 212(a)(3) consolidating security grounds around terrorist activity, actions with serious adverse foreign policy consequences, totalitarian-party membership recast inside a security framework, and Nazi persecution or genocide. Second, it repealed former INA section 212(a)(4), which had excluded persons on the basis of "psychopathic personality, sexual deviation," language long construed to cover homosexuality. The Supreme Court's decision upholding that construction, Boutilier v. INS, 387 U.S. 118 (1967), is recorded as abrogated by the Act. The rewrite moved the statute from belief-based to conduct-based security screening.

### Q: Why did the 1990 law create a separate diversity category instead of expanding existing preferences?

The existing preferences could not solve the problem Congress had identified, because the problem was structural to the preference system itself. The post-1965 preferences favored applicants with close relatives already in the United States, which meant nationalities without an established diaspora could not begin immigrating no matter how the existing categories were enlarged. Expanding the family or employment preferences would have deepened the flows that already existed rather than opening new ones. A separate diversity category, allocated by nativity in low-admission countries rather than by kinship or credentials, bypassed the chain logic entirely. The transitional programs had already proven the mechanism: NP-5, OP-1, and AA-1 showed that lotteries for adversely affected nationalities could operate at scale. The permanent category generalized that proof into a standing rule.

### Q: How did the NP-5, OP-1, and AA-1 programs lead to the permanent diversity lottery?

The three programs formed a sequence of experiments that taught Congress the lottery mechanism worked. NP-5, created by section 314 of the 1986 reform, offered 5,000 visas per year for fiscal years 1987 and 1988 to thirty-six adversely affected countries, extended at 15,000 per year for fiscal years 1989 and 1990, with Ireland taking about thirty-one percent. OP-1, the Berman visas, ran a lottery from 1989 through 1991. AA-1, the Morrison visas created by section 132 of the 1990 Act, ran at 40,000 per year for fiscal years 1992 through 1994 with at least forty percent earmarked for Ireland. Each program normalized the idea of allocating visas by lottery to nationalities the preference system had shortchanged. The permanent diversity category, effective in fiscal year 1995 at 55,000 per year, converted the nationality-specific corrective into a general rule for low-admission countries.

### Q: What did temporary protected status replace?

Temporary protected status replaced Extended Voluntary Departure, the ad hoc executive practice under which presidents had sheltered particular nationalities from removal during crises abroad. EVD had no statutory standards, no published criteria, and no regular procedure; each grant was an act of executive discretion. Section 302 of the 1990 Act substituted a statutory framework: designation by the Attorney General under INA section 244(b)(1) when ongoing armed conflict, environmental disaster, or extraordinary temporary conditions made return unsafe, with defined procedures and a clear legal status for beneficiaries. The Ninth Circuit captured the substitution in Ramos v. Wolf, writing that Congress created TPS in 1990 to establish formal criteria and procedures to replace EVD, a practice the executive branch had used for decades on an ad hoc basis. The protection resembled what EVD had offered; the legal form was new.

### Q: How was the 675,000 worldwide limit divided among family, employment, and diversity immigration?

The 675,000 figure, which governed fiscal year 1995 and after, was the sum of three streams: 480,000 for family-sponsored immigration, 140,000 for employment-based immigration, and 55,000 for diversity immigration. The streams interacted through spillover rules. The family-sponsored allocation was defined as 480,000 minus the prior year's immediate relatives and parolees, plus unused employment-based numbers, while unused family numbers could flow to the employment side. Congress also set a 226,000 floor under the family-sponsored preference categories, guaranteeing that the formula's subtractions could not shrink family preference immigration below that level. The limit was permeable: immediate relatives of United States citizens, refugees, and asylees stood outside it, so total legal immigration exceeded 675,000 by the size of those exempt flows.

### Q: What was the 226,000 floor in the family-sponsored system?

The 226,000 floor was a statutory guarantee that the family-sponsored preference categories would receive at least 226,000 visas per year regardless of how the worldwide limit formula operated. Because the family-sponsored allocation was defined as 480,000 minus prior-year immediate relatives and parolees plus unused employment numbers, the arithmetic could in theory have squeezed the preference categories; the floor prevented that outcome. It reflected the political economy of the bill: family immigration had the deepest organized support in the legislative coalition, and its defenders insisted on a number that implementation could not bargain away. The floor applied to the preference categories specifically, not to the exempt immediate relatives of citizens, and it held steady through the fiscal year 1992 through 1994 transition into the permanent regime.

### Q: What transitional immigration levels applied in fiscal years 1992 through 1994?

Congress set a transitional worldwide level of 700,000 for fiscal years 1992 through 1994, higher than the permanent 675,000 that followed, because the old system's backlogs and the 1986 legalization's family consequences needed dedicated capacity. The transitional 700,000 was composed of 465,000 for family-sponsored immigration, 55,000 for the spouses and children of aliens legalized under the 1986 reform, 140,000 for employment-based immigration, and 40,000 for the AA-1 transitional diversity program for adversely affected countries. The 55,000 for legalization families addressed a direct consequence of the earlier reform: newly legalized residents whose close relatives were pressing against a family system not built to absorb them. When the transition expired, the fiscal year 1995 regime of 675,000 across the three permanent streams took over.

### Q: When did the Immigration Act of 1990 take effect?

The core amendments took effect on October 1, 1991, applying to immigration beginning in fiscal year 1992, under section 161(a) of the Act. The year between signing on November 29, 1990, and the effective date gave the Immigration and Naturalization Service time to write regulations, redesign forms, retrain adjudicators, and reprogram visa allocation for the new worldwide limit and preference categories. Some provisions took effect on enactment, but the numerical restructuring and the preference changes belonged to the October 1991 date. Implementation then proceeded in stages: fiscal year 1992 brought the new categories and the transitional 700,000 level, while the permanent regime, the 675,000 worldwide limit and the permanent diversity category at 55,000 per year, began in fiscal year 1995. The staggered schedule was a judgment about administrative capacity as much as about policy.

### Q: Who sponsored S. 358 and what were the final congressional votes?

Senator Edward M. Kennedy of Massachusetts sponsored S. 358, introducing it on February 7, 1989, in the 101st Congress. The bill's official title described its program: amending the Immigration and Nationality Act to change the level and preference system for admission of immigrants, to provide for administrative naturalization, and for other purposes. The votes that enacted the law were the conference report votes, not the earlier passage tallies. The Senate adopted the conference report on October 26, 1990, by 89 to 8, recorded as Record Vote Number 323, with three senators not voting. The House adopted it on October 27, 1990, by 264 to 118, recorded as Roll Number 530, with fifty members not voting. President George H. W. Bush signed the enrolled bill, headed 104 STAT. 4978, on November 29, 1990.

### Q: How did the 1990 Act restructure temporary specialty-occupation workers?

Before 1990, the H-1 category existed without a dedicated specialty-occupation architecture or a numerical cap. Section 205 of the Act created both: the specialty-occupation classification at INA section 101(a)(15)(H)(i)(b), defining the category of temporary workers in occupations requiring specialized knowledge, and the annual cap at INA section 214(g)(1)(A), codified at 8 U.S.C. 1184(g)(1)(A), set initially at 65,000 per year. The restructuring separated temporary skilled admission from the permanent employment-based system that section 121 was building at the same time, giving each its own logic: generous ranked permanent categories funded by a 140,000 allocation on one side, and a finite, measurable temporary channel on the other. The cap made the temporary program legible as a number Congress could debate, which is one reason it became a durable focal point of later argument.

### Q: What did the NACARA law change about the diversity visa allocation?

The Nicaraguan Adjustment and Central American Relief Act, Public Law 105-100, enacted in 1997, reduced the 55,000 diversity visa ceiling by up to 5,000 visas annually beginning in fiscal year 1999. The reduction offset relief provisions for Central American and certain other applicants, treating the diversity allocation as a budget of visas available for reprogramming. The change is notable for what it reveals about the program's institutionalization: within a decade of its creation, the lottery Congress had built as a transitional corrective turned permanent category was being drawn down to fund unrelated relief, a sign of how thoroughly it had been absorbed into the system's arithmetic. The permanent category itself, effective in fiscal year 1995 for low-admission countries, continued; only the ceiling was trimmed. The episode belongs to the later trajectory of the 1990 creation, not to its enactment history.

### Q: Why was the exclusion for sexual orientation repealed?

Former INA section 212(a)(4) excluded aliens afflicted with "psychopathic personality, sexual deviation," language the immigration authorities had long construed to cover homosexuality, a construction the Supreme Court upheld in Boutilier v. INS, 387 U.S. 118 (1967). Section 601 of the 1990 Act repealed the ground as part of its Title VI rewrite of the exclusion provisions, removing sexual orientation from the statute's list of excludable conditions entirely. The repeal ended the use of a medicalized euphemism to police private consensual conduct at the border. Boutilier is recorded as abrogated by the Act, a formal acknowledgment that the decision's statutory foundation no longer existed. The change aligned the immigration code with a social consensus that had moved decisively since the ground was written, and it formed the second half of the exclusion rewrite alongside the repeal of the ideological grounds.

### Q: What happened to the Cold War ideological exclusions?

Section 601 of the Act, comprising Title VI, struck former INA section 212(a)(28), the provisions that had excluded aliens for membership in Communist and other totalitarian parties and for advocacy of doctrines of world communism. Those grounds had barred writers, artists, and political figures on the basis of belief and association, generating decades of controversy over viewpoint-based exclusion. The Act replaced them with a new INA section 212(a)(3) that consolidated security grounds in narrower, conduct-focused terms: terrorist activity, actions likely to have serious adverse foreign policy consequences, totalitarian-party membership recast within a security framework, and participation in Nazi persecution or genocide. The shift from what an alien thought to what an alien had done was the essence of the change. Enacted as the Cold War was ending, the repeal ratified a changed geopolitical reality in statutory text.
