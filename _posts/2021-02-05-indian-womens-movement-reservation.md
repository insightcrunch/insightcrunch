The Indian women's movement is usually introduced as a story of awakening, and the introduction does quiet damage to the record. Read against its own documents, the movement that runs from the Towards Equality Report of the Committee on the Status of Women in India to the Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act of 2023 behaves less like a rising consciousness and more like a sequence of formal renegotiations between women citizens and the Indian state, each conducted through a dated instrument: a committee report tabled in Parliament in 1975, a Supreme Court acquittal answered by an open letter and a statute, a set of judicial guidelines that governed workplaces for sixteen years, a pair of constitutional amendments that reserved a third of local office, and an amendment that wrote the same fraction into the Lok Sabha and the state assemblies. The claim defended here is that the Indian women's movement operates as citizen-renegotiation culminating in the 2023 Nari Shakti reservation, and that a five-feature framework captures what rival readings leave out.

## The Indian women's movement as citizen-renegotiation: the claim and its rivals

Two established readings divide the field between them. The feminist-history reading, carried in Geraldine Forbes's Women in Modern India (1996) and in Radha Kumar's The History of Doing (1993), treats the movement as a lineage of organised women: the suffrage campaigns of the 1920s, the All India Women's Conference founded in 1927, the post-Emergency resurgence of autonomous groups after 1977. Its unit of analysis is the organisation and its reward is continuity, a single thread of agency running from the colonial period to the republic. The gender-policy reading, carried in the policy literature that Mary E. John assembled in Women's Studies in India: A Reader (2008), treats the movement as an input into state machinery: committee, commission, plan chapter, statute. Its unit of analysis is the policy instrument and its reward is administrative legibility, a clean line from recommendation to enactment.

Both readings describe real things, and both miss the same transaction. A committee report is not only a policy input; when the Committee on the Status of Women in India reported in December 1974 that the sex ratio had fallen from 972 females per 1,000 males in 1901 to 930 in 1971, the committee was restating the terms on which half the citizenry belonged to the republic, and it was doing so from inside the state that had appointed it. A Supreme Court acquittal is not only a legal event; when the Court held on 15 September 1978 that Mathura's passive submission could not ground a conviction, four law teachers answered the bench in an open letter that treated the judgment as a failed act of membership, a refusal by one institution of the state to recognise a citizen's standing against another institution of the same state. The renegotiation frame names this recurring form: women, organised or unorganised, compelling the state to rewrite the recorded terms of their citizenship, and the state answering in text that outlasts the campaign. This article builds on [the movements-as-citizen-renegotiation framework](/2021/01/09/indian-movements-citizen-renegotiation/) and tests it against the densest documentary sequence any Indian movement has left behind.

### Why does the argument begin with a committee report rather than a street protest?

Because the renegotiation this article tracks was conducted in instruments before it was conducted in streets. The Committee on the Status of Women in India was appointed in September 1971, reported in December 1974, and set the evidentiary terms on which every later campaign argued. Its findings supplied the movement's shared record of fact.

The five features are dated and documentary. Feature One is the Towards Equality Report itself, submitted in December 1974 and tabled in Parliament in 1975, which converted the condition of women from a welfare topic into a constitutional audit. Feature Two is the Mathura sequence: the incident at Desai Ganj police station on the night of 26 March 1972, the sessions court's acquittal, the Bombay High Court's reversal of 12 October 1976, the Supreme Court's acquittal of 15 September 1978, the Open Letter of September 1979, and the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 1983 that rewrote the law of custodial rape. Feature Three is Vishaka and Other v State of Rajasthan, decided on 13 August 1997, in which a bench led by Chief Justice J.S. Verma converted a gang rape in a Rajasthan village into binding workplace law. Feature Four is the panchayat settlement: the Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act in force from 24 April 1993 and the Seventy-fourth in force from 1 June 1993, which reserved not less than one-third of seats and chairperson offices in local bodies for women. Feature Five is the Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act of 2023, the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, assented to on 28 September 2023, which reserved one-third of Lok Sabha and state assembly seats for women while deferring operation to a census and a delimitation still to come.

Each feature is an instance of the same grammar. A grievance that private ordering could not settle was carried to an institution; the institution answered in a text; the text changed the standing of women as citizens in a way later actors could cite, enforce and extend. The feminist-history reading sees the carriers and the gender-policy reading sees the texts. The citizen-renegotiation reading sees the exchange between them, and it explains a pattern neither rival explains: why the movement's largest victories arrived as amendments, guidelines and statutory presumptions rather than as electoral verdicts, and why its defeats, above all the twenty-seven-year failure of the Women's Reservation Bill between 1996 and 2023, were failures of one particular institution, Parliament, to complete an exchange that other institutions had already modelled. The balance of this article walks the five features in order, gives the strongest form of the objections at each step, and then adjudicates the three readings against the full sequence.

The scholarly disagreement is therefore genuine and worth deciding. Forbes's lineage reading predicts continuity of organisation; Kumar's history, for all its archival richness, ends its main narrative in the early 1990s and treats the state largely as antagonist. The policy reading predicts incremental administrative absorption; it cannot explain why the absorption took the jagged form of a report shelved for a decade, an acquittal that detonated a national campaign, and a bill that one House passed in 2010 and the other never voted on. The renegotiation reading predicts exactly that jaggedness, because renegotiation is episodic by nature: it moves when an institution can be made to answer, and it stalls where the answering institution holds an absolute veto, as the Lok Sabha held over the reservation bill for a generation. What follows tests that prediction feature by feature.

## Feature One: a committee the state appointed against itself, 1971 to 1975

The Committee on the Status of Women in India was constituted in September 1971 by the Department of Social Welfare, in a year when the United Nations was pressing member states to prepare for International Women's Year in 1975. Its chairperson was Phulrenu Guha, a former Union minister; its member-secretary was Vina Mazumdar, a political scientist whose research secretariat drove the inquiry; its members included the constitutional lawyer Lotika Sarkar, the trade unionist Maniben Kara and the educationist M.K. Indira. The committee travelled, took evidence and commissioned studies for three years, and it submitted its report, Towards Equality, in December 1974. Parliament received it in 1975, in the opening months of the same political season that produced [the 1975 to 1977 Emergency's political context](/2019/04/06/privy-purse-abolition-1971-india/), and the Emergency swallowed its reception whole. The report's public life began late, in photocopies and seminar rooms, but its terms governed everything the movement later demanded.

The committee's central finding was a paradox it stated without ornament: the Constitution's promises of equality had been followed, for most women, by measurable decline. The sex ratio, the committee recorded, had fallen from 972 females per 1,000 males in the census of 1901 to 930 in the census of 1971, a descent across seven decades of franchise extension, planned development and constitutional guarantee. The economic record ran the same way. The censuses the committee reviewed showed women's recorded work participation shrinking across the century, with the 1971 census returning a female work participation rate of about twelve percent, and the committee's studies found that the overwhelming bulk of women who did work, around ninety-four percent on the estimates it assembled, worked in the unorganised sector, outside the reach of labour law, maternity benefit and union protection. Political participation told the same story in a third register: the First Lok Sabha of 1952 had seated twenty-two women among 489 members, and two decades of universal franchise had raised the number only marginally, because parties would not nominate women for winnable seats.

These findings mattered because of who issued them. A movement pamphlet alleging decline could be dismissed as rhetoric; a committee of the Government of India, chaired by a former minister and staffed by the state's own researchers, alleging decline on census evidence converted the allegation into the state's own record. Lotika Sarkar and Vina Mazumdar, in the committee's internal correspondence preserved in the archives of the Centre for Women's Development Studies in New Delhi, argued over how far the report should go on the most charged question before it: whether to demand reserved seats. The committee's answer was split in a way that shaped the next fifty years. It recommended statutory reservation for women in local bodies, in panchayats and municipalities, where it judged the training ground argument strongest. On legislatures it withheld the demand, a majority holding that reservation in Parliament and the assemblies carried risks of tokenism that the women's organisations of the day had not settled among themselves. Feature Four of this article is that withheld half of the recommendation returning eighteen years later; Feature Five is the other half, the legislative demand the committee declined to make in 1974 and the movement spent until 2023 securing.

The report's recommendations began entering law within two years of its tabling. The Equal Remuneration Act of 1976, which wrote the constitutional directive of equal pay for equal work into an enforceable statute covering recruitment as well as wages, carried the committee's signature demand that women's work be priced as work rather than as supplement. The committee had also pressed for the expansion of maternity protection and for creche provision in establishments employing women, items that moved through amendment politics across the following decade rather than in a single season, which is the normal tempo of this field and a datum the framework records: a report renegotiates first the items whose price the answering institution finds small, equal pay in a statute book costing less than reserved seats in a legislature, and banks them as precedent for the dearer items to come.

The report also built machinery. It recommended a standing national apparatus for women, a lineage that runs through the women's chapter that entered the Sixth Five Year Plan for 1980 to 1985, the first plan to treat women as a development sector rather than a welfare residue, to the National Commission for Women Act of 1990 and the Commission's constitution in January 1992 under its first chairperson, Jayanti Patnaik. Mazumdar herself carried the committee's research programme into the Indian Council of Social Science Research's women's studies programme and then into the Centre for Women's Development Studies, founded in New Delhi in 1980, which kept the report's archive and its method. The method is the feature. Towards Equality established that the condition of women in India would be argued on the state's own numbers, in the state's own forums, against the state's own constitutional text. Every later feature in this article repeats that method: the Open Letter cites the Supreme Court's own headnote back to the Court; Vishaka cites the Constitution's own articles and a ratified treaty back to the executive; the reservation amendments cite the equality code back to a Parliament that had postponed honouring it.

The strongest objection to treating the report as the movement's first feature is Forbes's: that it privileges a state document over the organised women who preceded it, the suffrage campaigners who won the vote province by province between 1921 and 1929 and the Constituent Assembly's fifteen women members who shaped the equality articles themselves. The objection is fair as history and it is met on the record: this article's claim is not that the movement began in 1971 but that its post-1975 form, the form that produced statutes and amendments, began when a committee gave it a citable baseline. The Hindu Code battles of 1947 to 1956, in which women's organisations lobbied constituency by constituency for the reforms carried in [the Hindu Code Bill's long parliamentary passage](/2018/12/12/hindu-code-bill-politics-1947-1956/), show the earlier form at full strength: pressure on legislators, answered in law. Towards Equality changed the pressure's paperwork. After 1975, the movement argued from an official audit its opponents had co-signed, and the audit's bleakest table, the falling sex ratio, could not be unread.

## The inheritance the committee received: suffrage, the Assembly, and a declined demand

The committee of 1971 worked downstream of an earlier settlement whose terms explain its hesitations. The organised demand for women's political standing in India opened with the Women's Indian Association, founded at Adyar in 1917 by Annie Besant, Margaret Cousins and Dorothy Jinarajadasa among others, which that same year sent a delegation, with Sarojini Naidu as its most prominent voice, to the Secretary of State Edwin Montagu to demand the franchise on the same terms as men. The demand was won province by province across the following decade: Madras removed the sex disqualification for its legislative council in 1921, the first province to do so, and the other provinces followed through the 1920s, each victory a resolution of a provincial legislature rather than a grant from London. The Government of India Act of 1935 widened the female electorate and reserved seats for women in the provincial legislatures, and at that point the movement's own leadership divided over the device that dominates this article. Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, the most formidable organiser of the All India Women's Conference generation, opposed reserved seats and separate electorates for women on principle, holding that a sex-based allocation contradicted the equality the movement existed to claim; other leaders accepted the 1935 reservations as scaffolding. The division of 1935, between an equality that refuses classification and an equality that uses classification to arrive, is the same division that resurfaced in the committee room of 1974 and on the floor of the Lok Sabha in 1996.

The provincial elections of 1937, held under the 1935 Act with an electorate that included roughly six million women voters, seated women in the provincial legislatures in numbers the councils of the 1920s had never carried, and produced the republic's first rehearsal of a woman in executive office: Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit took charge of local self-government and public health in the Congress ministry of the United Provinces in 1937, the first woman to hold cabinet rank in India. The rehearsal matters to this framework because it fixes the starting ratio the later renegotiation inherited. When the Constitution commenced in 1950, women had already voted for a generation, legislated for thirteen years and held a portfolio, and their share of the first Lok Sabha still stood at twenty-two seats in 489. The founders could therefore treat identical citizenship as a completed gift rather than a pending one, and the post-1975 movement's documentary labour consisted, in large part, of demonstrating from the record that the gift's delivery had stalled at the level of form. Towards Equality's bleakest tables are precisely that demonstration, which is why the movement kept returning to the census rather than to the platform.

The Constituent Assembly carried the refusal further, and its doing so is the founding fact the renegotiation had to overcome. Fifteen women sat in the Assembly, among them Sarojini Naidu, Hansa Mehta, Durgabai Deshmukh, Rajkumari Amrit Kaur, Sucheta Kripalani and Dakshayani Velayudhan, the last the only Dalit woman in the chamber and a persistent voice against treating any section of the citizenry as a protected enclosure. The women members spoke for the equality articles and against special legislative reservation for their own sex, and the Constitution as adopted reserved seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in Articles 330 and 332 while reserving none for women. Hansa Mehta carried the same position to the United Nations, where her insistence in the drafting of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights helped replace the phrase all men with all human beings in its first article. The founders' settlement, stated fairly, was that women's citizenship would be identical citizenship, won by identical text, and that classification by sex belonged to the colonial grammar of separate electorates the republic was abolishing. The post-1975 movement's reservation demand is therefore not the founders' programme resumed; it is the founders' programme reversed on evidence the founders did not live to see tabulated, the Towards Equality tables showing what twenty-five years of identical citizenship had delivered in sex ratio, in work and in seats. The reversal is the renegotiation's opening move, and the movement's scholars have never hidden it: Kumar's history records the early movement's suspicion of reservation, and the later movement answered the suspicion not by denying it but by out-arguing it on the census. Feature One's committee, recommending reservation for local bodies while withholding it for legislatures, stands exactly on the seam between the founders' settlement and its reversal, which is one more reason the report, and not any earlier text, is where this framework begins.

## Feature Two: Mathura, the open letter, and the statute that answered it, 1972 to 1983

On the night of 26 March 1972, Mathura, a tribal girl of about fourteen to sixteen years, was at the Desai Ganj police station in Maharashtra because her brother Gama had reported that she had been kidnapped by the household she worked in and by Ashok, the young man she intended to marry. Statements were recorded; the party was told to leave at about half past ten. Head Constable Tukaram and Constable Ganpat kept her back. The prosecution case, recorded in the judgment that bears the constables' names, was that Ganpat raped her in the station latrine and Tukaram assaulted her there as well. The sessions court acquitted both men, reasoning that a girl the medical evidence showed to be habituated to intercourse, who bore no injuries and raised no alarm the court would credit, had consented. The Bombay High Court, on 12 October 1976, reversed the acquittal entirely: the High Court held that submission extracted inside a police station, to men in uniform who were strangers to her, could not be dressed up as consent, and it sentenced Ganpat to five years of rigorous imprisonment and Tukaram to one year.

The Supreme Court took the acquittal back. On 15 September 1978, a bench of Justices Jaswant Singh, P.S. Kailasam and A.D. Koshal allowed the constables' appeal in Tuka Ram and Another v State of Maharashtra, reported at 1979 AIR 185. The judgment reasoned that Mathura's conduct amounted at most to passive submission, that the prosecution had not proved she was put in fear of death or hurt within the meaning the Penal Code then gave, and that the absence of injuries and of any outcry told against her account. The distinction the bench drew, between consent and helpless submission under duress, dissolved on inspection: a detainee's submission to the police who hold her is precisely the condition the law of consent exists to test. The judgment attracted little notice for a year. Then Upendra Baxi of the University of Delhi, reading the reported volume, wrote to the Chief Justice of India, Y.V. Chandrachud, and three colleagues signed with him: Lotika Sarkar of Delhi, Vasudha Dhagamwar of Pune and Raghunath Kelkar of Pune. The Open Letter of September 1979 dissected the judgment line by line and asked whether the Court's consent doctrine had any room left in it for a citizen held in a police station at night.

### Why did four law teachers write to the Chief Justice instead of filing a case?

Because the ordinary routes were closed. The acquittal was final, the state had not sought review, and no fresh proceeding could reopen Mathura's own case without cruelty to her. An open letter addressed the judgment as public doctrine rather than as one woman's litigation, and it forced the profession and Parliament to argue in the open.

The letter worked as the committee report had worked, as an instrument. It was reprinted, debated in law faculties and taken up by the women's organisations that the late 1970s had produced in Bombay, Delhi and other cities; the Forum Against Rape, formed in Bombay in 1980 and renamed the Forum Against Oppression of Women later in the decade, coordinated a national campaign whose demands were legislative and precise. The campaign did not stand on Mathura alone. The Rameeza Bee case in Hyderabad in 1978 and the Maya Tyagi case in Baghpat in 1980, each an alleged custodial rape followed by official protection of the accused, gave the campaign a pattern to cite: the police station as a site where the state's custodian stood outside the state's law. The demand that followed was architectural rather than rhetorical. The campaigners wanted custodial rape named as its own aggravated offence, the burden on consent reversed where the state held the keys, trials taken out of the public gaze that shamed complainants, and the complainant's name taken out of print.

Parliament answered in 1983 in terms the campaign could recognise as its own draft. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 1983 created the custodial categories of rape in Section 376(2) of the Penal Code, covering rape by a police officer within the limits of his station, by a public servant abusing his position, and by staff of jails, remand homes and hospitals, and it attached a minimum sentence of ten years to those categories. The same Act inserted Section 114A into the Indian Evidence Act: in a prosecution for custodial rape, where the woman states in her evidence that she did not consent, the court shall presume that she did not consent, and the burden of proving consent moves to the accused. The same Act amended Section 327 of the Code of Criminal Procedure to take rape trials in camera and added Section 228A to the Penal Code, making disclosure of a rape complainant's identity an offence. Each clause is a clause of the Open Letter and the Forum's charter translated into the state's text. The acquittal of two constables had been answered by a statute that redefined what the state would presume about every custodian who followed them.

The settlement's limits are part of the record and were named by its own architects at the time. The 1983 Act left the definition of consent itself largely where the 1978 judgment had found it; it did not touch the marital rape exception; it addressed the courtroom's treatment of a complainant's past only partially, and the fuller bar on character cross-examination arrived only in later amendments. Feminist legal scholars, Flavia Agnes among them in her writings on the anti-rape campaign, recorded the movement's own verdict that 1983 was a partial capture: the presumption and the custodial category were real, and the surrounding doctrine of consent remained available to acquit in the ordinary case argued before an ordinary bench. Mathura herself, traced by journalists over the following decades, kept a documented distance from the emblem the campaign had made of her name, a fact the movement's honest historians record without flinching. The renegotiation frame absorbs both halves of this. The exchange of 1979 to 1983 was a genuine rewriting of the terms, done in statute, and it was incomplete in ways its own drafters itemised; the itemisation became part of the brief the movement carried into the next feature.

## The movement between landmarks: organisation and the widening brief, 1972 to 1995

The five features are peaks, and a framework that showed only peaks would invite the lineage objection it answers. The decades between the landmarks were filled with organisation of kinds the features presuppose, and three currents of it matter to the argument. The first is the mass politics of the early 1970s, in which women appeared in numbers before the vocabulary of the later movement existed. In the Shahada agitation in Dhule district of Maharashtra, from 1971, Bhil Adivasi labourers organised in the Shramik Sanghatana against landlord coercion, and women stood in the front of that agitation against two targets at once, the landlords' violence and the liquor trade that drained labourers' wages. In Bombay in 1973 and 1974, the United Women's Anti-Price-Rise Front, associated with Mrinal Gore and Pramila Dandavate, marched housewives with rolling pins against the cost of food and kerosene, a politics of the household budget carried into the street by women who did not yet call it feminism. Radha Kumar's history treats these currents as the bridge between the older women's organisations and the autonomous groups; the renegotiation reading treats them as the constituency being assembled, the women who would staff, sign for and vote around the texts to come.

The second current is institutional invention outside the state. In Ahmedabad in 1972, Ela Bhatt founded the Self-Employed Women's Association as a trade union of women who had no employer to bargain with, the head-loaders, vendors, cart-pullers and home-based piece workers whom the Towards Equality researchers would count two years later as the ninety-four percent in the unorganised sector. The association opened its own cooperative bank in 1974. The sequence matters to the framework: the committee of 1974 did not discover the unorganised woman worker in the abstract; organisations like Bhatt's were already making her visible as a collective actor with accounts, dues and demands, and the report's finding reads in part as the state catching up with an organisation's prior work. Renegotiation, in this record, is never a conversation the state holds with itself.

The third current is the legislative campaigning of the 1980s, which widened the brief from representation to the criminal law's daily operation and taught the movement the presumption device that Feature Two had pioneered. The dowry campaigns in Delhi, led by organisations including the Mahila Dakshata Samiti and Stri Sangharsh, counted deaths the police recorded as kitchen accidents and forced the category of dowry murder into public language; Parliament answered in stages that track Feature Two's architecture exactly, inserting Section 498A into the Penal Code in 1983 to make cruelty by a husband or his relatives a cognisable offence, strengthening the Dowry Prohibition Act in 1984 and 1986, adding the offence of dowry death as Section 304B in 1986, and writing evidentiary presumptions into Sections 113A and 113B of the Evidence Act so that a death within seven years of marriage, preceded by dowry cruelty, would be presumed against the household rather than explained by it. The sati of Roop Kanwar at Deorala in Rajasthan on 4 September 1987 produced the same grammar in a single season: a campaign against the glorification ceremonies that followed the death, and the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act of 1987, which criminalised glorification itself. The decade also carried the framework's standing warning, in the maintenance controversy that followed the Supreme Court's Shah Bano judgment of April 1985 and the statute of 1986 that displaced it: an exchange can be reversed when a government prices a different constituency's objection above the text already won, and the movement's historians record 1986 as the decade's defeat in the same ledger as its statutes. The ratchet described in the five-feature map is a ratchet of the features, not a law of the field, and the distinction keeps the framework honest.

By the middle 1990s the brief had widened past any single ministry's remit. The Fourth World Conference on Women at Beijing in September 1995, to which India sent an official delegation alongside a large non-governmental contingent, fed into the drafting that produced the National Policy for the Empowerment of Women in 2001, the first policy text to adopt the vocabulary of rights and representation across sectors in one document. The organisations that would petition in Vishaka in 1997, and the parliamentarians who would fight the bill's clauses from 1996, were formed in this widened field. The anti-arrack movement of Andhra Pradesh shows the ground being worked at its widest. On the account preserved in the literacy campaign's own record, the movement began in 1992 in Dubagunta village in Nellore district, where women attending a class under the Total Literacy Campaign read in their primer the story of a woman who had fought the liquor contractor draining her household, and resolved to act on the lesson in their own village: the women picketed the arrack shop, prevented the auction of the arrack contract and held their position against the contractors and the excise machinery. The example travelled village to village through the literacy network the state itself had built, until the demand for prohibition had reorganised the politics of the state. In the assembly election of December 1994, N.T. Rama Rao campaigned on the movement's demand, and his government imposed total prohibition in January 1995; his successor's government lifted it in 1997, pleading revenue, and the lifting is recorded here beside the imposition because both halves instruct. A movement of rural women, most of them the unorganised workers of the Towards Equality tables, had used a state primer as an organising text, extracted a statewide statute within three years, and then watched a later cabinet repeal it on a budget argument: the renegotiation grammar, complete with its reversibility where the counterparty's price changes. The national conference of women's organisations at Patna in 1988, which drew the autonomous groups into a single deliberative arena, belongs to the same connective record, the movement periodically constituting itself as a body that could negotiate at all.

The landmarks stand where they stand because the ground between them was worked.

## Feature Three: Bhanwari Devi, and the judgment that legislated for the workplace, 1992 to 1997

Bhanwari Devi was a saathin, a village-level worker in the Rajasthan government's Women's Development Programme, posted to Bhateri village in Bassi tehsil of Jaipur district. Her brief from the programme included persuading villagers against child marriage. In May 1992 she reported and tried to stop the marriage of a one-year-old girl in a Gujjar family of the village; the marriage went ahead despite the police visit her report brought. On 22 September 1992, working in the fields with her husband Mohan, she was gang-raped by five men of that family and its circle, an act the record of the case treats as reprisal for her official act. The state's machinery then failed her in sequence, and the sequence is documented in the case literature because the women's movement preserved it: the police station that kept her late into the night and took her lehenga as evidence, sending her back to the village wrapped in her husband's bloodstained dhoti; the primary health centre whose doctor declined to examine her; the Jaipur medical report that confirmed her age and recorded nothing of the rape. The trial court at Jaipur acquitted the accused in 1995, in reasoning the movement quoted back at the judiciary for years, doubting that men of one caste would rape a woman of another in her husband's presence.

The answer came from a different legal door. Women's organisations, petitioning under the name Vishaka alongside other groups, moved the Supreme Court under Article 32 not for Bhanwari Devi's own appeal but for the class of working women her case exposed: there was no law anywhere in India under which a woman harassed at work, by an employer, a colleague or a superior, could complain to anyone. On 13 August 1997, in Vishaka and Others v State of Rajasthan and Others, (1997) 6 SCC 241, a bench of Chief Justice J.S. Verma, Justice Sujata V. Manohar and Justice B.N. Kirpal held that sexual harassment at the workplace violates the fundamental rights to equality, to non-discrimination, to practise a profession and to life with dignity, under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 of the Constitution. Finding no statute to apply, the bench supplied one: guidelines having the force of law under Article 141, expressly until Parliament legislated.

The judgment's method is the feature. The bench reached for the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women, which India had ratified on 9 July 1993, and read Articles 11 and 24 of that treaty, on women's employment rights and the state's undertaking to eliminate discrimination, into the domestic guarantee, holding that international conventions not inconsistent with the Constitution may be used to construe it. A treaty the executive had signed became, through a movement's petition, the workplace law of every employer in India. The guidelines defined sexual harassment by its forms: physical contact and advances, a demand or request for sexual favours, sexually coloured remarks, showing pornography, and any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature. They imposed the duty of prevention on the employer, required a complaints committee headed by a woman with not less than half its members women and a third-party member from a non-governmental organisation, and required the complaint mechanism to be publicised and its outcomes reported. For sixteen years, from August 1997 to December 2013, the only law of workplace harassment in India was this judgment, applied by the Supreme Court itself in Apparel Export Promotion Council v A.K. Chopra in 1999 and reviewed for compliance, with findings of widespread default, in Medha Kotwal Lele v Union of India in 2013.

The guidelines' sixteen years as working law were not quiet years, and their enforcement record belongs to the feature. State governments and universities complied unevenly, and the compliance litigation returned the matter to the Supreme Court in Medha Kotwal Lele v Union of India, where the Court, in orders culminating in October 2012, recorded the patchiness state by state and directed the constitution of complaints committees and the amendment of service rules to treat sexual harassment as misconduct. In December 2012, after the gang rape of a young woman in Delhi on 16 December detonated a national reckoning over the criminal law's treatment of women, the government appointed a three-member committee under the retired Chief Justice J.S. Verma, the author of Vishaka, to recommend amendments to the criminal law; its report, submitted on 23 January 2013, ranged from the definition of sexual offences to the workplace bill then pending, and the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 2013 that followed made sexual harassment itself a penal offence in Section 354A of the Penal Code. The symmetry is part of the record: the judge who had supplied the workplace code as guidelines in 1997 was recalled in 2012 to audit the criminal code, and the two statutes of 2013, the criminal amendment and the workplace Act, closed between them the legal architecture that Bhanwari Devi's case had opened twenty-one years earlier.

Parliament's codification, when it came, kept the judgment's architecture and its name in the movement's memory. The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act of 2013, in force from 9 December 2013, reproduced the Vishaka definition almost intact, converted the complaints committee into a statutory Internal Complaints Committee for establishments with ten or more employees, added Local Complaints Committees for the unorganised sector and smaller establishments, and fixed employer duties and penalties in statute. The line from a saathin's unrecorded complaint in a Jaipur police station in September 1992 to a statutory committee in every covered workplace runs through one judgment and one petition; it is the clearest instance in the five features of renegotiation by citation, the movement carrying a grievance to the one institution positioned to answer in general terms and the institution answering in general law. Students of the constitutional line that runs from the basic structure doctrine through the privacy judgment will place Vishaka in the family of decisions, surveyed in [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/), in which the Court rewrote the working terms of citizenship while Parliament stood still.

The objection entered against Feature Three is the democratic one, and it deserves its strongest form: three judges, moved by an atrocity, wrote a regulatory code for every workplace in the country, and a code so written lacks both an elected author and an administrator's knowledge of its costs. The record answers in two parts. First, the bench wrote under an express constitutional commission, Article 32, in a vacuum the legislature acknowledged by leaving the guidelines untouched for sixteen years and then enacting them; judicial legislation that Parliament later adopts nearly whole reads, in retrospect, as drafting on commission rather than usurpation. Second, the constituency the guidelines protected had no other door: a saathin's harassment complaint against the social order of her own village was not going to be carried by any legislator of 1992. The renegotiation frame does not need the Court to be a parliament. It needs the exchange to be real, dated and enforceable, and on 13 August 1997 it was all three.

## Feature Four: one-third of local India changes hands, 1993

The reservation the Committee on the Status of Women in India had recommended for local bodies in 1974, and withheld for legislatures, arrived nineteen years later in the middle of an economic opening. The government of P.V. Narasimha Rao, whose larger renegotiation of the state's economic role is traced in [the economic-policy-as-politics framework](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/), carried the Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act and the (Seventy-fourth Amendment) Act through Parliament in December 1992, four years after Rajiv Gandhi's Sixty-fourth Amendment Bill had passed the Lok Sabha in August 1989 and failed in the Rajya Sabha that October. The Seventy-third entered into force on 24 April 1993, inserting Part IX, Articles 243 to 243-O, for rural panchayats; the Seventy-fourth entered into force on 1 June 1993, inserting Part IXA, Articles 243-P to 243-ZG, for municipalities. Article 243D and its municipal counterpart, Article 243T, reserved not less than one-third of all seats for women, not less than one-third of the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes for women of those categories, and not less than one-third of the offices of chairperson at every tier, the reserved seats and offices to be allotted by rotation. Karnataka had rehearsed the design a decade earlier, reserving a quarter of panchayat seats for women under its 1983 legislation, and the national text generalised the rehearsal to every village and town in the republic.

### Why did the panchayat settlement arrive a generation before the parliamentary one?

Because the cost accounting differed by tier. Reserving a third of panchayat seats displaced local notables whose resistance had no national amplifier, and the measure rode inside a decentralisation package state governments wanted for other reasons. Reserving a third of Lok Sabha seats displaced sitting members of the House asked to vote, by name and constituency, at every election thereafter.

Scale is what separates Feature Four from every symbolic measure that preceded it. The first panchayat elections under the new Parts, held across the states from 1994, seated women in numbers no Indian institution had carried before, and rotation carried the office through village after village rather than fixing it in a few strongholds. The figures the Ministry of Panchayati Raj placed before the Lok Sabha on 17 December 2015 give the settled position two decades on: 13.42 lakh elected women representatives in the panchayati raj institutions, constituting 46 percent of all elected representatives. The share exceeded the constitutional floor because states legislated past it and because women won unreserved seats as well. Bihar led the second movement in 2006, when the government of Nitish Kumar raised panchayat reservation for women in the state to one-half, the first state to do so; by the end of the following decade twenty states had written one-half into their panchayat acts, on the list the Ministry gave Parliament, among them Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Kerala, Karnataka and West Bengal. The Union Cabinet approved a matching constitutional amendment to Article 243D in August 2009, and a bill reached the Lok Sabha that November, but the national floor still stands at one-third in the text, the higher share resting on state law. The reservation architecture of which Feature Four is the working base is set out in full in [the constitutional reservation framework](/2020/02/25/constitutional-reservation-framework/).

What the seats did once held is an empirical question, and the movement's fourth feature is unusual among the five in possessing randomised evidence. Because reserved constituencies were allotted by rotation under published rules, Raghabendra Chattopadhyay and Esther Duflo could treat the reservation of a gram pradhan's office as a policy experiment; their study, published in Econometrica in 2004, covered Birbhum district in West Bengal and Udaipur district in Rajasthan. The complaint registers they coded, reproduced since in lecture decks a student may open in a [presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), showed women complainants raising drinking water in thirty-one percent of complaints in West Bengal against seventeen percent for men, and councils headed by women under reservation invested measurably more in the goods women raised: in West Bengal, in nine more drinking water facilities on average, and in better road repair where women had raised roads. In Rajasthan the same signature appeared on drinking water. The gender of the officeholder, the economists concluded, moved the investment pattern of the office, a result that answered the tokenism objection in the only currency the objection respects. Lori Beaman, Raghabendra Chattopadhyay, Esther Duflo, Rohini Pande and Petia Topalova extended the record in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2009: after two cycles of reservation in West Bengal, villagers' measured bias against women leaders had fallen and women were winning unreserved pradhan seats at rates the pre-reservation record never approached; a companion study in Science in 2012 found that two cycles of a woman pradhan closed a substantial share of the gender gap in adolescent educational attainment and in parents' aspirations for daughters.

The counter-evidence has a name of its own, and the charter of this article requires it at full strength: sarpanch pati, the sarpanch's husband, entered the language because villagers, journalists and researchers all met men who attended the meetings, signed the papers and gave the interviews for offices their wives held. Nirmala Buch's field studies in Madhya Pradesh through the 1990s, among the most careful the settlement produced, documented the proxy pattern in detail and documented its decay in the same notebooks: women first elected as placeholders learned the office across a term, asserted signatures and agendas against household resistance, and returned, where rotation permitted, as principals. The Ministry of Panchayati Raj's own commissioned study of elected women representatives in 2008 recorded the same gradient nationally, proxy functioning concentrated among first-term representatives and falling away with exposure and training. The municipal tier replayed the rural sequence in compressed form. The Seventy-fourth Amendment had carried the same one-third into nagar panchayats, municipal councils and corporations through Article 243T, and the states that raised the panchayat share carried the higher share into their municipal law in the same reforming years; Bihar, the pioneer of 2006, wrote one-half into its municipal bodies under its municipal act of 2007. Urban office added a distinct training to the settlement's ledger, since a woman mayor or councillor in a corporation commands budgets and contracts of a scale no gram panchayat approaches, and the pipeline argument the bill's supporters made in the 2008 debates, that a generation of local officeholders stood ready for the assemblies, drew its plausibility from corporation corridors as much as from village squares.

The honest summary of the evidence is neither the celebration nor the dismissal: reservation at the local tier manufactured political experience at a scale no apprenticeship scheme could match, the experience compounded across cycles, and the proxy residue, real in every survey, shrank in every panel that measured it twice. Feature Four is the renegotiation's proof of operation, the feature the parliamentary bill's opponents had to argue against for twenty-seven years while its results accumulated district by district beneath them.

## The bill that one House passed and the other would not touch, 1996 to 2010

The demand the Committee on the Status of Women in India had withheld in 1974, for reserved seats in the legislatures themselves, reached Parliament as government business on 12 September 1996, when the United Front government of H.D. Deve Gowda introduced the Constitution (Eighty-first Amendment) Bill in the Lok Sabha. The text proposed to reserve, as nearly as may be, one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and the state legislative assemblies for women, one-third of the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes to be reserved for women of those categories, the reserved seats to rotate as Parliament provided, with no expiry date. Every major party had promised the measure in its manifesto for the 1996 election. The introduction dissolved into disorder within the day, and the bill went instead to a Joint Committee of thirty-one members of both Houses, chaired by the Communist Party of India member Geeta Mukherjee, who had herself pressed a private member's initiative on the same subject. The committee took 102 memoranda from women's organisations and others, heard delegations including women who had signed their submissions in blood, and reported on 9 December 1996, recommending passage with amendments: the phrase as nearly as may be, one-third; the Delhi assembly brought within the scheme; a fifteen-year life for the reservation; the question of extending the benefit to the Other Backward Classes left, in the committee's diplomatic formula, for the government to consider at the appropriate time. The Eleventh Lok Sabha dissolved in December 1997 with the bill unpassed, and it lapsed with the House.

The pattern then repeated until repetition became the finding. The government of Atal Bihari Vajpayee introduced the bill in 1998, as the Eighty-fourth Amendment Bill, and again in 1999, as the Eighty-fifth; each time the introduction or the listing for debate produced an uproar in the Lok Sabha, members of the Samajwadi Party, the Rashtriya Janata Dal and allied formations physically disrupting proceedings, and each time the government of the day, commanding a coalition in which the objectors' outside tolerance mattered, withdrew rather than divide the House. The United Progressive Alliance, which had written the bill into its Common Minimum Programme in 2004, changed the venue instead: it introduced the Constitution (One Hundred and Eighth Amendment) Bill in the Rajya Sabha on 6 May 2008, a House a bill survives the dissolution of the other. A standing committee examined it and reported in December 2009, recommending that the bill be passed, and the Rajya Sabha's debate of 8 and 9 March 2010 then supplied the stall's defining images in the official record: the marshals removing the seven disruptors, members of the quota camp recording their objections in debate, and the division itself, 186 to one, recorded against a House whose full strength stood far higher, the absentees having priced absence below opposition. The solitary vote against belonged to Sharad Yadav, who had led the disruption of 1996; the record of the day thus contains the whole argument in miniature, a champion of the sub-quota strand voting his objection on principle while the majority for the flat bill assembled, for once, in the House that did not face the voters its decision would displace. On 9 March 2010, the day after International Women's Day, the Rajya Sabha passed the bill by 186 votes to one, after seven members who disrupted the House demanding sub-quotas were removed by marshals. The Lok Sabha never voted on it. The Fifteenth Lok Sabha was dissolved in 2014, and the bill, passed by one House and pending in the other, lapsed under the rules, twenty-seven years of the renegotiation stalling at a single doorway.

### What did rotation threaten in the eyes of sitting legislators?

Continuity of possession. Under the successive bills, reserved constituencies were to rotate, so a seat nursed for a decade could be reserved at the next election and lost not to a rival but to a category. Rotation spread the reservation widely, its democratic merit, and threatened every incumbent in turn, its parliamentary defect.

The opposition to the bill was never a single argument, and the renegotiation reading requires each strand at its strongest, because the strands explain the stall better than any tale of bad faith. The first strand was the quota within quota demand, pressed in the Lok Sabha by Sharad Yadav, Mulayam Singh Yadav and Lalu Prasad Yadav across the 1996 to 2010 debates: that a flat one-third for women, in a House where Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe seats alone carried sub-reservation, would be filled disproportionately by educated, urban, upper-caste women, and that justice required reserved seats within the women's quota for women of the Other Backward Classes and for Muslim women. Sharad Yadav's recorded interventions made the point in a deliberately coarse image, asking whether the House meant to fill its benches with the short-haired women of the cities, a phrase his opponents quoted against him for a decade; stripped of the image, the underlying claim was the Mandal claim, that representation without caste arithmetic reproduces privilege, and it drew on the same settlement that had reorganised north Indian politics after 1990. The second strand was the rotation objection, held well beyond the quota camp: that rotating reservation severs the bond between a member and a constituency, destroys the incentive to nurse a seat, and produces one-term legislators answerable to the party that allots tickets rather than to voters who cannot return them. The third strand was the minimalist objection, voiced by some women's movement veterans themselves: Madhu Kishwar, writing in Manushi, proposed instead a law requiring every party to nominate women in one-third of the seats it contested, with the electorate free to choose among them, arguing that reserved seats with rotation would manufacture dependence on party bosses and male patrons rather than independent legislators. The design questions raised in this long contest, and the architecture within which they were argued, are mapped in [the women's reservation architectural framework](/2020/03/29/2019-ews-reservation-amendment/).

The strands shared one property: each could be argued indefinitely without ever being voted. The governments of 1996, 1998, 1999 and 2004 to 2014 all discovered that the coalition cost of forcing a division exceeded the electoral cost of another lapse, because the women voters the bill courted were not organised as a constituency that punished the lapse, a condition the movement's own analysts, including contributors to Mary E. John's 2008 reader, diagnosed in print during the stall itself. The renegotiation frame states the lesson without varnish. Renegotiation succeeds where an institution can be made to answer: a committee, a court, a state legislature amending a panchayat act, a Rajya Sabha whose members do not face the displaced incumbents' constituencies. It stalls where the answering institution must vote its own members out of a third of their seats. The bill's twenty-seven years are not evidence against the renegotiation reading; they are its boundary condition, the one feature of the five in which the exchange demanded that the counterparty pay with its own composition. What changed in 2023 was not the argument. It was the counterparty's calculation.

## The machinery of follow-through: commission, credit and policy, 1990 to 2001

Between the panchayat amendments and the bill's long stall, the state built a standing apparatus whose existence the gender-policy reading treats as the story and the renegotiation reading treats as the next arena. The National Commission for Women Act of 1990 created a statutory commission charged with reviewing the constitutional and legal safeguards for women, investigating complaints, and recommending legislative change; the Commission was constituted on 31 January 1992 with Jayanti Patnaik as its first chairperson. Its annual reports through the 1990s pressed the reservation demand into the official record year after year, and its reviews of the successive bills were placed before the same Parliament that declined to vote on them, so that the bill's refusal was never a refusal for want of an official recommendation on file. The committee of 1974 had asked for permanent machinery; the machinery of 1992 then spent a generation restating the committee's unfinished item in the state's own annual paperwork.

The apparatus extended below the commission. The Mahila Samakhya programme, launched by the education ministry in 1989 out of the equality commitments of the National Policy on Education of 1986, organised rural women into village collectives, sanghas, across Karnataka, Gujarat and Uttar Pradesh first and other states later, treating literacy as the entry point to negotiation with the panchayat, the block office and the bank; its sanghas became, in district after district, the recruitment ground from which reserved panchayat seats were filled with women who had already run a collective. The Rashtriya Mahila Kosh, set up in March 1993 as a national credit fund, lent small sums to poor women through intermediary organisations on the finding the Towards Equality researchers had recorded, that the unorganised woman worker stood outside every institution that lent. Women's studies, the field that produced this article's scholarly authorities, institutionalised in the same years on the Indian Council of Social Science Research's programme and in university centres that the University Grants Commission recognised from the middle 1980s, the Research Centre for Women's Studies at SNDT Women's University in Bombay, founded in 1974 under Neera Desai, standing as the senior instance. On 20 March 2001 the government adopted the National Policy for the Empowerment of Women, a text that gathered the threads into a single commitment, pledging among its objectives the reservation of seats for women in legislative bodies and their equal presence in decision-making at every level.

The renegotiation reading takes this machinery seriously without mistaking it for the settlement. A commission that recommends is an instrument of the exchange, not its conclusion; its reports are citable texts, and they were cited, in the standing committee's examination of the 2008 bill and in the debates of 2010. The policy reading's strongest evidence sits in this section, and the framework concedes it: much of the movement's half-century was indeed processed through committee, commission, programme and policy. What the concession does not yield is the explanatory point. Machinery that processes a demand for thirty years without delivering the demand's central item, legislative reservation, while delivering the lesser items around it, is machinery whose blockage sits at one identifiable joint, and the joint is the subject of the section after next: the House that would not vote. The apparatus matters to the framework for a colder reason as well. When the amendment finally passed in September 2023, it passed into a state already staffed with the commission, the collectives, the studies centres and the policy text the long renegotiation had built, which is why the settlement, deferred as it is, did not arrive in an institutional desert.

## The interval of manifestos, 2010 to 2023

The bill's afterlife in the thirteen years between its Rajya Sabha passage and its rebirth is itself evidence for the framework, because the demand survived its instrument. The lapsed text of 2008 to 2014 stayed in every manifesto that mattered. The Bharatiya Janata Party's manifesto for the general election of 2014 committed the party to thirty-three percent reservation for women in Parliament and the state assemblies; its manifesto of 2019 repeated the commitment. The Congress manifesto of 2019 promised to pass the bill in the first session of the new Lok Sabha. In September 2017, Sonia Gandhi, as Congress president, wrote to Prime Minister Narendra Modi urging the government to use its Lok Sabha majority to pass the bill the Rajya Sabha had cleared in 2010, a letter the government acknowledged and did not act on within that Parliament. The women's organisations kept the anniversary calendar of the stall, marking 9 March in their campaigns as the date one House had decided and the other had not. None of this moved a division. What it moved was the price schedule: by 2019, refusal no longer cost a paragraph in a manifesto review, it cost a broken written promise addressed to an electorate in which, as the baseline section below records, women had begun to outvote men. The interval's lesson refines the framework's boundary condition. The renegotiation stalls where the answering institution must pay with its own composition, and it resumes when the price of refusal, accumulated through manifestos, letters and turnout tables, exceeds the price of payment. The special session of September 2023, a new building, a government's fourth year arithmetic and a bill renamed for the occasion, is the shape the resumption took when the prices crossed.

## The representation baseline the renegotiation argued against

The reservation features argue against a measurable condition, and the measurements belong in the record with their sources named. The Lok Sabha Secretariat's and PRS Legislative Research's compilations of the House's composition give the series: twenty-two women among 489 members in the First Lok Sabha of 1952, a share of about four and a half percent; forty-four in 1984, the high point of the Indira Gandhi decades; forty-nine in 1999; fifty-nine in 2009; sixty-two in 2014; and seventy-eight among 543 in 2019, a share of 14.4 percent, the highest the House had recorded and still less than half the share the panchayat amendments had normalised at the village tier a generation earlier. The state assemblies ran below the parliamentary figure throughout: the Association for Democratic Reforms' compilations of assembly composition put women's share of state legislature seats at roughly nine percent in the late 2010s. The Rajya Sabha, indirectly elected and therefore insulated from the ticket-allocation choices the bill's supporters blamed, stood at roughly one woman in nine members across the same period. Against the world, the Inter-Parliamentary Union's tables for 2023 placed the average share of women in national parliaments at about 26.5 percent, with India below the average it had itself legislated for its villages in 1993.

The voting figures moved the other way, and the divergence is the analytical heart of the baseline. The Election Commission's statistical reports record women's turnout climbing across the post-1990 elections until, in the general election of 2019, the female turnout rate, 67.18 percent, edged past the male rate of 67.01 percent for the first time in a national poll, with women casting the larger vote in a majority of states. Women candidates in 2019 were about nine percent of all contestants in the Election Commission's count, and those nominated won at a strike rate higher than the men's, evidence the bill's advocates had cited since the 1990s against the winnability objection: the barrier sat at the nomination desk, not at the ballot box. A constituency that votes in equal numbers, wins when nominated and holds office in one seat in seven at the highest tier and one seat in three at the lowest is a constituency whose exclusion is located precisely where the renegotiation reading locates it, in the allocation decisions of institutions. The stall of 1996 to 2010 and the passage of 2023 are both unintelligible without this baseline: the parties that would not pay the bill's price in 1996 were courting an electorate in which women voted at sixty percent against men's sixty-seven; the Parliament that passed it in 2023 was courting one in which women voted at the higher rate and in which every party's manifesto, for the fifth consecutive general election, already contained the promise the Act redeemed.

## Feature Five: the codification of September 2023, and its deliberate delay

The bill returned in the first week of a special session, as the first measure taken up in the new Parliament House. On 19 September 2023 the Law Minister, Arjun Ram Meghwal, introduced the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-eighth Amendment) Bill in the Lok Sabha; the Prime Minister, Narendra Modi, gave it the name under which it passed, Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam, an act in salutation of women's power, and the naming itself announced the government's reading of the event within [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/): a movement demand five decades old was being absorbed, on the government's terms and timing, into a state-led settlement. The Lok Sabha debated the bill on 20 September and passed it by 454 votes to two, the two against being the All India Majlis-e-Ittehadul Muslimeen members Asaduddin Owaisi and Syed Imtiyaz Jaleel. The Rajya Sabha passed it on 21 September by 214 votes to none. President Droupadi Murmu assented on 28 September 2023, and the Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act entered the statute book the same day.

Two formal properties of the Act deserve record beside its margins, because both connect it to the file this article has tracked. The first is ratification. An amendment that alters the representation of states in Parliament falls within the proviso to Article 368 and requires ratification by the legislatures of not less than one-half of the states before the President may assent; the state legislatures completed those ratifications in the days between the Rajya Sabha's vote on 21 September and the assent on 28 September, several of them in specially convened sittings, so that the settlement of 2023 carries the recorded concurrence of the federal units whose assemblies it reserves, a concurrence the bills of 1996 to 2010 never lived long enough to seek. The second property is the Act's deliberate narrowness, inherited whole from the lapsed bills. It reserves nothing in the Rajya Sabha or in the state legislative councils, repeating an exclusion the Geeta Mukherjee committee had already noted in 1996 when it recommended that the government work out the modalities for the upper Houses at an appropriate time; the appropriate time, on the record to 2023, had still not been taken. The Act likewise reserves no seat for the Anglo-Indian nominations, those provisions having lapsed from the Constitution in 2020, and it leaves the panchayat and municipal shares where Feature Four left them, in Articles 243D and 243T and in the state acts that raised them. A framework that reads the movement as renegotiation by text should say plainly what this text is: the largest single transfer of legislative presence in the movement's history, drafted to the boundaries of the demand as the demand had been framed since 1996, neither wider nor, in its deferral clause, readier than that framing allowed.

### Whose votes carried the 2023 amendment, and whose did not?

Nearly everyone's. The Lok Sabha division of 20 September 2023 recorded 454 votes in favour and two against, both cast by the All India Majlis-e-Ittehadul Muslimeen; the Rajya Sabha division recorded 214 in favour and none against. Parties that had stalled the bill for a generation voted for it while restating their reservations about its design.

The Act's text does three things and defers one. It inserts Article 330A, reserving as nearly as may be one-third of Lok Sabha seats for women, including one-third of the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes; Article 332A, doing the same for every state legislative assembly and, through an amendment of Article 239AA, for Delhi; and Article 334A, which governs the deferral. Under 334A the reservation takes effect only after a delimitation undertaken for that purpose, following the publication of the relevant figures of the first census taken after the Act's commencement; the reserved seats then rotate after each subsequent delimitation as Parliament by law determines; and the reservation runs for fifteen years from its commencement, extendable by Parliament. The government's stated case for the linkage, put by the Law Minister in the debate, was constitutional mechanics: the number and distribution of seats rests on census figures, delimitation has stood frozen since the Eighty-fourth Amendment of 2001 until the first census after 2026, and a reservation carved into unrevised constituencies would rest on population figures half a century stale. The text leaves every existing House untouched until its dissolution, so the first women elected under Articles 330A and 332A will sit in Houses elected after a census and a delimitation that had not been scheduled when the Act passed.

The counter-case was made at full strength in the same debates and belongs to the record beside the votes. Sonia Gandhi, leading for the Congress in the Lok Sabha, supported the bill and demanded its immediate operation, arguing that a right granted for an unspecified future session asked women to accept the promise in place of the seats, and she renewed the two demands the 1996 strand had carried: a caste census, and reservation within the women's quota for women of the Other Backward Classes. Opposition speakers across parties voted for the bill while recording that the deferral had no date, the census of 2021 having already been postponed without a successor schedule. Feminist scholars made the same point in print with the movement's own history as evidence: Nivedita Menon, author of Seeing Like a Feminist (2012), argued in her published responses that an enactment deferred to an unscheduled census, and silent on the sub-quota question the Mandal generation had raised, repeated the two oldest evasions in the file under new lettering. Owaisi's dissent in the Lok Sabha added a strand the majority debate largely declined to engage, that the Act reserved nothing for Muslim women, whose representation in the House stood lower than any major group's.

The brief's hardest test for the citizen-renegotiation reading sits here, and this article does not evade it: a reading that treats 2023 as culmination must explain why the culminating instrument operates at an unknown date while the panchayat instrument of 1993 operated at the next election. The explanation lies in what kind of act each was. The panchayat reservation disposed of seats the Constitution was creating in the same text, in bodies elected on short cycles under state control. The 2023 Act disposes of seats in Houses whose very boundaries await a census the executive controls the timing of; its authors wrote the dependency into Article 334A rather than risk the amendment on a delimitation fight in 2023 itself. The renegotiation reading captures precisely this distinction. What the movement extracted in September 2023 was codification: the claim of one-third moved from manifesto and lapsed bill into the constitutional text, where it binds every future delimitation commission and every future Parliament's drafting hand, and where its fifteen-year clock, once started, starts from a commencement the state must eventually perform. Codification is not operation, and the article records the deferral as a deferral. But a deferred entitlement written into Articles 330A and 332A is a different possession from a bill pending in a House that can let it lapse, as the twenty-seven years between 1996 and 2023 demonstrate in the negative. The exchange of Feature Five is complete as an exchange of texts; its performance is scheduled by its own clause, and the movement that waited from 1974 holds, for the first time, a text that waits with a claim on the calendar rather than a place in a queue.

## The five-feature map

The framework can be stated as a citable object. The table below, the five-feature map of the Indian women's movement as citizen-renegotiation, sets each feature against its instrument, its date and the term of citizenship it rewrote; it is the artifact this article leaves for citation, and the sections above carry the evidence for every cell.

| Feature | Instrument and date | Institution addressed | Term of citizenship rewritten |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

Read down the third column and the sequence shows its shape. The movement addressed itself to whichever institution could answer in general text: a committee the executive appointed, a court whose doctrine a letter could shame and whose guidelines a later bench could wield as law, a Parliament that could amend readily where its own seats were not the price, and the same Parliament, twenty-seven years later, where they were. Read down the fourth column and the renegotiation shows its ratchet. No feature was ever repealed. The baseline of 1975 was never un-tabled; the presumption of 1983 was never withdrawn; the guidelines of 1997 were not struck down but codified; the third of 1993 was raised to a half by twenty states rather than lowered anywhere; the third of 2023 was written into the Constitution with its deferral printed on its face. Settlements in this field move in one direction because each text becomes the floor from which the next demand is drafted, a property the framework shares with its sibling readings of other fields, [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/) and [the citizen-renegotiation through Sardar Sarovar framework](/2021/01/25/narmada-bachao-andolan-reanalysed/) alike.

## Adjudicating the three readings

The feminist-history reading, in Forbes's Women in Modern India (1996), explains the movement's personnel. The women who staff the five features descend in recognisable line from the organisations Forbes chronicles: Lotika Sarkar from the world of the All India Women's Conference generation's legal wing, the Forum Against Rape from the autonomous groups of the post-Emergency years, the Vishaka petitioners from the professionalised advocacy organisations of the 1990s. Where the lineage reading fails is its prediction of texture. A lineage accumulates; this record lurches. It contains a report ignored for a decade by the government that commissioned it, an acquittal that undid in one morning what a high court had done, a bill that failed in four successive Parliaments whose manifestos all contained it, and a final passage, in four September days of 2023, by margins the cause had never previously commanded. Lineage cannot price those lurches, because the lurches were institutional, not organisational.

The gender-policy reading, in the literature Mary E. John edited in 2008, explains the movement's paperwork. The five features are indeed a committee, a statute, a judgment, two amendment acts and a third: a policy historian's shelf. Where the policy reading fails is its prediction of agency. In its telling the state processes demands through machinery whose outputs vary with input pressure, and the long stall of 1996 to 2010 becomes a puzzle of insufficient pressure. The record shows pressure in surplus and passage withheld: 102 memoranda, a committee report in ninety days, cross-party manifesto commitments, a Rajya Sabha majority of 186 to one, and still no Lok Sabha division for fourteen further years. The missing variable is the identity of the institution being asked, and the price the ask exacted from it. A ministry can absorb a guideline; a court can absorb a doctrine; a House cannot absorb its own partial dissolution without a change in the price of refusal, and the price changed only when a government holding a majority of its own, in a new building, in a special session, calculated that the credit for passage exceeded the cost of the displacement to come.

The citizen-renegotiation reading prices all of it, and it is therefore the reading this article defends: the Indian women's movement operates as citizen-renegotiation culminating in the 2023 Nari Shakti reservation, and the five-feature framework captures the content the rival readings divide between them and then cannot recombine. It explains why the movement's characteristic weapon was the citable text rather than the crowd; why its victories cluster at moments when an institution could answer without self-harm, and its great defeat sits exactly where self-harm was the ask; why the panchayat third, won in 1993, became the empirical brief for the parliamentary third, the Chattopadhyay and Duflo tables doing in the 2008 debate what the Towards Equality tables had done in 1975; and why the settlement of 2023, deferral and all, was taken by its opponents as seriously as by its authors, each side reading Article 334A correctly as a postponed performance of an obligation already incurred. Nivedita Menon's Seeing Like a Feminist (2012) supplies the reading's necessary caution from inside the field: the feminist subject of these texts is not one woman but many, divided by caste, religion and class, and the renegotiation's texts distribute their gains unevenly, a caution the quota within quota strand had already entered into the parliamentary record in its own vocabulary. The caution qualifies the reading's celebration; it does not overturn its account of the mechanism.

Two cautions from inside the scholarship discipline the verdict before it hardens. Mary E. John's editorial work on the 2008 Reader insists that the knowledge of this field was itself produced inside institutions, universities, funding programmes and state committees, so that a history of the movement written from its documents risks mistaking the archive's shape for the movement's. The caution lands on this article's method and is accepted: the five features are five survivals in text, and the movement's unarchived majorities, the women of Shahada and of the price-rise marches whose names no committee preserved, enter the record only at the points where an organisation wrote them down. Nivedita Menon's caution in Seeing Like a Feminist (2012) cuts the other way, against the state's texts rather than the historian's: the law's subject, woman, is a legal fiction laid over divided lives, and a reservation that fills seats without asking which women arrive in them can perfect the form of the renegotiation while stratifying its substance. The parliamentary record shows the movement's own representatives making Menon's point before her book gave it canonical form, in the quota within quota interventions of the 1996 to 2010 debates. A framework that can state its critics' evidence inside its own account, and point to the clauses where the account remains honestly incomplete, the unresolved sub-quota, the unscheduled census, earns more confidence than one that wins by omission. The verdict for citizen-renegotiation is entered with both cautions on the record.

## What the long arc settles

The five features, taken together, settle the question this article opened with. The Indian women's movement, in its post-1975 form, is best read neither as a lineage of organisations, though the lineage is real, nor as a pressure upon policy, though the pressure was constant, but as a fifty-year renegotiation of the recorded terms of women's citizenship, conducted feature by feature through the institutions positioned to answer: the committee, the court, the local-body amendment, and at last the amendment of the legislatures themselves. Its documentary chain runs unbroken from a committee room in 1971 to a division lobby in September 2023: the report that made decline official, the letter that made an acquittal infamous and a presumption statutory, the judgment that made a treaty into workplace law, the amendments that seated fourteen lakh women in local office, and the amendment that wrote the same one-third into Articles 330A and 332A of the Constitution. Each link cites the ones before it. Geeta Mukherjee's committee of 1996 worked in the shadow of the Towards Equality baseline; the debaters of 2010 argued over the panchayat evidence of 2004; the draftsmen of 2023 lifted their fraction, their sub-reservation and their fifteen-year term from the bill that had lapsed in 2014. A movement that argues in citations can lose decades, as this one did, without losing its place in the text.

What remains open in the record is as precisely bounded as what is settled. The sub-quota question, entered by the Mandal generation in 1996 and renewed by Sonia Gandhi on the floor in September 2023, stands unresolved in the Act's text, which sub-reserves for Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe women alone; whether a future Parliament takes up the Other Backward Classes demand is a question the renegotiation's next round will answer, and the framework predicts only the form the answer will take, a text argued over in committees and on floors, not the answer itself. The rotation of the new reserved seats awaits the law Article 334A promises, and its design will decide whether the objection that dogged the bills of 1996 to 2010, that rotation unmakes legislators, is answered by the delimitation-linked cycle the Act sketches. The census that starts the clock remains, on the record this article may use, an event the executive has yet to schedule. None of these openings returns the settlement of 2023 to the queue where the bills of 1996, 1998, 1999 and 2008 waited to lapse. The claim has changed its legal address. It lives in the Constitution, and the five-feature map is the record of how it got there. When the census is taken and the delimitation commission sits, its members will work inside a text the movement wrote across fifty years: the fraction fixed, the sub-reservation fixed, the rotation principle fixed, the fifteen-year term fixed. The commissioners will argue, as every institution in this record has argued, over the application of terms already settled elsewhere and earlier. That is what a completed renegotiation looks like from inside the next one. The argument moves to new ground because the old ground was taken, feature by feature, text by text, between a committee room in 1971 and a division lobby in 2023.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 1975 Towards Equality Report?

Towards Equality was the report of the Committee on the Status of Women in India, appointed by the Department of Social Welfare in September 1971 under the chairpersonship of Phulrenu Guha, with Vina Mazumdar as member-secretary and Lotika Sarkar, Maniben Kara and M.K. Indira among its members. Submitted in December 1974 and tabled in Parliament in 1975, it audited women's condition against the Constitution's equality promises and recorded decline on the state's own evidence: a sex ratio fallen from 972 females per 1,000 males in 1901 to 930 in 1971, shrinking recorded work participation, and around ninety-four percent of women workers in the unorganised sector. It recommended reservation for women in local bodies and a permanent national machinery for women, and it supplied the evidentiary baseline on which the post-1975 movement argued.

### Q: What is the post-1992 73rd Amendment women's reservation?

The Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act of 1992, in force from 24 April 1993, inserted Part IX into the Constitution and, through Article 243D, reserved not less than one-third of all seats in panchayats for women, including one-third of the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, together with not less than one-third of the offices of chairperson at every tier, the reserved seats to be allotted by rotation. The companion Seventy-fourth Amendment Act, in force from 1 June 1993, wrote the parallel rule for municipalities in Article 243T. The reservation applied from the first elections under the new Parts in 1994, and the Ministry of Panchayati Raj reported to the Lok Sabha in December 2015 that elected women representatives in the panchayats numbered 13.42 lakh, about forty-six percent of all elected representatives.

### Q: What is the Women's Reservation Bill?

Women's Reservation Bill is the standing name for the successive constitutional amendment bills that proposed to reserve one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and the state legislative assemblies for women. The Constitution (Eighty-first Amendment) Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 12 September 1996 by the United Front government and examined by a joint committee chaired by Geeta Mukherjee, which reported that December; it lapsed with the Eleventh Lok Sabha. Successor bills followed in 1998 and 1999, each failing amid disruption and the quota within quota demand. The Constitution (One Hundred and Eighth Amendment) Bill, introduced in the Rajya Sabha in May 2008, passed that House on 9 March 2010 by 186 votes to one, was never voted on in the Lok Sabha, and lapsed when the Fifteenth Lok Sabha dissolved in 2014. The demand the bills carried was enacted, in revised form, by the amendment of September 2023.

### Q: What is the 2023 Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam?

The Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam is the Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act of 2023, the enactment that reserved one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha, the state legislative assemblies and the Delhi assembly. Introduced in the Lok Sabha on 19 September 2023 as the Constitution (One Hundred and Twenty-eighth Amendment) Bill during a special session, it passed the Lok Sabha on 20 September by 454 votes to two and the Rajya Sabha on 21 September by 214 votes to none, and received the President's assent on 28 September 2023. It inserted Articles 330A and 332A carrying the reservation, and Article 334A, under which the reservation operates only after the first census taken following the Act's commencement and a delimitation based on that census, rotating thereafter with each delimitation and running for fifteen years from its commencement.

### Q: How did the 1980s anti-rape architectural moments operate?

They operated by converting a courtroom defeat into legislative text through a chain of dated instruments. The Supreme Court's acquittal of two policemen in the Mathura case, delivered on 15 September 1978 and reported at 1979 AIR 185, was answered in September 1979 by an Open Letter from Upendra Baxi, Lotika Sarkar, Vasudha Dhagamwar and Raghunath Kelkar to the Chief Justice, which attacked the judgment's doctrine of consent. Women's organisations, coordinated from 1980 by the Forum Against Rape in Bombay, carried the letter's demands into a national campaign strengthened by the Rameeza Bee and Maya Tyagi cases. Parliament answered with the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 1983: custodial rape became an aggravated category under Section 376(2) of the Penal Code, Section 114A of the Evidence Act shifted the burden on consent to the accused custodian, rape trials moved in camera, and Section 228A made naming a complainant an offence.

### Q: What was the Mathura case 1979?

The Mathura case is the prosecution of two Maharashtra policemen, Head Constable Tukaram and Constable Ganpat, for the rape of Mathura, a tribal girl of about fourteen to sixteen, inside the Desai Ganj police station on the night of 26 March 1972, where she had been held in a family dispute over her intended marriage. The sessions court acquitted both men, treating her as having consented. The Bombay High Court reversed the acquittal on 12 October 1976 and sentenced the two men. The Supreme Court acquitted them again on 15 September 1978, reasoning that her conduct showed passive submission rather than absence of consent; the judgment is cited by its report year as 1979 AIR 185, which is why the case carries the 1979 label. The acquittal provoked the Open Letter of September 1979 and the anti-rape campaign that produced the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 1983.

### Q: What is the Vishaka 1997 verdict?

Vishaka and Others v State of Rajasthan and Others, decided on 13 August 1997 and reported at (1997) 6 SCC 241, is the Supreme Court judgment that made sexual harassment at the workplace unlawful across India. A bench of Chief Justice J.S. Verma, Justice Sujata V. Manohar and Justice B.N. Kirpal, hearing a petition by women's organisations after the gang rape of the Rajasthan saathin Bhanwari Devi, held that sexual harassment violates the fundamental rights in Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21, drawing on the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women, which India had ratified in 1993. In the absence of any statute, the bench issued the Vishaka Guidelines, defining sexual harassment, obliging employers to prevent it and requiring complaints committees headed by a woman, binding as law under Article 141 until Parliament legislated, which it did in the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act of 2013.

### Q: Who served on the Committee on the Status of Women in India?

The committee was chaired by Phulrenu Guha, a former Union minister of state for social welfare. Its member-secretary, who directed its research programme and much of its drafting, was Vina Mazumdar, the political scientist who later directed the Indian Council of Social Science Research's women's studies programme and co-founded the Centre for Women's Development Studies in New Delhi in 1980, where the committee's papers and correspondence are archived. The membership named in the report included the constitutional lawyer Lotika Sarkar of the University of Delhi, who had also been part of the committee's work on legal status; Maniben Kara, the veteran trade unionist from Bombay; and the educationist M.K. Indira. Appointed in September 1971 by the Department of Social Welfare, the committee travelled across the states taking evidence for three years before submitting Towards Equality in December 1974.

### Q: What burden of proof did Section 114A of the Evidence Act create?

Section 114A, inserted into the Indian Evidence Act by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act of 1983, created a mandatory presumption on the question of consent in prosecutions for the aggravated categories of rape listed in Section 376(2) of the Penal Code, which include rape by a police officer, a public servant, or staff of a jail, remand home or hospital. Where sexual intercourse is proved and the woman states in her evidence before the court that she did not consent, the court shall presume that she did not consent. The practical effect is to move the burden on consent to the accused in custodial cases: before 1983, the prosecution had to prove absence of consent against a defence built on the complainant's conduct, the doctrine that had acquitted the constables in the Mathura case; after 1983, in the custodial categories, her sworn denial of consent stands unless the accused displaces it.

### Q: What happened in Bhanwari Devi's own prosecution of her attackers?

Bhanwari Devi, a saathin in Rajasthan's Women's Development Programme, was gang-raped on 22 September 1992 at Bhateri village in Jaipur district by five men of the family whose infant daughter's marriage she had tried to stop in the course of her duties. Her own case then failed at every station of the system the prosecution had to pass through. The police station kept her late into the night and retained her lehenga as evidence, sending her home in her husband's dhoti; the local doctor declined to examine her; the medical report prepared at Jaipur recorded her age and nothing of the assault. The trial court at Jaipur acquitted the accused in 1995, in reasoning quoted against it for years afterward, which doubted that men of the accused's caste would rape a woman of hers in her husband's presence. Her appeal remained her own burden; the constitutional remedy that carries her case's name came not from her prosecution but from the public interest petition that women's organisations filed under the name Vishaka.

### Q: How did the Vishaka Guidelines bind employers before any statute existed?

The guidelines bound employers because the Supreme Court declared them to be law under Article 141 of the Constitution, which makes the law declared by the Court binding on all courts in India. In Vishaka, delivered on 13 August 1997, the bench of Chief Justice J.S. Verma, Justice Sujata V. Manohar and Justice B.N. Kirpal framed the guidelines expressly to operate until Parliament enacted legislation, filling a vacuum the judgment itself recorded. Employers in government and the private sector were placed under a duty to prevent sexual harassment, to treat it as misconduct under service rules, and to constitute complaints committees headed by a woman, with not less than half their members women and one member drawn from a non-governmental organisation. The Court enforced the guidelines as law in Apparel Export Promotion Council v A.K. Chopra in 1999, and reviewed state and institutional compliance in Medha Kotwal Lele v Union of India in 2013, months before the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace Act codified the scheme from 9 December 2013.

### Q: Which state first raised panchayat reservation for women to one-half?

Bihar. The government of Chief Minister Nitish Kumar amended the state's panchayat law in 2006 to reserve one-half of seats and offices in the panchayati raj institutions for women, the first state to move beyond the one-third floor that Articles 243D and 243T had written into the Constitution in 1993. The first elections under the raised share followed in the state that year. Other states legislated in sequence across the following decade, among them Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Kerala, Karnataka and West Bengal, and the Ministry of Panchayati Raj's answers to Parliament in the late 2010s listed twenty states whose panchayat acts carried the one-half share. The Union Cabinet approved a proposal in August 2009 to raise the constitutional floor itself to one-half, and a bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha that November, but it lapsed unenacted, leaving the higher share a creature of state law.

### Q: What does the sarpanch pati charge claim about women officeholders?

The charge, named for the Hindi phrase meaning the sarpanch's husband, claims that a share of the women elected to reserved panchayat offices function as proxies, with a husband or another male relative attending the meetings, keeping the records, signing the papers and speaking for the office the woman formally holds. It was pressed by journalists, by villagers and by opponents of reservation as evidence that the seat had changed hands without the power changing hands. The field record qualifies the charge rather than dismissing it. Nirmala Buch's studies of women representatives in Madhya Pradesh through the 1990s documented proxy functioning in detail and documented its decline across a term of office in the same research, as women first elected as placeholders acquired the procedures, the contacts and the confidence of the office; the Ministry of Panchayati Raj's commissioned national study of elected women representatives in 2008 recorded the same pattern, proxy functioning concentrated among first-term representatives and falling with exposure, training and re-election.

### Q: What did Raghabendra Chattopadhyay and Esther Duflo find in women-led panchayats?

In their study published in Econometrica in 2004, Chattopadhyay and Duflo used the randomised rotation of reserved pradhan offices in Birbhum district of West Bengal and Udaipur district of Rajasthan to measure what changed when a woman held the office. Coding the complaints villagers brought to councils and the investments councils made, they found the two matched along gender lines: in West Bengal, drinking water figured in thirty-one percent of complaints brought by women against seventeen percent brought by men, and councils whose pradhan's office was reserved for a woman invested in significantly more drinking water facilities, nine more on average, with road investment following the same pattern where women had raised roads; in Rajasthan the signature appeared on drinking water again. The characteristics of the individual pradhan did not explain the results away. Their conclusion, that the gender of the officeholder changes the policy output of the office, supplied the reservation debate with randomised evidence that the identity of the representative alters what the state delivers.

### Q: What did the Geeta Mukherjee committee recommend in December 1996?

The Joint Committee on the Constitution (Eighty-first Amendment) Bill, thirty-one members drawn from both Houses under the chairpersonship of Geeta Mukherjee, reported on 9 December 1996, three months after the bill's introduction. It recommended that the bill be passed with amendments: the reservation expressed as as nearly as may be one-third of seats; the legislative assembly of Delhi brought within the scheme; reservation extended to nominations under the Anglo-Indian provisions then in the Constitution; and the reservation given a life of fifteen years from the amendment's commencement. On the demand that would dominate the bill's later history, reservation within the women's quota for women of the Other Backward Classes, the committee recorded the issue and left it for the government to consider at the appropriate time, a deferral the bill's opponents treated as an answer in the negative. The committee had taken 102 memoranda, including submissions from women's organisations across the country, and heard delegations in person before reporting.

### Q: Why did the 2008 bill pass the Rajya Sabha yet lapse in 2014?

The Constitution (One Hundred and Eighth Amendment) Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 6 May 2008 by deliberate design: a bill pending in the Rajya Sabha does not lapse when the Lok Sabha dissolves, so the government insured the text against the electoral calendar. The Rajya Sabha passed it on 9 March 2010 by 186 votes to one, after members disrupting the House for sub-quotas were removed. In the Lok Sabha the bill met the arithmetic that had defeated its predecessors: coalition governments depended on parties, the Samajwadi Party and the Rashtriya Janata Dal among them, whose recorded price was a quota within the quota, and no government between 2010 and 2014 chose to spend its majority forcing the division the bill required. A constitutional amendment pending in the Lok Sabha, passed by the other House but not by it, lapses on that House's dissolution under the rules of parliamentary procedure, and the Fifteenth Lok Sabha's dissolution in 2014 carried the bill with it.

### Q: What is the quota within quota demand in the women's reservation debate?

The quota within quota demand holds that a flat reservation of one-third of legislative seats for women should itself contain reserved shares for women of the Other Backward Classes, and in some versions for Muslim women, on the argument that without sub-reservation the seats will be taken disproportionately by educated, urban women of the privileged castes. It was pressed in the Lok Sabha from the bill's first introduction in 1996 by Sharad Yadav, Mulayam Singh Yadav and Lalu Prasad Yadav and their parties, the Samajwadi Party, the Janata Dal formations and the Rashtriya Janata Dal, and it was the stated ground on which the bills of 1996, 1998 and 1999 were disrupted and withdrawn, and on which the Rajya Sabha's disruptors were removed before the vote of 9 March 2010. The Geeta Mukherjee committee had deferred the question in 1996; the bills carried sub-reservation only for Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe women, within their existing reserved seats. In the 2023 debates Sonia Gandhi renewed the demand, linking it to a caste census; the Act of 2023 again sub-reserved only within the Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe seats, leaving the demand formally open.

### Q: Which articles of the Constitution carry the 2023 women's reservation?

Three inserted articles carry it. Article 330A reserves seats for women in the Lok Sabha: as nearly as may be one-third of the total seats, including one-third of the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes under Article 330. Article 332A does the same for the legislative assembly of every state, within Article 332's Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe reservations, and the amendment also adapted Article 239AA so that the scheme covers the National Capital Territory of Delhi. Article 334A governs the scheme's life: the reservation takes effect after an exercise of delimitation undertaken for the purpose, following the publication of the figures of the first census taken after the amendment's commencement; the reserved seats rotate after each subsequent delimitation in the manner Parliament lays down by law; existing Houses are untouched until dissolved; and the reservation runs for fifteen years from its commencement, which Parliament may extend by law. The Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act received assent on 28 September 2023.

### Q: Why does the 2023 Act wait for a census and a delimitation before operating?

Because its operative clause, Article 334A, ties the reservation to the redrawing of constituencies, and the redrawing is itself tied to a census. India's delimitation of parliamentary and assembly seats has stood frozen on 1971 population figures since the Forty-second Amendment of 1976, a freeze the Eighty-fourth Amendment of 2001 extended until the publication of the first census taken after 2026, in order to protect states that had succeeded in family planning from losing seats. Reserving one-third of seats inside constituencies drawn on half-century-old populations would have distributed the reservation across a map the Constitution itself has scheduled for replacement. The Law Minister's stated case in the September 2023 debates was that the reservation must be carved into the revised map, with the census supplying the figures and a delimitation commission the boundaries. The opposition's stated counter-case accepted the mechanics and attacked the open date: the census due in 2021 had been postponed without a successor schedule, so the Act's promise operates from a census whose year the text does not fix.

### Q: How many women were elected to the Lok Sabha in 2019?

Seventy-eight, of 543 seats, a share of 14.4 percent and the highest the House had recorded to that election. The Election Commission's statistical report for the 2019 general election counts women as about nine percent of all candidates, and records that nominated women won at a strike rate higher than nominated men, a pattern the reservation debate had cited since the 1990s against the argument that women could not win general seats. The 2019 figure continued a slow series kept by the Lok Sabha Secretariat and PRS Legislative Research: twenty-two women in the First Lok Sabha of 1952, forty-four in 1984, forty-nine in 1999, fifty-nine in 2009 and sixty-two in 2014. Women's turnout in 2019 reached 67.18 percent in the Commission's count, edging past the male turnout of 67.01 percent in a national election for the first time, so the electorate that returned seventy-eight women had itself, by participation, reached parity the House had not.
