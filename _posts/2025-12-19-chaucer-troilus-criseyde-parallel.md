Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde is not a source of Romeo and Juliet, and this article begins by insisting on the distinction, because the distinction is where the value lies. The play's plot descends through the Italian line, from Luigi da Porto and Matteo Bandello to Arthur Brooke's English poem and William Painter's prose, and no scene, speech, or turn of the Verona story was lifted from Chaucer's long poem of Troy. What Chaucer lends the play is something a source list cannot record: a manner. The machinery by which the lovers reach each other through a scheming intermediary, the dawn parting that ends their one secured night, the language of Fortune that frames their ruin, and the habit of letting a lover's fine rhetoric expose the lover who speaks it all stand in English literature because Chaucer built them there, two centuries before the play. The verdict argued through every section that follows is that Troilus and Criseyde is the play's great English parallel and the ancestor of its manner, parallel rather than source, and that the parallel, drawn carefully, explains more about how Romeo and Juliet works than several entries on its official source list.

The claim needs its limits stated as firmly as its reach. Chaucer's poem and the play share no plot. Troy is not Verona, the siege is not the feud, and Criseyde's story ends in a yielding that Juliet's story answers with a refusal. The shared ground is structural and tonal: a secret love conducted through a go-between whose management both enables and endangers it; a heroine of formidable intelligence weighing her own safety against her desire; a hero fluent in the inherited idiom of courtly service; a cosmos described by its sufferers as a wheel or a book of stars; and a catastrophe produced less by villains than by the ordinary operations of politics, timing, and exchange. Where the two works meet, they meet as members of one tradition that Chaucer, more than any other English writer, had already naturalized. To read the play beside the poem is to watch a dramatist inherit a grammar and compress it, turning five books of deliberation into five days of action.

## Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde: The Poem Behind the Play's Manner

Chaucer composed Troilus and Criseyde in the middle 1380s, building his longest complete poem out of Boccaccio's Il Filostrato, the Italian account of Troilo and Criseida written half a century earlier. The scale of the English poem declares its ambition at once: five books, 8,239 lines, carried in rhyme royal, the seven-line stanza Chaucer raised to the dignity of tragic narrative. Its setting is Troy in the years of the siege, and its premise is a withdrawal from the war. The poem's subject is not the fighting but a private catastrophe conducted in chambers, gardens, and sickrooms while the public catastrophe gathers outside the walls. Troilus, youngest son of King Priam and the city's second warrior after Hector, falls in love with Criseyde, a young widow whose father, the priest Calchas, has defected to the Greeks after foreseeing Troy's fall, leaving his daughter exposed in a hostile city that has reason to hate her name. Her uncle Pandarus, Troilus's friend and her protector, becomes the engineer of the affair: he coaxes, carries, stages, and stage-manages the lovers into each other's arms, and the poem gives him nearly as much dialogue as the hero.

What Chaucer did to Boccaccio's poem is the founding fact of the parallel, and C. S. Lewis, in his 1932 essay "What Chaucer Really Did to Il Filostrato," stated it with a precision later scholarship has refined without overturning. Lewis showed that Chaucer aged Pandarus from a young gallant, a cousin of the heroine and a fellow suitor in his own right, into the middle-aged uncle whose love affair is the lovers' affair and whose comedy is the comedy of the excluded manager. He showed that Chaucer deepened Criseyde from the comparatively legible inconstant of the Italian poem into a consciousness, rendered from inside through long deliberative soliloquies, whose every step toward Troilus is weighed against her danger. And he showed that Chaucer wrapped the whole action in a philosophical frame drawn from Boethius, whose Consolation of Philosophy Chaucer had himself translated as the Boece, so that the love story keeps opening onto questions of Fortune, necessity, and free choice that the Italian poem never asks. The result, in Lewis's account, is less a translation than a refounding: the matter is Boccaccio's, the manner is Chaucer's, and the manner is what English literature inherited.

The poem's own texture enacts that inheritance at the level of the line. Its narrator presents himself as a mere servant of the servants of love, a historian translating an old Latin author he calls Lollius, apologizing for his material and pleading with his audience for the lovers. The fiction is transparent and deliberately so: the poem knows it is a remake, announces its debts, and builds its authority out of the handling rather than the invention. This is precisely the posture of the play that stands at the end of the same tradition two centuries later, a tragedy built by remaking Brooke's poem and Painter's prose, whose Chorus likewise apologizes for the stage and asks the audience to piece out the imperfections with their thoughts. Neither work pretends to originality of plot. Both stake everything on treatment, and the line of descent between them runs through that shared conviction.

Access matters to any claim of influence, and on access the record is unusually good. Chaucer's works were print classics of the sixteenth century. William Thynne's edition of the Workes appeared in 1532 and was reprinted through the century; John Stow's edition followed in 1561; Thomas Speght's folio Workes of 1598, with its glossary and life of the poet, was reissued in 1602, within the working lifetime of the playwright whose company was then staging the lovers of Verona. Troilus and Criseyde stood in those folios complete. A London writer of the 1590s did not need a patron's library to read Chaucer's poem; the book trade sold it, glossed it, and marketed it as the foundation of English poetry. Whatever Shakespeare took from Chaucer, the opportunity to take it was public, cheap, and continuous, and that fact disciplines every argument in this article: the parallel rests on a tradition the playwright demonstrably lived inside, not on a hypothetical encounter with a rare book.

Two accuracy points about the poem itself are flagged in the brief for this article and belong on the record. The first is attribution. The poem is Chaucer's beyond serious dispute: the manuscripts ascribe it to him, he lists a "book of Troilus" among his own works in the prologue to The Legend of Good Women, and the sixteenth-century folios print it in the canon of his Workes, which is where the play's generation encountered it. The second is the poem's own false attribution, the fiction that it translates a Latin author named Lollius. No such author existed; Lollius appears to be Chaucer's invention, possibly fathered by a misread line of Horace, and the pretended Latin original is a device rather than a deception, since the poem's actual main source, Boccaccio's Italian Filostrato, was a book Chaucer's learned readers could in principle consult. The fiction matters to the parallel because the play performs the same manoeuvre in reverse: its Chorus names no author and pretends to no source, yet the play is as thoroughly a remake as the poem, and both works prefer to let the story seem found rather than made. In each case the posture is a claim about authority. The story is old, the presenters say; only the telling is new; judge the telling. The play's earliest audiences, meeting that posture in the theatre, had been trained to recognize it by the most famous English poem of the previous two centuries.

The editions through which the poem is now read confirm the same standing. F. N. Robinson's edition of The Works of Geoffrey Chaucer, in its second form of 1957, set the text for generations of students, and Barry Windeatt's Longman annotated edition of Troilus and Criseyde, published in 1984, gave the poem a line-by-line commentary that treats it as a self-sufficient tragic masterpiece rather than a quarry for later writers. Windeatt's Chaucer is a poet of radical sympathy, reluctant to judge his heroine and exact about the pressures that move her, and that reading informs the comparison with Juliet offered in a later section. The poem the play inherited was already, by the time scholarship finished describing it, the English language's fullest study of love conducted under doom, which is exactly the study the play compresses.

## Parallel or Source? Weighing the Chaucer Claim

The discipline of source study reserves the word source for a text the writer demonstrably used in composition, a text whose matter passes into the new work in traceable form. By that standard the play's sources are the two English versions of the Italian novella: Brooke's Romeus and Juliet of 1562 and Painter's prose version in the Palace of Pleasure of 1566, and the full genealogy, from the Italian novelle through those English renderings, is traced in [The Sources of Romeo and Juliet: A Family Tree](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), which assigns Chaucer no branch on the tree. Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare set the modern standard for such questions, prints Brooke and Painter as the play's sources and places Chaucer's poem among the analogues, the works that illuminate by resemblance without supplying matter. Brian Gibbons, editing the play for the Arden Shakespeare in 1980, tells the same story in his introduction: the narrative line runs through the Italian tradition and its English translators, and Chaucer does not appear in it. These judgments are correct, and this article does not contest them. It contests only the inference, common in casual accounts, that a work excluded from the source list therefore contributed nothing.

### Did Shakespeare take any scene of the play directly from Chaucer's poem?

No scene of the play reproduces a scene of the poem, and no speech translates a Chaucerian passage, so the honest answer is that direct taking cannot be shown anywhere. The closest approaches, the dawn parting and the managed courtship, are conventions Chaucer shares with a wider European tradition rather than inventions he owned.

What the poem supplies is not scenes but the English precedent for treating such scenes at full seriousness and length.

The distinction between source and tradition is the article's central instrument, and it deserves a clean statement. A source supplies matter: incidents, names, arrangements, words. A tradition supplies manner: the available ways of building a scene, voicing a passion, or framing a catastrophe, absorbed from the literature a writer has read and the conventions a culture repeats. Claims about sources are proved by parallels of matter and collapse when the parallels are absent. Claims about tradition are proved differently, by showing that a manner exists in an earlier body of work, that the later writer had access to it, and that the later work deploys the manner at points where its immediate sources do not explain it. Chaucer's poem passes all three tests for Romeo and Juliet. The manner exists in the poem in its most developed English form. Access is established by the print record and by the playwright's demonstrable Chaucerian reading elsewhere. And the deployments occur exactly where Brooke's moralizing poem and Painter's prose run thin: in the management of the go-between, in the rhetoric of the dawn, and in the metaphysics of Fortune.

The playwright's Chaucerian reading elsewhere is not in serious dispute. A Midsummer Night's Dream takes Theseus and Hippolyta, and the frame of a wedding interrupted by entertainment, from the world of The Knight's Tale. The Two Noble Kinsmen, written with John Fletcher late in the career, dramatizes The Knight's Tale directly. The Legend of Good Women stands behind the catalogue of famous lovers that the play itself deploys when Mercutio, mocking the returned Romeo, runs through Laura, Dido, Cleopatra, Helen, and Hero as a roll call of literary ladies (2.4.41-44). A writer who worked from Chaucer at the beginning of his career and returned to Chaucer at its end cannot be supposed ignorant of the most celebrated English love poem of the tradition he was working in. E. Talbot Donaldson, in The Swan at the Well of 1985, built the largest version of this argument: that Shakespeare's reading of Chaucer was the deepest literary relationship of his life, a paternal inheritance absorbed into the imagination rather than copied onto the page. Ann Thompson, whose Shakespeare's Chaucer of 1978 remains the most disciplined study of the question, insists on sterner evidence for each claimed debt, and her book is the necessary check on Donaldson's eloquence. The adjudication between them is taken up in full near the end of this article; its outcome governs the wording of every claim made between here and there. Nothing in the sections that follow asserts a borrowing that Thompson's method would strike out, and everything in them asserts the kind of debt Donaldson described.

The clearest way to see what kind of debt this is runs through the parallel that scholarship grants without quarrel. The story of Tristan and Iseult, treated in [Tristan and Iseult: The Older Love-Death Story](/2025/12/12/tristan-and-iseult-parallel/), stands to the play exactly as Chaucer's poem does: no scholar lists it among the sources, and no serious account of the play's imaginative world excludes it, because the love-death pattern it perfected is part of what the Verona story means. Chaucer's poem holds the same status with one advantage over the Tristan legend: it is an English book, printed and glossed in the playwright's own city, written by the poet the age itself named the father of English verse. When the play's Chorus compresses the whole action into a sonnet and calls the lovers star-crossed (Prologue, 6), it speaks in a cosmos of hostile stars and managed secrecy that Chaucer's poem had made native. The tradition is the medium; the poem is its greatest English reservoir.

Keeping the poem and the play open side by side is a discipline of paired reading, and readers who pursue it build a private apparatus of parallels, passages marked against passages, that ordinary notebooks scatter and lose. A local-first research vault designed for exactly such paired study, such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), lets a reader keep the parallel chart, the marked passages, and the running verdict in one ordered place, which is the practical form the argument of this article takes when it leaves the page. The chart itself follows, and the sections after it argue the chart row by row.

## The Servant of Love's Servants: Chaucer's Narrator and the Play's Chorus

A further piece of the shared manner deserves its own section, because it governs how both works ask to be received. Chaucer's poem is told by a presenter. Its narrator introduces himself in the first book as a servant of the servants of the God of Love, a man unfit for love himself who has undertaken to translate the story out of his Latin author, Lollius, for the benefit of lovers in his audience. The posture is maintained for five books and it does heavy work. The narrator apologizes for his material when it shames his heroine, pleads with his hearers to judge her gently, confesses where his source fails him, weeps at the catastrophe he is obliged to report, and keeps reminding the audience that the ending is known in advance and cannot be amended by his telling. He is, in the technical term the poem invites, a compiler rather than a maker, and the fiction of compilation is the poem's licence for its sympathy: a mere translator cannot be blamed for the story, and so he is free to love it in public. The posture also frames the poem's deepest theme. A story told by a man who cannot intervene in it, about lovers who cannot escape it, for an audience that knows the ending, is a story about necessity at every level of its construction.

The play opens with the same device translated into theatrical terms. The Chorus of the Prologue is a presenter, not a participant: a single voice that steps forward before the action, announces the whole plot including its ending, apologizes for the limits of the stage, and asks the audience to supply with their thoughts what the performance cannot show. "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny" (Prologue, 3) and the lovers are "star-cross'd" and dead by line six, exactly as Chaucer's narrator foretells his catastrophe in his opening stanzas and returns to the foretelling throughout. In both works the foreknowledge is the point. Suspense is deliberately abolished so that attention can be transferred from what will happen to how it will feel as it happens, and pity, rather than surprise, becomes the governing emotion of the audience. The device is not unique to these two works, but its English pedigree runs through the poem: Chaucer made the apologetic, foreknowing presenter the natural voice of a tragic love story in English, and the play, which had no narrator available to it, rebuilt the voice as a Chorus and set it at the door. Even the second Chorus of the play, at the head of the second act, performs a Chaucerian office, summarizing the situation, weighing the lovers' difficulties with a compiler's detachment, and excusing the speed of the new passion by the violence of the old one's extinction. The manner descends because the problem descends: any English work that remakes a famous love story for an audience that knows it must first solve the problem of the presenter, and Chaucer's solution was on the shelf, in print, glossed, and canonical.

The difference between the two presenters is as instructive as the resemblance. Chaucer's narrator is implicated: he has served love's servants all his life, he suffers with his characters, and his final withdrawal from them, in the envoy and the palinode, costs him a visible effort. The Chorus is not implicated at all; it speaks twice and vanishes, and the play thereafter manages its sympathy without a guide, trusting the scenes to do what Chaucer trusted his narrator to do. The dramatist, that is, absorbs the presenter's functions into the action itself, giving the foreknowledge to the audience and the pleading to the characters. What descends is not the figure but the contract the figure negotiated: the audience agrees in advance to watch known events with fresh feeling. That contract, first drawn in English by Chaucer's poem for a story out of Boccaccio, is the contract under which the play still operates every time its Prologue is spoken.

The go-between row shows the machinery shared and the fate reversed; the heroine row shows the intelligence shared and the constancy reversed; the ending row shows the catastrophe shared and the vantage reversed. Read down the middle column and a pattern emerges that no single row proves: Chaucer's machinery is deliberative. Every device in the poem works by delay, consultation, and management, because the poem's world has time in it, the long time of a ten-year siege, and its people use that time to think. Read down the right-hand column and the same machinery appears with the time removed. The play's go-between carries messages but builds nothing; its meetings are snatched; its separation is decreed in an afternoon; its test of the heroine arrives within days of her wedding. The parallel therefore teaches a precise lesson about the later work's method. Shakespeare did not borrow Chaucer's devices. He inherited a tradition in which the devices existed in their spacious Chaucerian form and subjected them to the compression that defines his tragedy, so that the poem's slow machinery of management becomes the play's fast machinery of failure. The chart's deepest row is the last: Chaucer lifts his hero out of the story to laugh at its sorrows, and the play refuses its audience any such exit, holding the camera, so to speak, inside the tomb until the Prince has finished counting the cost. Tradition supplied the machinery. The refusal of consolation is the play's own.

## The First Sight: A Temple in Troy, a Feast in Verona

Both works begin their love stories with a public seeing, and the architecture of the two scenes is the same architecture. In the poem, the occasion is sacred and civic: the feast of the Palladion, when Troy crowds into the temple of Pallas to view the sacred image that guarantees the city's safety. Criseyde stands among the press in her widow's black, apart and conspicuous at once, and Troilus, moving through the temple with his young knights and amusing himself at the expense of every lover in the company, is caught by the sight of her in mid-mockery. Chaucer renders the fall as a wound delivered through the eye: the look pierces him, his scorn of love is repaid in the coin he had been spending, and the God of Love, whose power he had been deriding, takes his revenge with an economist's precision. The scene's irony is structural and is the poem's signature. The mocker is mocked by the event itself, and the hero's whole subsequent career of service is framed as the sentence imposed for an afternoon's pride.

In the play, the occasion is festive and hostile: the Capulet feast, into which Romeo has been smuggled in a mask, at the urging of friends, for the declared purpose of comparing Rosaline with other beauties and curing himself by the comparison. He sees Juliet across the room, dancing, unknown even by name, and the play gives the fall the same instantaneous and ocular form: "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight! / For I ne'er saw true beauty till this night" (1.5.51-52). The irony is again structural. Romeo has come to the feast as a connoisseur of his own suffering, a young man performing the lover's part for an absent mistress, and the event converts the performance into the reality in a single glance, exactly as the temple converts Troilus's scorn. In both scenes the hero falls in public, in company, in the middle of a ritual occasion whose public meaning has nothing to do with love, and in both the fall is mediated by the eye before a word is exchanged. The convention of love entering by the eye is as old as the romance tradition itself, and neither work invented it. Chaucer's poem is, however, the convention's great English staging, the place where the first sight of a beloved in a sacred public space was first given, in English, the full psychological and ironic treatment: the crowd, the black dress, the mocker's punishment, the wound of the look. The play's feast scene, with its torches, its masked intruders, and its hero struck still in the middle of a dance, is the convention's second great English staging, and it follows the first in everything except duration.

The reciprocity of the two scenes marks the distance travelled between them. In the poem, the seeing is one-directional for a long time. Criseyde does not fall at the temple; she does not, in any operative sense, see Troilus at all until Pandarus has spent a book preparing the encounter, and her first real sight of him is the managed glimpse from her window as he rides back from the field, blushing, in Book 2, a sight arranged by her uncle and consumed in private. Her desire is therefore constructed, deliberated, and argued into being, in keeping with the poem's whole method. In the play, reciprocity is immediate and verbal: within minutes of the first sight the lovers are exchanging a sonnet mouth to mouth, and Juliet's desire is as instantaneous as Romeo's, merely more intelligent in its expression. The tradition Chaucer represents builds love as a process conducted by intermediaries over time. The play, inheriting the first sight entire, abolishes the process at the moment of origin and transfers all the time pressure to what follows. That is why the play needs its go-between for logistics and its poem-descended heroine for deliberation, while Chaucer needs the opposite distribution. The first sight row of the parallel thus ends in the same verdict as the others: the device descends, the tempo is the play's own, and the consequences of the tempo are the tragedy.

## Pandarus and the Nurse: Two Go-Betweens, One Machinery

Every secret love in this tradition requires a manager, and the two works build their managers to opposite specifications. Pandarus is the most fully realized go-between in English literature before the Nurse, and the comparison begins with his sheer competence. He does not carry messages; he constructs situations. Visiting his niece in Book 2, he finds her reading a romance of the siege of Thebes with her women, and he dismantles her defences in a single long scene of pressure, teasing, and staged despair, extracting permission to plead Troilus's cause before he will even name the suitor. He coaches the hero's letter and manages its delivery. He builds the supper at the house of Deiphoebus, where a feigned illness in Troilus and a fabricated threat to Criseyde's reputation from a invented suitor named Horaste conspire to place the lovers in one room under his choreography. He engineers the consummation itself, choosing a night of rain, installing Troilus in a closet of his own house, inventing the jealousy of Horaste a second time to hurry his niece's consent, and withdrawing at the threshold with the poem's most quoted piece of stage management, his instruction to the hero to be bold because the game is won. He even manages the morning after, arriving with jokes and a covered face, absorbing the embarrassment his own engineering created. Pandarus is author, director, and prompter of the affair, and the poem knows it: his delight is the artist's delight in a plot that is coming true.

### Did Chaucer invent the go-between, or inherit him?

He inherited him. Boccaccio's Pandaro is already the affair's manager, but he is a young man, the heroine's cousin and Troilus's contemporary, with a gallant's stake in the game. Chaucer's decisive act was to age him into the uncle, a man past his own loving, whose pleasure in the affair is entirely vicarious.

That exclusion is the invention: not the go-between himself, but the go-between shut out from what he arranges, his comedy darkening into something the Italian poem never attempts.

The Nurse inherits the office with its powers halved and its comedy doubled. She carries messages between the orchard and the Capulet house, and the play makes comedy of the carrying itself: her errand in 2.4 stretches across an afternoon of teasing by Mercutio, her return in 2.5 becomes a small masterpiece of delay, in which the most urgent news in Juliet's life is held hostage to the Nurse's back, her breath, and her grievances about her bones, while Juliet circles her with "O God, she comes! O honey nurse, what news?" (2.5.39). She procures the rope ladder by which the bridegroom will climb. She is, in the play's first half, exactly what Pandarus is in the poem's first three books: the indispensable third party whose labour makes the lovers' privacy possible. But the division of Pandarus's functions in the play is the telling point. His errands descend to the Nurse; his strategy ascends to Friar Laurence, who devises the marriage, the potion, and the letter with an engineer's confidence the Nurse never attempts. Pandarus is one figure in Chaucer. In Verona he is split in two, and the split is the play's diagnosis of its own world: the warmth and the planning that Chaucer lodged in a single uncle have come apart, and the affair will be managed by a servant who cannot plan and a planner who cannot be present.

### Why is Pandarus struck silent at the poem's end while the Nurse is sent away talking?

Because their failures are of opposite kinds. Pandarus fails in knowledge: he has managed everything and understood nothing of the exchange of prisoners or of his niece's heart under pressure, and when Troilus turns on him in Book 5 the poem's great talker finds that speech itself has failed, confessing that he hates Criseyde and can say almost nothing more.

The Nurse fails in counsel: she talks to the end, and her last advice, that Juliet should marry Paris since Romeo is as good as dead (3.5.217-223), is fluent, practical, and a betrayal, for which Juliet dismisses her from the plot's confidence. Chaucer silences his manager; Shakespeare's heroine silences hers, and the difference measures the redistribution of intelligence from the go-between to the heroine that the later sections of this article trace.

The end of each go-between seals the comparison. Pandarus, having sworn he would stand by the affair, is left in Book 5 with his shame and his hatred of the niece he served, a figure the poem neither punishes nor forgives, simply sets down. His last function is to be unable to help. The Nurse, dismissed by Juliet in 3.5, survives into the lamentations of 4.5 and the final tableau as a mourner, her office ended but her affection unextinguished, and the play grants her some of its rawest grief. The tradition that Chaucer founded thus presents the play with a figure whose arc can run either way, toward the manager's disgrace or the servant's sorrow, and the play, characteristically, keeps the sorrow and drops the disgrace, because its catastrophe needs mourners more than it needs culprits. Readers who want the delivered Nurse in her own right, her history, her bawdy, and her long service anatomized, will find her owned elsewhere in this series; here she matters as the inheritor of an office, and the office is Chaucer's creation. The verdict on the go-between row of the parallel is therefore unambiguous: of all the play's machinery, this is the piece whose descent from the tradition Chaucer shaped is most visible, because no Italian novella in the play's direct line equips its lovers with a manager of anything like this centrality, and the English poem that does so predates the play by two hundred years.

One more feature of Pandarus deserves its paragraph, because it migrates into the play almost intact: his proverbial wisdom. Pandarus talks in proverbs the way other men talk in sentences, deploying the collected commonplaces of the culture as instruments of persuasion, and the poem lets a reader feel both their usefulness and their inadequacy, since a proverb can open any door except the one marked by genuine novelty of situation. The Nurse is the play's great proverb-speaker in the same register, her head, as she says of herself, full of the lore of Juliet's childhood, her counsel in the crisis of 3.5 nothing but proverbial prudence applied one scene too late. In both works the go-between's language is the language of the received, and in both works the lovers' catastrophe is, among other things, the failure of received wisdom to govern an unprecedented passion. Chaucer built that irony into the office itself. The play inherited the office with the irony installed.

## Troilus's Rhetoric of Service and Romeo's Petrarchan Apprenticeship

Troilus falls in love in a temple, at the feast of the Palladion in April, and the poem is exact about the mechanics. He has been mocking the lovers among his companions, the practiced young courtier amusing himself with other men's subjection, when he sees Criseyde standing apart in her widow's black, and the God of Love, whose scorned servant he has been, bends his bow. Chaucer's image for the conversion is a piercing: the look goes through him, and his pride, as the poem says, is broken in the instant of the glance. What follows is the full curriculum of the courtly lover as the tradition defined it: the sickness, the pallor, the sleeplessness, the formal prostrations before the lady's worth, the abject rhetoric in which the lover is servant, thrall, and petitioner, and the lady a sovereign whose mercy is his only medicine. Troilus writes, sings, and despairs in the idiom for three books, and the poem's sympathy for him never cancels its clarity about the idiom: this is a language learned from books, and Troilus speaks it the way a brilliant student speaks a language he has mastered without yet having lived in the country where it is native.

### Is Romeo's love for Rosaline a failure of love or of language?

Of language. The feelings are real enough to cost him his sleep and his company, and the play never calls them counterfeit; what it exposes, through Mercutio's mockery and the Friar's rebuke, is that the idiom arrives ready-made, a set of oxymorons and conceits learned from the sonnet tradition rather than earned from the woman.

Juliet cures the language by answering it, and the cure is the play's point: the apprenticeship ends when the beloved starts talking back.

Romeo enters the play as Troilus's fellow student, one stage further back in the curriculum. His passion for Rosaline is conducted entirely in the inherited idiom and entirely at a distance: the beloved does not speak, does not appear, and has refused the whole enterprise in advance, having sworn to live chaste. What remains is the rhetoric itself, which Benvolio and Mercutio quote back at him as a set piece. "O brawling love, O loving hate, / O anything of nothing first create!" (1.1.176-177): the oxymorons are the tradition's small change, the currency of the Petrarchan lover since the sonneteers Englished the Canzoniere, and the play's handling of them is Chaucerian in the precise sense that Chaucer's handling of Troilus established. The poem treats its hero's high style with affectionate irony, letting the rhetoric be beautiful and diagnostic at once, the symptom of a noble nature performing a role. The play compresses the same double judgment into Mercutio's cruelty and the Friar's aphorism: "Young men's love then lies / Not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3.67-68). Both works refuse the simple verdicts. The early rhetoric is not hypocrisy, and it is not yet love. It is apprenticeship, and the tradition Chaucer bequeathed is the tradition that knows the difference.

The place where the inheritance runs through a named channel deserves its sentence, at the length the neighbouring articles allow. In Book 1, at the depth of his first despair, Troilus sings the poem's first song, the Canticus Troili, and the song is Chaucer's rendering of Petrarch's sonnet "S'amor non è" from the Canzoniere, the earliest substantial Englishing of Petrarch's lyric voice. Chaucer is, among his other titles, Petrarch's first English translator, and the line of transmission that runs from the Canzoniere through the English sonneteers to Romeo's Rosaline speeches passes at its head through Chaucer's poem, where the Petrarchan paradoxes of sweet wounding and icy fire are naturalized in English a century and a half before the sonnet vogue. The conceits themselves, and the play's mockery of them, are owned by the study of [Petrarchan Conceits and How the Play Mocks Them](/2025/01/10/petrarchan-conceits-romeo-juliet/), and by the article that follows this one on Petrarch's Canzoniere behind Romeo; the point that belongs here is narrower and structural. In Chaucer, the Petrarchan song is a set piece inside a narrative that will test it against events. In the play, Romeo's Petrarchan speeches are set pieces inside a drama that tests them the same way. The test, in both works, is administered by the beloved's reply.

For the reply is where both works turn. Criseyde, once the affair is joined, talks back to Troilus's high style with a realism that keeps puncturing it: she teases, she stipulates, she reminds him of her danger, and her prose sense inside the poem's verse is the standard against which his fluency is measured. Juliet does the same at higher speed. The sonnet the lovers share at the feast (1.5.93-106) begins as Romeo's set piece and ends as a duet she co-authors, redirecting his pilgrim conceit toward the kiss it was designed to reach; the balcony scene then subjects his vows to her editing, as she refuses the moon as a witness because it is inconstant (2.2.109-111) and warns him that the contract is "too rash, too unadvised, too sudden" (2.2.118) in the very act of making it. Troilus's rhetoric survives Criseyde's replies diminished but intact, because the poem's world still believes in the idiom. Romeo's rhetoric is not diminished but transfigured by Juliet's, exchanged for a plainer music the play marks as maturity. The difference is the difference between the fourteenth century and the sixteenth, between a tradition at its height and a tradition being consciously outgrown, and it is visible only because Chaucer's poem preserves the height so fully. That is the service the parallel performs for the play's most famous quality, its language: it gives the critic a baseline. Without Troilus, Romeo's early style looks like a young man's folly. With Troilus, it looks like what it is, the last fluent performance of a two-hundred-year-old English manner, delivered on the eve of its supersession by the woman standing on the balcony.

One scene of the poem completes the account of the idiom, because it shows the idiom being taught. In Book 2, in the garden of Criseyde's house, her niece Antigone sings a song in praise of love, a formal lyric celebrating the lover's estate as the highest human happiness, and Criseyde, listening, asks who made it and remarks on the strangeness of its confidence. The scene is the poem's quiet thesis about its own language: the rhetoric of love circulates as art before it is lived as experience, and the heroine is being educated by a song at the very moment her uncle is arranging for her to become its subject. The play stages the same education at theatrical speed. Juliet has plainly read nothing and heard everything; her fluency at the feast, where she completes Romeo's sonnet as if she had rehearsed it, is the dramatist's version of Antigone's garden, the beloved already fluent in the idiom the hero thought was his private accomplishment. In both works the discovery that the woman knows the language better than the man is the hinge on which the love story turns from performance toward reality, and the discovery is Chaucer's scene before it is Shakespeare's.

## Criseyde's Sliding of Courage and Juliet's Constancy

The heroine is where the parallel does its hardest work, because Criseyde and Juliet are built from the same materials and driven to opposite ends. Begin with the materials. Both are very young women in cities that regard them as assets in a political settlement. Both are fatherless in effect if not in fact: Criseyde's father has defected and become her chief danger, and Juliet's father treats her consent as a formality in a transaction with the Prince's kinsman. Both are guarded, watched, and dependent on intermediaries for any contact with the men they love. And both are, by the standards of their fictions, formidably intelligent, the quickest minds in their respective works, running rings around the rhetoric of the men who court them. Chaucer's poem renders Criseyde's intelligence as deliberation. Her great soliloquy in Book 2, after Pandarus has named the suitor, is a sustained act of self-counsel in which she weighs Troilus's rank, his reputation, her widow's estate, her freedom, and the peril of refusal, and arrives at inclination by an audit any modern reader recognizes as rational. The poem's most famous tribute to her is her own sentence upon herself: she is, she reflects, her own woman, well content with her arrangement of life, and the tragedy of the poem can be stated as the process by which a woman who begins with that sentence is manoeuvred out of it.

### Is Criseyde a model for Juliet, or a contrast to her?

A contrast, constructed from shared parts. Both heroines deliberate, stipulate, and outthink the men who court them, and both face a second settlement pressed on them by politics when the first love is removed, and at that fork the two works part company for good.

Criseyde, exchanged and unprotected, yields by degrees to Diomede, while Juliet, threatened with Paris, chooses the potion, the vault, and finally the dagger. Chaucer writes the heroine who survives by bending. The play answers with the heroine who refuses to bend and does not survive.

The yielding itself must be described exactly, because the poem's greatness lies in its refusal to make the yielding simple. In Book 4 the Trojan parliament, against Hector's protest, votes to exchange Criseyde for the captured Antenor, and Calchas, her father, receives her in the Greek camp. She leaves Troy swearing to return within ten days, and the poem makes the oath credible: she goes intending to outwit her father and steal back. What undoes her is not appetite but arithmetic. Diomede courts her with a soldier's directness, offering the protection of the winning side; her father's house is her only shelter; the city she loves is visibly doomed; and Troilus, for all his rank, cannot fetch her. The poem narrates the transfer of her allegiance as a slide, the word critics have fastened on for its exactness, a gradient rather than a fall, each concession small, the sum irreversible. Her own verdict on herself, in the letter and in the narrator's report, anticipates the tradition's verdict with horror: she knows that her name will be rolled on many tongues, that no good word will be written or sung of her henceforth, and the poem grants her the dignity of knowing it. Chaucer's narrator, at the crisis, performs the poem's characteristic act of mercy, begging his audience to excuse her yet for pity, and the plea is the measure of the poem's method. It does not acquit her. It declines to enjoy her condemnation.

Juliet's plot is Criseyde's plot run backward under compression. The second settlement, Paris, is pressed on her not after a long separation but within days of her marriage, and not by an enemy camp but by her own father in her own house. Her deliberation, the soliloquy of 4.3 with the vial in her hand, matches Criseyde's Book 2 audit fear for fear: the waking in the vault, the suffocation, the ancestral bones, Tybalt's ghost. Where Criseyde's reasoning ends in accommodation with the facts, Juliet's ends in the sentence that closes the speech and opens the catastrophe, her resolution to drink rather than be false. The play even gives its heroine Criseyde's situation in miniature and refuses it aloud: the Nurse's counsel in 3.5, to take Paris and let Romeo be forgotten, is precisely the counsel of survival that Criseyde's circumstances eventually impose, and Juliet's rejection of it, her cold dismissal of the Nurse from her confidence, is the play's written answer to the poem. Critics of Chaucer from Lewis onward have insisted that Criseyde is not a villainess but a study in what fear and exposure do to a fine intelligence. The play does not dispute the study. It constructs the counter-case: the same intelligence, the same exposure, a shorter clock, and a refusal so complete that it costs her life. The verdict of the parallel on the heroine row is that the play needs Chaucer's poem the way an argument needs the strongest opposing case. Juliet's constancy reads as commonplace until it is set against the most persuasive account in English of why a woman in her position might not be constant, and then it reads as an achievement.

There is a final, quieter correspondence. Criseyde disappears from her poem before it ends, her last appearance a letter, her fate unrecorded, the narrator unwilling to say more of her death than that authorities differ. The tradition punished her by erasure as much as by infamy. Juliet is granted the opposite ending: a death onstage, a body displayed, a golden statue promised, her name joined to Romeo's in the Prince's closing summary and in the play's own title. The play that inherits Chaucer's machinery also inherits, and reverses, his heroine's sentence. Where the poem's woman is written out of the story she dominated, the play's woman is written into its monument. A reader who knows only the play sees a heroine celebrated. A reader who knows the poem sees the celebration as a correction, two centuries late, of English literature's first great act of female erasure.

## The Aubade: Dawn Parting from Troy to Verona

The dawn parting is the parallel's most celebrated row, and the one where the discipline of this article matters most, because the aubade is a European convention older than Chaucer and the claim must be fitted to the evidence. The dawn song, the alba of Provençal lyric, stages lovers parted by morning and a watchman or a bird announcing the day; Chaucer did not invent it, and the play's scene does not quote his. What Chaucer did was give the convention its fullest English narrative realization, in the morning that ends the consummation night of Book 3, and the realization fixes the elements that the tradition thereafter keeps in stock: the curse on the sun and on the light, the lover's plea that the day be misnamed or disbelieved, the woman as the keeper of practical time against the man's rhetoric of denial, and the parting conducted as an argument about what the morning is. Troilus, at that dawn, turns on the light itself, cursing the sun whose arising ends his bliss and demanding why the day must hurry to its own beginning; Criseyde, holding him, works the other side of the scene, soothing, stipulating, and managing the exit her uncle will stage-manage from below stairs. The scene's emotional logic is that day is not a fact but an opponent, and the lovers' first shared act as lovers is to conspire against it.

The play's dawn scene in 3.5 conducts the same conspiracy with the elements sharpened into the form readers now quote. Juliet opens it in denial: "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day. / It was the nightingale, and not the lark" (3.5.1-2), and the debate that follows, over which bird has sung and whether the light in the east is morning or a meteor, is the Chaucerian dawn argument compressed into thirty lines and decided, as in the poem, by the woman. Romeo is willing to die on the point of interpretation, offering to stay and accept capture if she will call the light night; Juliet, watching the day actually break, reverses herself and drives him out, choosing his life over her rhetoric in the scene's great act of practical love. The scene's own genealogy inside the play and the wider dawn tradition is treated at length in [The Aubade: Lark or Nightingale at Dawn](/2023/10/13/lark-or-nightingale-aubade-analysis/), which also carries the comparison with Criseyde's parting from the scene's point of view; the claim that belongs to this article is the tradition claim. Between Chaucer's Book 3 dawn and the play's 3.5 stands no provable borrowing, and behind both stands the alba. But the English line of the convention runs through Chaucer's poem as its widest channel, and the play's scene is unintelligible as an achievement unless a reader knows how much of its work the convention is doing: the misnaming of the bird, the curse on the light, and the woman's surrender of the argument to the facts are all inherited moves, executed at a speed that turns inheritance into virtuosity.

One asymmetry rewards attention. In the poem, the dawn parting is not final. The lovers part for a day, meet again, and conduct their affair across a spacious middle of the poem in which dawns recur as punctuation; the convention is a recurring toll, not a death knell, and its pain is the pain of repetition. In the play, the dawn parting is the last time the lovers speak alive together. The compression that governs the whole comparison governs this row most cruelly: Chaucer's device for marking the rhythm of an affair becomes Shakespeare's device for ending one, and the audience that has been trained by the scene's opening wit to enjoy the debate discovers, in the space of a minute, that the debate was the farewell. Juliet's ill-divining soul, her vision of Romeo as one dead in the bottom of a tomb (3.5.55-56), is the play stepping outside the convention to say what the convention cannot: that this dawn, unlike all the dawns of Troy, is the last one. The tradition supplied the instrument. The play plays it in a register the tradition had not used.

The poem's dawns repay one more look, because Chaucer rings the convention's changes with a completeness that amounts to a survey of its possibilities. The Book 3 dawn is erotic and reluctant, the lovers' conspiracy against the light conducted in whispers while the manager waits below. But the poem returns to morning at its catastrophe and reverses the valence: in Book 5, Troilus rides out daily to the gates of Troy to watch for a return that the reader knows will never come, and the rising and setting of the sun become the machinery of his torment, each dawn renewing a hope that each evening executes. The convention that began as the enemy of a night's pleasure ends as the metronome of a life's disappointment. The play, with one dawn available to it, fuses the poem's two uses into a single scene: the 3.5 parting is at once the reluctant erotic dawn of Book 3, with its debate over the bird, and the first stroke of the Book 5 metronome, since Juliet's vision of Romeo as a corpse in a tomb converts the morning into a forecast. Compression, here as everywhere in the comparison, is not mere shortening. It is the discovery that the tradition's separate effects can be made to occur simultaneously, in the same lines, for an audience trained by the convention to feel both at once.

## The City Around the Lovers: Parliament and Prince

Neither catastrophe is caused by a villain, and in both works the destructive agent is a city's ordinary machinery working as designed. Troy in Chaucer's poem is a functioning polity under siege, and its procedures are the tragedy's engine. The catastrophe of Book 4 is a vote. The Trojan parliament, pressed by the Greek demand and by Calchas's prophecy-mongering on the other side, decrees the exchange of Criseyde for the prisoner Antenor, and the poem stages the decree with a lawyer's attention to its forms: the commons cry for the bargain, the lords assent, and Hector, the one man whose judgment the poem wholly respects, protests in vain that the Trojans do not sell women and that Criseyde is no prisoner to be traded. His protest is recorded and overridden by the very order he defends. Chaucer's point is cold and Boethian: no malice is required. A city managing a siege by exchange and requisition will produce this result the way a wheel produces a revolution, and the lovers, who have built their happiness in the city's interstices, are crushed by the city's normal business resuming. Even the war's politics are inherited folly: Paris's theft of Helen, an elder generation's crime, is the reason the city is besieged at all, and Troilus pays for his brother's offence with a completeness the poem never stops noticing.

Verona's machinery is the same machinery in civic dress. The play has no villain either; it has a feud, which is an elder generation's crime inherited by children who cannot name its origin, and it has a Prince whose decrees, reasonable in themselves, generate the catastrophe by sequence. The banishment of Romeo in 3.3 is an act of measured justice, a mitigation of the death sentence the law allowed, and it is precisely the mitigation that kills, since exile separates the lovers, drives the secret marriage into the potion plot, and sets the clock that the failed letter cannot beat. The Capulet household operates as Troy's parliament in miniature: Paris's suit is a settlement negotiated between men, Juliet's consent treated as a ratification to be assumed, and her father's rage when the ratification is refused is the rage of a polity whose forms have been breached. In both works, then, the lovers are not destroyed by opposition to the social order but by the social order itself, proceeding by votes, decrees, exchanges, and settlements, each step defensible and the sum fatal. Chaucer's poem is the earliest English masterpiece built on that grim recognition, and the play's civic frame, so different from the moralizing frame Brooke imposed on the same story, in which disobedient children are simply punished, stands closer to Chaucer's politics than to its own source's theology. Brooke blames the lovers. Chaucer blames the arrangements. The play, whatever its Chorus says about stars, shows the arrangements doing the work, and a reader tracing that preference to its English root arrives at the poem.

This is also the place to register the household parallel that underlies the civic one. Both heroines live under patriarchal roofs where their marriages are state business: Criseyde in her uncle's protection and her traitor father's long shadow, Juliet in her father's house with a mother who delivers the marriage proposal as a courtier delivers a brief. Both works give the fathers' generation the language of settlement and the daughters the language of experience, and both derive their sharpest ironies from the gap. Chaucer's Troy and Shakespeare's Verona are, in this respect, one city at two dates, a place where a young woman's hand is a treaty term and her heart is a state secret. The poem taught English literature to build that city. The play inherited the ground plan.

## Fortune's Wheel: Boethius Behind Both Catastrophes

Chaucer is the only major English poet who translated the Consolation of Philosophy entire, and Troilus and Criseyde is saturated with the book he translated. Boethius, writing in prison under sentence of death, personified Fortune as a blind power turning a wheel that raises and casts down without regard to desert, and set against her a Philosophy arguing that the goods she distributes are not goods at all. Chaucer's poem argues with that book for five books. Its lovers live at the wheel's mercy: Troilus raised to the height of the third book and cast down by the exchange of the fourth, Criseyde lifted by love and lowered by politics, and the poem keeps supplying the wheel as the characters' own explanation of their case. At the poem's argumentative centre, in Book 4, the deserted Troilus delivers a long set speech on predestination, adapted from the Consolation's fifth book, reasoning that if all things are foreseen then all things are necessitated and his ruin was never in his power. The poem does not endorse the speech. It surrounds it with Pandarus's common sense, with the narrator's pity, and with events that keep showing human choices inside the machinery of fate, and the Boethian question, whether the wheel turns us or we turn ourselves within it, is left standing as the poem's deepest unresolved chord.

The play inherits the question in the play's own idiom, which is stellar rather than Boethian, and the inheritance is visible precisely in the mixture of the two systems that no single source explains. The Chorus announces "a pair of star-cross'd lovers" (Prologue, 6), Romeo enters reading his own fortune in his misery (1.2), cries "O, I am fortune's fool!" over Tybalt's body (3.1.131), and finally addresses the cosmos in the play's most famous act of defiance: "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24). Juliet, at the dawn parting, apostrophizes Fortune directly and begs her to be fickle, to return Romeo as quickly as she removes him (3.5.181-182), treating the goddess as a person whose inconstancy might be prayed into usefulness. The play's characters thus hold, simultaneously and without embarrassment, the stellar determinism of the astrological tradition and the personal Fortune of the Boethian one, appealing to whichever system offers a lever at the moment of need. That doubleness is Chaucer's legacy in English. The poem had already staged the two systems in collision, the foreknown fall of Troy grinding against the characters' strenuous freedom inside it, and had taught English literature to let its sufferers argue metaphysics in the middle of their ruin rather than merely endure it.

The adjudication this article offers on the Fortune row parallels its verdict everywhere else. Brooke's poem moralizes Fortune into a sermon against disobedient youth, and Painter's prose inherits the moral; neither supplies the play's metaphysical restlessness, its sense that the cosmos is a system the characters can address, bargain with, and defy. The system-addressing habit, the apostrophe to Fortune as a negotiable power, the long Boethian shadow behind a story of a wheel's single turn, descends to the play through the tradition Chaucer founded when he put his Boece and his Troilus side by side as the twin labours of the same decade. A reader who wants the play's Fortune language analysed on its own terms will find it owned by the series' dedicated study of fortune, chance, and accident; the argument here is confined to descent, and on descent the evidence of the idiom is strong. When Romeo calls himself Fortune's fool he speaks a sentence Chaucer's poem had spent five books teaching English to understand.

The poem's darkest piece of Fortune machinery deserves naming, because it has no equivalent in any of the play's direct sources and because its spirit pervades the play's second half. Book 4 of the poem opens not with the lovers but with an invocation: the narrator summons the Furies, the daughters of Night, to assist a book that must now tell of Fortune's reversal, and describes the goddess at her work of casting down the proud. The proem is Chaucer's addition to his Italian material, and it transforms the exchange plot that follows from a piece of war logistics into a demonstration of the wheel: the machinery of state that votes Criseyde away is presented, before it operates, as the instrument of a power that delights in reversal as such. The play has no narrator to perform that office, so it distributes the invocation among its victims. Romeo's cry in 3.1, Juliet's apostrophe in 3.5, and the Friar's exclamation over the failed letter in 5.2 are the proem of Chaucer's fourth book broken into speeches and placed, as drama requires, at the moments of reversal themselves. The inheritance is one of framing habit: both works insist that the audience watch the human machinery and the cosmic claim at the same time, and refuse to let either explanation cancel the other.

## Letters: The Written Machinery of Both Catastrophes

A love conducted in secret is conducted in writing, and the two works are the tradition's twin studies of the letter as an instrument that serves love faithfully and betrays it completely. Chaucer's poem is full of letters, and they are load-bearing. Troilus's first letter, composed in Book 2 under Pandarus's coaching, is the affair's true opening move: a document of formal service, sealed, as the poem notes with a straight face, with the writer's tears for want of other sealing, and thrust by the go-between into his niece's bosom with an instruction to read it alone. Criseyde's reply is a small masterpiece of the deliberative art the poem gives her: warm enough to continue, cool enough to commit her to nothing, written, as she explains, in haste and under observation. Thereafter the affair has a written life parallel to its spoken one, and when distance replaces presence in Book 5, the letters are what remains: Troilus writes into the Greek camp, the replies thin, Criseyde's final letter promises a return she no longer believes she can make, and the written record of the affair ends in the documentary equivalent of her slide. The truth, when it comes to Troilus, comes by a thing rather than a letter, the brooch he had given her, seen on the captured armour of Diomede; but the poem has trained its reader by then to understand that in this story objects carry messages, and that messages, faithfully delivered or not, are the medium in which doom travels.

The play concentrates the whole epistolary tradition into a single letter and makes its non-arrival the hinge of the catastrophe. Friar Laurence's letter to Mantua is composed onstage in substance, entrusted to Friar John, and stopped by plague quarantine, as its bearer reports in 5.2 in the play's most quietly devastating scene of explanation. Everything Chaucer distributes across an exchange, the play loads onto one undelivered sheet. The correspondence of the situations is exact and inverted. In the poem, letters arrive and deceive by degrees, each one true when written and false by the time it is acted on, so that writing is the medium of the slide. In the play, the letter that would have told the truth never arrives, and the false report travels by mouth at the speed of a horse, so that writing's failure is the medium of the fall. Both works understand the letter as a go-between made of paper, subject to the same frailties as the human kind: delay, interception, misreading, and the tendency of the message to be overtaken by the event it describes. Chaucer's poem, in which the management of an affair is substantially the management of its correspondence, is the tradition's fullest exploration of that frailty. The play's single failed letter is the same exploration at the speed of tragedy, and the audience's agony in 5.2, listening to Friar John describe a door he could not pass, is the agony Chaucer spreads across the whole of his fifth book, miniaturized into one errand. The play even closes its documentary circuit as the poem closes its own: Romeo's letter to his father is produced in the tomb scene and read into the record by the Prince, a message that arrives, like Criseyde's last, exactly when its arrival can no longer change anything except the judgment of the survivors.

## Time: The Siege's Decade and the Play's Four Days

The largest single difference between the two works is a quantity, and quantities in narrative are never innocent. Chaucer's poem unfolds across the long duration of the Trojan War: seasons turn inside it, the affair is built across months of management, enjoyed across a spacious middle, and destroyed across the slow logistics of an exchange, a journey, a ten-day promise, and a correspondence that thins over weeks. The poem is full of the calendar. Troilus falls in April, at the Palladion feast; the consummation waits for a night of rain deep into the poem's third book; the separation is dated by the promise of a return on the tenth day, and Troilus counts the days of the promise one by one as they expire, in one of the poem's most quietly terrible sequences of hope administered by arithmetic. Time in the poem is the medium in which character is revealed: given months, Criseyde deliberates; given weeks, she slides; given a decade of war, everyone is worn into the shape the catastrophe requires. The poem's length is not an indulgence. It is the argument, since only duration can show a fidelity dissolving at the speed of life rather than the speed of plot.

The play takes the same story shape and removes the duration, and the removal is its most consequential single act of inheritance management. Romeo and Juliet runs from a Sunday to a Thursday, four days that contain a meeting, a marriage, a killing, a banishment, a forced engagement, a feigned death, and a double suicide. Every device the tradition supplied arrives compressed: the go-between's management, which Chaucer spreads over a book, is squeezed into errands between scenes; the deliberation that Chaucer gives Criseyde across hundreds of lines is concentrated by Juliet into single speeches delivered at the speed of decision; the dawn parting that recurs in the poem as the affair's punctuation occurs once and is final. Critics of the play have long noted that its time scheme is double, with references suggesting both a few days and a longer acquaintance, and the doubleness itself reads like a fossil of the spacious original: the play's clock is the poem's calendar crushed, and the seams show. To set the two works side by side is to learn what compression costs and buys. It costs the slide, the slow credibility of change, which the play replaces with the shock of reversal. It buys the fever, the sense that the lovers' world is burning its fuel at a rate no management can match, which the poem, for all its sorrows, never attempts. Chaucer's lovers are destroyed by time in its ordinary quantity. Shakespeare's are destroyed by its absence, and the parallel's time row may be its most instructive, because it shows a dramatist reading a tradition and identifying, with an engineer's eye, the one variable whose alteration would transform everything else.

## Two Endings: Laughter Above the Sphere, Silence in the Tomb

Chaucer ends his love story by leaving it. Troilus, betrayed and raging through the war in a deliberate seeking of death, is slain by Achilles, and the poem performs its most astonishing manoeuvre: the released spirit rises to the eighth sphere, looks down at the little spot of earth, beholds the vanity of the world he has left, and laughs, in himself, at the woe of those who weep for his death (5.1807-1827). The passage, adapted from Boccaccio's Teseida rather than from the Filostrato, detonates the poem's scale. Five books of the most intimate sympathy ever lavished on a pair of lovers are suddenly viewed from a altitude at which their sorrows are small, and the narrator follows the spirit's cue, turning to his young readers to urge them away from earthly love toward the love that does not fail, before the famous envoy sends the little book out to kiss the steps of the great poets and names, as its earthly patrons, moral Gower and the philosophical Strode (5.1856-1859). Critics have argued for a century over whether the ending retracts the poem or completes it. What is not arguable is its direction: upward and outward, away from the tomb of the hero, into judgment.

### Why does Chaucer's poem end above the world while the play ends inside the tomb?

Because the two works assign judgment to different places. Chaucer, writing a narrative whose fall of Troy is foreknown, can afford to withdraw his hero to a vantage from which earthly love is weighed and found light, and the withdrawal is the poem's final act of meaning.

A stage tragedy has no eighth sphere to withdraw to; its judgment must be delivered in the room where the bodies lie, by the city's ruler, in the hearing of the families who caused the deaths, and the play accepts that confinement as its own final statement about where responsibility lives.

The play's ending is the systematic refusal of Chaucer's exit. No spirit rises from the Capulet tomb. Romeo dies believing a false report, Juliet dies on his body with his dagger, and the play's last movement is not ascent but accounting: the Friar's confession, the letters produced, the Prince's distribution of punishment and pardon, and the promise of statues in pure gold (5.3.291-310). Where Chaucer's ending judges the world from above it, the play's ending judges the city from inside its own crime scene, and the difference is generic as well as temperamental, the difference between a poem that can close in a narrator's voice and a play that must close in the voices of the survivors. Yet the kinship underneath the contrast deserves the last word in this section, because both endings perform the same structural act: both step back from the lovers at the moment of maximum sympathy and insist on a frame larger than the love. Chaucer's frame is cosmic and Christian; the play's is civic. In both, the step back is what makes the work a tragedy of more than private feeling, and the play's civic version is the form the Chaucerian step back takes when the cosmos is no longer available as a stage. The tomb is the play's eighth sphere, the one vantage it permits, and it is underground.

## What Chaucer Lends the Play: Critics, Disagreement, and a Verdict

The evidence can now be weighed, and the weighing should begin with the disagreement the brief for this article requires, stated as fairly as it can be stated. On one side stands E. Talbot Donaldson, whose The Swan at the Well argued that Shakespeare's relationship with Chaucer was the formative literary relationship of his career, a deep imaginative apprenticeship in which the later poet learned from the earlier how a story thinks: how sympathy is distributed, how a narrator or a scene can love its characters and judge them in the same breath, how comedy and catastrophe share a household. On Donaldson's account, the absence of verbal borrowing is beside the point, because the debt is not verbal. It is temperamental and structural, and it will therefore show itself exactly where this article has found it, in machinery, management, and metaphysical habit rather than in lifted lines. On the other side stands the discipline represented by Ann Thompson's Shakespeare's Chaucer, the study that combed the canon for Chaucerian debts play by play and demanded, for each, evidence stronger than resemblance: sustained engagement, demonstrable reading, parallels that coincidence and common tradition cannot explain. Thompson's method, applied strictly, acquits Chaucer of almost every specific charge in the case of this play, and her acquittal is honest, because the specific charges are not provable. No speech is borrowed, no scene is copied, no name is taken. Geoffrey Bullough's classification of the poem among the analogues rather than the sources is the institutional form of the same verdict, and Brian Gibbons's Arden introduction, which derives the play wholly from the Italian line through Brooke and Painter, is its editorial form.

The adjudication offered here is that both parties are right about the thing each is measuring, and the dispute dissolves once the two kinds of debt are kept apart. Thompson measures matter, and on matter the verdict is final: Chaucer supplied the play with nothing, and any account that lists Troilus and Criseyde among the play's sources is wrong and should be corrected wherever it appears. Donaldson measures manner, and on manner the verdict is equally firm in the opposite direction: the play's way of conducting a love affair through a manager, of arguing with the dawn, of apostrophizing Fortune, and of letting rhetoric indict its speaker descends through an English tradition whose supreme early monument is Chaucer's poem, a poem printed throughout the playwright's lifetime and demonstrably read by him at the start and close of his career. C. S. Lewis supplies the synthesis the dispute needs, since his account of the poem locates Chaucer's originality precisely in the manner, in the aged Pandarus, the deliberative heroine, and the Boethian frame, rather than in the matter he took from Boccaccio. If Chaucer's greatness lies in what he did to his source, then Shakespeare's debt to Chaucer will lie in what the play does with the same inheritance, and the two debts are the same kind of fact. The tradition is not a lesser category of influence. For a dramatist working, as this one always worked, by remaking inherited stories, the tradition is the main category, and the sources are merely its most recent deliveries.

What Chaucer lends the play can therefore be listed with confidence, provided the list is headed by its own rule: everything on it is a manner, not a matter. He lends the go-between as a major character, the office split in the play between the Nurse's warmth and the Friar's strategy, neither half intelligible without the whole figure Chaucer built. He lends the dawn parting as an argument about the morning, the lovers conspiring to misname the light, with the woman as the keeper of the facts. He lends Fortune as an addressee, a power to be cursed, bargained with, and defied, behind whom stands the Boethius he translated. He lends the deliberative heroine, the woman whose audit of her own situation is the intellectual centre of her work, and lends her in a form the play could answer, so that Juliet's constancy is legible as a reply to Criseyde's slide. He lends the self-indicting rhetoric of the courtly lover, Troilus's high style admired and diagnosed at once, which becomes Romeo's apprenticeship and Mercutio's target. And he lends the final step back, the insistence that a love story be closed inside a frame larger than love, which the play performs in the tomb because it can no longer perform it in the heavens. The play that resulted is not Chaucerian in its allegiances; it is faster, crueller, and more civic than its ancestor, and it reverses the ancestor's heroine, its ending's direction, and its judgment of the go-between. Reversal, however, is a form of descent. A writer does not trouble to reverse what he has not absorbed.

The cliché this series exists to correct forgets Chaucer almost completely, remembering the Italian novella and its English translators and stopping there, as if the play's manner had been invented in the 1590s out of nothing. Restoring him shows an English ancestor of the manner standing behind the imported matter, and it changes the reading of the play at every point where the play exceeds its sources: in the management scenes, in the dawn, in the metaphysics, and in the heroine. The final verdict is therefore the one announced at the outset, now with its evidence behind it. Troilus and Criseyde is not a source of Romeo and Juliet. It is the great English parallel, the poem in which the tradition the play inherits had already achieved its fullest form, and the play is best read as that tradition's greatest act of compression and reply.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Chaucer parallel, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Chaucer parallel is the element-by-element chart, set out in the body of this article, that aligns Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde with Romeo and Juliet across thirteen features of the story, from the first sight to the final vantage. The pattern it exposes is consistent enough to serve as the article's thesis in miniature. Wherever the alignment holds, it holds in machinery and manner: the go-between, the managed meeting, the dawn parting, the fortune language, and the correspondence all descend through the tradition Chaucer shaped. Wherever the alignment breaks, it breaks in outcome: the heroine who yields in the poem is answered by a heroine who refuses in the play, the hero who is slain in battle is answered by a hero who dies by his own hand, and the spirit who laughs from above the world is answered by bodies counted in a tomb. The chart therefore maps both the inheritance and the reply, showing a dramatist absorbing a two-hundred-year-old English manner and reversing its conclusions at the points of maximum pressure.

### Q: Who wrote Troilus and Criseyde, and whose Italian poem stands behind it?

Geoffrey Chaucer wrote Troilus and Criseyde, in the middle 1380s, and the Italian poem standing behind it is Giovanni Boccaccio's Il Filostrato, composed roughly half a century earlier. Boccaccio supplied the story's skeleton: the Trojan prince Troilo, the widow Criseida, her cousin Pandaro as go-between, the exchange of the heroine to the Greek camp, and her transfer of allegiance to Diomede. Chaucer's poem follows that skeleton while transforming almost everything hung on it, expanding the Italian work's scale, deepening its heroine into a deliberative consciousness, ageing its go-between into the heroine's uncle, and framing the whole with the philosophy of Boethius, whose Consolation Chaucer had translated. Chaucer complicates the attribution inside the poem itself by pretending to translate a Latin author named Lollius, who never existed; the fiction is a device of modesty and authority, not a real source. The ascription of the poem to Chaucer is secure in the manuscripts, in his own list of his works in The Legend of Good Women, and in the printed folios of his Workes through which later centuries read it.

### Q: How long is the poem, and what stanza carries it from book to book?

The poem runs to five books and 8,239 lines, and it is carried throughout by rhyme royal, the seven-line stanza rhyming ababbcc that Chaucer raised into the prestige form for serious English narrative. The scale matters to the poem's meaning. At that length, Chaucer can give the affair a long construction, a consummation placed at the structural centre in Book 3, and a slow undoing across Books 4 and 5, so that the love story has the duration of a life rather than the span of an episode. Rhyme royal suits the method exactly: each stanza is a small room of thought, closed by a couplet that can settle, qualify, or reverse what the stanza's first five lines proposed, and the poem thinks in those rooms, deliberation by deliberation. The contrast with the play is the article's running theme. Where Chaucer's stanza builds delay into the very unit of composition, the play's blank verse and compressed calendar abolish delay, and the same story machinery, forced through the narrower form, produces tragedy at a different temperature.

### Q: In what ways is Pandarus a more active go-between than the Nurse?

Pandarus is more active in every dimension of the office: he originates the affair, engineers its situations, and manages its crises, while the Nurse executes errands within situations others create. Pandarus selects the moment to name the suitor to Criseyde, coaches and delivers Troilus's first letter, constructs the supper at the house of Deiphoebus out of a feigned illness and an invented rival, stages the consummation on a night of rain in his own house, and manages even the morning after. The Nurse carries messages between the orchard and the Capulet chamber, procures the rope ladder, and keeps the secret, but the play's strategy belongs to Friar Laurence, who devises the marriage and the potion. Chaucer concentrates the whole office, warmth and engineering together, in one figure whose competence drives three books of plot. The play divides the office between a servant who cannot plan and a planner who cannot be present, and the division is itself an interpretation: the integrated manager of the poem has no counterpart in a Verona where affection and authority have come apart, and the affair suffers for the split.

### Q: What stratagem does Pandarus build at the house of Deiphoebus?

Pandarus builds a supper party whose real purpose is to place Troilus and Criseyde in one room under respectable cover, and he constructs it from two coordinated fictions. Troilus, at the house of his brother Deiphoebus, takes to his bed with a feigned illness that explains his pallor and licenses visitors to his chamber. Criseyde is summoned on a fabricated emergency: a suitor named Horaste, Pandarus warns, is preparing a legal assault on her reputation and her property, and only Deiphoebus's protection, begged in person, can save her. Brought to the house to plead her cause, she is led after supper to the sick man's bedside, where the assembled company withdraws by arrangement and the lovers speak at length for the first time, with Pandarus orchestrating the entrances, the exits, and the kiss of reconciliation that seals the interview. The episode, which fills much of Book 2, is the poem's fullest display of its go-between as dramatist, building a set, casting a company, and scripting an encounter whose participants believe they are improvising. Nothing in the play's errands approaches its architectural completeness.

### Q: Where does Troilus first see Criseyde, and what does the seeing do to him?

Troilus first sees Criseyde in the temple of the Palladion, at the April feast when Troy crowds in to view the sacred image, and she stands among the company in her widow's black, apart from the other women. The seeing undoes him on the spot. Troilus has spent the occasion mocking the lovers in the company, amusing his young knights with the spectacle of other men's subjection to love; the God of Love, whose servants he has been deriding, answers the derision by bending his bow, and the glance at Criseyde pierces Troilus in the instant of his pride. Chaucer marks the conversion as a wound taken through the eye and a repayment exacted in kind: the scorner becomes the poem's most abject servant of love, and his sickness, sleeplessness, and formal prostrations follow as consequences of a single look. The scene fixes the poem's pattern of the first sight, public, sacred, and ironic, which the play restages at the Capulet feast when Romeo, arrived as a connoisseur of his own suffering for Rosaline, is struck still by a stranger across a dance floor.

### Q: What is the Canticus Troili, and where does Chaucer place it in Book 1?

The Canticus Troili is the song Troilus sings in Book 1 at the depth of his first despair, and it is the poem's first lyric set piece: a formal address to Love constructed from paradoxes, asking if love is not a god then what it is, if it is a god why it wounds, if it is a wound why it is sweet. Its importance for literary history exceeds its length, because the song is Chaucer's close rendering of a sonnet by Petrarch, the "S'amor non è" of the Canzoniere, and it therefore stands as the earliest substantial Englishing of Petrarch's lyric voice, made roughly two centuries before the English sonnet vogue. Chaucer places it at the exact point where Troilus's sickness needs a voice grander than complaint, and the placement is the poem's method in miniature: the hero's private feeling is expressed in the most public and inherited form available, a translated sonnet. The device descends to the play, whose hero likewise suffers first in borrowed forms, though the sonneteer behind Romeo is treated in the article that follows this one.

### Q: Why does Troilus curse the morning light near the end of Book 3?

Troilus curses the morning light because the dawn ends the consummation night, the single secured interval of the whole affair, and the curse is the aubade convention turned into a lover's quarrel with the cosmos. The night at Pandarus's house has been engineered against every risk, and it succeeds; what no engineering can defeat is the rotation of the heavens, and Troilus, waking to the approaching day, turns on the sun itself, demanding why it must hurry to rise and rehearsing the mythology of envious light that the dawn-song tradition had stocked for centuries. Criseyde answers from the other side of the convention, holding him, soothing him, and managing the practicalities of a departure her uncle is arranging below, so that the scene divides the aubade's labour exactly as the play's dawn scene in 3.5 will divide it: the man curses the morning in rhetoric, the woman concedes to the morning in fact. The curse matters beyond its scene because it is the poem's purest statement of the lovers' position, granted everything except time, and time is the one grant neither Troy nor Verona ever makes.

### Q: What part does Calchas play in his daughter's fate?

Calchas, Criseyde's father, is the remote author of her whole catastrophe, and his part is played almost entirely offstage, which is the point. A priest of Apollo possessed of prophetic knowledge, he foresees the fall of Troy and defects to the Greeks before the poem opens, leaving his daughter behind in a city that has every reason to treat a traitor's child as a hostage. His treason is the reason she lives guarded, watched, and dependent on her uncle's protection, and it is therefore the precondition of the affair. In Book 4 he completes the ruin he began by demanding her return, using the exchange of prisoners as his instrument and the Greek camp as her destination; the father who abandoned her to Troy now reclaims her from Troy, and both motions are presented as calculations of safety by a man who knows the future. Chaucer's handling is quietly devastating: Calchas is never a villain in manner, only a prophet in practice, and his daughter's tragedy is that she is destroyed by being correctly valued by everyone who has power over her, beginning with her own father.

### Q: Why does Hector resist the parliament's vote to exchange Criseyde?

Hector resists the exchange because he judges it dishonourable in itself and worthless as policy, and his protest in the parliament of Book 4 is the poem's clearest statement of the values its catastrophe overrides. When the Trojan assembly, urged by the crowd and by the apparent logic of the trade, moves to surrender Criseyde for the captured Antenor, Hector objects that the Trojans do not sell women, that Criseyde is no prisoner of war to be entered in a ledger of exchanges. The objection is exact: she is a citizen widow under the city's protection, and her exchange converts protection into property. Chaucer gives the protest to the one figure whose authority in Troy is moral as well as military, and then has the assembly proceed anyway, the commons' cry and the lords' assent burying the best judgment in the city beneath the forms of decision. The scene is the poem's political thesis in one sitting. The lovers are not destroyed by wickedness but by a polity transacting its ordinary business, and Hector's defeated decency is the measure of how ordinary the destruction is.

### Q: What argument about necessity and free choice does Troilus make in Book 4?

In Book 4, deserted by the parliament's vote, Troilus delivers the poem's great set speech on predestination, arguing that human choice is an illusion because everything that happens is foreseen by God and therefore necessitated. The speech, adapted from the fifth book of Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, which Chaucer had translated entire, reasons from divine foreknowledge to universal necessity: if the future is certainly known, it cannot be otherwise than known, and the will's apparent freedom is only ignorance of its own chains. Troilus applies the conclusion to himself with grim satisfaction, since it absolves him of acting against the exchange. The poem pointedly refuses to endorse the reasoning. It surrounds the speech with Pandarus's robust common sense, with Criseyde's practical counter-proposal of an elopement, and with the Boethian answer available to any reader of the Consolation, that foreknowledge no more compels an event than eyesight compels what it sees. The speech therefore functions as characterization rather than doctrine: it is the philosophy of a man converting his paralysis into a system.

### Q: Why does the narrator keep asking readers to excuse Criseyde?

The narrator asks readers to excuse Criseyde because the poem's whole method is the suspension of the verdict its audience has already passed, and the pleas are the instruments of that suspension. Chaucer inherits a heroine whose name, through Boccaccio and the wider tradition, is already a byword for female inconstancy, and his readers arrive with the condemnation ready. The narrator's interventions, his confessions that he dares not and will not speak her ill, his plea at the crisis that she be excused yet for pity, and his reminder that she acted under pressures of fear, exposure, and abandonment, are designed to slow that verdict down. The pleas do not acquit her; the poem records the yielding and its cost without flinching. What they refuse is the pleasure of condemnation, the easy enjoyment of a woman's ruin that the tradition had made conventional. Critics from C. S. Lewis to Barry Windeatt treat this refusal as the poem's moral signature, and the plea remains the standard against which later treatments of Criseyde, including the play's silent counter-case of Juliet, are measured.

### Q: Why do critics describe Criseyde's yielding to Diomede as gradual rather than sudden?

Critics describe the yielding as gradual because Chaucer narrates it as a sequence of small, pressured accommodations rather than a decision. Criseyde enters the Greek camp intending fidelity and plotting escape: she swears to return to Troy within ten days and means the oath when she swears it. What follows is erosion under stable conditions. Her father offers no rescue, Troy offers no retrieval, Diomede offers the protection of the ascendant side and courts her with a patience that matches her circumstances to her fears, and the city whose fall her father prophesied is visibly losing. Chaucer marks the process with the poem's most debated gestures of withholding, declining to narrate the interior moment of surrender and letting the reader infer it from the brooch's appearance on Diomede's armour. The gradualness is the poem's defence and its indictment at once: no single moment can be pointed to as betrayal, which is why the narrator's plea for pity is possible, and yet the end of the gradient is betrayal entire, which is why the plea is necessary.

### Q: Where does Troilus's released spirit look down from, and what does it do there?

Troilus's released spirit rises, in the poem's ending, to the eighth sphere of the heavens, the sphere of the fixed stars, and from that altitude it looks down on the little spot of earth with complete detachment and laughs. The passage, adapted from Boccaccio's Teseida, reports that the spirit gazed back at the world it had left, beheld the vanity of earthly things, condemned the weeping of those who mourned his death, and laughed in itself at woe it had so lately inhabited as the whole of existence. The laughter is not cruelty but scale: from the eighth sphere, the siege, the affair, and the catastrophe shrink to the size the cosmos assigns them, and the poem's five books of intimate sympathy are suddenly framed by a vantage from which sympathy itself looks parochial. Chaucer's narrator adopts the vantage for the poem's closing movement, turning from the lovers to his readers and to heaven. The play permits itself no equivalent position, which is why its ending must be delivered by a prince standing among bodies rather than by a spirit laughing among stars.

### Q: How does Chaucer close the poem, and which two contemporaries does he name in the envoy?

Chaucer closes the poem with an envoy in which the book itself is sent out into the world and two living contemporaries are named as its judges. After the palinode that turns from earthly love to divine, the narrator addresses his "litel bok" directly, bidding it kiss the steps where Virgil, Ovid, Homer, Lucan, and Statius pass, submit to correction by all true poets, and be understood according to the diversity of English dialects. He then dedicates the work, in the envoy's famous lines, to "moral Gower" and "the philosophical Strode," asking Gower to correct the book where need is and Strode to receive it as a philosophical friend. John Gower, the poet of the Confessio Amantis, and Ralph Strode, a logician and fellow of Merton College, stand for the poem's twin audiences of poetry and philosophy, the two disciplines the Troilus itself has spent five books uniting. The play closes with no envoy and no named judges; its book is a stage, and its correctors are the audience walking out of the tomb's light into the street.

### Q: In which later Shakespeare play does Pandarus walk the stage as a character?

Pandarus walks the stage as a character in Troilus and Cressida, the bitter Trojan War play Shakespeare built from Chaucer's poem and its tradition around the turn of the century. There Pandarus, the go-between of Chaucer's Book 2 management become a diseased and jeering pander, presides over the affair of Troilus and Cressida, sings for the lovers, brokers their meeting, and closes the play by addressing the audience directly and bequeathing it his diseases. The character is the strongest single proof that the playwright knew the Chaucerian material at first hand: Shakespeare's Pandarus is unintelligible without Chaucer's, since his dramatic meaning is the degradation of the poem's warm, competent, excluded manager into a figure of contempt. The later play is, among other things, a reading of Troilus and Criseyde conducted in a spirit of disillusion, and its existence disciplines every claim made for Chaucer's influence on the earlier tragedy. A dramatist who would one day stage Pandarus himself cannot be treated as a stranger to the poem in which Pandarus was perfected, even where the earlier play's debts must be stated as tradition rather than borrowing.

### Q: What is the strongest evidence that Shakespeare read Chaucer at first hand?

The strongest evidence is the pattern of direct dramatization across the career, above all the two plays that take their matter from The Knight's Tale and the one that takes its principals from Troilus and Criseyde. A Midsummer Night's Dream frames its action with Theseus and Hippolyta, figures whose literary home is Chaucer's tale of Palamon and Arcite. The Two Noble Kinsmen, written with John Fletcher near the career's end, dramatizes The Knight's Tale itself, its prison, its tournament, and its gods. Troilus and Cressida inhabits the world and the personnel of Chaucer's poem, Pandarus included. Around these anchors, scholarship has assembled a wider web of Chaucerian allusion, including the catalogue of famous heroines that Mercutio deploys in this play, and the printed folios of Speght's edition, 1598 and 1602, put a glossed Chaucer in the bookshops during the playwright's maturity. Ann Thompson's disciplined survey of the evidence concludes that the reading was real and sustained. The verdict relevant here is narrower: whatever reached Romeo and Juliet from Troilus came from a poet its author had genuinely read, in a tradition he demonstrably inhabited, not from hearsay.

### Q: How did the poem reach readers of Shakespeare's generation in print?

The poem reached that generation as part of the printed Chaucer, in the collected Workes of Geoffrey Chaucer that the sixteenth-century book trade issued and reissued as a national classic. William Thynne's edition of 1532 first gathered the canon in print, Troilus and Criseyde included, and the collection was reprinted repeatedly in the following decades; John Stow's edition of 1561 refreshed it, and Thomas Speght's folio of 1598, reissued in 1602 with an expanded life of the poet and a glossary of the hard words, gave the age a Chaucer equipped for study. The consequences for the parallel argued in this article are practical. Chaucer's poem was not a manuscript rarity locked in a patron's library; it was a trade book, sold in London, glossed for comprehension, and marketed as the foundation of English poetry, available to any professional writer whose work depended on the inherited stock of English story. When the audience of Romeo and Juliet heard its Chorus present a remade old tale, they heard it in a culture where the greatest previous remaking of an old tale in English sat in print on the same city's shelves.

### Q: Which devices of the play descend through the tradition Chaucer shaped rather than through Brooke's poem?

The devices that descend through the Chaucerian tradition, rather than through Brooke's Romeus and Juliet, are the play's instruments of manner rather than its incidents: the go-between as a major character whose management structures the plot; the dawn parting staged as an argument about the nature of the morning; Fortune addressed as a person who can be cursed, bargained with, and defied; the deliberative heroine whose audit of her own danger forms her work's intellectual centre; the hero's inherited rhetoric of courtly service, beautiful and diagnostic at once; and the closing step back into a frame larger than the love story. Brooke supplies the play's plot, its names, its sequence of meetings, killings, and potions, and a moral framework that blames the lovers' disobedience. What Brooke does not supply is the machinery's warmth or its metaphysics: his poem has no Pandarus, no developed dawn debate, no Boethian shadow, and no sympathy structured like Chaucer's. Where the play exceeds its narrative source in those dimensions, it draws on the reservoir Chaucer filled, which is the precise and limited sense in which this article calls the poem the ancestor of the play's manner.

### Q: How can the Chaucer parallel anchor an essay on tradition rather than direct source in the play?

The Chaucer parallel anchors such an essay by providing the one case in the play's background where the two kinds of influence can be cleanly separated. The essay's structure follows the article's: first, the negative demonstration, that no incident, speech, or name passes from the poem to the play, so that the source claim fails by every test source study applies; second, the positive demonstration, that the play's machinery of mediation, dawn argument, fortune address, and deliberative heroine exists in developed English form in a poem the playwright demonstrably read and his culture continuously reprinted; third, the adjudication between Ann Thompson's evidentiary discipline, which governs the first demonstration, and E. Talbot Donaldson's account of imaginative descent, which governs the second. The essay's conclusion then states the distinction as a general instrument: a source is where a work's matter comes from, a tradition is where its manner comes from, and conflating the two produces both the inflation that lists Chaucer among the play's sources and the deflation that denies him any part in it. The parallel, handled exactly, corrects both errors at once.
