Set the two plays side by side and a familiar story tells itself: a young playwright idealizes love in Romeo and Juliet, and the same playwright, some seven years older, sours on the whole subject in Troilus and Cressida, replacing the sonnet with the sneer and the tomb with a bawd's diseased farewell. The story is neat, quotable, and wrong in the way that matters. Troilus and Cressida does not refute Romeo and Juliet. It audits its predecessor, taking one element that the Verona play contains, isolates, and criticizes from inside, the Petrarchan posture of the lover who prefers longing to knowledge, and building an entire world in which that posture can never be outgrown, because the world runs on price. In Verona, a boy performs Petrarch for an absent girl, is mocked for the performance by his best friend, and is cured of it in a single evening by a girl who answers back. In Troy, a prince performs the same idiom before a whole civilization of appraisers, and nothing in his world ever answers back with anything except a valuation. The difference between the plays is not that one believes in love and the other has stopped believing. The difference is what each world permits love to become when it is tested.

![Troilus and Cressida weighed against Romeo and Juliet as Shakespeare's two visions of love - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict of this comparison can be stated plainly at the outset. Romeo and Juliet gives its lovers a feud that kills them and a language that vindicates them; Troilus and Cressida gives its lovers a war that prices them and a language that keeps dissolving into negotiation. Juliet is constant because the play builds scenes in which constancy can be proved, at a balcony, in a cell, over a vial, in a tomb. Cressida is faithless, if faithless is even the right word, because her play builds scenes in which proof is impossible: she is discussed as cargo, exchanged as a counter in a prisoner swap, kissed in turn by a row of enemy generals, and left alone to diagnose herself in a speech nobody answers. Troilus, the lover, ends the play alive and promising revenge, his rhetoric of eternity converted without remainder into a rhetoric of hatred. Romeo ends it dead beside the woman he married, his rhetoric cashed out in act. One play is a tragedy of love destroyed by its world. The other is a satire of a world that has made love's language legal tender and then debased the coinage.

That reading has to be earned through the texts, scene by scene (quotations throughout follow the Folger Shakespeare Library texts, cited by act, scene, and line), and the texts are unusually ready to supply the evidence, because the later play seems almost designed as a commentary on the earlier one's machinery. Both plays open with a young man disabled by unreturned love while his world demands his labour elsewhere. Both give the lover a wit for a companion and a go-between for an instrument. Both set private desire against a public conflict that has outlived its cause. And at every joint where the machinery is shared, the later play tightens a screw the earlier one leaves mercifully loose.

The inventory of shared machinery deserves a moment's attention in its own right, because its completeness is the comparison's foundation. Each play contains a pair of young lovers separated by a public quarrel; an older generation that manages the quarrel and mismanages the young; a professional or semi-professional intermediary who carries the love plot on his or her back; a licensed scoffer who comments on the action from its edge; a set of formal vows exchanged under supervision; a token whose fate stands for the love's fate; a scene of watching in which one lover misreads or refuses to read the other; and a closing audit in which the survivors total the cost. A dramatist does not reproduce an inventory of that length by accident across seven years. The later play is built on the earlier play's chassis, and the differences that matter are therefore not differences of design but differences of outcome at identical stations. Wherever the earlier machine produces a marriage, a rescue plan, a reconciliation, the later machine, fed the same parts, produces a bargain, an exchange, and a bequest of diseases. The experiment is controlled, and controlled experiments are how a mind tests a belief it once held in earnest.

## A Feud Without a Stated Cause, a War Without an End: Troilus and the Two Backdrops

Verona's feud has no origin the play will state. The Prologue calls it an "ancient grudge" (Prologue, 3) and leaves the adjective to do all the work; no character, in five acts, ever says what the Capulets and Montagues are fighting about, and the omission is structural, because a feud with a stated cause could be argued with, settled, or priced. Troy's war, by contrast, has a cause so well stated that the Trojans debate it in council: Helen, taken by Paris, kept by vote. Yet the effect is identical. A conflict whose cause is unspeakable and a conflict whose cause is indefensible both become permanent weather. They consume the young as a matter of routine, they make every private plan an act of trespass, and they ensure that love, when it comes, must be conducted as smuggling.

The Trojan council scene shows the machinery at its most articulate. Hector, the war's greatest soldier and its clearest mind, argues that Helen "is not worth what she doth cost / The keeping" (2.2.53-54) and that she should be returned, then, having won the argument entire, votes the other way: "Hector's opinion / Is this in way of truth; yet, ne'ertheless, / My sprightly brethren, I propend to you / In resolution to keep Helen still" (2.2.190-200). Verona never stages such a scene, because Verona's feud has no council, no vote, and no Hector to expose its irrationality in ordered speech. The Prince issues edicts and threatens lives; the old men reach for their swords; nobody deliberates. Troy deliberates beautifully and decides insanely, which is the colder diagnosis. A world that cannot discuss its madness might be cured by discussion. A world that discusses its madness in perfect syllogisms and then votes for more of it has placed reason itself in the service of the disease.

### Why does the Trojan council debate its war while Verona never debates its feud?

Troy debates because its war has a statable object, a woman who can be named, valued, and voted on. Verona never debates because its feud has no object left to name; nothing could be proposed, seconded, or surrendered, so the violence stays pre-political, a reflex of households rather than a policy of states, answerable to no argument.

The consequence for the love plots is exact. Romeo and Juliet must hide their marriage because the feud forbids it; the secrecy is a wound inflicted on something whole. Troilus and Cressida must hide nothing at first, their union is arranged by her uncle with the air of a commercial introduction, and yet their love is more thoroughly compromised by its setting than the Veronese marriage ever is, because Troy contaminates by valuation rather than by prohibition. The feud says: this love must not exist. The war says: this love exists, and here is what it is worth, and the second sentence proves harder to survive than the first. When Ulysses delivers the Greek camp's great speech on degree, "Take but degree away, untune that string, / And hark what discord follows" (1.3.109-111), the disorder he describes is a hierarchy eating itself; Verona's disorder needs no speech, because it was never ordered to begin with. Both plays therefore begin from worlds in which the public order has failed, and the failure in each case is measured by what it does to the young pair placed at the centre.

The Greek camp completes the symmetry, since the besiegers are as disordered as the besieged. Ulysses's degree speech is delivered as a diagnosis of his own army: Achilles, the war's indispensable fighter, has withdrawn to his tent to sulk over a slighted reputation, and the command, rather than compel him, promotes the blockish Ajax as a rival exhibit in the hope that emulation will sting the greater man back to work. Honour, in the Greek camp as in the Trojan council, is a managed market in reputations, its prices adjusted by policy. Verona has nothing like this administrative cynicism; its disorder is older and simpler, a blood feud between households that no one administers because no one profits from ending it. The distinction refines the backdrop comparison into a single sentence: the feud is a disorder of the passions, the war a disorder of the institutions, and a love affair has some chance of hiding from the first, in gardens, cells, and tombs, while the second reaches everywhere a form can be filed, a council convened, or a price agreed. Troilus and Cressida are not hiding from their world's attention. They are drowning in it.

The two sovereigns measure the same failure at different altitudes. Escalus governs Verona actively and ineffectually: he decrees death for brawling in the first scene, fines and lectures after the second, banishes after the third, and discovers at the end that his own kinsmen are among the feud's dead, a ruler whose instruments were all sound and all too slow. Priam governs Troy passively: he presides over the council that votes to keep Helen, a decision his own reasoner has just dismantled, and the play never shows him overruling, enforcing, or even commenting; the crown sits in the room while the family decides. One monarchy fails by intervention without enforcement, the other by presence without authority, and the love plots inherit both failures directly: Romeo is destroyed by a sentence from the first kind of sovereign, banishment pronounced in haste and never revisited, and Cressida by a transaction of the second kind, an exchange ratified around a table where no one present was charged with her interests. Between them, the two kings sketch the complete range of ways a state can kill its young without intending to: by acting wrongly and by declining to act at all.

Scale sharpens the point. Verona's feud kills six people in five days and then stops, shamed into statues and a glooming peace. Troy's war has run for years before the play opens and runs on past its close, past Hector's corpse dragged at a horse's tail, toward a fall the audience knows and the play refuses to stage. The earlier play's backdrop is a fire that burns the house down and is then, at terrible cost, put out. The later play's backdrop is a climate. Lovers can, with luck and help, escape a fire for a night. Nobody escapes a climate; they can only dress for it, and Cressida's much-abused adaptability is, on the coldest reading, a girl dressing for the weather her world has made.

Duration is the two backdrops' deepest difference, and it governs everything downstream. Verona's emergency is measured in days: the play's famous compression crowds meeting, marriage, murder, banishment, and the double funeral into less than a week, and the feud's violence, however lethal, has the character of a spasm, after which the exhausted city can be brought to its senses by the sight of the bodies. Troy's emergency is measured in years, and a decade of siege does to a society what four days of brawling cannot: it normalizes. Prices, ranks, reputations, and women are all traded inside a going concern whose participants have forgotten any alternative arrangement and experience the abnormal as routine administration. The lovers' plots inherit the timescales exactly. Romeo and Juliet are destroyed by speed, by decisions taken inside hours and messages delayed by hours. Troilus and Cressida are destroyed by duration, by a separation arranged at leisure, debated in council, executed by heralds, and absorbed into a war with years left to run. The earlier play needs a friar's cell and a plague-quarantined messenger to break its lovers. The later play needs only the calendar.

## Romeo and Troilus: The Petrarchan Posture on Trial

Romeo enters his play sick with a style. The oxymorons of the first act, "O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1.176), are not feelings but quotations from a tradition, the Petrarchan lover's licensed contradictions, and the play wastes no time in identifying the performance as a performance. Mercutio names the author of the script outright: "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in. Laura to his lady was but a kitchen wench" (2.4.38-40). The Friar supplies the diagnosis in doctrinal form: "Young men's love then lies / Not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes" (2.3.67-68). And the beloved who occasions the posture does not exist as a person even in the play's own accounting; Shakespeare took from Arthur Brooke's poem a first beloved whom Brooke never names and gave her a name, Rosaline, while withholding from her a body, a voice, and a single entrance. The name without the woman is the posture's perfect emblem: a label for a feeling that requires no object beyond a direction to sigh in.

What cures Romeo is not time, suffering, or masculine resolve. It is reciprocity. At the feast, the girl he approaches does not receive his prepared idiom as tribute; she picks it up, completes it, and corrects it, turning a pilgrim conceit into a shared sonnet and then teasing the performer for kissing "by th' book" (1.5.110). The love at first sight that the scene stages, examined closely in [the reading of the torches speech and the feast](/2025/03/07/she-doth-teach-the-torches-analysis/), is less a thunderbolt than an audition Romeo passes by surrendering the solo part. From that moment the posture is finished in him. He climbs walls, swears badly and is stopped, marries, kills, grieves, and dies, all in a prose-and-verse register increasingly stripped of ornament, because the ornament belonged to the phase in which love was a monologue.

The two introductions of the lovers stage the whole difference in miniature. Romeo meets Juliet in public, at a feast, unchaperoned by any arranger, and the meeting's form, a sonnet improvised between strangers, is a collaboration neither party could have rehearsed. Troilus meets Cressida in private, in an orchard, delivered veiled by her uncle like goods presented for inspection, and the meeting's form is a negotiation supervised by the broker, who unveils the merchandise, orders the parties to speak, and greets their first kiss with the language of a property transaction: "How now? A kiss in fee-farm? Build there, carpenter; the air is sweet" (3.2.51-52). A fee-farm is a perpetual lease. Even the play's tenderest first moment is described, by its stage manager, as a conveyance of real estate, and the description is allowed to stand uncorrected because nobody in Troy hears anything odd in it. Juliet's first kiss generates a theological joke between equals about pilgrims and saints. Cressida's generates a leasehold joke from a watching relative. The beloveds are comparably witty; the acoustics around them could hardly differ more, and a love's prospects are set less by its own music than by the room it must sound in.

Troilus enters his play with the same sickness and never meets the cure. His first speech abandons the war for the beloved in terms Romeo would have recognized from his own first act: "Why should I war without the walls of Troy / That find such cruel battle here within?" (1.1.1-2). The self-portrait that follows is the Petrarchan lover's complete inventory of delicious incapacities: "But I am weaker than a woman's tear, / Tamer than sleep, fonder than ignorance, / Less valiant than the virgin in the night, / And skilless as unpracticed infancy" (1.1.9-13). Romeo's version of this condition is treated by his play as a fever to be sweated out. Troilus's version is treated by his play as a permanent post. No Mercutio tells him that Cressida makes Laura a kitchen wench. No Friar informs him that his love lies in his eyes. His world contains exactly one licensed commentator on the love plot, Thersites, whose commentary corrodes everything it touches and therefore corrects nothing.

### Is Troilus simply Romeo frozen at the Rosaline stage?

Substantially, yes, with one refinement: the freezing is not a failure of Troilus's character but of his world's resources. Romeo escapes the posture because Juliet answers it, corrects it, and marries him inside a day. Troilus's Cressida answers too, but Troy interposes a war market between the answer and any future, so the posture hardens into permanence.

The refinement matters, because it locates the later play's bitterness in circumstances rather than in a revised opinion of young men. Troilus is not exposed as a fraud. His great speech on desire in the consummation scene is as philosophically serious as anything in the earlier play: "This is the monstruosity in love, lady, that the will is infinite and the execution confined, that the desire is boundless and the act a slave to limit" (3.2.80-84). Romeo, given a mistress who consents, discovers the same disproportion from the fortunate side and calls it bliss. Troilus states it as a law of frustration, and the play then arranges for the law to govern his whole experience: the one night, the dawn parting, the exchange, the watched betrayal. He is a Romeo for whom the plot declines to provide a Friar, a balcony, or a wedding, only a broker, a bedroom, and a transfer order.

Act 5 shows what the posture becomes when its object defects: not disillusion, which would at least be a form of knowledge, but transfer of the same absolutism to revenge. Troilus spends the play's last movement hunting Diomedes across the field, demanding combat over the sleeve, his eternity rhetoric intact and merely readdressed; the energy that was pledged to worship forever is pledged to pursuit forever, and the play closes on his resolve that hope of revenge shall hide the inward woe. Romeo's violence has the opposite trajectory: the boy who would not fight in Act 1 kills Tybalt in grief and Paris in desperation, each killing a step away from the posture and toward the feud's terms, until the final act's violence is turned inward, against himself, in the tomb. Both lovers end as killers or would-be killers; the difference is the direction of the last act of violence, inward in Verona, where it terminates the feud's account in the lover's own body, outward in Troy, where it opens a new account that the war will happily keep. A posture that can rotate from worship to revenge without changing its temperature was never chiefly about its object, which is the audit's hardest single finding about Troilus, and the one his own final couplet confirms.

Even Troilus's finest quality, his fidelity to an idea of value, belongs to the posture's advanced stage. In council he defends Helen with the lover's axiom turned into public philosophy: "What's aught but as 'tis valued?" (2.2.53). Hector's reply is the play's one attempt at an objective standard: "But value dwells not in particular will; / It holds his estimate and dignity / As well wherein 'tis precious of itself / As in the prizer" (2.2.54-57). Troilus cannot accept the correction in politics, and he cannot accept it in love. To price Cressida by anything except his own prizing would be, for him, the first step of betrayal, so he builds her value into a metaphysical absolute, swearing that posterity shall cite "As true as Troilus" as the crown of all comparisons of truth (3.2.178-182). Romeo's hyperboles are spent on a living girl who keeps interrupting them. Troilus's are invested in an image, and an image cannot interrupt, negotiate, or climb down from the pedestal to explain that circumstances have changed. When the image appears to move of its own accord in the Greek camp, the metaphysics has no resource except to split the woman in two: this is, and is not, Cressid. The posture, denied a cure, ends as a philosophy of denial.

## Juliet and Cressida: Constancy, Craft, and the Price of Circumstance

The heroines invite the crudest version of the contrast: Juliet faithful unto death, Cressida false within days, and generations of moralists have accepted the invitation, most brutally in the Scots tradition that Shakespeare inherited. Robert Henryson's Testament of Cresseid, appended to Chaucer in the printings through which the story circulated, takes Chaucer's ambiguous heroine, has her abandoned by Diomede, struck with leprosy for blasphemy against the gods of love, and left to die a beggar after the passing Troilus, arrested by a face he half knows, throws her alms and rides on. That is the tradition's cheap verdict in its purest form: the woman's transfer is a crime of essence, and the poem executes her for it. Geoffrey Chaucer himself had refused the verdict, surrounding Criseyde with excuses, explanations, and a narrator who keeps stepping between his heroine and the reader's sentence. Shakespeare, working the same material, sides with Chaucer's procedure against Henryson's, and the evidence is in how carefully the play prices the circumstances before it permits anyone to judge.

Begin with what Cressida is in her own scenes, before the camp gets its hands on her story. Alone, she coolly audits her own strategy: "Achievement is command; ungain'd, beseech" (1.2.292), the maxim of a woman who understands that in her market, surrender ends bargaining power. Brought to Troilus by her uncle, she makes the most self-aware confession in either play: "Boldness comes to me now, and brings me heart. / Prince Troilus, I have loved you night and day / For many weary months" (3.2.113-115). Then, astonishingly, she annotates her own confession while making it: "Perchance, my lord, I show more craft than love, / And fell so roundly to a large confession / To angle for your thoughts" (3.2.152-154). Juliet confesses too, on the balcony, and calls her own frankness a risk; but Juliet's world lets a confession become a contract by morning. Cressida's confession is immediately notarized by the broker standing over the bed, in a speech that dictates the future's vocabulary with terrible accuracy: "Let all constant men be Troiluses, all false women Cressids, and all brokers-between panders" (3.2.202-205). The verdict on Cressida is pronounced before the crime, by the play's most compromised mouth, as a piece of sales patter. A careful audience is being warned where verdicts in this world come from.

### Does Cressida love Troilus at all, on the evidence of her own speeches?

Yes, on the only evidence the play treats as private: her unguarded speeches report a love of long standing, concealed as policy and confessed with embarrassment at its own forwardness. What the play never gives her is a setting in which that love could be proved over time, a different deprivation from not feeling it.

Her intelligence deserves a fuller description, because the comparison with Juliet turns on the uses to which each heroine's intelligence is put. Cressida's wit is the wit of a negotiator in a permanent minority: she manages information. Her opening soliloquy resolves to conceal a love already felt, on the stated ground that achievement ends a suitor's devotion, and the resolution is less coquetry than statecraft, the one policy available to a fatherless girl in a besieged city whose uncle is her only advocate and whose beauty is her only estate. Juliet's wit, by contrast, is the wit of a testifier: she manages truth. Faced with a mother arranging her marriage, a nurse evading her questions, a lover swearing by the inconstant moon, she insists at each point on exact statement, refuses false oaths, sets terms, and prices risks aloud before accepting them. One heroine is intelligent the way a diplomat is intelligent, because the cost of plain speech would be ruin; the other is intelligent the way a judge is intelligent, because her world, for all its violence, still contains rooms, the balcony, the cell, the tomb, where plain speech is possible. When plain speech becomes impossible for Juliet too, in the scenes of her entrapment between the Paris marriage and the vial, her intelligence changes register and starts to resemble Cressida's, managing appearances, temporizing with Paris in equivocations worthy of the Greek camp. The resemblance is the plays' quiet verdict on the whole question of her sex's constancy: given Troy's conditions, Verona's heroine behaves like Troy's.

Then the machinery of war takes her. Her father Calchas, a Trojan priest who has defected to the Greeks, asks for his daughter as the price of his services, and Troy trades her for the captured Antenor (4.1). The exchange is the play's thesis in a single transaction: a person converted into a counter, her value fixed by treaty. In the Greek camp she is kissed in sequence by the enemy command, Agamemnon, Nestor, Achilles, Patroclus, Menelaus, a ceremonial inspection of the goods, and she survives it by wit, out-talking Menelaus and declining Ulysses with a creditor's joke. Nestor's praise is exact: "A woman of quick sense." Ulysses, the man she has just refused, supplies the official verdict moments later: "There's language in her eye, her cheek, her lip; / Nay, her foot speaks... set them down / For sluttish spoils of opportunity / And daughters of the game" (4.5.63-71). The play's pattern is consistent and damning, though not of her: in Troy, every judgment of a woman is delivered by an interested party, a broker, a refused suitor, a spy, and the audience is positioned to hear the interest inside the judgment. Juliet is judged too, by her father, her nurse, her suitor, but the play keeps handing her the stage alone, the balcony, the vial soliloquy, the tomb, so that her self-account can outweigh the accounts of others. Cressida is granted exactly one such moment, and it comes after the catastrophe, in a soliloquy of self-diagnosis that lands like a medical report on her own ruin: "Troilus, farewell! One eye yet looks on thee, / But with my heart the other eye doth see. / Ah, poor our sex! This fault in us I find: / The error of our eye directs our mind" (5.2.128-133). She names her failure as an error of perception, a mind misdirected by the eye, in a play where the Friar's parallel diagnosis of young men's love, that it lies in their eyes and not their hearts, is treated as the beginning of wisdom. The same insight that educates Romeo's world arrives in Cressida's mouth as an epitaph nobody hears.

The transfer itself must be described accurately if the comparison is to mean anything. Cressida does not run to Diomedes. She is delivered to the Greek camp as state property, lodged in her father's tent among enemies, in a war where an unprotected woman attached to a defected priest has precisely one asset and one prospective protector. Diomedes courts her with the leverage of that position, asking for the token she wears, and the scene of the surrender shows resistance ground down in real time: she fetches the sleeve, snatches it back, bargains, weeps over it, and yields it while naming its owner in everything but name, "'Twas one's that loved me better than you will" (5.2.108-109). Juliet, tested, is tested by a potion plot whose risks are hers to accept, in her own city, among her own people, with a friar engineering her survival. Cressida, tested, is tested by abandonment inside an enemy's camp with no engineer at all. To call the outcomes a simple contrast of female characters is to grade two swimmers without mentioning that one was thrown into the sea in chains.

None of this acquits Cressida of the deed; the sleeve changes hands, the assignation is made. It relocates the deed's meaning. In Verona, constancy is the heroine's achievement, the thing she builds against her world's prohibition. In Troy, inconstancy is the world's achievement, the thing it manufactures out of a traded girl by removing every condition under which constancy could be practised, witnessed, or rewarded. The plays are mirror images at the level of structure: each heroine is finally the measure of what her world makes possible for a woman who loves.

## The Watching Scene: Troilus in the Shadows of the Greek Camp

The catastrophe of the Trojan love plot is staged as an act of spectatorship. Troilus, smuggled into the Greek camp by night, stands in the dark with Ulysses at one elbow and Thersites at the other and watches Cressida surrender his sleeve to Diomedes. The scene (5.2) is the later play's answer to the balcony: there, a lover overheard a beloved who did not know he listened, and the overhearing produced a marriage; here, a lover watches a beloved who does not know she is seen, and the watching produces a metaphysical crisis. "This is and is not Cressid" (5.2.172) is the sound of the Petrarchan metaphysics breaking on contact with evidence. Troilus had defined Cressida's value as resident in his own prizing of her; the scene confronts him with a Cressida who acts outside the definition, and since the definition admits no revision, the woman must divide instead. The comparisons with [Othello's jealousy plot, where a watcher is fed a staged show by an interested manager](/2031/03/07/vs-othello-jealousy-and-love/), measure the difference precisely: Othello is shown a false scene and believes it; Troilus is shown a true scene and cannot.

The commentary track matters as much as the action. Thersites annotates the seduction in the idiom of the sty: "How the devil Luxury, with his fat rump and potato finger, tickles these together. Fry, lechery, fry!" (5.2). Where the earlier play stations Mercutio outside the orchard wall to mock a Romeo he loves, the later play stations Thersites in the dark to reduce a scene he hates to a single appetite. Between the two commentators stands Ulysses, managing the watcher, urging patience, extracting maximum intelligence from another man's agony. Every function that Verona distributes among friend, friar, and nurse, Troy concentrates into handlers. Diomedes, the watched scene's third party, completes the triangle by demonstrating how courtship is conducted by a man native to the market. His wooing is brisk, contractual, and entirely untroubled by metaphysics: he states his desire, names his evidence (he has seen her, he has heard of her), demands a token as surety, and treats her coyness not as a mystery to be worshipped but as a negotiating position to be waited out. Where Troilus's rhetoric inflates under resistance, Diomedes's simply persists, and the scene's grim comedy is that persistence works: the sleeve changes hands to the man who asked for it as a receipt. Set the Greek's method beside Romeo's and the audit finds its most uncomfortable entry, since Romeo's method, for all its poetry, also worked, and worked in an evening. The plays do not sort their lovers into the sincere and the cynical by technique; they sort them by the metaphysics each brings to the same technique. Romeo risks everything on the proposition that the beloved is a soul to be joined. Diomedes risks nothing on the proposition that she is a prize to be collected. Troilus, watching, holds the first proposition and watches it processed by the second, and his tragedy in the scene is that he can conceive no third.

The watcher's own performance under observation is the scene's second subject. Troilus governs himself by a vow repeated like a charm: "Hold, patience!" (5.2); "I did swear patience"; "I will not be myself nor have cognition / Of what I feel. I am all patience" (5.2). The lover who opened the play declaring himself weaker than a woman's tear has become a man maintaining an official fiction about his own perceptions by main force, and the parallel with his beloved is exact and unremarked by either of them: each is sustaining a double account, she of her allegiance, he of his knowledge, and each calls the doubleness by a virtue's name, policy in her mouth, patience in his. Romeo, set to watch at a wall, climbed it. Troilus, set to watch in a camp, stands still and administers himself, and the stillness is not maturity; it is the posture's final form, feeling maintained as an institution after its object has resigned.

And when the watcher finally reads a text instead of a scene, the result is the play's most quoted act of criticism: Pandarus brings him Cressida's letter, and Troilus reads it as the whole civilization has taught him to read: "Words, words, mere words, no matter from the heart. / Th' effect doth operate another way. / Go, wind, to wind! There turn and change together" (5.3.119-123). He tears the letter and throws the pieces to the air. In Verona, a letter that never arrives destroys two lives, and the failure of delivery is the tragedy's hinge. In Troy, a letter arrives on time, is read accurately, and is torn up, because the world has taught its prince that words are wind by definition. The messenger machinery of the earlier play, audited elsewhere in this series, fails by accident; the later play's machinery works perfectly and signifies nothing, which is the bleaker finding.

## The Nurse and Pandarus: The Go-Between Degraded

Every love plot in this period runs on an intermediary, and the two plays' intermediaries mark the moral distance between their worlds more sharply than the lovers do. Verona's Nurse is a servant of the Capulet household, a foster-mother whose love for Juliet is the one wholly undisguised affection in the play. Her go-betweening is labour performed inside a family: she carries messages, arranges the ladder, keeps the secret, and describes her own work with a bawdy tenderness that never prices the girl: "I am the drudge and toil in your delight, / But you shall bear the burden soon at night" (2.5.80-81). When the marriage collapses into exile she gives Juliet the worst advice in either play, to marry Paris and forget the banished husband, and the play treats the counsel as a failure of nerve inside a genuine attachment. She errs as kin errs.

Pandarus is Cressida's uncle, and his kinship is the first of the play's corruptions, because he experiences it as a commercial opportunity. From the opening scene he is keeping accounts: "I have had my labor for my travail, ill thought on of her, and ill thought on of you; gone between and between, but small thanks for my labor" (1.1.71-73). His wooing of Troilus on Cressida's behalf is conducted in the language of milling and merchandising, the cake that must be ground, bolted, leavened, and baked (1.1), and when he finally delivers the girl he stages the consummation as a contract signing, witness included: "Go to, a bargain made. Seal it, seal it. I'll be the witness" (3.2.199-201). Troilus, reaching for a figure for the arrangement, produces the play's most honest sentence about it: "Her bed is India; there she lies, a pearl. / Between our Ilium and where she resides, / Let it be called the wild and wand'ring flood, / Ourself the merchant, and this sailing Pandar / Our doubtful hope, our convoy, and our bark" (1.1.100-107). The beloved is a pearl, the lover a merchant, the uncle a ship. Verona's lovers speak of pilgrims and saints; Troy's speak, even at their most lyrical, of cargo.

### What does Pandarus sell, and what does he think he is owed for it?

He sells access, and dresses the sale as generosity: his niece's favour to a prince, a prince's desire to his niece, each delivered with the other as implied payment. His ledger is kept in gratitude and never balances; he experiences himself throughout as an unpaid tradesman, which, in his play's economy, is exactly what he is.

The Nurse's failure, weighed fairly, even sharpens the contrast in her favour. Her catastrophic counsel in Act 3 Scene 5, that Juliet should marry Paris and treat the banished Romeo as dead, is the advice of a woman who has spent her life inside great households and knows their arithmetic: a live count in hand outweighs an exiled husband in Mantua. It is worldly, faithless, and offered in the idiom of care by someone who has nursed the girl from infancy and will grieve over her body within two days. Pandarus's failures are not failures of nerve at a crisis; they are his ordinary business method, applied at every stage, and his play never once shows him tempted by the Nurse's error, since temptation requires an allegiance to betray. The Nurse betrays a trust. Pandarus administers a market in which trust was never the medium, and the difference between a betrayed trust and an absent one is the difference between the two plays' moral weathers.

The geography of the two mediations maps the same opposition onto the stage. The Nurse's work is itinerant: she crosses Verona on foot with her fan and her aching back, finds Romeo in the street among the young men, visits the Friar's cell, carries messages between two great households whose enmity turns every errand into a small smuggling run, and her labour is measured in journeys, delays, and physical comedy, the comedy of a body doing love's logistics in a city built to obstruct them. Pandarus's work is sedentary to the point of paralysis: a garden for the introduction, a chamber for the vows, a doorway for the commentary; his lovers come to him, and the one journey the plot requires of anyone, Cressida's transfer to the Greek camp, is performed by the state, not the broker, leaving Pandarus behind in Troy with nothing to deliver and no one to deliver it to, complaining of neglect. Mediation in Verona is a service performed by walking. Mediation in Troy is a concession operated from a chair, and when the concession's territory is evacuated, the concessionaire is left with his diseases and his grievance, assets and liabilities in a single portfolio.

The endings of the two intermediaries complete the contrast. The Nurse disappears from her play in grief, her last service a lament over the body she helped to the tomb, unpunished and unforgotten because the tragedy has larger accounts to settle. Pandarus is dismissed by his own client with a curse, "Hence, broker, lackey! Ignomy and shame / Pursue thy life, and live aye with thy name!" (5.11.35-36), and then left alone on the stage to address the audience directly, the only character in either play permitted to break the frame. What he does with the privilege is the subject of the endings, but its premise belongs here: in Verona the go-between is absorbed back into the household's sorrow, while in Troy the go-between outlives the love plot entirely and inherits the play. The instrument has consumed the purpose. A world in which the broker survives the lovers, and gets the last word, has announced its hierarchy of values more plainly than any council speech could.

## Mercutio and Thersites: Wit That Loves, Wit That Corrodes

Each play keeps a licensed scoffer at its margin, a figure exempt from the heroic and romantic codes and therefore licensed to describe them. The resemblance is close enough to look like a deliberate rhyme across the seven years. Mercutio mocks Romeo's Petrarchan sighing, invents the Queen Mab fantasy out of thin air and lets it curdle in his own mouth, and punctures every inflation the feud attempts, including the honour code that is about to kill him. Thersites mocks the entire Greek command, reduces the war to its appetites, and punctures every inflation Troy attempts, including the love plot unfolding in front of him. Both men are the smartest talkers in their plays. The difference is the direction of the intelligence. Mercutio's wit is an act of friendship performed at high speed; he mocks Romeo because Romeo is his, and the mockery is the form his loyalty takes when the beloved object is a posture. Thersites's wit is an act of contempt performed from a fixed position; he mocks everyone because everyone is his enemy's fool, and the mockery is the only form of participation his world offers a man of his station.

Thersites is explicit about his reduction. Watching the seduction he has predicted, he files the report that his play keeps inviting and keeps refusing to be reduced to: "Lechery, lechery; still, wars and lechery; nothing else holds fashion" (5.2). The sentence is accurate as an inventory and false as an account, in the same way that a coroner's list of ingredients is false as a description of a meal. Mercutio's reductions work the other way: his bawdy deflation of Romeo's love is inaccurate as an inventory, since the love turns out to be the realest thing in Verona, and true as an act, because it is aimed at a friend it wants to cure. One scoffer's wit serves a person; the other's serves a thesis. A play in which the thesis wins is a play that has arranged for nobody to be curable.

### Why is Mercutio killed by the play he livens, while Thersites survives the play he sours?

Mercutio dies because his wit is attached to a body that loyalty keeps putting in harm's way; he steps into a fight that is not his, for a friend who will not fight. Thersites survives because his wit is attached to nothing: no loyalty summons him into danger, and a commentator nobody loves is safe.

Thersites's dramatic function exposes the same sterility from the other side. He is, in effect, the later play's substitute for a chorus: the figure stationed at the action's edge to tell the audience what it is watching. Verona's Chorus performs that office in sonnets, guaranteeing the story's meaning in advance and retiring after two appearances. Thersites performs it in abuse, scene after scene, and his commentary has the peculiar property of never altering anything, not even the temperature of a room. He baits Ajax, is beaten for it, and resumes; he annotates a seduction and the seduction proceeds on schedule; he sums the war and the war continues. A chorus that cannot be heard is a fair emblem for a world saturated with intelligence and starved of consequence. Mercutio, who holds no office at all, changes everything by dying; Thersites, who holds the commentator's office throughout, changes nothing by living. The comparison of the two wits is finally a comparison of two theories of speech: in one world, words bind, curse, marry, and kill; in the other, words are the exhaust of a machine that runs on other fuel.

Set the two men's set pieces side by side and the kinship turns uncanny. The Queen Mab speech is a catalogue: a fairy visiting sleepers in turn, galloping through lovers' brains, courtiers' knees, lawyers' fingers, soldiers' throats, each dreamer supplied with the dream his appetite ordered. Thersites's speeches are catalogues of the same construction run in the opposite moral direction: the Greek command inventoried man by man and filed under lechery, folly, and fraud, the Trojan court dismissed in a sentence, the whole war summarized as an argument between a cuckold and his brother over a placket. Mercutio's catalogue expands as it proceeds, appetite after appetite, until the speech's own energy frightens its speaker and Romeo has to call a halt; it is a mind enjoying the world too much to stop describing it. Thersites's catalogues contract as they proceed, man after man, until nothing remains in any entry except the appetite, described with a relish that is its own refutation: the play's great hater of lechery cannot leave the subject alone for two consecutive speeches. Wit in the earlier play is appetite celebrated by a man who will die defending a friend's honour. Wit in the later play is appetite audited by a man whose only consistent appetite is for the auditing. The audience laughs in both theatres, and the second laugh keeps catching in the throat, because Thersites's reductions are mostly accurate, and accuracy without love turns out to be the lonelier condition.

The structural consequence completes the rhyme. Mercutio's death is the hinge of his play, the moment comedy's machinery locks into tragedy's, and his exit line is a curse on both houses that the rest of the action proceeds to honour. Thersites's survival is the anti-hinge of his: he is still commenting in the fifth act, unchanged and unchangeable, and his play ends not when he falls silent but when the broker takes the stage from him. Verona kills its truth-teller and is haunted by the accuracy of his curse. Troy keeps its truth-teller alive and demonstrates, by his irrelevance, that accuracy was never the scarce resource. What Troy lacks is not perception. Everyone in the Greek camp perceives everything; Ulysses perceives the whole play in advance. What Troy lacks is a single institution, friendship, marriage, council, command, in which a true perception could be converted into an action that binds. Mercutio's curse works because Verona, at the last, can still be bound by words: by a friar's plan, a prince's sentence, a pair of statues. Thersites's truths bind nothing, and drift off with the armies.

## The InsightCrunch Love-Vision Comparison

The argument so far can be compressed into a single reference table, and the compression is itself instructive, because row after row shows the same device present in both plays with its moral sign reversed. Teachers assembling the two plays into a seminar deck will find the table transfers cleanly through [the ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), and the rows are ordered to follow the machinery rather than the plots: the world, the lover, the beloved, the broker, the scoffer, the evidence, the ending. Read down the Verona column and a pattern of conversion appears, posture converted into act, secrecy into marriage, mockery into mourning. Read down the Troy column and the conversions run in reverse, act into posture, marriage into metaphor, perception into paralysis. The table is named, as the series requires, the InsightCrunch love-vision comparison.

One row deserves a worked reading at full length, since it concentrates the table's method: the evidence of betrayal. Verona's machine builds a misreading and then corrects it offstage, Romeo acting on Balthasar's true report of a staged death and dying of the correction's failure to arrive; the evidence is accurate, the inference reasonable, and the catastrophe a postal accident. Troy's machine inverts every term: the evidence is staged before the lover's eyes, the inference drawn from it is the one the tradition drew for centuries, and the catastrophe is that the inference, though reasonable, is incomplete, since the scene watched began before the watcher arrived, in a history of exchange and duress that no single night's viewing could contain. The row teaches the table's central lesson in miniature: the two plays distribute epistemic luck in opposite directions. Verona's lovers are killed by too little context arriving too late. Troy's lovers are ruined by an excess of spectacle and a famine of context, and the second famine is the harder one to dramatize, which is why the later play needs its watchers, its councils, and its chorus of abuse: context is precisely what a world of brokers will not supply for free.

| Element | Romeo and Juliet: the idealized vision | Troilus and Cressida: the cynical audit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The public world | A feud with no stated cause, ended by the lovers' deaths | A war with a stated cause, voted on and continued past the lovers' ruin |
| The lover's opening | Petrarchan monologue for an absent, voiceless beloved | Petrarchan monologue for a present, negotiating beloved |
| The cure for posture | Reciprocity: the beloved answers, corrects, and marries him | None: the beloved is traded away before an answer can become a life |
| The beloved's test | A potion plot she accepts, among her own people, with a friar engineering rescue | A prisoner exchange she endures, among enemies, with no rescuer |
| The go-between | The Nurse: kin-like, loving, catastrophic only in counsel | Pandarus: kin by blood, commercial by practice, rewarded with a curse |
| The scoffer | Mercutio: wit in the service of friendship, killed by the feud | Thersites: wit in the service of contempt, surviving everything |
| The evidence of betrayal | A true report, believed, that arrives through a trusted servant | A true scene, watched, that the watcher refuses to believe |
| The letter | Written, sent, and never delivered; its failure kills | Delivered, read, and torn to the wind; its success signifies nothing |
| The woman judged | Judged by father and nurse, then vindicated by her own staged acts | Judged by broker, refused suitor, and spy, with no stage of her own |
| The ending | Double suicide, reconciliation, statues in pure gold | No lovers' deaths; a revenge vow, a dragged corpse, a bawd's bequest of diseases |

Two rows deserve emphasis because they carry the comparison's whole weight. The evidence row isolates the plays' epistemologies: Verona's lovers die from believing something true on insufficient context, while Troy's lover is destroyed by refusing to believe something true with complete context, and the pair of errors maps the distance between a world where knowledge is scarce and a world where knowledge is worthless. The ending row isolates their eschatologies, what each world finally does with love's wreckage: Verona converts it into public monuments and a peace, however glooming; Troy converts it into a vow of revenge and a prostitute's legacy of disease, distributed to the audience as a parting gift. Between those two conversions lies the entire distance the word range can be made to cover when applied to a single dramatist writing about a single subject.

## Endings: Golden Statues Against a Bawd's Bequest

The earlier play ends by spending everything it has. Romeo dies by poison in the tomb, Juliet by his dagger on waking, and the survivors inherit a stage arranged as evidence: the Friar's narrative, the Prince's audit, the fathers' competing grief converted into endowments, "For I will raise her statue in pure gold" (5.3.299). It is the love-death the tradition learned to call by a single word, and its full anatomy, set beside [the liebestod pattern in which love and death are entwined as one event](/2024/01/05/love-and-death-liebestod-analysis/), shows how completely Verona's ending belongs to that pattern: the deaths are mutual, witnessed, interpreted, and then monumentalized. Even the reconciliation is a form of pricing, gold statues as the feud's final currency, but it is pricing in the service of honour, agreed by both houses, and it closes the account.

Set beside [the double suicide of Antony and Cleopatra, the mature lovers who stage their deaths as coronations](/2031/02/21/vs-antony-and-cleopatra/), the Verona ending already looks young, hasty, and private; set beside Troy it looks like a state funeral. Troilus and Cressida ends by spending nothing, because its world has devalued every currency an ending might be paid in. Troilus does not die. He exits alive, his last extended speech a vow to haunt Diomedes "like a wicked conscience still" (5.11), the lover's eternity rhetoric transferred intact to hatred: "Hope of revenge shall hide our inward woe" (5.11). Cressida does not die either; she simply stops appearing after Act 5 Scene 2, denied even the dignity of an exit line in her own plot's conclusion, her story absorbed into a sleeve carried off as an enemy's trophy. The death the play does stage at length is Hector's, and it is staged as an ambush of an unarmed man: "I am unarmed. Forgo this vantage, Greek" (5.9.9), answered by Achilles' order, "Strike, fellows, strike! This is the man I seek" (5.9.10), after which the corpse is tied to a horse's tail and trailed through the field. Verona's tomb concentrates its deaths into meaning. Troy's battlefield disperses its one great death into procedure.

Troilus's exit deserves a closer look as the love plot's true conclusion, before the epilogue confiscates the stage. Chaucer had ended his hero's story outside the world, with the released spirit laughing at the whole economy of earthly desire from the eighth sphere. Shakespeare ends his Troilus inside the world and inside the war, walking toward the field with revenge for a philosophy and a torn letter for an epitaph, his last self-description a fraud perpetrated on his own side, hope offered as a cosmetic for woe. The trajectory from the prince who would not arm in Act 1 to the captain manufacturing morale in Act 5 is the posture's complete biography: the feelings were always enormous, always eloquent, and always finally about their owner's state, and a world that priced everything could find only one buyer for that eloquence in the end, the war itself, which pays in the oldest currency there is. Romeo's last self-description, by contrast, is an accurate account rendered beside the woman he loves, a toast and a truth in a single breath.

Even the victors' closing audit is rigged in the Trojan manner. Agamemnon, hearing the shout that Hector is dead, marches his commanders off with the satisfied arithmetic of a staff officer: "If in his death the gods have us befriended, / Great Troy is ours, and our sharp wars are ended" (5.10.9-10). The arithmetic is wrong, as every schoolchild in the audience knew: Troy does not fall to the sword at all, and the war's remaining business, the horse, the night assault, the city's sack, lies outside the play in a future the Greeks are mispricing exactly as they mispriced everything else. Verona's Prince ends his play by distributing blame with an accuracy the audience can verify item by item. Troy's general ends his part of it by filing a false return, and nobody on stage corrects the filing. The last word of the war plot is an error of accounting, delivered in triumph.

### What does Troilus do with Cressida's letter, and why could that scene exist in only one of the plays?

He reads it, pronounces its words empty of the heart, and tears it to pieces in the air, converting his beloved's last message into waste. In Verona, where an undelivered letter carries the catastrophe, tearing up a delivered one would be unintelligible: there, a letter's failure is an accident of plague and timing, never a judgment on words themselves.

Then the broker takes the stage. Pandarus, dismissed by Troilus with ignomy and shame, turns to the audience and delivers the play's epilogue, a bequest: "Good traders in the flesh, set this in your painted cloths... at that time bequeath you my diseases" (5.11). Every element of the tragic close is inverted. Where Verona ends with the highest authority in the city interpreting the deaths, Troy ends with the lowest agent in the love plot interpreting the audience. Where the Prince distributes punishment and pardon, Pandarus distributes syphilis. Where the fathers endow statues, the bawd endows an infection, and the bequest is addressed outward, to the traders in the flesh sitting in the yard, as if to say that the play's world and the audience's are continuous and the contagion is transferable. It is the most shocking ending in the canon precisely because it refuses every consolation the earlier play had taught its audience to expect: no corpse to weep over, no reconciliation to approve, no sententia to carry home, only a sick man's joke about the family trade. The refusal is the meaning. A world that priced love has no rite for burying it, so the play cannot end; it can only be abandoned, by its characters, its broker, and finally its audience, in that order.

## Paris and Helen: The Lovers Troy Already Had

One further pairing disciplines the whole comparison, because Troy contains, inside itself, a completed version of the Verona story. Paris is a Romeo who succeeded: he saw another man's wife, crossed the sea for her, brought her home, and kept her. The elopement that Verona's feud renders impossible, Troy's royal house ratified, and the ratification is the war. Where the Capulet marriage plot produces a secret wedding and a private catastrophe, the Trojan elopement produces a public siege and a continental one, and the play keeps the successful lovers in view as a standing exhibit of what Romeo's desire looks like after it wins. Helen, the object over whom the armies bleed, is given a single scene of dalliance with Paris (3.1), in which she teases him about the day's fighting and sings, at Pandarus's request, a song in praise of the love that is undoing them all. She has no soliloquy, no vial speech, no tomb. The most fought-over woman in literature is granted less interiority than Rosaline's name, and the withholding is diagnostic: a world organized by valuation does not need its prize to have an inner life, since a prize's function is exhausted by being prized.

The pairing cuts both ways, and the return cut is the one critics of the earlier play should feel. If Paris is Romeo with the obstacles removed, then the obstacles were never the meaning of Romeo's story; remove the feud and the balcony becomes a bedroom in a palace, the sonnet becomes small talk, and the lovers dwindle into the decorative cause of other men's deaths. The feud, for all its murderous stupidity, at least forces Verona's love to become an act with consequences the lovers choose and pay for themselves. Troy's lovers pay nothing themselves; the bill is distributed across ten years of other men's bodies, which is why the Trojan play's centre of moral gravity sits not in its title lovers at all but in Hector, the man who sees the account clearly, states it in council, and then pays it anyway. Any reading that treats the two plays as celebrations and denunciations of the same emotion misses this geometry. The earlier play tests love against prohibition and finds it stronger than the prohibition and weaker than death. The later play tests love against permission, publicity, and price, and finds that a love the world agrees to value can rot faster than a love the world agrees to forbid.

## Knight Against Campbell: The Critics' Quarrel

Criticism of the later play divides along a fault line that the comparison with Verona can settle, because the quarrel is really about whether Troilus's love is the play's victim or its accomplice. Oscar J. Campbell, in Shakespeare's Satire (1943), read the play as a deliberate scourging: a comical satire in the Jonsonian mode, in which heroes and lovers alike are deflated by design, the war plot exposing honour as appetite in armour and the love plot exposing desire as appetite in verse. On Campbell's account the cynicism is the author's, aimed with a satirist's steadiness, and Troilus's high style is part of the target, a young man's self-flattering music played while the world's real business, bargaining and butchery, goes on around it. G. Wilson Knight, writing in The Wheel of Fire (1930), heard the same music and reached the opposite conclusion. For Knight, Troilus stands for intuition, the soul's direct knowledge of value, set against the Greek camp's intellect, the calculating reason of Ulysses and the committee; the love poetry is not ironized from inside but is the play's affirmative pole, the one place where a worth beyond price is genuinely apprehended, and the tragedy is that such apprehension cannot survive in a world organized by the opposite faculty.

The texts adjudicate the quarrel with unusual cleanness, and they split it. Campbell wins the war plot outright. Troy's council endorses his reading in advance: the debate over Helen prices her like cargo, Hector exposes the pricing as idolatry and then joins it, and the Greek camp's parallel plot, Achilles sulking in his tent while the war is subcontracted to Ajax, confirms that honour in this play is a market position. Against that record, any account of the war as a noble backdrop is untenable, and Knight himself never seriously contests it. But Knight wins the love plot's verse. Troilus's speeches are not written as self-deception; the great aria on the infinity of the will (3.2.80-84) is allowed a philosophical reach that the play gives to no satiric butt, and Ulysses, the intellect's own spokesman, describes the young man to Agamemnon in terms no satire would permit its target: "The youngest son of Priam, a true knight, / Not yet mature, yet matchless firm of word... His heart and hand both open and both free" (4.5.110-116). A play intent on scourging its lover does not hire its shrewdest character to certify the lover's worth to the enemy commander. What the play scourges is not Troilus's apprehension of value but the world's refusal to build any institution in which that apprehension could be kept: no marriage, though the lovers exchange vows and tokens as formal as any in Verona; no household, though a chamber and a bed are provided; no future tense at all, since the exchange for Antenor is already being arranged while the vows are still warm.

The epilogue is the test case on which the two readings can be finally separated. Campbell's satire account claims it naturally: a bawd bequeathing diseases to the audience is the satirist's signature, the play spitting at its customers as it leaves. Knight's intuition account has more trouble, and its trouble is instructive, because the epilogue is not spoken by the play but by Pandarus, the one character whose every utterance the action has discredited, a sick broker complaining that his clients underpaid him. To take the bequest as the author's verdict is to repeat, at the level of criticism, the error the play keeps staging at the level of plot: accepting an interested party's summary as the record. The epilogue is evidence about Pandarus and about the market that produced him; as a verdict on Troilus's love it has the standing of Ulysses's verdict on Cressida, which is to say, none that survives inspection of its source. Campbell is right that the play is saturated with satire and wrong about its target list: the lovers' rhetoric is exhibited, priced, and ruined, but the ruin is charged throughout to the world's account.

A. P. Rossiter, in the lectures published after his death as Angel with Horns (1961), named the resulting condition ambivalence and made it the play's method: both camps rotten, both codes hollow, the audience kept in a double vision that forbids settlement. Rossiter describes the experience exactly, and his term has governed discussion since, but as an account of the comparison it stops one step early. Ambivalence is what the later play feels like; the question the Verona play answers is why it feels that way, and the answer is not a temperamental darkening in the author. Set the two dramaturgies side by side and the ambivalence resolves into a sequence: the earlier play shows a posture being outgrown inside a world that still contains corrective instruments, a friend's mockery, a friar's doctrine, a beloved's reply; the later play removes the instruments one by one and watches the posture, uncorrected, become a metaphysic, then a grievance, then a revenge vow. Rossiter's double vision is real, but it is produced by a single vision denied its sequel.

The stage history ratifies the critics' unease in the bluntest possible terms. For roughly three centuries the play was a text to be read rather than a production to be mounted; the professional stage, which kept Romeo and Juliet in near-constant repertory, found almost no use for its Trojan counterpart, and the neglect is itself a critical datum, since theatres avoid plays whose endings refuse to send an audience home with a settled feeling. The twentieth century reversed the verdict, and the timing of the reversal is suggestive: generations formed by industrial war and its propaganda recognized the council scenes, the managed heroics, and the priced reputations as documentary rather than perverse, and the play entered the repertory as a classic of disillusion. A work misread for three hundred years as a failure of tone and then recognized, within living memory of the trenches, as a work of reportage has not changed. Its readership has been educated by events, which is the strongest external evidence that the audit performed in 1602 was aimed at permanent features of its material rather than at a passing mood of its author's.

The editorial record supports the same conclusion from outside the text. David Bevington, editing the play for the Arden Third Series (1998), stresses its generic homelessness: the 1609 quarto title page calls it a history, "The Historie of Troylus and Cresseida"; the First Folio of 1623 prints it between the histories and the tragedies and omits it from the catalogue, as if the book itself could not decide where the thing belonged; and a prefatory epistle added to the quarto's second issue boasts that the comedy was "never staled with the stage, never clapper-clawed with the palms of the vulgar," advertising its unperformed cleverness as a selling point. F. S. Boas had already supplied the category such a play seems to demand when, in Shakespeare and His Predecessors (1896), he grouped it with Hamlet, All's Well That Ends Well, and Measure for Measure as a problem play, a drama whose questions are posed in a form that forbids comfortable answers. Romeo and Juliet, for all its cruelties, is not a problem play in Boas's sense, because its form delivers the answers its world withholds: the lovers' worth is certified by the poetry, the feud's guilt by the Prince, the meaning of the deaths by the statues. The later play's refusal to certify anything, not even its heroine's guilt, is the formal signature that the editorial tradition keeps stumbling over. Genre trouble of this persistence is not an accident of filing. It is the shape of a play that audits the forms, including the form of the love tragedy its author had perfected seven years before, and declines to issue any of them a clean bill.

## Seven Years Apart: Does the Later Play Prove a Darkening?

The biographical version of the contrast runs as follows: a dramatist in his early thirties writes the century's defining celebration of young love; the same dramatist, older, sadder, and soured on romance, writes its demolition. The dating evidence underneath the story is genuine and should be stated at its proper strength. Romeo and Juliet belongs to the middle of the 1590s, printed in a first quarto in 1597 and a fuller second quarto in 1599, its composition graded in this series at 1595 or 1595-96 on documentary anchors that include the Lord Hunsdon's Men's billing on the 1597 title page. Troilus and Cressida was entered in the Stationers' Register on 7 February 1603 as a play acted by the Lord Chamberlain's Men, and the consensus of the chronology places its composition in 1601-02, some six or seven years after the Verona play. A gap exists. What the gap proves is another matter.

A career narrative is a critical construction, not a document, and this series grades such labels as conveniences wherever they appear: the lyric period that contains the earlier play is a handbook convenience of exactly the same kind, useful for shelving and treacherous for thinking. Three bodies of evidence warn against loading the seven years with a conversion story. The first is generic. The two plays descend from different shelves of the library: a novella tradition filtered through Brooke's moralizing poem, which the young dramatist systematically de-moralized, against Chaucer's great poem and the classical war matter, where the deflation of heroes was already the inheritance, centuries old before Shakespeare touched it. A writer who takes up the Troy matter takes up a story in which the woman is given away and the hero is butchered unarmed; the darkness is in the material, on loan.

Chaucer's version of the story, which stands behind the later play as Brooke's poem stands behind the earlier one, makes the same point from the opposite direction. Chaucer grants his Troilus the ending Shakespeare withholds: slain by Achilles, the hero's spirit rises to the eighth sphere and laughs at the littleness of the world and the vanity of its loves, a cosmic audit from outside the system. Shakespeare, who knew the poem intimately, keeps his Troilus earthbound, unlaughing, and vowing revenge in a corridor of tents, and withholds from Cressida even the punishment Henryson had devised to close her account. The refusal of both inherited exits, the lover's apotheosis and the woman's execution, is a deliberate construction, not a mood. A dramatist soured on his material does not bother to refuse its consolations one by one; a dramatist conducting an audit refuses them because consolation is the one entry his ledger is designed to exclude.

The second body of evidence is internal, and it is decisive against the conversion story in its simple form. The cynicism supposedly discovered in 1602 is already present, fully armed, inside the play of 1595: Mercutio's Queen Mab speech turns from fantasy to a vision of soldiers cutting foreign throats in the space of a breath; the Friar's doctrine that young men's love lies in their eyes is Troilus and Cressida's whole critique of its hero, stated as pastoral common sense; Brooke's preface, which the play silently reverses, had condemned the lovers in terms Henryson would have approved. Conversely, the idealism supposedly abandoned by 1602 survives inside the later play in the mouths of its most authoritative speakers: Hector's doctrine that value dwells not in particular will is a metaphysics of objective worth, and Ulysses's unsolicited certificate of Troilus's open heart and hand is spoken in the enemy's council. What changed between the plays is not the presence of the two registers but their distribution: in Verona, idealism holds the centre and cynicism heckles from the margins; in Troy, cynicism holds the centre and idealism heckles from a prince's tent and a doomed brother's speech. A redistribution is not a conversion.

The two plays' frames make the same point in carpentry. Romeo and Juliet is introduced twice by a sonnet-speaking Chorus, the most rule-bound form in the language's repertoire, which announces the ending in advance and thereby converts suspense into ceremony: the audience is promised that the chaos to come has a shape, and the promise is kept. Troilus and Cressida is introduced once, by a Prologue in armour, who declines to summarize anything, explains that the scene begins in the middle, and leaves the outcome to the forces already mustered. The first frame is a contract; the second is a shrug in steel. Critics who narrate a darkening between 1595 and 1602 are, at bottom, narrating the distance between those two frames, and the distance is real. What the darkening story adds, and what the evidence will not carry, is the claim that the distance measures the author's inner weather rather than his choice of instrument. A sonnet and a shrug are both available to the same hand in the same season; the hand that wrote Twelfth Night's genial exactness in the Trojan play's own winter was not convalescing from faith or relapsing into it. It was working.

A redistribution is not a conversion.

### If the vision darkened, why does Twelfth Night stand between the two plays?

Because the chronology refuses a steady descent into bitterness. John Manningham's diary records Twelfth Night at the Middle Temple on 2 February 1602, inside the very window assigned to the Trojan play's composition, and that comedy's treatment of love is neither rapture nor audit but a third thing, genial and exact at once. The register follows the assignment.

The occasion hypothesis is strengthened by the play's own rhetoric, which is saturated with the forms of the schools and the Inns: the Trojan council is a formal disputation on a set question, honour against policy, with speeches assigned, answered, and summed; Hector's demolition of the Helen case proceeds by numbered heads like an exercise in the laws of nature and nations; Ulysses's degree speech is a sermon on political philosophy delivered to a general who has asked for a remedy. A playwright writing for an audience of law students and young gentlemen trained in exactly these exercises would load his play with them as a matter of craft, and their prominence in Troilus and Cressida says at least as much about the room it was built for as about the man who built it. Verona's play, built for the public stage, puts its philosophy in a friar's mouth and its disputation in a duel of sonnets. The registers differ because the commissions differed, and a criticism that reads the difference as a spiritual biography is mistaking the theatre's division of labour for a diary.

The third body of evidence is the audience's own record. The 1609 epistle's boast that the Trojan play was never staled with the stage suggests, however the boast is read, a coterie piece, a play aimed at the Inns of Court temper that delighted in debate, paradox, and the unsettling of received pieties. A commissioned exercise in an Inns of Court manner is evidence about a market, not about a soul. None of this denies that the later play is darker in effect; the endings alone settle that. It denies that the darkness is best explained as autobiography. The stronger explanation is the one the comparison itself supplies: Shakespeare returned, in a different genre and for a different audience, to the precise weak point his early tragedy had identified and quarantined, the lover's self-regarding rhetoric, and asked what would happen to it in a world with no Juliet to correct it, no Friar to bless it, and a war to price it. That is not a man losing his faith in love. That is a dramatist stress-testing his own most famous construction, which is a colder and more interesting activity.

## The Verdict: An Audit Is Not a Refutation

The comparison yields a verdict in three parts. First, on the lovers: Troilus is Romeo's first-act condition preserved in a world that never cures it, and Cressida is not Juliet's opposite but Juliet's counterfactual, a heroine of comparable wit and candour subjected to a test no constancy could be expected to pass in the form in which it is administered, traded, exhibited, and abandoned among enemies. The moral asymmetry the tradition reads off the two women is an artefact of the two dramaturgies: one play keeps clearing a stage on which its heroine can prove herself, and the other keeps filling the stage with men pricing her.

Second, on the worlds: Verona's feud destroys love by prohibition, Troy's war destroys it by valuation, and the second destruction is the more complete, because prohibition leaves the prohibited thing intact in its hiding place, while valuation dissolves the thing into the price agreed for it. The feud kills the lovers and then, confronted with the bodies, repents in gold. The war prices the lovers, spends them, and feels nothing in particular, because a market has no body on which repentance could register. Hector's dragged corpse and Pandarus's diseased bequest are the correct emblems of a civilization that can still fight, bargain, and joke, but can no longer consecrate.

A practical corollary follows for the order in which the plays repay reading. The audit presupposes the theorem: Troilus and Cressida's cross-examination only bites on an audience that has first granted Romeo and Juliet its full force, felt the sonnet at the feast land, watched the posture cured, and mourned at the tomb. Read in the reverse order, the Trojan play's cynicism arrives unearned and flattens into mere knowingness, and the Verona play read afterwards looks naïve only because its examiner has been allowed to mark the paper before the candidate sat the examination. The plays are best kept in their chronological sequence, not because the later one improves on the earlier, but because the earlier one is the later one's evidence, and evidence is heard before cross-examination in any court that expects its verdicts to mean anything.

Third, on the author: the relation between the plays is not belief followed by disbelief but construction followed by audit. Romeo and Juliet builds the strongest available case for young love, stronger than its sources allowed, by stripping out the moralizing frame and letting the lovers' own eloquence carry the verdict. Troilus and Cressida then subjects that case to the hardest available cross-examination, in a setting where every witness is compromised and every term of the original brief, value, faith, honour, the go-between, the scoffer, the letter, is redefined under pressure. The case survives the audit in the only place the audit cannot reach: the earlier play itself, whose lovers remain vindicated by the very instruments, reciprocity, secrecy kept as fidelity, death accepted as proof, that the later play shows failing for want of a world to hold them. To read the two plays together is to watch a dramatist first prove a theorem and then publish the strongest known objections to it, and the theorem is not diminished by the objections. It is promoted, from a beautiful assertion, into a tested one.

What the comparison finally establishes about love's rhetoric can be stated without reference to either plot. Rhetoric of the Petrarchan kind is neither true nor false in itself; it is a technology for sustaining a feeling across the interval between desire and knowledge, and its value depends entirely on what a world does with the interval. Verona shortens the interval to a day and fills it with reciprocity, so the technology is retired honourably, its hyperboles cashed into a marriage. Troy stretches the interval across a war and fills it with appraisal, so the technology runs until it burns out its user, its hyperboles inflated into a metaphysic and then repudiated into revenge. The same speech, "weaker than a woman's tear" in one city and a soldier's impatience in the other, is in the first case a symptom with a prognosis and in the second a diagnosis of the city itself. That is why the pairing endures as the sharpest instrument in the canon for thinking about Shakespeare's treatment of love: not because the two plays disagree about what lovers say, since on inspection they say much the same things, but because they demonstrate, with experimental rigour, that what lovers say is the least variable part of love. The variable is the world listening, and worlds, unlike lovers, cannot be charmed out of their nature by a sonnet. Verona's world, confronted with the cost of its deafness, builds statues. Troy's world, confronted with the same bill, loses it in the accounts and orders the trumpets sounded for the morning.

The standard for judging the earlier play, then, is set by the later one, and the judgment runs in the earlier play's favour with interest. Any dramatist can protect a love story from scrutiny by keeping it brief, private, and fatal before the questions accumulate; the Verona play does all three and is sometimes patronized for the protection. The Trojan play removes the protection piece by piece, length, privacy, and the mercy of an ending, and shows the same love failing under audit in ways that are explicable at every step by the conditions rather than by the feeling. A love that fails only when its world is rebuilt to fail it has not been exposed as a fraud. It has been priced, and the price recorded is the world's, entered in the ledger against Troy, in a hand the audience is invited to recognize as its own whenever it repeats Ulysses's sentence on the girl or Pandarus's proverb on the lovers without remembering who minted either.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why does Troilus refuse to fight when his play opens, and how does Romeo's opening sickness differ from his?

Both heroes open disabled by unreturned love while their worlds demand labour: Troilus unarms and asks why he should war outside Troy's walls when he finds "such cruel battle here within" (1.1.1-2), and Romeo mopes through Verona at dawn with the same Petrarchan symptoms. The difference is the prognosis each play assigns the condition. Romeo's sickness is diagnosed on sight by friends and friar, mocked as a posture, and cured within a day by reciprocity. Troilus's sickness meets no diagnosis at all; Pandarus feeds it, the war ignores it, and it metastasizes into the play's philosophy, so that the opening symptom becomes the closing worldview.

### Q: What does Troilus claim when he asks "What's aught but as 'tis valued?", and how does the love plot test the claim?

The question, put to Hector in council (2.2.53), claims that worth is conferred by the valuer: a thing, a woman, a cause, is worth whatever the person prizing it will pay. Hector answers that value dwells also in the thing itself, not in particular will (2.2.54-57), and the love plot runs the experiment. Troilus prizes Cressida without reserve and builds the prizing into a metaphysic, so when she acts outside his valuation he cannot revise the price; he can only split the woman into a true Cressid who fits the value and a false one who does not. The claim fails its test: a worth that exists only in the prizer cannot survive the prized person's freedom.

### Q: Why does Hector demolish the case for keeping Helen and then vote to keep her?

Hector argues in council that Helen is not worth the Trojan lives her keeping costs, that she is another man's wife, and that the laws of nature and nations require her return (2.2). He then announces that his opinion stands "in way of truth" and votes with his brothers to keep her anyway, because the cause has become a matter of the family's joint and several dignities (2.2.190-200). The reversal is the war plot in miniature: the play's best reasoner demonstrates that reason governs Troy's speeches and honour governs its decisions, and a world whose clearest mind cannot act on its own conclusions has no machinery left with which to stop anything, including the ruin of its lovers.

### Q: What is the sleeve Cressida gives Diomedes, and why does its transfer strike Troilus harder than her words?

The sleeve is Troilus's parting token, exchanged for Cressida's glove when the lovers separate (4.4), a portable piece of the man's identity worn on the woman's arm. In the watched scene in the Greek camp (5.2), Diomedes demands a token as surety, and after fetching it, snatching it back, and bargaining over it, she surrenders the sleeve while refusing to name its owner: "'Twas one's that loved me better than you will" (5.2.108-109). Words could be explained, contextualized, or disowned; she had already shown a mistress's command of all three. The sleeve is evidence that cannot be talked away, a piece of Troilus himself handed over in his sight, which is why his crisis follows the object and not the conversation.

### Q: Why is Cressida sent to the Greek camp, and what room for choice does the play leave her there?

She is sent because her father Calchas, a Trojan priest defected to the Greeks, claims her as his price, and the Trojan command agrees to exchange her for the captured Antenor (4.1). The transaction is a prisoner swap ratified by both states; her consent is neither sought nor mentioned. In the camp she is lodged in her father's tent among enemies, kissed in turn by the Greek command on arrival, and dependent for protection on the very men competing for her. The play leaves her wit, timing, and the management of appearances, the equipment of survival, and withdraws everything that would let fidelity be practised safely or witnessed fairly. Her subsequent transfer to Diomedes is judged by the tradition as a free fall; the play stages it as the last move available on a board her world had already cleared.

### Q: What does Pandarus bequeath the audience in his epilogue, and what has replaced blessing in his mouth?

Dismissed by Troilus with a curse, Pandarus turns to the spectators, addresses them as "good traders in the flesh," and promises that some two months hence his will shall be made, at which time he will "bequeath you my diseases" (5.11). The epilogue replaces the tragic blessing, the sententia or reconciliation that closes the earlier play, with an inheritance of venereal infection, distributed to the audience as the love plot's final dividend. Where Verona's Prince ends by interpreting the dead, Troy's broker ends by infecting the living, and the substitution is the play's last audit entry: in a world that ran its love through a broker, the broker is the proper residuary legatee, and disease is the proper estate.

### Q: How does Thersites differ from Mercutio as a licensed scoffer inside his play?

Both men stand outside their plays' codes and describe them without reverence, but the licence is granted by opposite authorities and spent in opposite directions. Mercutio's mockery is licensed by friendship and spent on cure: he ridicules Romeo's Petrarchan sighing to laugh him out of it, and his wit remains an act of love even when it curdles into the Queen Mab vision. Thersites is licensed by contempt, his own and everyone's for him, and spends his wit on reduction: the whole war and love plot filed under "wars and lechery" (5.2). Mercutio's scoffing is falsifiable and is falsified, since the love he mocks proves real; Thersites's is unfalsifiable, since a reduction that explains everything predicts nothing, and his play keeps him alive to prove the sterility of being right in that way.

### Q: Why did F. S. Boas class Troilus and Cressida among the problem plays, and does Romeo and Juliet escape the class?

Boas, in Shakespeare and His Predecessors (1896), grouped the Trojan play with Hamlet, All's Well That Ends Well, and Measure for Measure as dramas that pose moral and emotional questions in a form refusing comfortable settlement: heroes compromised, heroines unjudged by any standard the play will ratify, endings that abandon rather than resolve. Romeo and Juliet escapes the class despite its cruelties because its form settles what its world cannot: the poetry certifies the lovers, the Prince certifies the guilt, and the statues certify the meaning of the deaths. The Trojan play withholds every one of those certificates, beginning with its heroine's guilt, which is why the filing systems of four centuries, quarto, Folio, and criticism alike, have never agreed on where it belongs.

### Q: Why is Cressida denied a death, and what does her disappearance do to the comparison with Juliet?

Cressida makes her final appearance in Act 5 Scene 2, delivers her soliloquy of self-diagnosis, and is never seen or sentenced again; the play's remaining scenes belong to the war. A death would have closed her account with the dignity Juliet's dagger purchases, forcing the audience to total her story as tragedy. Her disappearance instead leaves the account open and the judgment unrendered, which is the play's procedural point: Troy has no rite, court, or monument for a ruined woman, only a proverb coined in advance by her uncle. Set against Juliet, whose death is the most elaborately witnessed event in her play, Cressida's vanishing measures the distance between a world that monumentalizes its victims and a world that simply stops mentioning them.

### Q: What does Ulysses mean by "daughters of the game," and why is his verdict on Cressida not the play's?

Ulysses delivers the sentence moments after Cressida has declined his kiss with a joke about debts, in the council of generals who have just kissed her in sequence: she and women like her are to be set down "for sluttish spoils of opportunity / And daughters of the game" (4.5.63-71), game meaning quarry, sport, and prostitution at once. The verdict is not the play's because the play has shown the audience the verdict's manufacture: a refused man, an intelligence officer by temperament, converting a witty refusal into a general law about women within a minute of sustaining it. Verona's judgments of Juliet are similarly interested, but the play supplies Juliet's own staged evidence against them; for Cressida it supplies the interest itself, exposed, and leaves the weighing to the audience.

An essay should work down the table's rows and show, for each shared device, that the later play preserves the earlier play's construction while altering the conditions around it: the posture is the same posture, denied its cure; the letter is the same letter, denied its failure; the beloved is the same wit under a harsher administration. The essay should close on the evidence and ending rows, where the reversal is sharpest, and should concede the strongest opposing datum, the genuine bitterness of the epilogue, before showing that bitterness aimed at the brokers of the system rather than at the lovers it consumed.

### Q: What does Troilus mean by "This is and is not Cressid," and why can he neither believe nor dismiss what he has seen?

Watching Cressida yield his sleeve to Diomedes, Troilus splits the woman before him from the woman his metaphysic requires: "This she? No, this is Diomed's Cressida... This is and is not Cressid" (5.2.168-175). The division is a logic of last resort. Because he has defined her worth as resident in his own fidelity of perception, evidence against her constancy registers as evidence against the evidence, against his eyes and ears themselves, organs he briefly suspects were "created only to calumniate." He cannot believe the scene without dissolving the absolute he has built, and cannot dismiss it because he watched it in company with the play's two most unsentimental witnesses. The sentence's grammar, affirmation and negation in one breath, is the sound of an absolutist epistemology failing in real time.

### Q: What does Cressida's "Ah, poor our sex" speech concede, and why does the play give her no answering voice?

Alone after Diomedes leaves, Cressida concedes that her mind has been directed by her eye: "The error of our eye directs our mind. / What error leads must err. O, then conclude: / Minds swayed by eyes are full of turpitude" (5.2.128-135). The concession is a self-diagnosis in the play's own clinical idiom, the same location of love in the eyes that the Friar had used to instruct Romeo, and it is more self-knowing than anything Troilus manages in the scene he shares with her. No answering voice follows because Troy contains no institution that receives confession: no friar, no friend, no chorus, only Thersites in the dark, converting her honesty into an obscenity before the speech's echo has faded. The silence after her exit is the verdict her world passes on insight itself.

### Q: How did the word "pander" pass from a man's name into the common stock of the language?

In Chaucer's poem Pandarus is the heroine's uncle and the hero's friend, an energetic and largely benevolent engineer of the affair, and his name carried no stain beyond complicity. Shakespeare's play re-mints the figure as a commercial broker who prices his niece, notarizes her vows as a bargain, and is cursed by his client, and the character himself predicts the linguistic outcome in the vow scene, decreeing that all brokers-between shall be called panders after his name (3.2.202-205). Usage obeyed the decree: the name descended into a common noun and then a verb for procurement, so that the language keeps the play's judgment in permanent circulation. Few characters in drama have been punished so efficiently as by becoming a dictionary entry.

### Q: Which of the four lovers is most changed by love's failure, and what does each survivor carry out of the plays?

Romeo and Juliet are not changed by failure at all in the only sense their play recognizes, since they die inside the love rather than after it, constant to the last breath each spends. Cressida is changed in circumstance and reputation but is granted no closing scene in which a change of soul could be registered; she exits as she was managed, mid-sentence in her own story. Troilus is transformed entirely and on stage: the prince who opened the play too tender for war closes it as revenge's volunteer, his eternity rhetoric reassigned from Cressid to Diomed, his heart converted by betrayal into the war's most willing instrument. The lover who survives his love is the one it ruins; the lovers it kills, it completes.

### Q: Why does Troilus and Cressida open with an armed Prologue instead of a Chorus, and what does the substitution announce?

The Prologue enters in armour and apologizes for beginning in the middle of the war, "In Troy, there lies the scene," promising no judgment of the outcome, which depends on chance. Verona's Chorus enters unarmed with a sonnet and delivers the whole plot, ending included, as settled knowledge. The substitution announces the later play's epistemology before a line of dialogue is spoken: where the earlier play frames its action inside a form, the sonnet, that masters time by foreknowing it, the later play frames its action inside a weapon, knowledge replaced by equipment, summary replaced by a shrug about fortune. Even the frames disagree about whether the story they introduce can be understood, and each frame turns out to be right about its own play.

### Q: What bargain does Pandarus strike over the lovers' heads in Act 3 Scene 2, and how does the play keep his prophecy?

As the vows conclude, Pandarus appoints himself witness and legislates for posterity: let all constant men be Troiluses, all false women Cressids, and all brokers-between panders, say amen (3.2.202-207). The bargain he strikes is that the love shall be remembered in his vocabulary, proverbial, flattened into types, with the woman's name reserved for falsehood and his own for the trade. The play keeps the prophecy with terrible fidelity, since the tradition, from Henryson's leprous Cresseid backward and forward to the dictionary's pander, has largely repeated his terms, and even Troilus's crisis in Act 5 is fought inside the categories the broker minted. The essay of resistance the play offers is procedural: it shows the prophecy being coined, by that mouth, at that moment, so that an attentive audience can decline the currency.

### Q: What kills Hector, and why is his death staged as an ambush rather than a duel?

Hector, the Trojan war's one complete man, kills a Greek for his splendid armour, unarms to rest, and is found by Achilles, who declines single combat and orders his Myrmidons to strike: "Strike, fellows, strike! This is the man I seek" (5.9.9-10). The corpse is then tied to Achilles' horse's tail and dragged through the field. A duel would have paid Hector in the war's own currency, honourable death at a great opponent's hand, and the play refuses the payment, because its argument is that the currency is counterfeit: the age's greatest warrior is not defeated but processed, by a committee, at a disadvantage, for a trophy. Verona's deaths are all, in their terrible way, chosen; Troy's greatest death is an administrative act, and the difference is the two plays' worlds in one stage picture.

### Q: How do the two plays distribute blame for love's failure among persons, prices, and worlds?

Verona's play distributes blame upward and outward: the Prince's closing audit names the feud, the fathers' haste, and his own lax governance, while the lovers themselves are absolved by the manner of their deaths and the Friar's testimony. Troy's play distributes blame downward and inward, onto a traded girl and a self-deceiving prince, because its institutions decline to audit themselves; the council that voted for the war blames fortune, the spy who priced the heroine blames her sex, and the broker blames his clients. The comparison's lesson for readers is to watch where a play permits blame to settle. In the earlier tragedy the settlement is just and therefore tragic; in the later one the settlement is rigged, and the rigging, more than any betrayal, is the bitterness the audience carries out of the theatre.
