Cow protection politics after 2014 is usually narrated as a story of crowds: a killing at Dadri in September 2015, a dairy farmer beaten to death on a highway near Alwar in April 2017, a teenager stabbed on a train in June 2017. The crowds were real, and this article records their cases from court files and official statements, without spectacle and without euphemism. Yet the crowd is the least durable part of the story. Crowds disperse. What remained after each crowd dispersed was paper: an amendment assented to at Mumbai on 4 March 2015 that made possession of beef a crime, a Haryana statute of November 2015 that raised the price of slaughtering a cow to ten years of rigorous imprisonment, a Gujarat amendment of 31 March 2017 that priced the same act at life. The argument of this article is that the post-2014 transformation of cow protection politics was architectural before it was anything else. State legislatures rewrote the legal relationship between a religious and cultural practice and the recognition that public authority gives it, and they did so state by state, provision by provision, inside a federal design that the Constituent Assembly had built for exactly this subject in 1948. The violence that fills the headlines operated inside that rewritten architecture, drew its occasions from it, and cannot be understood apart from it. Neither can the electoral story, which is the second familiar narration: cow protection as a mobilising symbol in the Bharatiya Janata Party's rise. Symbols do not imprison anyone. Statutes do, and the statutes are where the change can be measured.

The claim defended here can be named in one sentence: the post-2014 cow-protection politics renegotiates the architectural relationship between religious-cultural practice and state recognition through state-level variations, and the architectural content of that renegotiation shapes the post-2014 secularism settlement. Three rival readings organise the commentary. The violence reading treats the period as a climate: a sequence of lynchings, a politics of fear, an atmosphere in which minorities calculate risk before loading a truck. The strategic reading treats the period as a campaign: the cow as an instrument of consolidation, raised or lowered in volume as elections require. The reading defended here treats the period as a reconstruction: legislatures and governments altered what the law notices, what it punishes, what it presumes, what it funds and what it builds, and those alterations outlast any climate or campaign. Christophe Jaffrelot's Modi's India, the most substantial scholarly account of the period, documents the vigilante phenomenon as part of a wider de facto transformation of the republic's operating rules; Hilal Ahmed's essays on the period read the cow as a sign in a politics of representation; Suhas Palshikar's essays on the post-2014 order describe a hegemonic project remaking common sense. Each reading catches something the others miss. The adjudication offered below accepts the violence reading's facts and the strategic reading's electoral logic, and shows that neither explains the period's most permanent deposit, which is a body of state law whose provisions differ from one another in ways that only an architectural analysis can even describe. A politics that had been content with symbolism would not have needed section 5D of a Maharashtra statute, a burden-of-proof clause at Haryana, or a night-transport prohibition at Gandhinagar. The drafters wrote those clauses because the substance of recognition, not its theatre, was the object.

## Article 48 and the Founding Bargain of November 1948

The constitutional text at the head of this subject is short enough to quote in full, and its wording matters. Article 48 of the Constitution of India, placed among the Directive Principles of State Policy, reads: "The State shall endeavour to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines and shall, in particular, take steps for preserving and improving the breeds, and prohibiting the slaughter, of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle." Every later actor in this story, legislator, judge, minister and vigilante, has claimed descent from that sentence, and the sentence's own history explains why the claim is always available and never conclusive. The Constituent Assembly debated the cow on 24 November 1948, when the draft article that became Article 48 came up for consideration. Seth Govind Das, a Congress member from the Central Provinces and a lifelong campaigner for cow protection, pressed for the prohibition to be placed among the justiciable fundamental rights, where a court could enforce it against a reluctant government. Pandit Thakur Dass Bhargava moved the amendment that carried the day: the prohibition entered the draft article on animal husbandry, framed as a step in organising agriculture on modern and scientific lines, and the Assembly adopted it in that economic dress. Dr. B. R. Ambedkar, as chairman of the Drafting Committee, accepted the amendment on the floor. The placement decided the legal character of the commitment for the next seventy years. Directive Principles are instructions to legislatures; they create no enforceable right in any citizen and no enforceable duty that a court can compel a legislature to discharge. The cow entered the Constitution as an aspiration addressed to lawmakers, not as a command addressed to judges, and the Constituent Assembly's own division of labour between the two parts of the text is examined in this series through [the Directive Principles and fundamental rights settlement](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/).

The bargain of November 1948 had three terms, and the post-2014 period renegotiated each of them. The first term was location: cow protection sat in the chapter of non-justiciable principles, honoured through legislation or not at all. The second term was level: because the subject fell to the states under the federal distribution of powers, the aspiration would be discharged, if anywhere, in state capitals, at speeds and severities that would differ. The third term was language: the prohibition was justified on the floor in the vocabulary of animal husbandry and breed improvement, not in the vocabulary of religious sanctity, even though many members who voted for it held the cow sacred and said so in the same debates. Seth Govind Das spoke of the cow in openly religious terms; the amendment that passed spoke of milch and draught cattle. That doubleness, a religiously motivated commitment carried in an economic text, is the founding condition of cow protection politics, and it has never disappeared. Ministers defending the amendments of 2015 and 2017 reached for both vocabularies, sometimes in the same speech, citing Article 48 as constitutional warrant and the cow's sacredness as political warrant. Critics of the amendments attacked both vocabularies, arguing that the economic case had collapsed with mechanised agriculture and that the religious case could not be enacted by a secular state without favouring one community's doctrine. The Assembly of 1948 did not resolve that argument. It institutionalised it, handing later generations a text under which both sides could plausibly claim to be the faithful heirs. The Assembly's wider negotiation of religion and public authority, the settlement within which the cow clause was one clause among many, is traced in this series through [the secularism debates of the Constituent Assembly](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/). What the founders built, in short, was not a policy but a venue: a directive, a federal subject and a double vocabulary, inside which India's cow politics would be conducted by legislation. The post-2014 renegotiation took place inside that venue, on its terms, and its architects said so whenever they cited Article 48, which was often.

## A Federal Subject: Entry 15 and the Pre-2014 Patchwork

The venue's federal term deserves precision, because the state-level variation that defines the post-2014 period was not an improvisation but the working of a deliberate allocation. Entry 15 of the State List in the Seventh Schedule assigns to state legislatures the preservation, protection and improvement of stock and the prevention of animal diseases, together with veterinary training and practice. Article 246 gives the states exclusive legislative competence over State List subjects. Cow slaughter law in India is therefore state law by constitutional design, in the same family as public order and police, and no Union statute has ever imposed a national prohibition. The design belongs to [the asymmetric-federal design](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) that runs through the Indian Constitution, in which uniformity is the exception and negotiated difference the rule. Between 1950 and 2014 the states used that competence to build a patchwork of regimes so varied that a single national description of cow law is impossible. Gujarat prohibited cow slaughter under its Animal Preservation Act of 1954. Madhya Pradesh legislated in 1959 and again, comprehensively, in 2004. Uttar Pradesh enacted its Prevention of Cow Slaughter Act in 1955. Maharashtra prohibited cow slaughter in its Animal Preservation Act of 1976, while permitting the slaughter of bulls and bullocks certified as fit. West Bengal's Animal Slaughter Control Act of 1950 took the opposite architectural route: slaughter was lawful under regulation, with a certificate that an animal was over fourteen years of age or unfit for work or breeding, issued through a municipal or veterinary authority. Kerala enacted no cow slaughter prohibition at all, regulating slaughter through municipal licensing and its Panchayat Raj framework. Several northeastern states maintained no prohibition, and beef remained an ordinary food in much of the region. The patchwork was not random. It tracked demography, the place of beef in local diets, the strength of cow protection movements in each state's politics and the composition of governing coalitions, and it held roughly steady for six decades, amended at the margins, rarely at the foundations.

### Why does the Constitution leave cow slaughter to the states?

Cow slaughter falls under Entry 15 of the State List, which assigns the preservation and improvement of stock to state legislatures, while Article 48 sits among the non-justiciable Directive Principles that instruct legislatures rather than courts. The settlement of 24 November 1948 therefore made prohibition a choice each state would make for itself.

The patchwork had a second layer, less visible than the statutes: the machinery of permission. In the regulated states, the operative document was the fit-for-slaughter certificate, a small administrative artifact that decided whether a particular animal could lawfully die. In the prohibiting states, the operative facts were exemptions and enforcement discretion: the diseased animal that could be destroyed, the police officer who decided whether a truckload of cattle was bound for a dairy or a slaughterhouse. Both layers matter for what followed, because the post-2014 amendments worked on both. They widened prohibitions, and they thickened the machinery around them, adding offences of possession and transport, reversing burdens of proof, creating funds, shelters and dedicated enforcement expectations. A reader who compares only the headline bans of 1950 and 2020 will conclude that little changed, since most prohibiting states already prohibited. A reader who compares the full apparatus, offences, presumptions, penalties, institutions, will see the transformation plainly. That is the first reason the architectural reading outperforms its rivals as an account of the period: it looks at the whole machine, not at the slogan painted on its side. The second reason is that the machine is where the state's recognition is actually conferred. A legislature recognises a practice when it writes that practice's object into criminal law with severe penalties and funds its protection with public money; it withdraws recognition, or withholds it, when it leaves the practice as private sentiment. Between 2014 and 2020, state after state moved cow protection from the second condition toward the first, and the movement was registered in statute books that any citizen can read.

## The Courts' Two Settlements: Quareshi in 1958 and Mirzapur in 2005

Before the legislatures could renegotiate the settlement, the Supreme Court defined its outer walls, twice. In Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar, decided in 1958 and reported at AIR 1958 SC 731, a Constitution Bench considered the total prohibitions enacted by Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh against a challenge by butchers and cattle traders whose occupation the bans extinguished. The Court upheld a total prohibition on the slaughter of cows and calves, accepting the Directive Principle of Article 48 and the animal's place in the agricultural economy as a reasonable basis for classification. It refused, however, to extend the same protection to bulls, bullocks and buffaloes that had ceased to be useful, holding that a total ban on those animals imposed an excessive burden on the butchers' right to practise a trade under Article 19(1)(g) and served no comparable public purpose. The Quareshi settlement was a compromise with a scalpel: the cow absolutely protected, ageing male cattle slaughterable, the line drawn at usefulness. For nearly five decades that line held, and state laws were drafted around it. The second settlement moved the line. In State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat, decided on 26 October 2005 by a seven-judge bench and reported at (2005) 8 SCC 534, the Court upheld Gujarat's prohibition as extended to bulls and bullocks without an age limit, departing from Quareshi's usefulness test. The majority reasoned that cattle dung, urine and draught capacity retained value across the animal's life, that the Directive Principles should be read with the fundamental duty of compassion for living creatures in Article 51A(g), and that a legislature could rationally conclude that no bovine of the cow family was ever useless. Justice A. K. Mathur's dissent warned against reading a religious sentiment into an economic analysis, a warning the majority answered by insisting that its grounds were economic and constitutional rather than devotional.

The two settlements frame the post-2014 legislature as an actor working inside a judicially widened space. After Mirzapur, a state assembly that wished to prohibit the slaughter of the entire cow family, to criminalise the trade around it and to punish severely faced no serious constitutional obstacle on the prohibition itself; challenges would have to run through procedure, presumption and proportionality instead. That is exactly where the litigation of the period went. The Bombay High Court's 2016 judgment on the Maharashtra amendment struck at the possession offence and the reversed burden, not at the slaughter ban, which it upheld. The pattern is architectural evidence in itself: the contested frontier of cow law after 2014 was not whether the state may protect the cow, a question the Court had answered, but how far the protecting state may reach into kitchens, trucks and marketplaces, and with what presumptions against the citizen. Legislatures pushed the frontier outward; courts pruned it at intervals; vigilantes operated in the space the contest left ambiguous. Any account of the period that begins with the crowd and never reaches the case law will mistake that sequence, treating as spontaneous an atmosphere that was, in large part, legislated and litigated into being.

## The Inheritance: The March of 7 November 1966 and the Demand for a Central Ban

The post-2014 legislatures inherited a demand with a long organisational pedigree, and the pedigree explains the demand's eventual state-level form. Organised cow protection in India runs from the Gaurakshini Sabhas of the 1880s and Dayananda Saraswati's campaign literature through the freedom movement's ambivalent embrace, in which Mahatma Gandhi revered the cow while refusing, on principle, to compel others' practice by law. The demand's sharpest pre-2014 confrontation came on 7 November 1966, when a vast procession organised by the Sarvadaliya Goraksha Maha-Abhiyan Samiti, uniting Hindu religious orders with the Bharatiya Jana Sangh and allied parties, marched on Parliament in New Delhi demanding a central statute prohibiting cow slaughter across India. The day ended in disorder at Parliament Street, police firing in which at least eight persons were killed by the commonly cited count, a curfew in the capital and the resignation, days later, of the Union Home Minister Gulzarilal Nanda, himself a protection sympathiser whose handling of the march his government judged a failure. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's government answered the demand with the constitutional fact this article has stressed: slaughter legislation belonged to the states, and the Union would not, and on the prevailing reading could not, enact the central ban the marchers sought. A committee of experts and parliamentarians was appointed to examine the question and reported without producing legislation, and the demand settled into the pattern it would keep for five decades, pressed at the centre, discharged or refused in the states.

The 1966 precedent matters to the adjudication at this article's centre because it falsifies, in advance, the two easiest stories about 2014. Against the claim that cow protection arrived in Indian politics as a recent invention of one party's strategy stands the record of a mass movement, older than the Bharatiya Janata Party and older than its predecessor, that filled the capital's streets two generations earlier on the same demand and drew support across the religious orders and several parties. Against the claim that the post-2014 transformation was a central government's imposition stands the demand's own history of defeat at the centre: 1966 sought a national statute because the state route was slow and uneven, and 1966 failed for the reason 2017's market rules failed, the Seventh Schedule's allocation. What changed after 2014 was not the demand, which was old, nor the venue, which the Constitution had fixed, but the occupation of the venue: for the first time, governments committed to the demand held office simultaneously in the Union and in most of the large prohibiting states, and the state route, pursued in parallel across a dozen assemblies with shared texts and competitive severity, produced in five years the aggregate effect the central route had failed to produce in fifty. The Jana Sangh's heirs legislated where their predecessors had marched. That sentence, and not any slogan, is the strategic reading's true core, and the architectural reading absorbs it whole: strategy explains the occupation, architecture records what the occupants built.

## Three Readings of the Post-2014 Turn

The scholarly and journalistic literature of the period divides, on inspection, into the three readings named at the outset, and the division is worth stating fairly before it is adjudicated. The violence reading rests on a documented sequence of killings and assaults, many of them recorded by data journalism projects that counted attacks linked to cow protection allegations from 2010 onward and found the overwhelming concentration after 2014. Its strongest form, argued in essays by Hilal Ahmed among others, holds that the cow became a device through which a majoritarian public sphere marked out Muslim citizens as permanently suspect, with each incident teaching a lesson far beyond its village. The strategic reading rests on electoral evidence: the Bharatiya Janata Party's manifestos, the timing of amendments in election seasons, the place of cow protection in the party's longer ideological inheritance from the Jana Sangh, which had mounted a mass campaign for a central prohibition in November 1966 and marched on Parliament in one of the largest demonstrations of that decade. Its strongest form, developed in Suhas Palshikar's analyses of the post-2014 party system, holds that cow protection functions as one strand of a hegemonic project that redefines the terms of national belonging while presenting itself as the guardian of ordinary sentiment. Christophe Jaffrelot's Modi's India of 2021 gives the violence reading its most documented scholarly form, treating cow vigilantism as an instrument through which an ethnic democracy disciplines minorities while the formal constitution stands unrepealed, and his account is used throughout this article for incident chronology, with the caution that the book appeared after the period it describes and is cited as scholarship, not as a source contemporary with the events.

The architectural reading defended here does not ask the reader to discard either rival. It asks where each rival's explanation runs out. The violence reading explains the terror of a truck journey through Alwar district; it does not explain why the same truck journey is a criminal offence carrying ten years in Haryana, a licensed transaction in West Bengal and an unremarkable one in Kerala, nor why a Maharashtra householder in 2015 faced prosecution for the contents of a refrigerator while a householder in Goa did not. The strategic reading explains why a governing party might want the cow in its campaigns; it does not explain the content of what its legislatures wrote, the grading of offences, the choice to criminalise possession rather than only slaughter, the decision in Gujarat to reach for life imprisonment, or the decision in Madhya Pradesh, under a Congress government in 2019, to punish violence committed in the cow's name. Electoral strategy predicts volume, not text. Architecture is text. The renegotiation thesis also explains the period's most underreported fact: the change survived changes of government and party. Statutes enacted by one party were administered by its rivals; the Madhya Pradesh amendment of 2019 tightened the anti-vigilante side of a prohibition statute that a Bharatiya Janata Party government had written in 2004 and sharpened in 2012, and no major party in the prohibiting states campaigned to repeal a cow law in this period. A settlement, in the sense used by [the contested-settlement framework](/2020/04/09/indian-secularism-contested-settlement/), is precisely an arrangement that contending parties fight inside rather than over, and by 2020 cow protection law had acquired that character in most of northern and western India. The fight continued, fiercely, over presumptions, penalties, enforcement and violence. The existence of the protected status itself had moved toward settlement, and the movement is measurable only in the architecture.

## The Three-Stage Map of State-Level Variation

The article's findable artifact, designed for citation across this pillar, is the Three-Stage State-Level Architectural-Variation Map, which sorts the post-2014 changes by the kind of architectural work each stage performed rather than by the noise each generated. Stage One, from 2015, is the stage of stringency amendments: existing prohibitions were widened from slaughter to possession and transport, penalties multiplied, burdens of proof reversed. Stage Two, from 2017, is the stage of institutional additions: governments built enforcement expectations, shelters, funds and dedicated machinery around the statutes, and Gujarat converted the offence's ceiling into life imprisonment. Stage Three, also from 2017, is the stage of ambiguous policing: non-state cow protection groups operated alongside, and at times inside the penumbra of, state enforcement, and the boundary of authorised coercion blurred until the Supreme Court redrew it in July 2018. The stages overlap in time and coexist within single states; the map sorts mechanisms, not moments.

The map's discipline is that every cell names a statute, a date or a judgment that a sceptical reader can open. It excludes rhetoric, however heated, and it excludes incidents, however grave, except where an incident produced law. On that discipline the map supports the article's claim directly: between 2015 and 2020 the Indian state's recognition of cow protection changed in kind, from a prohibition carried on the books of most northern and western states to an offence structure with reversed presumptions, an enforcement apparatus with dedicated funds, and a penumbra of private enforcement that the Supreme Court found it necessary to answer with the most detailed anti-lynching directions in its history. Each of those three elements is a fact about architecture. None of them is a fact about atmosphere, and a history of the period written only in the vocabulary of atmosphere would be unable to state them.

## Stage One: Maharashtra Extends the Ban from Slaughter to Possession

Maharashtra opened the sequence, and the manner of its opening is itself architectural evidence. The Maharashtra Animal Preservation Act of 1976 had prohibited the slaughter of cows while allowing bulls and bullocks to be slaughtered on a fit-for-slaughter certificate, the Quareshi compromise enacted into state law. An amendment bill to extend the prohibition to bulls and bullocks had been passed by the state legislature in 1995 and then waited, unassented, for nineteen years, a draft recognition suspended between two levels of the federation. On 4 March 2015 the President of India gave assent to the Maharashtra Animal Preservation (Amendment) Act, and the Devendra Fadnavis government brought it into force within the month. The amendment's provisions repay reading. Section 5 prohibited the slaughter of cows, bulls and bullocks alike. Section 5A prohibited transport for slaughter. Section 5B prohibited the sale and purchase of the protected animals for slaughter. Section 5C prohibited possession of the flesh of a cow, bull or bullock slaughtered in contravention of the Act, and section 5D extended the possession offence to flesh of animals slaughtered outside Maharashtra altogether. Section 9A fixed the penalty for the slaughter, transport, sale and possession offences at imprisonment of up to five years and a fine of up to ten thousand rupees, and the offences were made cognizable and non-bailable. Section 9B, the provision with the longest consequences, placed the burden of proof on the accused: a person found with beef was presumed to hold it in contravention of the Act unless he proved otherwise.

The movement from slaughter to possession is the hinge on which the whole period turns, and it deserves exact statement. A slaughter prohibition polices an act at a place: the slaughterhouse, the backyard killing, the point of death. A possession prohibition polices a substance anywhere: the truck, the cold storage, the restaurant kitchen, the family refrigerator. The first kind of law addresses butchers and traders. The second addresses eaters, and it converts a dietary practice into a permanent evidentiary exposure for every household that practises it. Maharashtra's Muslim and Dalit beef-eating communities, for whom beef had been the cheapest available animal protein and a lawful food when the animal was slaughtered under the old certificate regime, woke in March 2015 inside a reversed legal world: the meat itself, regardless of where the animal died, was contraband in their hands unless they could prove a lawful origin. The Fadnavis government defended the amendment as the fulfilment of Article 48 and of a two-decade-old legislative decision, and its law officers argued before the Bombay High Court that the state was entitled to pursue the Directive Principle by the means it judged effective. The architectural fact stands independent of the defence: Maharashtra 2015 was the first post-2014 demonstration that the renegotiation would be conducted by widening the offence's radius, from the act of killing to the condition of holding. Every subsequent statute in the sequence learned from it, and the courts' answers to it, delivered in May 2016, set the terms on which the widening would be contested.

## Stage One: Haryana Writes a New Statute and Repeals 1955

Haryana chose a different instrument to the same end: not amendment but replacement. The state had inherited the Punjab Prohibition of Cow Slaughter Act of 1955, which applied to Haryana after the state's creation in 1966 and which punished cow slaughter with imprisonment of up to five years or a fine of up to five thousand rupees. The Manohar Lal Khattar government passed the Haryana Gauvansh Sanrakshan and Gausamvardhan Act through the state assembly on 16 March 2015, and the Act came into force in November 2015, repealing the 1955 Punjab Act as applicable to Haryana. Its very title announced the double programme of the period's legislation: sanrakshan, protection, and samvardhan, the development and flourishing of indigenous breeds, joined in one statute. Section 3 prohibited the slaughter of cows outright, with narrow exceptions for certified disease and suffering. Section 4 regulated the export of cows from the state by permit. Section 8 prohibited the sale of beef, including canned beef. The penalty section, section 13, prescribed rigorous imprisonment of not less than three years and up to ten years, with a fine of thirty thousand to one lakh rupees, for slaughter; three to seven years for export for slaughter; and three to five years for the sale of beef. Section 14 placed the burden of proof on the accused in the defined cases. Section 15 made the offences cognizable and non-bailable. Section 17 provided for the confiscation of vehicles used in an offence. The Act also established the institutional seed that Stage Two would grow: provisions for laboratories to test seized meat and for institutions to shelter infirm, stray and uneconomic cows at public expense.

Read beside Maharashtra's amendment, Haryana's statute shows the variation inside the convergence. Both states widened offences, raised penalties and reversed burdens within the same year, under governments of the same party, citing the same Directive Principle. Yet the texts differ in their centres of gravity. Maharashtra's amendment is a police text, organised around offences and presumptions, grafted onto a 1976 framework. Haryana's is a programme text as well, organised around the protected animal's whole life cycle, slaughter prohibited at one end and publicly funded shelter promised at the other. The difference mattered in administration: Haryana's law committed the state to a population of seized and stray cattle whose care required budgets, land and staff, so that the recognition conferred on the cow generated a visible fiscal and administrative trail by which later auditors could measure it. It mattered in politics too, because the shelter commitment gave the statute's defenders an answer to the charge that protection was merely punitive, and gave its critics a measurable promise against which to set the state's performance when stray cattle multiplied on Haryana's roads. The pattern recurs across the map: the post-2014 statutes characteristically pair a prohibition with a provision, a stick with a shelter, and the pairing is not decoration. It is the form that recognition takes when a practice's object is a living animal that must be fed for fifteen years after the law forbids its slaughter and its sale. The economics of that pairing, and the crisis it produced when the arithmetic failed, belong to a later section of this article.

## Stage One: Madhya Pradesh and the Stringency Ratchet

Madhya Pradesh demonstrates that the ratchet did not begin in 2014, and its chronology disciplines any account that treats the period as a rupture from nothing. The state's Govansh Vadh Pratishedh Adhiniyam of 2004 had already prohibited the slaughter of cow progeny comprehensively, with imprisonment of up to three years. An amendment enacted in 2012, assented to by the President after the bill's reservation, raised the maximum punishment for cow slaughter to seven years with a minimum fine of five thousand rupees, placed the burden of proving innocence on the accused, and empowered any police officer not below the rank of head constable, or any person authorised by a competent authority, to enter, inspect, search and seize where an offence was believed likely. The 2012 Madhya Pradesh text thus anticipated, under a Bharatiya Janata Party state government of the pre-2014 period, the three devices that define Stage One nationally: the multiplied penalty, the reversed burden and the widened power of search. What changed after 2014 was not the invention of these devices but their diffusion and normalisation. By the end of 2015 the devices stood in the law of Maharashtra, Haryana and Madhya Pradesh alike; by 2017 Gujarat had carried the penalty device to its logical maximum; Jharkhand's Bovine Animal Prohibition of Slaughter Act of 2005, with penalties reaching ten years for the sale and possession offences, was enforced with a new energy in the same years, and Rajasthan's Bovine Animal Act of 1995, prescribing up to five years, acquired companion rules on the transport and temporary migration of cattle. A ratchet, in legislative terms, is a mechanism that turns in one direction and locks against return. The post-2014 states built one cooperatively, each amendment citing the others' severity as the going rate, no assembly willing to be recorded as the soft jurisdiction, and none, in this period, repealing or diluting a predecessor's severity.

### What did the post-2015 amendments change in practice?

The amendments changed the offence's object, its price and its proof. The object widened from slaughter to the possession, sale and transport of beef and cattle. The price rose to five years in Maharashtra, ten in Haryana and life imprisonment in Gujarat. The proof inverted, with burden-of-proof clauses requiring the accused to establish lawful origin.

The ratchet's mechanism deserves one further observation, because it is the point where the architectural reading captures something the strategic reading states only vaguely. Party competition in the period operated on the texts themselves. When Gujarat's assembly in March 2017 raised the slaughter penalty from the seven years its 2011 amendment had set to life imprisonment, ministers justified the increase by pointing to the law's failure to deter, not to any electoral calendar, and the justification was recorded in the assembly's proceedings for any reader to test. When Haryana's ministers defended ten years, they pointed to smuggling routes into neighbouring jurisdictions with weaker penalties, an argument that only makes sense inside a federation of unequal statutes. The variation, in other words, was not noise around a national policy. It was the policy's medium: governments competed through texts, borrowed from texts and answered texts, and the cumulative body of those texts is the renegotiation. The scholarly literature's tendency to treat state law as the mere footprint of a national politics inverts the actual sequence visible in the record, in which the national politics of the cow after 2014 was substantially composed of state texts, state enforcements and state scandals, aggregated by national media into a single story. Recovering the state level, statute by statute, is therefore not pedantry. It is the recovery of the level at which the renegotiation was actually executed, a level the Constitution's federal allocation had designated in 1948 and to which [the four-dimensional comparative framework](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/) directs attention whenever Indian political change is disaggregated by state settlement.

## Stage Two: Gujarat Reaches for Life Imprisonment, 31 March 2017

Gujarat's amendment of 31 March 2017 is the period's most legible single text, because its drafters said on the record what the text was for. The Gujarat Animal Preservation Act of 1954, strengthened by an amendment of 2011 that had imposed a complete prohibition on the slaughter and transport of cow progeny with imprisonment of up to seven years, was amended again on the final day of the assembly's budget session. The Gujarat Animal Preservation (Amendment) Act of 2017 raised the punishment for the slaughter of cows, calves, bulls and bullocks to imprisonment for a term of not less than ten years and extending to imprisonment for life, with a fine that could extend to five lakh rupees. The illegal transport of cattle for slaughter, and the sale and storage of beef, drew seven to ten years and fines between one and five lakh rupees. Vehicles used in the offences were made liable to permanent confiscation, the offences were made cognizable and non-bailable, and the transport of cattle between defined night hours was prohibited outright. The bill as introduced had proposed a maximum of ten years; the life term was added on the floor, on the motion of the Minister of State for Home, Pradeepsinh Jadeja, who described the result as the harshest cow protection law in the country and presented the enhancement as a response to representations from religious orders during the Chaitri Navratri festival then in progress. Chief Minister Vijay Rupani, defending the amendment, stated that his government wished to make Gujarat vegetarian, that the cow held economic as well as religious significance in the state's life, and that Gujarat was the land of Mahatma Gandhi, of Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and of Prime Minister Narendra Modi. The Congress opposition, suspended from the house that day after protests on an unrelated matter, criticised the amendment from outside the chamber as theatre over enforcement.

Two features of the Gujarat text repay architectural attention. The first is calibration. Indian criminal law reserves life imprisonment, in the main, for homicide and the gravest offences against the person and the state. By attaching it to the slaughter of an animal, the Gujarat assembly made a statement about the protected object's rank in the state's scale of values that no speech could have made with equal authority, and every commentary on the period, sympathetic and hostile alike, registered the statement. The second feature is the transport architecture. The amendment reached beyond slaughter to the logistics of the cattle economy: night movement prohibited, vehicles forfeitable, storage criminalised. A cattle economy runs on movement, from village to market to dairy to slaughterhouse, and a law that polices movement polices the economy's every participant, the farmer selling an unproductive animal, the trader aggregating a truckload, the driver, the buyer. Gujarat's text is therefore best read not as a slaughter law with additions but as a comprehensive legal environment around the cow, in which the animal's death is the most severely punished of a graded family of offences that begins with its unauthorised movement after dark. That comprehensiveness is the mature form of the Stage One widening that Maharashtra had begun, and it is the form toward which the federation's severe jurisdictions converged by the decade's end.

## Stage Two: Uttar Pradesh Builds the Apparatus

Uttar Pradesh, the state whose politics this series has treated as [the bellwether laboratory of Indian politics](/2020/01/06/uttar-pradesh-bellwether-laboratory/), entered the sequence with the oldest statute in the severe group, its Prevention of Cow Slaughter Act of 1955, under which slaughter drew imprisonment that amendments had raised over the decades to a maximum of seven years, with attempt punishable at half that scale. The Yogi Adityanath government, sworn in on 19 March 2017, did not begin by amending that Act. It began with machinery. Within days of taking office the government ordered the closure of illegal slaughterhouses and a drive against the illegal cattle trade, a campaign executed through municipal and police machinery in March and April 2017 that shut licensed premises alleged to be in breach of pollution and licensing conditions alongside unlicensed ones, and that the state's meat traders answered with a strike and litigation in the Allahabad High Court, which directed that licences be renewed and processed according to law. In December 2018 the state cabinet approved a cow welfare cess, an additional levy of half a percent on excise and other specified revenue streams, earmarked for the construction and maintenance of cattle shelters, together with a programme under which every district would maintain shelters for stray and seized cattle and urban local bodies would build kanji houses. In January 2019, confronted with a stray cattle emergency in which farmers, unable to sell unproductive animals into a frightened market, drove them onto roads and into government compounds, the government directed district administrations to move strays into shelters and schools found locked by villagers against herds of abandoned cattle became the period's most reproduced image of the policy's feedback loop. Through the same years the state government announced, in case after case made public by its own police, the invocation of the National Security Act and the Gangsters Act against accused cattle smugglers and slaughterers, preventive and organised-crime statutes layered over the 1955 Act's own penalties.

The Uttar Pradesh record completes the definition of Stage Two. Where Gujarat changed the price of the offence, Uttar Pradesh built the institutions that would administer the protected status at scale: a revenue line, the cess; a built environment, the district shelter; an enforcement doctrine, the closure drive and the preventive-detention overlay. The Adityanath government stated its rationale in terms drawn from both founding vocabularies, Article 48's directive and the cow's place in the religious life of the majority, and it framed the shelter programme as evidence that protection meant care rather than only punishment. The critics of the programme, including the state's own opposition parties and the press of the stray-cattle winter, answered on the same institutional ground: shelters underfunded relative to the cattle population the market collapse had stranded, cess collections modest against maintenance costs, and a rural economy absorbing losses that the statute book did not record. Both the defence and the critique were arguments about apparatus, which is the point. By 2019, in India's largest state, cow protection was no longer adequately describable as a slogan, a sentiment or a threat. It was a budget line, a building programme, a detention practice and a municipal problem, an administrative fact with which every succeeding government would have to reckon on taking office, whatever its ideology.

### Did the Union government ban cow slaughter across India?

No national prohibition was enacted in this period, and none could be enacted by the Union acting alone, since the subject sits in the State List. The Union's single direct intervention, the livestock market rules notified on 23 May 2017, was stayed by the Madras High Court and then across India by the Supreme Court on 11 July 2017.

## Stage Two's Wider Field: Jharkhand, Rajasthan, Uttarakhand and the Karnataka Limit

The map's remaining cells are briefer, and their brevity is informative. Jharkhand administered its 2005 prohibition, among the older severe texts, with renewed prosecution drives, and the state figures in the violence record below. Rajasthan's government administered its 1995 Act and in 2015 created a ministry-level department for cow affairs, an early instance of the institutional addition that Stage Two generalised; Rajasthan also became, in December 2019, one of the states to enact a dedicated anti-lynching statute, the Rajasthan Protection from Lynching Act, passed by a Congress government, so that the same state carries both a severe cow statute and a severe anti-lynching statute on its books, a juxtaposition the architectural reading alone can hold in one frame. Uttarakhand administered its Protection of Cow Progeny Act of 2007, with penalties of three to seven years. Karnataka marks the map's honest limit. Its Prevention of Cow Slaughter and Cattle Preservation Act of 1964 operates the certificate model, permitting the slaughter of cattle certified as aged or unfit, and repeated proposals to replace it with a total prohibition were debated in the period without enactment inside the evidence window this article covers; the Karnataka cell of the map therefore records the certificate regime's survival under pressure, which is itself a datum about variation: the renegotiation was a federation of moves, not a uniform tide, and the states that did not move are part of its explanation. Kerala and West Bengal, holding to regulation, and the northeastern states, holding to no prohibition, complete the federation's spread. The spread is the settlement's federal signature, and it is why the article's claim is framed in terms of renegotiation through variation rather than national conversion.

## The Union's One Move: The Livestock Market Rules of 23 May 2017 and Their Stay

The federation's allocation held throughout the period, with one attempted exception that proves its strength. On 23 May 2017 the Union Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change notified the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (Regulation of Livestock Markets) Rules, made under the central Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act of 1960. Rule 22 of the notified text prohibited the sale and purchase of cattle in animal markets for slaughter, required written declarations from sellers that animals were not brought for slaughter, barred buyers from reselling purchased cattle for slaughter, and constituted animal market committees in every district to administer the regime. Because most cattle in India change hands in markets, a market rule against slaughter sales would have functioned as a near-national restriction on the cattle trade, achieved by delegated legislation under an animal welfare statute rather than by primary legislation on a state subject. The reaction was immediate and came from several directions at once: the governments of Kerala and West Bengal objected that the rules invaded the states' legislative field; cattle traders and meat exporters projected losses across the leather and meat industries; and challenges were filed in the high courts. On 30 May 2017 the Madurai Bench of the Madras High Court stayed the rules' operation. On 11 July 2017 a Supreme Court bench of Chief Justice J. S. Khehar and Justice D. Y. Chandrachud extended the stay to the whole country, recording the Union government's own submission that it was reconsidering the rules in the light of objections, and observing that the livelihood of people could not be subjected to uncertainties. The Centre informed the Court it would re-notify a revised text after considering representations; within the period covered here, the operative restriction on market sales never came into force, and the slaughter prohibition map of India remained what the state legislatures had made it.

The episode is the cleanest natural experiment the period offers, and its lesson supports the article's thesis from an unexpected direction. A national executive commanding large parliamentary majorities, publicly committed to cow protection, attempted one national instrument and was halted within seven weeks by the combined resistance of federal structure, judicial review and organised economic interests, and it did not return with a statute, because a statute on a State List subject was constitutionally unavailable to it. The renegotiation therefore continued where the Constitution had always required it to continue, in the state assemblies, and its national character was an aggregate effect of state actions, not a national act. Analysts who describe the period's cow politics as a central imposition mistake the aggregate for the instrument. The instruments were twenty-eight state legal orders, amended at different times to different depths, and the one genuinely central instrument of the period failed. The failure also clarifies the role of [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) through which this series reads the post-2014 order: on the cow, unlike on taxation or citizenship, the centralising tension met a textual wall in the Seventh Schedule and stopped, and the federal tension absorbed the policy whole.

## Stage Three: Where Authorised Policing Ends and Private Coercion Begins

The third stage is defined by a boundary problem. Cow protection law created offences that occur on roads, in markets and in transit, across vast rural geographies, and the state's enforcement machinery was thin in exactly those geographies. Into the gap stepped the gau raksha dals, cow protection squads, and a wider penumbra of local groups, some registered as charities running shelters, some attached to established organisations within [the post-2014 RSS-BJP coordination architecture](/2019/10/21/post-2014-rss-bjp-relationship/), many unaffiliated and village-level. Their documented practices ranged across a spectrum that the record requires be kept distinct. At one end stood cooperation that governments openly acknowledged: groups tipping off police about suspected smuggling, assisting at checkpoints, receiving seized cattle into affiliated shelters. In the middle stood tolerated parallelism: patrols stopping cattle trucks on highways, detaining drivers and handing them to police, in a division of labour that no statute authorised and few district administrations publicly disowned. At the far end stood the violence recorded in the following sections, in which groups beat, robbed and killed men they accused of slaughter or smuggling, sometimes filming the assaults. The state governments' stated positions were consistent in form: cow protection was lawful and honoured, vigilantism was condemned, and no one would be permitted to take the law into private hands, a formula Prime Minister Narendra Modi himself used in public statements of August 2016, when he said that most self-described cow protectors were anti-social elements by night, and again in June 2017, when he stated that killing people in the name of cow devotion was not acceptable. The critics' strongest counter, made by opposition parties and by the petitioners in the Supreme Court, was that the formula's repetition alongside continued attacks, slow prosecutions and occasional official indulgence of accused persons converted condemnation into licence. The architectural point, which both positions presuppose, is that by 2017 the enforcement penumbra was a standing feature of the legal order in the severe states: the law on the road was, in part, what armed or mobilised civilians did in the law's name, and everyone in the cattle economy knew it and priced it into their decisions.

The most telling evidence for Stage Three's reality is a class of records the brief for this article flags as under-cited: the correspondence and orders preserved in state home department archives, in which district officials sought instructions on the handling of cow protection groups, reported their movements, registered shelters, assigned seized cattle and minuted the terms on which cooperation would proceed. Read closely, such files show neither the clean command of a state directing auxiliaries nor the clean absence of a state confronting criminals, but a negotiated interface, improvised district by district. That improvisation was the stage's substantive content. A legal order in which the boundary of coercion is negotiated locally, case by case, has been renegotiated as surely as one whose statutes have been rewritten, and with more immediate consequences for the citizen on the road. It was this boundary, and not any statute, that the Supreme Court addressed in July 2018, in the judgment examined below. Before that judgment, however, the record of the boundary's failure in three documented cases must be stated, because the Court was answering those cases and the country knew it.

### How do cow protection laws shift the burden of proof?

Several statutes require the accused, rather than the prosecution, to prove that seized meat or cattle were held lawfully. Haryana's Act of 2015 does so in its section 14, Madhya Pradesh's amended law does the same, and Maharashtra's section 9B did so until the Bombay High Court struck the provision down in May 2016.

## The Bombay High Court Prunes Maharashtra: 6 May 2016

The judiciary's pruning of Stage One arrived early and precisely. On 6 May 2016 a division bench of the Bombay High Court, Justices A. S. Oka and S. C. Gupte, delivered a 225-page judgment on the batch of petitions challenging the Maharashtra amendment. The Court upheld the prohibition on the slaughter of bulls and bullocks, accepting the legislature's competence and the Article 48 lineage of the law. It then cut back the widening at its two furthest points. Section 5D, which criminalised the possession of beef from animals slaughtered outside Maharashtra, was struck down as a violation of the right to privacy within Article 21: the Court held that preventing a citizen from possessing the flesh of an animal slaughtered outside the state amounted to prohibiting the consumption of food of his choice, and that consuming food not injurious to health formed part of an individual's autonomy and right to be let alone. Section 9B, the reversed burden, was struck down as violative of Article 21 for failing the test of a fair, just and reasonable procedure. Section 5C, the possession offence for animals slaughtered inside Maharashtra, survived but was read down: only conscious possession, possession with knowledge that the flesh came from an animal slaughtered in contravention of the Act, would constitute the offence, and the prosecution would bear the burden of proving that knowledge. The state government's health-based defences, pressed in affidavits though moderated in argument, were not accepted as a basis for the struck provisions.

The judgment's architectural significance is that it sorted the amendment's devices into the durable and the vulnerable. Prohibition: durable. Possession of externally slaughtered meat: unconstitutional. Reversed burden: unconstitutional. Possession of internally slaughtered meat, with knowledge proved by the state: durable. Legislatures drafting after May 2016 could read the sorting as instructions, and the later texts show the lesson absorbed in part: the severe statutes that followed kept their prohibitions and penalties maximal while their possession and presumption clauses attracted the litigation. The Maharashtra government appealed to the Supreme Court, which issued notice and tagged the matter with connected cases; the appeal stood pending through the period covered here, leaving the High Court's pruning operative in India's second most populous state. The privacy reasoning of May 2016 also acquired a second life: delivered a year before the nine-judge bench in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India confirmed privacy as a fundamental right in August 2017, the beef judgment stands as one of the working precedents through which the doctrine entered Indian law by application before it entered by declaration.

## Dadri, 28 September 2015: The Case That Named the Period

The first killing of the sequence fixed its vocabulary. On the night of 28 September 2015, in Bisara village in the Dadri area of Uttar Pradesh, a crowd gathered after an announcement, made according to the police investigation from the village temple's loudspeaker, that a cow had been slaughtered and its meat kept in a house. The crowd entered the house of Mohammad Akhlaq, a fifty-year-old ironsmith, dragged him and his son Danish into the lane and beat them with bricks and sticks. Akhlaq died of his injuries that night; Danish survived after surgery for severe head injuries. Police recovered meat from the vicinity, and its identity became an early and bitter contest: an initial veterinary examination described the sample as mutton, while a forensic laboratory report later described the sample, whose chain of custody the defence disputed, as beef, and the case proceeded to trial in the district court at Surajpur, where it remained pending through the period covered here, with the accused, who included a son of a local Bharatiya Janata Party worker among the eighteen initially arrested, released on bail over time. The Union and state governments condemned the killing; ministers and party figures also made statements, recorded in the press of those weeks, variously describing the event as a spontaneous reaction, questioning the meat's identity or urging that the accused not be prejudged, and the opposition parties made those statements a charge of indulgence. Akhlaq's younger son Sartaj, a corporal in the Indian Air Force, appealed publicly for peace and against the politicisation of his father's death, a statement the record preserves and this article repeats because it belongs to the family's own account of itself.

Dadri matters to this article for three reasons that survive the case's notoriety. It occurred six months after the Maharashtra amendment had made possession a crime two states away, in a state, Uttar Pradesh, whose own slaughter law dated to 1955 and had not changed: the crowd's operative theory, that meat in a Muslim house was itself an offence demanding punishment, travelled faster than any statute and drew on the period's widening logic rather than on the local text. It demonstrated the enforcement penumbra in its most primitive form, a temple announcement and a crowd constituting themselves as the law's instrument before any officer arrived. And it set the pattern of official response that the Supreme Court would eventually answer as a system: condemnation of the killing, contest over the meat, procedural delay, and a trial measured in years. The violence reading of the period begins, properly, at Dadri. The architectural reading adds only that Dadri's crowd acted on a theory of law, a wrong theory in that place, since possession was no offence in Uttar Pradesh, but a theory assembled from the period's real legal materials, and that the distance between the crowd's theory and the statute book is itself a measure of how far the renegotiation's penumbra extended beyond its texts.

## Alwar Twice: Pehlu Khan in April 2017 and Rakbar Khan in July 2018

On 1 April 2017 Pehlu Khan, a fifty-five-year-old dairy farmer from Nuh district in Haryana, was returning from Jaipur, where he had purchased milch cattle at a fair, travelling with his sons and companions and carrying the purchase documents, when the party's vehicles were stopped near Behror in Alwar district of Rajasthan by men who accused them of smuggling cows for slaughter. The men were beaten with sticks and rods; Pehlu Khan died of his injuries on 3 April 2017. Before his death he named six men in a statement to the police. The Rajasthan police investigation took a course that the record must state exactly: in September 2017 the police closed the case against the six named men, citing the inquiry's findings, and proceeded against others identified from video of the assault; a chargesheet against six accused was filed in 2019, and in August 2019 the district court at Alwar acquitted the six who stood trial, giving them the benefit of doubt, a judgment the Rajasthan government announced it would appeal. The state also charged, in a separate case that drew wide criticism, the transport of cattle without proper documentation against Pehlu Khan's sons and companions under the Rajasthan bovine law. On the night of 20 July 2018, in the same district, Rakbar Khan, a thirty-one-year-old dairy farmer from Kolgaon in Haryana, was beaten by a crowd at Lalawandi village in Ramgarh while walking two cows home on foot; he died that night. The official record of that case contains a documented failure inside the state itself: the first information report and the subsequent departmental proceedings recorded that the police party which reached the scene took the seized cows to a shelter and the accused to the station before taking the injured Rakbar Khan to hospital, a delay of hours that the state government acknowledged by suspending officers and ordering an inquiry, and the case entered the chargesheet stage with local cow protection activists among the accused.

The two Alwar cases, read with Dadri, supplied the factual spine of the petitions that reached the Supreme Court in Tehseen S. Poonawalla's public interest litigation, and they show Stage Three at its most consequential: men transporting cattle lawfully, with documents, inside a federation where their journey crossed jurisdictions of differing severity, stopped by civilians whose claim to authority was the protection of the cow. Rajasthan's stated position, under its government of the day, was that the law would take its course and that the police inquiry's exoneration of the named men rested on evidence, a position the opposition and the victims' families rejected as a predetermined protection of the accused. The architectural reading does not need to resolve that dispute to use the cases. What the cases establish on any version of their facts is the existence of a standing civilian enforcement presence on the highways of the severe states, confident enough to stop vehicles, inspect documents and inflict punishment, and the consequential migration of risk into every lawful cattle transaction in those states. Traders altered routes, farmers sold at distress prices or not at all, transporters demanded premiums, and the cattle economy of the region reorganised itself around the penumbra's existence. That reorganisation, measurable in market behaviour, is an effect of the period's architecture as surely as any prosecution, and it is the bridge between the institutional record and the climate that the violence reading describes: the climate had a mechanism, and the mechanism was the blurred boundary of coercion on the road.

## Counting the Violence: The Data Projects and the Jharkhand Record

The violence reading of the period rests on counts, and the counts deserve the same discipline as the statutes. Because the Indian Penal Code contained no offence of lynching and the National Crime Records Bureau published no category for it, the period's numbers were assembled by data journalism. IndiaSpend's Hate Crime Watch project, which tracked religiously motivated crimes reported in the English-language press from 2009, recorded that cow-related violence was rare before 2014 and concentrated overwhelmingly after it, with the large majority of the dead and injured in the cow-related cases being Muslim, and with the incidents clustered in the severe-statute states of the north and west. A separate count published by IndiaSpend in June 2017, covering 2010 to 2017, found that eighty-six percent of the persons killed in cow-related violence across that span died in the years after the 2014 general election. The projects' methods were transparent about their limits: press-based counting under-records incidents in regions and languages the English press covers thinly, and classification by motive depends on the reporting available. Governments disputed the counts' framing rather than their individual cases, arguing that law and order incidents were being aggregated into a communal pattern by selective attention, and ministers pointed to convictions and arrests in individual cases as evidence that the state answered violence as violence. The dispute is itself data for the adjudication: the pattern claim and the incident claim were both made in public, by named holders, and the pattern's geographic correlation with the severe statutes is the kind of fact a purely atmospheric account cannot use and an architectural account can, since the statutes supplied the occasions, the vocabulary of accusation and the enforcement penumbra within which the counted incidents occurred.

Jharkhand's record within those counts requires separate statement, because the state combined an older severe statute, its Bovine Animal Prohibition of Slaughter Act of 2005, with a sequence of incidents that reached the courts. In March 2016, in Latehar district, a cattle trader named Mazloom Ansari and a twelve-year-old boy, Mohammed Imtiyaz Khan, were found hanged from a tree after leaving home with a herd of cattle for a market; the police investigation and trial that followed treated the killings as the work of men who had targeted the pair over the cattle trade, and the case entered the period's record as its starkest instance of the trade's exposure. In June 2017 Alimuddin Ansari, a meat trader, was beaten to death by a crowd at Ramgarh after being accused of carrying beef in his vehicle; in March 2018 a fast-track court convicted eleven of the accused, including a local Bharatiya Janata Party leader, and sentenced them to life imprisonment, one of the period's few completed trials, before the Jharkhand High Court suspended the sentences on bail within the year. The Jharkhand sequence cuts in two directions at once, and this article records both: it supplied the critics with their most documented instances of the penumbra's lethality, and it supplied the governments' defence with a completed prosecution and life sentences delivered by the state's own courts. An account that keeps only one of those directions has left the evidence behind.

## Poonawalla, 17 July 2018: The Court Answers the Street

The Supreme Court's judgment in Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India, delivered on 17 July 2018 by a bench of Chief Justice Dipak Misra and Justices A. M. Khanwilkar and D. Y. Chandrachud and reported at (2018) 9 SCC 501, is the period's central judicial text on the enforcement penumbra, and its directions are stated here by holding, as this pillar's discipline requires. The Court held that no individual or group may take the law into private hands, that mob violence and lynching have no place in a constitutional order, and that the state bears an affirmative obligation to prevent, remedy and punish such violence. Its preventive directions required every state to designate a senior police officer, not below the rank of Superintendent of Police, as nodal officer in each district, charged with constituting a special task force to gather intelligence on persons and groups spreading hate or planning violence, with identifying within three weeks the districts and highway stretches where lynching incidents had occurred, and with ensuring intensified patrolling in them. Its remedial directions required the first information report in every such case to reach the nodal officer, required state governments to frame, within one month, victim compensation schemes under section 357A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, weighing bodily and psychological injury and loss of earnings and employment, with interim relief payable within thirty days, and required cases to be tried in designated fast-track courts on a day-to-day basis, preferably concluded within six months of cognizance, with the maximum sentence ordinarily awarded on conviction. Its punitive directions required disciplinary proceedings, independent of criminal trial, against officials who failed to prevent an incident or to act against its perpetrators. The Court recommended that Parliament consider enacting a distinct offence of lynching with a deterrent sentence, and it warned that unchecked mob violence would turn a functioning democracy into a mobocracy in which fear, not law, governs the road.

The judgment's place in the architecture is double. Formally, it is a boundary-restoring text: it reassigns every coercive act to the state's authorised officers and surrounds their failure with personal disciplinary consequence, answering Stage Three with the grammar of Stage Two, through institutional specification. Substantively, it is an admission, from the Constitution's own interpreter, that the penumbra had become a system requiring system-level answer: nodal officers in every district of India, a compensation architecture, a trial timetable. State legislative responses followed within the window: Manipur enacted a protection from lynching statute in December 2018, Rajasthan in August 2019 and West Bengal in August 2019, each creating offences and officer duties modelled on the Court's template, each enacted by governments in opposition to the ruling party at the Union. The Union government constituted a group of ministers and a committee of secretaries to consider a central law, which had not been enacted within the period. The pattern is the federation's signature once more: a national judgment, state statutes, a national law deferred. The Court, like the legislatures before it, worked through the federal distribution, and the renegotiated settlement that emerged by 2020 included, as one of its load-bearing elements, a judicially specified duty structure that had not existed in 2014 and that any future government, of any party, must administer or answer for.

### Which states enacted anti-lynching laws after the Poonawalla judgment?

Manipur led in December 2018 with a statute creating offences for mob violence and duties for police officers, followed by Rajasthan and West Bengal in August 2019. Each state built on the Supreme Court's template of nodal officers, victim compensation and fast-tracked trials, while other states worked through executive instructions under the same directions.

## The Enacting Governments' Case, at Its Strongest

Evenhandedness requires the defence of the statutes to be stated as their authors stated it, at full strength, before it is weighed. That defence has four pillars, each anchored in the record. The first is constitutional warrant. Article 48 directs the state to take steps for prohibiting the slaughter of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle; the enacting governments argued, in assembly debates and in court affidavits, that they were discharging a commitment the founders had written and their predecessors had neglected, and that the Supreme Court had twice, in 1958 and in 2005, sustained the prohibition's constitutionality. The second pillar is the animal economy. Ministers from Vijay Rupani downward argued that indigenous breeds had been depleted by slaughter and neglect, that the cow's lifetime yield of milk, dung and draught exceeded its carcass value, and that a state serious about breed preservation, the samvardhan half of Haryana's title, could not leave the animal's end to the meat market. The third pillar is democratic mandate. Governments pointed to manifestos that promised cow protection and to elections won on them; Pradeepsinh Jadeja's floor speech of 31 March 2017 presented the Gujarat life term as responsiveness to the representations of religious orders and of the public, and cow protection had figured in the Bharatiya Janata Party's national manifestos and in several state manifestos of other parties across the decades, including Congress manifestos in Madhya Pradesh promising shelters and protection. The fourth pillar is order. Governments argued that cattle smuggling was a real and violent trade, that rustling and illegal transport generated the highway confrontations, and that severe statutes with professional enforcement were the alternative to private enforcement, not its cause; on this pillar the same governments condemned lynching in the terms the Prime Minister used in August 2016 and June 2017, and the enacting states prosecuted vigilante accused, however contested the quality of those prosecutions remained.

Stated together, the four pillars amount to a coherent theory of recognition: a civilisation's majority holds an animal sacred; the founders acknowledged the sentiment in the Constitution's text while deferring its enactment to legislatures; legislatures elected on explicit promises have enacted it, with severity proportioned, in their submission, to the failure of milder laws; and the state accompanies the prohibition with shelters, funds and breed programmes that give the recognition material form. A reader who finds the theory unpersuasive should still be able to recognise it as a theory, held by identifiable governments, argued from identifiable texts, and continuous with positions taken inside the Constituent Assembly itself in November 1948, when the religious vocabulary was present in the chamber alongside the economic one. The evenhandedness charter of this pillar forbids the easier treatment, in which the statutes are read as nothing but a code for majoritarian aggression, because that treatment cannot explain the texts' actual content: the shelter commitments, the breed programmes, the laboratory provisions for testing seized meat, or the fact that the sharpest anti-vigilante amendment of the period, Madhya Pradesh's of 2019, was written into a cow protection statute by a government of the opposing party, accepting the statute's frame while disciplining its penumbra.

## The Critics' Case, at Its Strongest

The critics' case, at its strongest, is likewise fourfold, and it is anchored in the same record read from the other side. The first charge is selective incidence. The offences created by the widened statutes fall, in the nature of the trade, overwhelmingly on Muslims, whose communities dominate cattle trading, meat work and leather, and on Dalit communities engaged in carcass work and beef consumption; a law neutral in text thus operates, the critics argue, as a communal filter in life, and its reversed burdens and cognizable, non-bailable offences hand every local accuser a state-forged instrument. The second charge is the collapse of the economic rationale. Quareshi in 1958 protected the cow partly as an economic asset; the critics, citing the mechanisation of ploughing, the spread of crossbred and buffalo milk production and the economics literature on India's livestock sector, argue that the unproductive cattle population is a burden the statutes convert into a crisis, since an animal that can neither be sold nor slaughtered must still be fed, and the farmer, not the state, feeds it until he abandons it. The third charge is impunity's pattern. Drawing on the Dadri, Alwar and Jharkhand cases, in Jharkhand a cattle trader, Mazloom Ansari, and a boy, Mohammed Imtiyaz Khan, were found hanged in Latehar district in March 2016 after being attacked while herding cattle, critics argue that investigations repeatedly softened against accused aligned with cow protection groups, that acquittals and closures accumulated, and that occasional official honours for accused persons, documented in the press record, signalled the state's divided mind. The fourth charge is constitutional morality: that a secular republic may not enact one community's dietary doctrine into criminal law binding on all, that the Constituent Assembly deliberately withheld fundamental-right status from the prohibition for that reason, and that the post-2014 legislatures used the Directive Principle as a laundering device for a religious enactment the founders had declined to make.

Each charge has its documented core, and the article's adjudication does not proceed by denying them. The incidence of the offences is what the trade's demography predicts; the stray cattle crisis of 2018 and 2019 in Uttar Pradesh is a matter of the state's own administrative record; the Latehar case and the Alwar closure are on the files; the founders' refusal of justiciability is in the debates. The critics err, in this article's judgment, only when they move from these cores to the conclusion that the statutes are epiphenomenal, a pretext whose content does not matter because its motive is known. Motive attribution cannot grade a penalty, distinguish a possession offence from a slaughter offence, or explain why the Bombay High Court could uphold half an amendment and strike half, changing the real exposure of real households by its reasoning. The critics' strongest institutional argument, the impunity pattern, is itself an architectural claim: it says the renegotiated settlement includes a distribution of enforcement energy, severe toward the trade, indulgent toward the penumbra, and that claim is best tested, as this article has tested it, at the level of texts, trials and directions, where it emerges substantially borne out for the years 2015 to 2018 and under judicial correction thereafter. Hilal Ahmed's essays give this critique its most careful form, reading the period's cow politics as a redefinition of the terms on which Muslim citizens appear in public life; the article accepts the reading's force while insisting that the redefinition was executed, and can be contested, in law.

## The Trade, the Tanneries and the Farm: The Renegotiation's Ledger

Between the statute book and the street stands the economy that the renegotiation reorganised, and its entries can be stated without rhetoric. India's cattle economy before 2014 moved animals through a long chain: the farmer selling an unproductive cow or a male calf; the village aggregator; the weekly market; the licensed transporter; and at the chain's end the dairy, the draught pool, the tannery or the slaughterhouse, with buffalo meat, legally distinct from cow beef in every prohibiting state, supplying one of the world's largest meat export trades and the leather industry of Agra, Kanpur and Kolkata drawing on the hides of legally slaughtered animals of both species. The widened statutes and the penumbra together taxed every link of the cow portion of that chain. Markets reported thinner attendance by traders unwilling to risk seizure; transporters raised rates or left the trade; seized cattle, confiscated pending trials measured in years, accumulated in shelters whose maintenance the seizing state funded imperfectly; and the farmer at the chain's head faced the arithmetic that produced the stray crisis, an animal consuming fodder worth more than its milk, unsellable into a frightened market, unkillable under the statute, until abandonment shifted the cost to the public road and the neighbour's field. The Uttar Pradesh government's shelter programme and cow welfare cess of December 2018 were the period's most direct official acknowledgment that the ledger existed: protection had created a population, and the population required a budget. The meat and leather export figures, buffalo-based and therefore lawful throughout, fluctuated with global prices and with the 2017 market rules episode rather than with the cow statutes directly, a distinction the record supports and this article preserves, since collapsing buffalo exports into the cow story is the commonest factual error in the period's commentary on both sides.

### Why do farmers appear on both sides of the cow protection debate?

The cow is two assets in one animal. As a milch animal of an indigenous breed it is capital whose preservation governments fund through shelters and breed programmes, and many households support protection in that form. As an unproductive animal it is a daily fodder cost the older market once absorbed through sale.

The ledger's political consequence is the least expected finding of the architectural reading. The farm interest, the constituency in whose name Article 48 was written in the language of animal husbandry, became by 2019 a source of pressure on the enacting governments themselves, demanding shelters, compensation for crop damage and relief from the stray population, and in places voting on the issue. A politics that the strategic reading describes purely as a majoritarian instrument had generated, by its own institutional operation, a material grievance inside the majority community's largest occupation. That development does not refute the strategic reading's electoral account of the statutes' origins. It demonstrates the thesis of this article from within the rival's territory: once recognition is built into institutions, the institutions generate their own politics, independent of the symbolism that built them, and the analyst who tracked only the symbolism would have been surprised by the winter of the locked schoolrooms, while the analyst tracking the architecture, the shelter budgets, the cess, the seizure inventories, would not.

## Adjudication: What the Architectural Reading Proves, and the Strongest Objection to It

The disagreement named at the outset can be adjudicated on the record assembled. The violence reading, in the form given it by Jaffrelot's Modi's India and by the data journalism of the period, establishes the incidents and their concentration, and no honest account may proceed without them; this article has stated them from investigations, chargesheets and judgments. The strategic reading, in Palshikar's form, establishes the electoral embedding of cow protection within a hegemonic project and explains the timing of several enactments. The architectural reading establishes a third body of fact that neither rival reaches: the graded, dated, citable transformation of the legal relationship between a religious-cultural practice and the state's recognition of it, executed through three stages of state-level variation, pruned by courts at identifiable points, answered by the Supreme Court with a duty-structure in 2018, and generating second-order politics, the stray crisis, the shelter budgets, the anti-lynching statutes, that neither violence nor strategy predicts. On the question this article set itself, what the actual content of the post-2014 renegotiation is and through what mechanism it operates, the architectural reading is the answer, and the namable claim stands: the post-2014 cow-protection politics renegotiates the religious-cultural-practice and state-recognition relationship through state-level variations, and its architectural content shapes the post-2014 secularism architecture. The claim connects the cow to the pillar's larger subject through [the religious-personal-law-state-intervention renegotiation](/2020/04/20/shah-bano-case-1986-act-india/), where the state likewise redrew the boundary of a community practice by statute, and through the audit of the Ayodhya aftermath in [the Liberhan Commission's seventeen-year inquiry](/2020/05/01/1992-demolition-liberhan-commission/), where institutional response, not event, carried the politics. Gujarat, the state that wrote the period's most severe cow text, carries an institutional history of its own, traced in this series through [the institutional-audit-trajectory reading](/2020/05/06/2002-gujarat-riots-politics/).

The strongest objection to the architectural reading, stated in the brief for this article and pressed by the violence reading's best exponents, is that it underweights the climate: that what changed after 2014 was not primarily the statute book, most of whose prohibitions predate the period, but the political atmosphere in which a crowd could assemble at a temple announcement or stop a truck on a highway confident of indulgence, and that an analysis of texts and institutions therefore studies the shadow and misses the act. The objection is half right, and the half in which it is right is incorporated in Stage Three, which is an account of climate given institutional form: the penumbra existed because enforcement design left a boundary ambiguous, because governments spoke in two registers, condemnation and honour, and because prosecutions lagged. Where the objection overreaches is in treating climate as uncaused. Atmospheres in a legal order are not weather. The confidence of the Behror crowd in April 2017 was compounded of the Gujarat life-term amendment passed four days earlier and celebrated across the region's press, of two years of Stage One and Stage Two signals about the protected object's rank, of the visible presence of organised protection groups operating with administrative tolerance, and of the Dadri precedent's lesson that consequences arrive slowly and uncertainly. Each ingredient is institutional. The architectural reading does not deny the climate; it supplies the climate's mechanism and its documentary trail, and it alone predicts the climate's partial correction through institutional means, the Poonawalla directions, the anti-lynching statutes, the Madhya Pradesh amendment punishing violence in the cow's name, which a pure climate account must treat as inexplicable changes in the weather. A settlement renegotiated through law can be renegotiated again through law, and the record of 2018 and 2019 shows the second movement beginning inside the first. That, finally, is the analytical dividend of the reading defended here: it identifies the terrain on which the contest continues, the terrain of texts, presumptions, budgets and duties, which is the terrain on which citizens, lawyers and legislatures can actually act.

## The Settlement as It Stands in the Statute Book

A closing inventory, bounded strictly by the period's record, states the renegotiated settlement's elements as they stand. The prohibition of cow slaughter is the law in the large majority of states, graded from Gujarat's life imprisonment through Haryana's ten years and Madhya Pradesh's seven to the older one and two year statutes, and it is regulation, not prohibition, in West Bengal and Kerala, with no prohibition across much of the northeast. The offence family around the prohibition has widened, in the severe states, to possession, sale, transport and export, with reversed burdens surviving in several jurisdictions after the Bombay High Court struck Maharashtra's version down. An apparatus of shelters, funds, a cess in Uttar Pradesh and dedicated departments or ministries in several states administers the protected animal's life at public cost, and the stray population that the apparatus cannot absorb stands as the settlement's acknowledged failure, recorded in the enacting states' own administrative documents. A judicial duty-structure, the Poonawalla directions of July 2018, assigns named officers personal responsibility for preventing the penumbra's violence, and three states have converted the template into anti-lynching statutes. The Union's single attempt at a national instrument, the market rules of May 2017, remains stayed and unrevived, and the subject remains the states' by constitutional allocation, as it has been since Entry 15 was written. Readers who wish to test this inventory against the primary texts can open the gazette copies of the state acts, which circulate as scanned office documents, through an [office file viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) and read the penalty clauses side by side; the variation the map records is visible on the face of the documents.

Within the series' architecture, the cow settlement belongs to the religion and politics pillar as its most complete specimen of the contested settlement at work: a founding text that authorised everything and compelled nothing, a federal venue that multiplied the answers, courts that widened and then pruned, a national executive halted at the Seventh Schedule, and a street that twice forced the judiciary to redraw the boundary of lawful coercion. The pillar's earlier studies show the same grammar in other dialects, in [the Ayodhya movement's long mobilisation](/2020/04/25/ayodhya-movement-reanalysed/) and in the Hindutva tradition's ideological self-definitions traced in [the three distinct ideologies of Hindutva](/2019/09/02/hindutva-three-distinct-ideologies/), and the cow case adds the grammar's statutory dialect in its purest form. The renegotiation was not a resolution. The possession clauses remain litigated, the appeals in the Alwar and Dadri cases remain within the judicial system, the stray herds remain on the roads of the severe states, and the Directive Principle that began the story remains, as the founders left it, an instruction awaiting each legislature's pleasure. What the record of 2014 to 2020 settles is narrower and more durable than any of the period's slogans: the Indian state, acting through its federated legislatures, rebuilt the legal environment of the cow from a prohibition on a bookshelf into an apparatus of offences, presumptions, shelters and duties, and in doing so redrew, state by state, the boundary between a community's sacred practice and the republic's public recognition. The boundary will be argued over for as long as the republic lasts. It will be argued, as this period established, in the language of statutes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Article 48?

Article 48 is a Directive Principle of State Policy in the Constitution of India. It directs the state to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines and, in particular, to take steps for preserving and improving the breeds and prohibiting the slaughter of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle. The Constituent Assembly adopted it on 24 November 1948 after declining to place cow protection among the enforceable fundamental rights, so the article instructs legislatures without empowering courts to compel them. Every cow slaughter statute in India claims lineage from this text, and every debate over such statutes returns to the founders' choice of a directive over a right.

### Q: Which Indian states ban cow slaughter?

Most Indian states prohibit cow slaughter in some form, but the map is a patchwork because the subject belongs to the states under Entry 15 of the State List. Gujarat, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Jammu and Kashmir have maintained broad prohibitions, graded from Gujarat's life imprisonment after 2017 down to older statutes carrying one or two years. West Bengal permits slaughter under a fit-for-slaughter certificate for aged or unfit animals, Kerala regulates slaughter through licensing without a cow prohibition, and several northeastern states, including Meghalaya and Nagaland, have no prohibition at all. The variation, not any single rule, is the federation's settled pattern.

### Q: What is the post-2015 Maharashtra cow law?

The Maharashtra Animal Preservation (Amendment) Act, assented to by the President on 4 March 2015, extended the state's 1976 prohibition from cows to bulls and bullocks and widened the offence family around slaughter. Its section 5C criminalised possession of the flesh of an animal slaughtered in Maharashtra in contravention of the Act, section 5D extended the possession offence to animals slaughtered anywhere, and section 9B placed the burden of proof on the accused, with penalties of up to five years imprisonment under section 9A. The Bombay High Court struck down sections 5D and 9B in May 2016 and read the surviving possession offence down to conscious possession, leaving the slaughter prohibition itself fully intact.

### Q: What is cow vigilantism?

Cow vigilantism is the enforcement of cow protection norms by civilians rather than by authorised officers: groups styling themselves cow protectors who stop cattle vehicles on highways, inspect documents, seize animals, and in the gravest cases beat or kill the persons they accuse of slaughter or smuggling. The documented cases of the period include the killing of Mohammad Akhlaq at Dadri in September 2015, of Pehlu Khan near Alwar in April 2017 and of Rakbar Khan in the same district in July 2018. The Supreme Court held in Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India in July 2018 that no individual or group may take the law into private hands, and it imposed preventive, remedial and punitive duties on every state government.

### Q: What was the Pehlu Khan case?

Pehlu Khan was a fifty-five-year-old dairy farmer from Nuh district in Haryana who purchased milch cattle at a fair in Jaipur and was transporting them home with documents on 1 April 2017 when his party was stopped near Behror in Alwar district, Rajasthan, by men accusing them of cow smuggling. He was beaten with sticks and rods and died of his injuries on 3 April 2017, having named six men to the police before his death. The Rajasthan police closed the case against the six named men in September 2017 and proceeded against other accused identified from video; in August 2019 the Alwar district court acquitted the six who stood trial, and the state government announced an appeal.

### Q: How are state cow laws different?

State cow laws differ on every architectural dimension a statute can vary. They differ in the animals covered, some protecting only cows and others the whole cow family including bulls and bullocks of any age. They differ in the offences created, some stopping at slaughter and others extending to possession, sale, transport and export. They differ in penalties, from one or two years in the older statutes to ten years in Haryana and life imprisonment in Gujarat after 2017. They differ in proof, several placing the burden on the accused. And they differ in kind altogether at the map's other end, where West Bengal runs a certificate regime and Kerala and the northeast run none.

### Q: Why is cow protection politically charged?

Cow protection is politically charged because the cow sits at the crossing of religion, livelihood and constitutional design. For many Hindus the animal is sacred, and the Constituent Assembly recorded that sentiment in Article 48 while withholding enforceable status from it. Beef is at the same time an inexpensive food and a trade for Muslim and Dalit communities, so a prohibition distributes its costs along communal lines whatever its text says. The subject's assignment to the states makes every assembly election a possible occasion for a new severity, and the mass campaign of November 1966 showed the demand's mobilising power two generations before the post-2014 amendments gave it statutory form across most of northern and western India.

### Q: What did the Constituent Assembly decide about cow protection in November 1948?

On 24 November 1948 the Constituent Assembly debated the draft article that became Article 48 and decided the question's constitutional location for the next seventy years. Seth Govind Das and other members pressed for cow protection to be guaranteed as an enforceable fundamental right, speaking of the cow in openly religious terms. Pandit Thakur Dass Bhargava's amendment instead placed the prohibition on slaughter within the article on organising agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines, and the Assembly adopted that form, with Dr. B. R. Ambedkar accepting the amendment for the Drafting Committee. The decision made prohibition a Directive Principle: a standing instruction to legislatures, discharged state by state or not at all.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court hold in Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar in 1958?

In Mohd. Hanif Quareshi v. State of Bihar, reported at AIR 1958 SC 731, a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court tested the total cow slaughter prohibitions of Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh against the trade rights of butchers and cattle traders. The Court upheld a total prohibition on the slaughter of cows and calves, accepting Article 48 and the cow's place in the agricultural economy as a sufficient public purpose. It refused to extend the prohibition to bulls, bullocks and buffaloes that had ceased to be useful, holding that a total ban on those animals burdened the butchers' right to practise a trade under Article 19(1)(g) beyond what the public interest required. The usefulness line drawn in 1958 governed state drafting for nearly five decades.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court hold in the Mirzapur Moti Kureshi case in 2005?

In State of Gujarat v. Mirzapur Moti Kureshi Kassab Jamat, decided on 26 October 2005 by a seven-judge bench and reported at (2005) 8 SCC 534, the Supreme Court upheld Gujarat's prohibition on the slaughter of bulls and bullocks without an age limit, departing from the usefulness test of the Quareshi judgment. The majority reasoned that dung, urine and draught capacity gave the animals value across their lives, read Article 48 together with the fundamental duty of compassion in Article 51A(g), and accepted the legislature's conclusion that no member of the cow family was useless in the relevant sense. The judgment widened the constitutional space inside which the post-2014 legislatures later wrote their more severe statutes.

### Q: What penalty does Gujarat law prescribe for cow slaughter after the 2017 amendment?

The Gujarat Animal Preservation (Amendment) Act of 2017, passed on 31 March 2017, prescribes the most severe cow slaughter penalty in India. The slaughter of a cow, calf, bull or bullock carries imprisonment for a term of not less than ten years and extending to imprisonment for life, with a fine that may extend to five lakh rupees. The illegal transport of cattle for slaughter and the sale or storage of beef carry seven to ten years and fines between one and five lakh rupees. Vehicles used in the offences are liable to permanent confiscation, the offences are cognizable and non-bailable, and the transport of cattle during defined night hours is prohibited outright.

### Q: What did the Bombay High Court decide about the Maharashtra beef law in 2016?

On 6 May 2016 a division bench of Justices A. S. Oka and S. C. Gupte upheld Maharashtra's prohibition on the slaughter of bulls and bullocks while striking down the amendment's two furthest extensions. Section 5D, which criminalised possession of beef from animals slaughtered outside Maharashtra, was held to violate the right to privacy within Article 21, since consuming food not injurious to health forms part of an individual's autonomy. Section 9B, which placed the burden of proof on the accused, was struck down as failing the requirement of a fair, just and reasonable procedure. Section 5C survived in read-down form: only conscious possession, with knowledge proved by the prosecution, constitutes the offence.

### Q: What happened at Dadri in September 2015?

On the night of 28 September 2015, in Bisara village in the Dadri area of Uttar Pradesh, a crowd gathered after an announcement, attributed by the police investigation to the village temple's loudspeaker, that a cow had been slaughtered. The crowd entered the house of Mohammad Akhlaq, a fifty-year-old ironsmith, and beat him and his son Danish in the lane outside. Akhlaq died that night; Danish survived severe head injuries. Eighteen men were arrested initially, and the prosecution proceeded in the district court at Surajpur, where the trial remained pending for years. The identity of meat recovered from the vicinity was contested between an early veterinary description of mutton and a later forensic report, disputed by the defence, describing beef.

### Q: Who was Rakbar Khan and what happened in July 2018?

Rakbar Khan was a thirty-one-year-old dairy farmer from Kolgaon in Haryana who was walking two purchased cows home on foot on the night of 20 July 2018 when a crowd stopped and beat him at Lalawandi village in the Ramgarh area of Alwar district, Rajasthan, accusing him of cow smuggling. He died that night. The official record of the case documents a failure within the state response itself: the police party that reached the scene transported the seized cows to a shelter and took detained men to the station before taking the injured man to hospital, a delay of hours that the Rajasthan government acknowledged by suspending officers and ordering an inquiry. Cow protection activists from the area were among those chargesheeted.

### Q: What directions did the Supreme Court issue in Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India in 2018?

In Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India, decided on 17 July 2018 and reported at (2018) 9 SCC 501, the Supreme Court held that no individual or group may take the law into private hands and issued directions in three registers. Preventively, every state must designate a nodal police officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police in each district, identify areas where lynching has occurred and intensify patrolling there. Remedially, states must frame victim compensation schemes within one month, with interim relief inside thirty days, and lynching trials must run in designated fast-track courts, preferably within six months. Punitively, officials who fail to prevent or pursue such violence face disciplinary proceedings. The Court also recommended a distinct parliamentary offence of lynching.

### Q: What did the central livestock market rules of May 2017 propose and what happened to them?

On 23 May 2017 the Union environment ministry notified the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (Regulation of Livestock Markets) Rules under the central Act of 1960. Rule 22 prohibited the sale and purchase of cattle in animal markets for slaughter, required sellers to declare in writing that animals were not brought for slaughter, barred buyers from reselling for slaughter and created district animal market committees. Because most Indian cattle change hands in markets, the rules would have functioned as a near-national restriction achieved by delegated legislation on a state subject. The Madurai Bench of the Madras High Court stayed them on 30 May 2017, the Supreme Court extended the stay across India on 11 July 2017, and the operative restriction never came into force.

### Q: What is a fit-for-slaughter certificate under the West Bengal law?

The West Bengal Animal Slaughter Control Act of 1950 regulates rather than prohibits cattle slaughter, and the fit-for-slaughter certificate is its operative instrument. An animal may lawfully be slaughtered at a licensed place when the prescribed authority, a municipal or veterinary officer within the Act's scheme, certifies that the animal is over fourteen years of age or has become permanently unfit for work or breeding through injury, deformity or incurable disease. The certificate converts the question of slaughter from a communal absolute into an administrative finding about one animal's usefulness, the model the Supreme Court's Quareshi judgment constitutionalised in 1958. West Bengal retained the certificate model through the whole post-2014 period, declining to join the prohibiting states' amendments.

### Q: How did Haryana's 2015 Act change the law that applied in the state before it?

Haryana's Gauvansh Sanrakshan and Gausamvardhan Act of 2015 replaced, rather than amended, the inherited law: it repealed the Punjab Prohibition of Cow Slaughter Act of 1955 as applicable to Haryana, under which slaughter had carried up to five years imprisonment or a fine of five thousand rupees. The new Act raised the slaughter penalty to rigorous imprisonment of three to ten years with fines of thirty thousand to one lakh rupees, prohibited the sale of beef including canned beef, regulated the export of cows by permit, placed the burden of proof on the accused in defined cases, made the offences cognizable and non-bailable, provided for vehicle confiscation, and added shelter institutions and meat-testing laboratories to the statute's machinery.

### Q: What does the Uttar Pradesh Prevention of Cow Slaughter Act of 1955 prohibit and punish?

The Uttar Pradesh Prevention of Cow Slaughter Act of 1955 prohibits the slaughter of a cow, bull or bullock anywhere in the state, subject to narrow certified exceptions, and prohibits the sale of beef and the transport of cattle for slaughter in related provisions. As amended across the decades, the Act's penalty for slaughter reached imprisonment of up to seven years with fine, with attempt punishable at half that scale, in the form the statute stood in through the period this article covers. The Yogi Adityanath government after March 2017 administered the Act alongside machinery the text did not contain: closure drives against illegal slaughterhouses, a cow welfare cess funding district shelters, and publicly announced invocations of the National Security Act against accused smugglers.

### Q: How did the post-2014 laws affect cattle traders, farmers and the leather trade?

The widened statutes and the enforcement penumbra together raised the cost of every lawful cattle transaction in the severe states. Traders faced seizure of animals and vehicles on suspicion, with trials measured in years and confiscated cattle maintained at their owners' risk in the interval; many left the trade or demanded risk premiums that reached the farmer as lower prices. Farmers, unable to sell unproductive animals and forbidden to slaughter them, bore fodder costs until abandonment produced the stray cattle emergency that Uttar Pradesh answered with shelters and a cow welfare cess in December 2018. The leather and buffalo meat export trades, lawful in themselves, were shaken chiefly by the stayed market rules of May 2017 and by the climate of inspection rather than by the cow statutes directly.
