## The Argument That Never Gets Settled

Every discussion of the greatest strikers and how defenders coped with them ends in the same place, which is nowhere. One side says the forwards of earlier eras would be swallowed whole by modern athleticism, that they were slower, that the pitches were slower, that the defending was amateur. The other side says the forwards of today are protected by the laws to a degree their predecessors would find comical, that a modern number nine would be kicked into the stand inside twenty minutes of a match played under the old permissions, and that goal totals from earlier periods therefore understate what those men actually did. Both positions get repeated until everybody is tired, and nobody ever changes their mind, because the argument is conducted entirely with opinions about outcomes rather than with any attempt to isolate what changed.

That is the failure this article is built to correct. The question is answerable. It is answerable because the striker duel has been altered by a small number of identifiable changes, and each of those changes did something specific and traceable to the defender's job. Four of them matter. The backpass rule, which removed the goalkeeper's hands as a safety valve. The progressive outlawing of the tackle from behind, which converted the defender's primary tool from dispossession into delay. The move away from sweeper systems and dedicated man-marking toward coordinated zonal lines, which changed the striker's problem from beating one assigned opponent to beating an organized structure. And the material improvement in pitch surfaces and ball behavior, which altered first touch, shooting technique, and the reliability of aerial delivery.

Isolate those four and the era war becomes a set of separate questions with separate answers, some of which favor the old forwards and some of which favor the modern ones. Then, once the variables are stripped out, something more useful appears underneath: the part of the duel that has not changed at all. Separation, timing, first contact, and the geometry of the near post are the same problems for a centre-back today that they were for a centre-back marking a penalty-box specialist in the era of the sweeper. That is the reason a coach should care about any of this. Old defensive craft is not nostalgia. Large parts of it are still legal, still effective, and still coachable on Tuesday evening with fourteen players and half a pitch.

This is the hub article for a cluster of eight forwards, each of whom asked a different question of the defenders who faced him. Ronaldo Nazario, Thierry Henry, Didier Drogba, Samuel Eto'o, Andriy Shevchenko, Gabriel Batistuta, Marco van Basten, and Gerd Muller are treated here at the level required to compare them, with each routing down to his own full breakdown. The modern category, meaning the forwards playing now and the plans against them, belongs to the [complete guide to stopping elite strikers](/2020/01/01/stopping-elite-strikers-complete-guide/) and is not re-run here. This article owns the cross-era question and the framework for answering it.

A note on tone before the analysis starts. Nothing in what follows should be read as a claim that defenders of any period were inferior professionals. That framing is both lazy and wrong. Defenders solve the problem the laws and the systems of their time hand them, using the tools those laws permit, and they solved it well enough to have long careers against forwards who are still discussed generations later. When this article says that a defensive habit is obsolete, it means the conditions that made it correct no longer exist, not that the men who used it were doing something stupid. Anyone who watches old footage and concludes that the defending was primitive has usually failed to notice that the defender was doing something the laws allowed him to do and which no longer exists as an option.

## Why the Cross-Era Argument Fails as Normally Conducted

The standard version of this debate makes three errors, and they compound.

The first is comparing raw output. Goal totals across eras are not a common currency. They sit on top of different numbers of fixtures, different competition structures, different squad sizes, different substitution allowances, different injury management, different levels of tactical specialization in the opposition, and different conventions about who takes penalties. A forward who played in a two-man attack in a league where nearly every side used a sweeper and a dedicated marker was solving a different problem from a forward operating alone against a compact zonal block with a screening midfielder in front of it. Neither problem is harder in some absolute sense. They are different problems, and the number at the end of a career says almost nothing about which was harder because the number is generated by different machinery.

The second error is treating rule changes as neutral. People acknowledge that the laws changed and then proceed to compare as though they had not, or as though the changes affected both sides equally. They did not. Every one of the four changes examined here moved value from one side of the duel to the other, and in several cases the direction is not the one people assume. The backpass rule is usually filed as a change that made the sport faster and more entertaining. Its more interesting effect was on what pressing a forward could achieve, and therefore on where a forward's defensive work became valuable.

The third error is treating athleticism as the only axis. Modern professional football is faster and the athletes are better prepared, and this is not seriously disputed. But the duel between a centre-back and a centre-forward is not decided by top speed. It is decided by who wins half a yard at a specific moment, which is a function of body position, timing, deception, and the geometry of the space available. Athleticism moves the baseline for both men simultaneously. If everyone is quicker, the duel is quicker, and the half-yard is still the half-yard. This is the central claim of this article and it deserves a name.

**The constant duel.** Strip out the rules, the surfaces, and the systems, and what remains in every era is one contest over half a yard of separation at the moment the ball is struck. Everything else is context. That is why old defensive craft is still worth teaching even when the systems around it are obsolete, and it is why a coach can watch a forward from an era he never saw live and take something usable from it.

The method that follows is simple. Take each of the four variables in turn. State what it removed from the defender, what it gave the forward, which forward archetype it favored, and which defensive skill it made more or less valuable. Then set the variables aside and describe what is left.
## Variable One: The Backpass Rule and the Loss of the Safety Valve

### Why did the backpass rule make a pressing forward dangerous?

It removed the goalkeeper's hands as an escape from pressure, which meant a defense under pressure had to solve the problem with its feet. Pressing a forward stopped being a gesture and started producing turnovers in dangerous areas, so a forward's willingness to press became a genuine attacking weapon rather than a courtesy.

Before the change, the pressing sequence had a guaranteed terminator. A defender in trouble played the ball back, the goalkeeper picked it up, and the pressure ended. Not reduced, ended. The forward could work for eight seconds, force the ball backwards, and receive nothing for it. This shaped the striker's job description in a way that is easy to miss. If pressing cannot produce a turnover, then the energy spent pressing is energy removed from the phase where the forward actually generates value, which is the attack. A forward who conserved himself and stayed high was making a rational allocation, not being lazy, and the sport's habit of describing forwards of earlier eras as reluctant defenders usually ignores that the defensive work available to them was largely unrewarded.

Once the goalkeeper could no longer use his hands on a deliberate pass from a teammate, the terminator disappeared. Now the same eight seconds of pressure ended with a goalkeeper attempting to play with his feet under duress, and every one of those situations is a potential turnover in the most valuable area of the pitch. The value of a pressing forward went up immediately and permanently.

The knock-on effects for the defender are larger than they look. The first is that the centre-backs' own technical requirements changed. A defender who could not receive under pressure and play forward became a liability in a way he simply had not been before, because the option that used to bail him out no longer existed. This is one of the clearest cases in the sport of a law reshaping the personnel a position requires. The second effect is that the goalkeeper became the last defender in a positional sense as well as a nominal one, which changed the depth at which a defensive line could safely sit and therefore changed the space a forward could attack behind it.

The third effect runs through the entire counter-plan literature that this series is built on. Because pressing now produces turnovers, defending teams have to consider what their build-up looks like against a forward who presses well, and attacking teams have to consider whether their forward's pressing is worth the positional cost. The forward who both scores and presses became a different category of problem for an opponent, and several of the forwards in this cluster were early and severe versions of exactly that problem.

There is a counter-reading worth stating fairly. Some argue the change mostly affected goalkeepers and build-up play and had only a marginal effect on the striker duel itself. The response is that the duel is not just the moment of contact. It includes where the ball comes from and under what pressure, and a rule that changes how often the ball is turned over thirty metres from goal changes how often the duel happens in a dangerous state. A forward's threat is partly a function of the quality of the situations he receives in, and this law improved those situations for any forward willing to work.

### Which forward archetype did the backpass rule favor?

It favored the mobile, aggressive forward who could press a centre-back and a goalkeeper and then finish the turnover he created. It slightly disfavored the pure penalty-box specialist, whose value sat entirely in the finishing act and who now shared a pitch with forwards generating chances through defensive work.

The word "slightly" is doing real work in that answer, and the reason is developed later in this article. A penalty-box specialist did not become obsolete. He became a specialist in a sport that had found an additional way for a forward to be useful, which changes the selection calculation without eliminating the profile. That distinction matters, and the [poacher against complete forward decision](/2021/03/15/poacher-vs-complete-forward/) is where the series resolves it properly.

## Variable Two: The Tackle From Behind and the Conversion of the Defender's Primary Tool

The single largest change to the striker duel is the progressive removal of the tackle from behind and the broader reduction in tolerated contact. It came after the backpass change and it went considerably deeper into the mechanics of defending.

Understand first what the old permission actually gave a defender, because it is routinely mis-described as merely permission to be violent. It was not. It was a tool that removed the cost of being beaten. A defender who had been comprehensively beaten by a forward's first touch still had a legal action available to him from a position behind the forward, and that action could end the attack. The consequence is structural rather than moral: when a beaten defender retains a way to end the attack, positional excellence is less valuable, because the penalty for a positional error is smaller.

That is the mechanism, and it explains a great deal of what modern coaches find confusing when they watch old footage. A defender in that era often appears to engage far too aggressively, to commit early, to accept being turned in situations where a modern coach would scream at him to delay and wait for support. He was not being reckless by the standards of the sport he was playing. He was making a correct risk calculation under a different law. His downside was capped.

Remove the tool and the calculation inverts. A defender who is beaten is now genuinely beaten, and the only remaining resources are recovery running, the covering defender, and the block behind him. So the defender's primary tool converted from dispossession into delay. Delay is a completely different craft. It requires an approach angle that shows the forward into the least dangerous space, a body position that keeps the covering defender in the picture, a deceleration into the duel rather than through it, and the patience to stay on the front foot without committing. Almost the entire modern one against one defending curriculum is a response to the loss of that tool.

### Why did shielding the ball become a primary forward skill?

Because the removal of the tackle from behind made it safe. A forward with his body between the defender and the ball can no longer be legally dispossessed from behind, so holding became a viable primary skill rather than a dangerous one, which raised the value of upper body strength and receiving facing away from goal.

This is the tool-substitution effect that the series develops in more detail in the wide duel, and it runs in a consistent direction. When a defensive tool is removed by the laws, the attacking quality that tool suppressed reappears within a generation. Shielding was always technically possible. It was suppressed because the cost of standing still with your back to a defender was unacceptably high. Once the cost fell, forwards developed the quality, and coaches began teaching it deliberately.

The reverse effect is the one most people miss. The removal of the tool did not only help forwards. It forced defending to become better. A defender who cannot rely on a recovery tackle from behind must be positionally excellent before the duel begins, must win the ground the forward wants before the forward gets there, and must be supported by a structure that makes his individual error survivable. The defending in the modern professional game is, on any reasonable technical assessment, more sophisticated than it was under the old permissions, and it is more sophisticated precisely because it had to be. That is the honest answer to the claim that defenders were better before. They were operating with a larger toolkit, which is not the same thing.

There is a further point that has to be made plainly, because the topic invites bad writing. Deliberate attempts to injure an opponent were indefensible under any set of laws and they remain so. Nothing in this analysis treats them as a legitimate tactic or as a charming feature of a lost era. The analysis here concerns legal contact under the standards of the time and the tactical consequences of its permitted range, which is a different subject from foul play and should not be confused with it.

The disciplinary economics are also worth stating, because they are usually ignored by people who claim that forwards of earlier eras were simply kicked out of matches. Fouling is a purchase, not a gift. A defender who fouls to stop an attack has conceded a set piece, has moved himself closer to a caution, and has told the forward exactly what his plan is. Against a forward who could be fouled repeatedly without consequence, the tactic worked. Against a forward whose side punished set pieces, or who could absorb contact and retain the ball, the tactic was expensive. The forwards in this cluster who thrived under permissive contact were, almost without exception, forwards who made fouling expensive.
## Variable Three: From the Marker and the Sweeper to the Coordinated Line

The third variable changed what a forward was actually trying to beat. Under a dedicated man-marking system with a sweeper behind, a forward's problem was a person. Under a zonal line, his problem is a structure.

Reconstructing the older assignment properly is worth doing, because almost nobody who coaches now has seen one delivered as a job description rather than as a phrase. The marker had a specified starting distance from his forward, typically close enough to contest a first touch and no further, and a goal-side reference he was not supposed to lose. He had an instruction on whether to follow his man across the width of the pitch or to hand him off at an agreed boundary, and that instruction varied by side and by opponent. Behind him sat the sweeper, playing at a covering depth chosen so that a forward who beat the marker met a second defender before he met the goalkeeper. In front of him, the midfield had an obligation to screen the entry pass so that the marker was not asked to defend a pass and a man simultaneously. When the system worked, it worked because all three parts were present. When it failed, it usually failed at the third part, because a marker without a screen is isolated by definition.

What the system offered was clarity. Every defender knew exactly what his job was and could be held accountable for it, which is not a small advantage and is the reason the approach survives in modified form at set pieces and in specific match plans. What it cost was flexibility. A marker who followed his man was, in effect, being positioned by the opponent, which meant a forward who moved intelligently could drag a centre-back out of the defensive structure without touching the ball. That vulnerability is the seed of everything that replaced it.

The zonal line answers the problem differently. Defenders own space and the passes into it rather than owning a person, which means the structure holds its shape regardless of where the forward goes, and the forward's movement no longer disorganizes anything by itself. The cost is that responsibility becomes collective, so a forward who receives in a seam between two zones is momentarily the responsibility of nobody, and the answer has to be a rehearsed hand-off rather than an instinctive one.

### What did a sweeper actually do behind a marker?

He played at a covering depth chosen so that a forward who beat the marker met a second defender before he met the goalkeeper. That permission to be beaten is what allowed a marker to engage aggressively, and it is the whole reason the marking system functioned at all.

The trade was numerical. Committing two defenders to one forward left the rest of the defense thin, and a forward with intelligent movement could occupy both of them and create space for everybody else without touching the ball.

The system's fate was decided less by any single forward than by the arrival of coordinated pressing and higher defensive lines, which made a spare defender sitting deep an expensive luxury. A team defending with a sweeper cannot compress the pitch vertically in the way a flat line can, and a team that cannot compress vertically gives the opposition midfield room to play. The sweeper role and its evolution are owned by the article on [the libero and how the role actually worked](/2029/04/01/beckenbauer-libero-explained/), and the Italian defensive tradition that refined both the marking system and its zonal successor is treated in [the lessons from Italian defending](/2029/02/01/italian-defending-lessons/). This article links rather than expands, because both subjects deserve their own treatment.

What matters for the cross-era question is the change in the forward's task. Beating a person is a technical and physical problem, solvable with a first touch, a change of direction, a shoulder drop, or simply being stronger. Beating a structure is a timing and positioning problem, solvable by receiving where two defenders disagree about who is responsible. A forward optimized for the first task is not automatically good at the second, and this is the single strongest argument the modern side has in the era war. Some earlier forwards built their game around beating an assigned opponent, and that specific skill has a reduced application against a line that will not follow them.

The argument runs the other way too, and it should be conceded honestly. A forward optimized for beating structures is not automatically good at beating a person. Modern forwards are rarely asked to hold off a dedicated marker for ninety minutes with legal contact permitted at a level they have never experienced, and it is not obvious that all of them would enjoy it. The correct verdict is that both groups would need to adapt, and the more useful question is which group's core qualities transfer more completely, which is addressed later.

## Variable Four: Pitch Surfaces and Ball Behavior

The fourth variable is the one most often waved away as trivial, and it is not trivial at all. It changed the technical requirements of both jobs.

Start with the surface. A heavy, rutted, waterlogged pitch does three things to a forward. It slows acceleration, which compresses the separation a quick forward can generate. It makes the first touch unreliable, which means a forward cannot plan a two-touch sequence with confidence and must play with a larger margin for error. And it rewards physical strength and low body position over fine technique, because keeping your feet is a precondition for everything else. Watch old footage without accounting for this and you will conclude that the technique was worse. Some of it was. A great deal of it was technique correctly adapted to a surface that punished ambition.

For the defender the same surface offers a matching set of gifts. Sliding recovery challenges are more effective on a soft surface. A forward's touch running slightly long is common enough to be a plannable event rather than an accident. And a forward who has been knocked off balance takes longer to recover his base, which extends the value of legal contact well beyond the moment of contact itself.

Now the ball. Older balls absorbed water and became heavier through a match, which affected long delivery and shooting more than short passing. A cross into the penalty area behaved differently late in a wet match, which made aerial delivery less reliable and rewarded forwards who attacked the ball early and decisively rather than waiting for it to arrive. Shooting from distance was a genuinely different action with a genuinely different risk profile.

### Why does the playing surface change how you read old footage?

Because technique adapts to conditions. A forward taking a heavy touch on a rutted pitch and a defender declining to play out from the back are both making correct decisions for the surface in front of them. Judge either against modern grass and you are marking down a sensible adaptation.

This variable is the reason the athletic comparison is harder than it looks. A forward whose game was built on strength and timing rather than acceleration was not necessarily slower than a modern forward. He was optimized for a surface where acceleration paid less. Judge his technique by modern standards, on modern grass, and you are marking him down for adapting correctly to conditions he did not choose. The honest analytical position is that we cannot cleanly observe how a forward from a heavy-pitch era would move on a modern surface, and anybody who claims certainty in either direction is guessing.

There is a specific and checkable consequence for coaches who study old footage, which is worth stating because it is the practical payoff of this whole section. When you watch a defender from an earlier era, separate what he is doing from why he is doing it. A defender who stands off a forward more than a modern coach would like may be respecting a surface on which committing was a genuine risk. A defender who dives into a challenge may be exercising a legal option that no longer exists. A defender who follows his forward across the pitch may be executing an instruction rather than losing his shape. Import the action without importing the condition and you will teach a young defender something that is either illegal or wrong.
## Three Changes That Mattered Less Than People Claim

Isolating variables is only half the method. The other half is refusing to credit changes that did not do what people think they did, because a framework that accepts every plausible factor explains nothing.

### Squad depth, substitutions, and fixture density

The argument runs that modern forwards are protected by rotation and by the ability to be replaced when tired, while forwards of earlier eras played through everything, and that this makes the modern job easier. The premise is partly true and the conclusion does not follow.

What larger squads and more substitutions actually changed is the intensity at which a match can be played, and they raised it for both sides simultaneously. A forward who knows he may be replaced can empty himself; so can the defender marking him. The duel is contested at a higher intensity throughout, which is a change in the conditions of the contest rather than an advantage handed to either party. If anything, the ability to introduce a fresh defender against a tiring forward is a defensive tool that earlier defenses did not have, which cuts against the claim.

Fixture density is a genuine difference but it belongs to a discussion about careers rather than about duels. It affects availability, preparation, and how a season is managed. It does not change what happens when a forward and a centre-back contest a cross.

### The offside interpretation

This one gets over-credited in both directions. The interpretation of the law has moved, and the practical effect is that a forward's timing now operates within a narrower tolerance and is judged more precisely.

The reason it changes less than people assume is that it acts on both parties in the same plane. A forward gains from a permissive interpretation and loses from a strict one; a defensive line gains from a strict interpretation and loses from a permissive one. The line step and the timed run are the same two skills regardless, and the tolerance around them shifts the risk without changing the technique. A defender still steps on a trigger. A forward still delays and accelerates.

Where it does something real is at the margin of tactical viability. A stricter, more precisely enforced interpretation makes a high line a more attractive proposition, because the offside trap becomes a reliable mechanism rather than a gamble on an official's angle of view. That is a genuine effect and it belongs in the account, but it is a second-order consequence of the third variable rather than a variable in its own right.

### Professionalized preparation

Nutrition, conditioning, recovery, and sports science have transformed the athlete. They have not transformed the duel, and the reason is the one this article keeps returning to: they applied to everybody at once.

The place where preparation genuinely changed the striker duel is in the length of a forward's peak and the consistency of his output across a long season, which is a durability question rather than a duel question. It is also the area where the temptation to over-claim is strongest, because it is easy to look at an earlier era and assume that any physical difference is a preparation difference. Some of it is. A good deal of it is body-type selection, because a sport that rewards different qualities recruits different bodies, and a forward built to survive a heavy pitch and permitted contact is not the same physical specimen as a forward built to sprint repeatedly on a fast surface.

That last point is the one to hold onto. The bodies changed partly because the sport changed, not only because the preparation improved. Treating every physical difference across eras as evidence of better training is an error of the same family as treating every tactical difference as evidence of better coaching.
## The Era-Variable Table

This is the article's findable artifact and the thing to save if you save nothing else. Each of the four changes is crossed with what it took away from the defender, what it gave the forward, the forward archetype it favored, and the defensive skill it made more or less valuable.

Read the table as a whole and a pattern appears. Three of the four changes moved value toward the forward and one of them, the shift to zonal defending, moved value toward the collective defensive structure while moving it away from the individual defender. The net effect is not that defending got easier or harder in some absolute sense. It is that defending stopped being a job you could do well on your own and became a job you can only do well inside an organized unit. That is the most important single sentence a coach can take from the cross-era question.

## The Constant Duel: What No Rule Change Has Touched

Set the four variables aside. What is left is the constant duel, and it has four components. Every one of them is as true for a centre-back playing today as it was for a marker in the era of the sweeper, and every one of them is coachable this week.

### Separation

The forward's entire job, reduced to its irreducible core, is to be unmarked at the moment the ball arrives. Not unmarked in general. Unmarked at that moment, in that place. Half a yard is enough. Half a yard is what separates a header into the net from a header into the goalkeeper's hands, and a first-time finish from a touch that lets a defender get a leg across.

The methods for creating half a yard have not changed and cannot change, because they are functions of human perception rather than of the laws. A forward creates separation by moving in one direction to make the defender move his weight, then moving in the other. He creates it by starting his run from a position where the defender cannot see him and the ball simultaneously. He creates it by attacking the front of the defender's body so the defender must turn, and turning is slow. He creates it by arriving late into a space the defender has already stopped watching. Every forward in this cluster used some combination of these four, and so does every forward playing now.

The defender's counters are equally durable. He denies the blind side by adjusting his body angle so that both the ball and the forward are in his field of view, which is the single most valuable habit in the position and the one most under-coached at amateur level. He refuses to commit his weight to the first movement, because the first movement is usually the lie. He wins the ground rather than the ball, arriving at the space the forward wants before the forward gets there. And he uses legal contact early and continuously to disrupt the forward's timing rather than saving it for the moment of the pass, when it becomes a foul.

### Timing

Separation is worthless if it arrives at the wrong moment. A forward who is unmarked a second early is offside or has been tracked; a forward who is unmarked a second late has missed the delivery. The forward's timing problem is to synchronize his own movement with the moment his supplier can actually play the pass, which means he is watching the supplier's body, not the ball.

This is the most transferable thing in the whole article for a young forward, because it costs nothing physically. The cue is the supplier's hips and his head position. When the passer's body opens toward the target area, the run starts. Start earlier and you are running before the pass is available; start later and the window has closed. The forwards who scored heavily across long careers were almost all exceptional at this, and it is not an athletic quality.

The defender's counter is to watch the same cue. A defender who reads the passer rather than the forward gets a head start on the run, and a head start is the only reliable answer to a forward with better acceleration. This is why the best line defending looks like anticipation and is actually just earlier information.

### First contact

The moment the ball arrives is a physical contest with rules about where the ball goes next, and the winner is usually decided before the ball gets there. First contact is who gets their body into the contested space first and on what terms.

For the forward, the components are the pre-contact step that establishes his position, the body angle that puts his frame between the defender and the ball, and the strength to hold that position for the fraction of a second the touch requires. For the defender, the components are the front-foot position that denies the forward the space to set his body, the arm and shoulder contact that is legal and disruptive, and the decision about whether to attack the ball or attack the reception. That decision is the whole of defending compressed into one choice, and it has never had a general answer. It depends on the covering support behind, the area of the pitch, and whether the forward is stronger than you.

### The geometry of the near post

The fourth constant is the least discussed and the most reliably profitable. The near post is where a delivery arrives first, where the goalkeeper has least time, and where a defender who is goal-side but ball-watching is most easily beaten. A forward who attacks the near post at pace converts the defender's positional advantage into nothing, because the defender cannot see him coming and cannot turn quickly enough to contest.

This geometry is fixed by the dimensions of the goal and the penalty area, which have not changed. It is the reason near-post movement appears in the game of a penalty-box specialist from one era and a mobile forward from another and looks identical. It is also the reason near-post defending is worth a disproportionate amount of a coach's session time. If your defenders learn one thing about defending crosses, it should be that the first defender attacks the near post as though the ball is coming there regardless of whether it is.

Those four components are the constant duel. ## How the Supply Changed, and Why That Is Half the Duel

A forward's threat is not generated only by the forward. It is generated by what he receives, from where, and in what state, and the supply has changed more than the finishing has. Any honest cross-era account has to include it, because a large share of what looks like a difference between forwards is a difference between the passes they were given.

Start with the delivery from wide areas. In earlier eras the standard wide product was a cross delivered from close to the byline or from deeper, aimed at a contested aerial zone in front of goal. The forward's job in that arrangement is to win a contested ball, which is a duel of timing, strength, and courage, and it explains why the aerial forward was such a dominant archetype for so long. Defending it is a matter of holding a position, attacking the flight of the ball, and surviving contact.

Modern wide production is weighted differently. The cutback from the byline, played backwards to a runner arriving at the penalty spot, is now a primary weapon rather than an occasional one, and it asks a completely different question of a defense. There is no aerial contest, there is no contested ball, and the defender's positional reference is wrong, because a defender who is goal-side of a forward attacking the six-yard area is not goal-side of a runner arriving behind him. The rise of that single delivery type is one of the most consequential tactical changes in the modern penalty area, and it has nothing to do with any law.

Then consider the entry pass into a forward between the lines. The volume of that pass has risen enormously, and so has its accuracy, because the technical standard of midfielders and defenders in possession has risen and because the surfaces reward a firmly played ground pass in a way that older surfaces did not. A forward who drops between the lines in the modern game is fed. The same movement in an earlier era was frequently made in vain, and forwards learned not to repeat unrewarded movements.

That last observation matters for reading old footage and it is often missed entirely. When a forward from an earlier period appears to make fewer intelligent movements between the lines, the explanation may be that the movement was not being found. Movement is a habit shaped by reinforcement. A forward whose drop into the pocket was rewarded once in ten attempts stopped doing it, and a modern forward whose drop is rewarded five times in ten does it constantly. The difference sits in the supply, not in the intelligence.

The through ball behind the line has changed less than either of the other two, which is why the diagonal runner is the archetype that transfers most cleanly across eras. A pass played into space behind a defense is roughly the same action on any surface, and defending it is roughly the same problem.

For a defense, the practical consequence of all this is that the plan against a great forward is increasingly a plan against his supply. That is a modern conclusion and it is worth being explicit about it, because the old plan against a great forward was overwhelmingly a plan about the forward himself. The shift from marking the man to defending the pass is the through-line of everything in this article, and it is the honest answer to what defending has actually learned.

## The Goalkeeper Inside the Striker Duel

Goalkeeping deserves its own paragraph in the era account because two of the four variables act on it directly, and because the goalkeeper's position changes the space the forward is attacking.

The backpass rule made a goalkeeper a footballer with obligations rather than a specialist with a safety net. That is well understood. Less well understood is the second-order effect on the striker duel, which runs through the defensive line's depth. A goalkeeper who can play as a covering defender behind a high line lets that line sit higher, which compresses the pitch, which reduces the space a forward has between the lines while increasing the space behind. The forward's problem is therefore reshaped rather than made harder or easier, and it is reshaped by a change in the goalkeeper's job description.

The surface and ball changes act on the goalkeeper too. A predictable ball flight makes a goalkeeper's decision to leave his line calculable, which extends his effective range on crosses and through balls. An unpredictable flight in wet conditions makes the same decision a gamble, which is why goalkeepers of earlier eras were often coached to stay and why forwards attacking a contested aerial ball had a better return on aggression than the same action offers now.

What has not changed is the geometry the goalkeeper defends. The angle he closes, the near post he protects, and the ground he must cover on a shot struck across him are functions of the dimensions of the goal, and they are the same in every period. This is the constant duel in its goalkeeping form, and it is the reason the finishing techniques that worked against a goalkeeper generations ago still work now.
## The Dimension That Actually Separates These Forwards

Ranking eight forwards is a parlour exercise. Separating them on the dimension that matters is analysis, and the dimension that matters is which part of the defensive sequence each one attacked.

Defending a forward is a sequence with five stages, and it runs in this order. The supply, meaning the pass that reaches him. The reception, meaning the moment the ball arrives and his body position when it does. The duel, meaning the physical and technical contest immediately after. The structure, meaning the collective shape that has to absorb him once the duel is over. And the finish, meaning the strike itself and the goalkeeper's problem. A defense has an answer at every stage, and every forward in history has been most dangerous at one or two of them.

Sort the cluster this way and the comparison becomes usable. Some of these forwards attacked the reception, arriving in positions where the defender's body was wrong before the ball got there. Some attacked the duel directly, taking the ball into contact and inviting the defender to try, which is a bet that the forward wins the contest and a bet most of them won. Some attacked the structure, positioning themselves at the joins where two defenders each thought the other was responsible. Some attacked the finish, generating a shot from body positions and distances that the defensive plan had written off as safe. And a small number attacked more than one stage well enough that no single answer covered them, which is the definition of the hardest problem a defense can face.

This is the correct frame for comparison because it maps directly onto what a coach does. You cannot prepare against greatness. You can prepare against a stage. If a forward's danger sits at the reception, your work is in the screening and the body angle before the ball is played. If it sits in the duel, your work is in the second defender's arrival and the direction he arrives from. If it sits in the structure, your work is in the hand-off boundary and the communication that executes it. If it sits in the finish, your work is in delivery denial and in the goalkeeper's starting position, because nothing you do in the box will reliably cover a shot you cannot predict.

The frame also explains why the era variables hit different forwards differently, which is the connection back to the four changes. A forward whose danger sat in the duel was the biggest beneficiary of the removal of the tackle from behind, because the duel is exactly where that law operated. A forward whose danger sat in the structure gained most from the shift to zonal lines, because zonal lines are the thing that has joins. A forward whose danger sat in the reception gained most from the improvement in supply quality, because his movement only pays when somebody finds it. A forward whose danger sat in the finish is the one least affected by any of the four changes, which is why the penalty-box specialist is simultaneously the archetype people assume is obsolete and the archetype whose core quality transfers most completely.

That last point deserves emphasis because it is counter-intuitive and it is the most important comparative finding in this article. The forward type that modern football selects for least is the forward type whose skills depend least on the conditions of any particular era. The decline of the specialist is a selection decision driven by what else a side needs from an attacking position, not a verdict on whether the skill still works. It still works. It is simply no longer sufficient on its own.

One more thing this frame does, and it matters for the audience of coaches who read this series for preparation rather than for argument. It gives you a question to ask about any forward you are about to face, whether or not he is famous. Watch him for one match and ask which of the five stages he is attacking. Most forwards below the elite level attack exactly one, and identifying it is worth more than a week of general defensive work.
## The Greatest Strikers and the Question Each One Asked a Defender

The cluster's eight subjects are the worked examples, and they are chosen because they span the archetype range rather than because they can be ranked. Each is treated here at the depth needed to compare him with the others, and each routes down to the article that owns his duel in full. The comparison that follows is about the problem each man set a defender, not about who was better.

### Ronaldo Nazario

The question he asked was the one defenders have the fewest good answers to, which is what do you do when both of your options lose.

A defender facing a forward has two basic settings. Engage him tight, deny the turn, accept that he might spin you. Or drop off, protect the space behind, accept that he receives facing you with the ball under control. Almost every forward is beaten by one of these settings, which is why opposition meetings exist. This forward, across his time at Cruzeiro, PSV, Barcelona, Internazionale, Real Madrid, Milan, and Corinthians, was elite at beating both. Tight, he could turn a defender in a phone box with a change of direction from a standing start. Dropped off, he could receive facing you and beat you over forty metres. There is no third setting.

The consequence for a defense is that the duel itself was not the place to solve the problem, and defenses that treated it as the place to solve the problem lost. The only structural answer that ever worked with any consistency was to deny the reception rather than to contest it, which means screening the entry pass, defending the supplier rather than the receiver, and accepting that when he did get the ball facing forward with space, the situation was already lost and the correct response was to delay and funnel rather than to engage. That answer, and the reasons a defense usually cannot sustain it, belong to [the full breakdown of how defenders stopped Ronaldo Nazario](/2020/11/15/stopping-ronaldo-nazario/).

For the cross-era question he is the most useful single data point in the cluster, because his career straddled the transition. He played under the older permissions and under the newer ones, at the point where the tackle from behind was being progressively removed, and his game was one of the reasons the removal mattered so much. A forward who could be legally hit from behind while running at speed is a solvable problem. The same forward, once that option disappears, is not.

Two World Cup wins with Brazil sit in a career that had clearly distinct phases in terms of role and physical profile, and this is where the article stops, because the reasons for those phases are not football analysis and are not this series' business.

### Thierry Henry

The question he asked was a positional one, and it is the question that changed how defenses think about the wide areas.

Across Monaco, Juventus, Arsenal, Barcelona, and New York Red Bulls, his defining structural contribution was the forward who starts from the left touchline and finishes in the center. That sounds unremarkable now because a generation of forwards has been developed to do it. It was not unremarkable when he was doing it, and the reason it worked is worth stating precisely. A centre-back's positional reference is the goal and the ball. A full-back's reference is his winger and the touchline. A forward who begins in the full-back's territory and arrives in the centre-back's territory during the same possession is, for a moment, nobody's clear responsibility, and the moment is exactly long enough.

The defensive answer required something defenses of that period were not organized to provide, which was a rehearsed and verbal hand-off between the full-back and the centre-back, executed at speed, with the covering midfielder aware of it. Where sides got that right, the threat was containable. Where they did not, the forward received in a diagonal channel with the defense already turning, which is the worst possible starting position for a defender.

He is also the cluster's clearest illustration of the third variable. The move to zonal lines is usually described as a change that helped defenses, and against most forward types it did. Against a forward who deliberately operates in the boundary between two zones, it created a new vulnerability that man-marking did not have, because a marker follows and a zone does not. That is the specific reason a forward of this profile is a modern problem rather than an old one, and the mechanics of defending it live in [the breakdown of how defenders stopped Thierry Henry](/2020/12/01/stopping-thierry-henry/).

### Didier Drogba

The question he asked was the oldest one in the sport, asked at a standard almost nobody has matched, which is what do you do about a forward who can hold the ball with his back to you and you cannot get it off him.

The centre-forward across Marseille, Chelsea, Shanghai, Galatasaray, and Montreal built his game on receiving with his back to goal, absorbing contact, and either turning or laying the ball off and attacking the space he had just created. Every part of that sequence is legal, and every part of it became more valuable once the tackle from behind was removed. He is the cluster's purest demonstration of the tool-substitution effect: a forward whose primary skill was one the older laws would have made extremely expensive to use.

The defensive problem he set has two layers, and defenses that solved only one of them still lost. The first layer is the physical duel itself, which most defenders lose and should expect to lose. The second layer is what happens after. A forward who holds the ball successfully is not just retaining possession; he is pinning a centre-back with his back to his own goal while his teammates run past him. The dangerous action is usually not the hold. It is the lay-off and the third-man run behind the pinned defender.

The practical consequence, and it is the transferable lesson, is that the answer to a holding forward is almost never a better individual defender. It is a second defender arriving from a direction the forward cannot see, plus a midfielder who defends the lay-off rather than the man. The full treatment sits in [the breakdown of how defenders stopped Didier Drogba](/2020/12/15/stopping-didier-drogba/).

### Samuel Eto'o

The question he asked was about defensive workload, and it is the question the backpass rule created.

Across Real Madrid, Mallorca, Barcelona, Internazionale, Anzhi, Chelsea, and Everton, he was a mobile forward whose value was distributed across phases in a way that older forward profiles simply were not. He scored, he ran the channels, he pressed, and in some periods of his career he did a genuinely substantial amount of defensive work in wide areas. That last part is the interesting part for this article, because it is a direct product of the first variable. In an era where pressing terminated in a goalkeeper's hands, a forward doing that volume of work would have been wasting himself. In an era where pressing produces turnovers, the same work is a weapon.

For a defense, the difficulty is that this profile does not present a single duel to prepare for. The centre-back who prepares for a penalty-box forward finds him in the channel. The full-back who prepares for a wide runner finds him attacking the near post. The build-up that expects to be pressed by a midfielder finds itself pressed by a forward at an angle that cuts the pass it wanted to make. Preparing against him is a squad-level problem rather than an individual assignment, which is exactly the kind of problem that man-marking systems handled badly and which zonal systems were built for.

He also complicates the era argument in a useful direction. The claim that modern forwards are protected and would not survive earlier defending has to reckon with the fact that modern forwards carry defensive obligations their predecessors did not, in matches played at higher repeated-sprint volume. Whether that is harder is genuinely arguable. That it is different is not. The duel-level detail belongs to [the breakdown of how defenders stopped Samuel Eto'o](/2021/01/01/stopping-samuel-etoo/).
### Andriy Shevchenko

The question he asked was about the diagonal, and it is the question a flat defensive line is least equipped to answer.

The forward across Dynamo Kyiv, Milan, and Chelsea ran at angles rather than straight lines, and he was comfortable enough on both feet that the direction of the diagonal carried no information. That combination is more awkward than it sounds. A defensive line facing a forward who runs straight can defend him with a coordinated step and a shared trigger, because everybody is moving in the same plane. A forward who runs across the line from outside to inside, or from inside to outside, forces every defender in the line to make a different decision at the same moment, and forces at least two of them to communicate about a hand-off while retreating at speed.

The two-footedness compounds it because it removes the standard forcing instruction. Almost every defensive coaching point against a forward reduces to a forcing decision: show him onto his weaker side, make him take the touch he does not want. A forward with no meaningful weaker foot removes the instruction and leaves the defender with only the general options of denying the reception and delaying, which are harder and less specific.

He is also the cluster's best example of a forward whose profile transfers cleanly across eras, because nothing in his game depended on a law that has since changed. Diagonal running against a line, two-footed finishing, and attacking the space behind a defender who has turned are all as effective now as they ever were. The detailed answer, including the line-height question and which defender profile handles the diagonal best, is in [the breakdown of how defenders stopped Andriy Shevchenko](/2021/01/15/stopping-andriy-shevchenko/).

### Gabriel Batistuta

The question he asked was about distance, and it is the question that makes a low block uncomfortable.

The forward across River Plate, Boca Juniors, Fiorentina, Roma, and Inter combined a powerful and quickly generated shot with the willingness to strike from areas most forwards treat as low value. That combination attacks a specific and rarely examined assumption in defensive organization. A compact block is built on the premise that shots from distance and from wide angles are acceptable concessions, because they usually are. Against a forward whose strike from those areas is a genuine threat, the premise fails, and the block has to choose between staying compact and conceding the shot, or stepping out to close the shot and conceding the space behind.

That is a real dilemma rather than a rhetorical one, and it has no clean answer. The best available response is to make the shot happen on terms you choose, which means allowing it from the areas and body positions where his conversion is lowest and denying the ones where the mechanics are easiest for him. That is a scouting task rather than a defending task, which is a useful reminder that a substantial part of defending a great forward happens before the fixture rather than during it.

His era placement makes him a useful control in the cross-era argument. He operated in a league culture organized around defensive structure and in the period where the marking systems were being replaced rather than after the replacement was complete, which means the defending he faced was a hybrid and the footage shows both approaches inside the same match. Anyone learning to read historical footage should study that specific transition, and the duel detail is in [the breakdown of how defenders stopped Gabriel Batistuta](/2021/02/01/stopping-gabriel-batistuta/).

### Marco van Basten

The question he asked was about the finishing action itself, and it is the question that exposes the limits of positional defending.

The forward at Ajax and Milan, and a European Championship winner with the Netherlands, was a tall centre-forward with the technical range of a much smaller one. The defensive difficulty that creates is specific. A defender's positional plan against a tall forward is built on the assumption that the dangerous actions come from a limited menu, which is the header, the close-range finish, and the hold. When the same forward can strike a ball cleanly from an angle and a body position that the menu does not include, the positional plan stops covering the danger, and the defender is left defending a possibility rather than a pattern.

The practical response is to move the defending earlier, which is a theme this article keeps returning to. If the finishing action cannot be predicted, the delivery has to be. Defending the cross, the cutback, and the entry pass is the only reliable way to reduce a forward whose finishing range is wider than your positional plan can cover, and this is one of the strongest arguments in the series for spending session time on delivery denial rather than on the box duel.

He also belongs in the cross-era discussion for a reason unrelated to the laws. His game required a surface and a ball good enough to reward technique, and he played in a period where both were improving. That is the fourth variable acting on a single career, and it is the cleanest illustration in the cluster of why the surface question is not trivial. The full duel, including the marking scheme that gave defenses their best results, is in [the breakdown of how defenders stopped Marco van Basten](/2021/02/15/stopping-marco-van-basten/).

### Gerd Muller

The question he asked is the one modern football has quietly stopped asking, and it is the most instructive question in the cluster.

The forward at Bayern Munich, a World Cup and European Championship winner with West Germany, operated almost entirely inside the penalty area, with an unusual body shape for the position, a very short first step, and an ability to generate a shot from positions where the ball was not properly under his control. The archetype is the penalty-box specialist, and it is worth being precise about what made it work, because the lazy description is that he simply stood in the six-yard box.

What he actually did was solve the box duel with three things. The first is the short first step, which converts a two-yard gap into a shot before a defender can transfer his weight. The second is the willingness to shoot from body positions that look wrong, which removes the defender's ability to predict when the danger begins. The third, and the most under-appreciated, is the refusal to compete for the ball he could not win in favor of occupying the space where the ball would go next. The last of these is a decision-making quality rather than a physical one, and it is why the archetype produced output that raw athletic measurement would never predict.

The defensive answer of his era was the dedicated marker with a sweeper behind, and it is fair to say the system was in part designed for the problem this kind of forward represented. Whether the same forward would function against a modern zonal line is the most contested question in this whole cluster, and it deserves a proper answer rather than a slogan. The answer is in [the breakdown of how defenders stopped Gerd Muller](/2021/03/01/stopping-gerd-muller/), and the general version of the archetype question, meaning whether the specialist or the complete forward is harder to defend, is resolved in [the poacher against complete forward decision](/2021/03/15/poacher-vs-complete-forward/).
## The Defender Archetypes Each Era Rewarded

If the forwards changed, the defenders changed with them, and the sequence is instructive because it shows selection following the laws rather than following fashion.

Under permissive contact and man-marking, the valuable centre-back was a specialist in the individual duel. He needed the strength and aggression to win a physical contest, the tolerance for being isolated, the discipline to hold a goal-side reference for ninety minutes, and the concentration to follow one opponent through every movement without being dragged into errors. What he did not particularly need was elite positional judgment across a line, because the line was not the unit of defending, and he did not need to be comfortable in possession, because the goalkeeper's hands were available and the expectation on him with the ball was modest.

The sweeper alongside that profile required something almost opposite: reading, anticipation, and the willingness to make a decision on behalf of teammates. The two roles were complementary specialists, and a defense that had one and not the other was structurally incomplete.

As the tackle from behind disappeared, the value of pure duel strength fell relative to the value of positional judgment, because a defender who could no longer recover through contact had to avoid needing to. The centre-back who wins the ground rather than the ball became more valuable than the centre-back who wins the ball through the man. This is a genuine change in the ideal profile of the position and it happened within a generation of the law changing, which is about as clean a demonstration of cause and effect as this sport offers.

As zonal lines replaced marking, two further qualities rose. The first is line discipline, which is a communication and timing skill rather than a physical one, and which is the reason a defense can now be organized around defenders who would have struggled in an isolated individual duel. The second is comfort in possession, driven by the backpass rule and then amplified by the tactical preference for building from the back. A centre-back's technical requirements with the ball rose faster than any other quality in the position, and it is the most visible change in what the job now asks.

The version of the position that emerged from all four changes is a defender who is judged first on where he stands, second on what he does with the ball, and third on whether he wins duels. That ordering would have looked absurd to a marker in the era of the sweeper, and it is correct now for the same reason his ordering was correct then, which is that both follow from the laws and the systems in force.

There is a warning inside this for anybody developing young defenders. The ordering above describes the professional game. It does not describe most amateur and youth football, where duel strength and simple positional competence still decide a very large share of outcomes, and where a defender coached exclusively as a distributor will be beaten in the air every week. Coach the modern priorities, but do not skip the individual duel, because the duel is still where matches are lost at every level below the top of the sport.
## Which Archetype Is Hardest, and Hardest for Whom

The question of which forward type is hardest to defend has no answer in the abstract. It has a very clear answer once you specify the defense.

Against a low block with numbers behind the ball, the hardest forward is the one who can generate a shot inside a crowd, because the block's entire logic is to deny space and a forward who does not need space is immune to it. That is the penalty-box specialist and the distance striker, which means the archetypes people assume are outdated are precisely the ones a modern compact defense finds most awkward. This is not a paradox. A defensive method optimized against space is weakest against a threat that does not use space.

Against a high line with a compressed pitch, the hardest forward is the one who attacks the space behind at the moment the line steps, because the line's logic depends on the offside law doing work that a well-timed run removes. That is the diagonal runner and the direct forward, and it is the archetype modern football produces most reliably because modern football contains the most high lines.

Against a man-oriented system, the hardest forward is the one who can beat his assigned opponent in a confined space, because the system delivers exactly that confrontation by design. That is the dribbling forward and the holding forward, and it is a large part of why man-oriented defending declined.

Against a zonal line, the hardest forward is the one who lives in the seams between zones and forces hand-offs at speed, because the system's one structural weakness is the boundary between two responsibilities. That is the wide-starting forward who finishes centrally, and it is why that profile became the dominant one.

Against a mid-block, which is the most common defensive setting in professional football and the one least often discussed in this argument, the hardest forward is the one who can receive between the lines and turn, because the mid-block's defining feature is the vertical distance between its midfield and defensive lines and a forward who occupies that gap forces one of them to break shape. Neither option is comfortable. If the midfield drops, the block loses its ability to press the ball and invites sustained possession. If a centre-back steps, he leaves the line he is part of, and the runner behind him is the whole point of the movement. This is the setting in which the modern between-the-lines forward is most valuable and the setting in which the era's supply improvement matters most, because the movement only pays if somebody plays the pass.

Against an amateur or youth defense, which is where most readers of this series actually coach, the hardest forward is almost always the one who is simply stronger and quicker than everybody on the pitch, and the honest advice is that no archetype analysis solves that. What solves it is doubling the duel, denying the supply, and accepting concessions elsewhere, which is a squad-level decision rather than a defender-level one.

There is a pattern in those five answers and it is the most useful synthesis in the article. Every defensive method has a blind spot, and the blind spot is always the mirror image of the method's strength. A method that defends space is vulnerable to a threat that ignores space. A method that defends a person is vulnerable to a forward who beats that person. A method that defends a structure is vulnerable at the joins. This is why no era has ever produced a general solution to great forwards, and why the sport keeps cycling between approaches rather than converging on one.

## Settling the Era War: Three Questions, Three Different Answers

The debate is unresolvable as stated because it collapses three separate questions into one. Separate them and each becomes answerable, and they do not all resolve in the same direction.

### The athletic question

Would a forward from an earlier era be physically overwhelmed by modern professional football?

The honest answer is that the baseline has risen for everybody and the comparison is therefore between a forward's physical qualities relative to his own peers, not against an absolute standard. A forward who was the fastest man on his pitch would, with modern preparation, nutrition, surfaces, and training from an early age, be a fast man on a modern pitch. The counterfactual that transplants a forward from one era into another without also transplanting his development environment is not a serious argument, and it is used almost exclusively by people who want to win the debate rather than understand it.

Where the modern side has a real point is in repeated-sprint volume and in the defensive workload attached to a forward's job. Modern forwards cover ground in a pressing structure and are held accountable for it. A forward whose entire career was built on conserving himself for the attacking phase would find that expectation genuinely difficult, and adapting to it is not trivial. Concede that point honestly. It is the strongest thing the modern side has.

Where the older side has a real point is that physical dominance relative to peers is transferable in a way pure measurement is not. The qualities that made a forward hard to handle, which are strength through contact, balance, a short first step, and the willingness to attack a contested ball, do not depreciate when everyone gets fitter, because they are relative and contextual rather than absolute.

**Verdict on the athletic question.** It is close to a draw and the draw is not a dodge. The transplant argument fails in both directions, and the only defensible position is that a forward's physical qualities relative to his contemporaries are the meaningful measure, which leaves the athletic question unable to settle the debate on its own.

### The tactical question

Would a forward from an earlier era cope with modern defensive organization?

This is where the modern side wins, and it should be conceded clearly. Coordinated pressing, rehearsed line steps, compact vertical distances, and the collective defending of the entry pass are genuine tactical advances, and a forward who never encountered them would have to learn a great deal. In particular, a forward whose game was built on beating an assigned individual marker would find that the individual marker no longer exists, which removes the specific problem he was optimized to solve.

But the concession has a limit and the limit is important. Tactical organization is learnable, and it is learnable by the forward as well as by the defense. Modern forwards are not physically different from earlier forwards in a way that lets them find seams in a zonal line. They have been coached to do it since they were children. A forward with elite perception and timing, developed in a modern environment, would learn it. The tactical question therefore says something true about the transplant scenario and almost nothing about the underlying quality of the player.

**Verdict on the tactical question.** Modern football wins on organization, and the win is real. It says less about the forwards than people think it does, because organization is a coached quality and coaching is the variable that changed, not the player.

### The rules question

Would a modern forward survive the contact permitted in earlier eras, and did earlier forwards benefit from anything the modern ones do not have?

Here the older side wins, and the win is larger than the modern side usually admits. The permitted contact was a genuine defensive tool, it capped the cost of being beaten, and it meant that a forward's every action carried a physical risk that no longer exists in the same form. A modern forward dropped into that environment without preparation would find shielding, receiving with his back to goal, and running at a defender all considerably more expensive.

But this side of the argument also has a limit, and it is the limit the nostalgic version always skips. Permissiveness is not skill. A defensive era that permitted more contact developed less sophisticated positional defending precisely because it did not need to, which means the same forward would be facing a defense that was better equipped in one dimension and worse equipped in several others. The trade is not a straight downgrade for the forward.

**Verdict on the rules question.** Earlier eras were harder in the specific dimension of permitted physical contact, and this is the strongest argument the older side has. It is also the argument that concedes the most, because it locates the difficulty in the laws rather than in the quality of the defending.

### The combined verdict

Put the three together and the answer is that the era war has no winner, because the three questions point in different directions and none of them dominates. Athletically it is a draw once the transplant fallacy is removed. Tactically the modern game wins on organization, which is a statement about coaching rather than about players. On the laws, the earlier eras were physically harder in a way that is real and should not be waved away.

What survives all three is the constant duel. The half yard of separation, the timing of the run against the passer's body, the first contact, and the near-post geometry did not move. That is the finding a coach can use, and it is the reason this cluster exists.
## Four Claims From the Argument, Each With a Verdict

Beyond the three core questions, four further claims recur whenever this topic is raised. Each contains something true and each is overstated as usually delivered, so each gets a fair reading and a verdict.

### "Defenders were better before zonal systems arrived"

The fair reading. Defenders in marking systems were, on average, better at the isolated individual duel, because they did it constantly and were held individually accountable for it. That is a real skill, it was more developed then, and modern coaching does under-serve it. Anyone who has watched a well-drilled young side defend a shape competently and then collapse the moment a forward runs at a single defender has seen the gap.

The overstatement. Better at one component is not better overall. The same defenders were not required to hold a line, execute a rehearsed step, defend a compressed vertical distance, or receive under pressure and play forward, and there is no evidence that they would have been good at those things without being coached in them. The individual duel is one of perhaps six competencies the modern position requires.

**Verdict.** Better at the individual duel, not better as defenders, and the reason is that the job description was narrower. The correct response is not to argue about it but to take the missing component back, which is what the transfer section of this article is for.

### "The game is faster now, therefore it is harder"

The fair reading. It is faster, in ball speed, in decision speed, and in the number of high-intensity actions per match. That is measurable and not in dispute, and it does raise the cognitive and physical demand on both forwards and defenders.

The overstatement. Faster is not the same as harder, because difficulty in a duel is relative. If everybody is quicker, the reference points move together and the half yard is still contested at the same relative margin. Speed also cuts against the defense in some phases and against the attack in others: a faster transition helps a counter-attacking forward, while a faster collective press helps the defending side. There is no clean directional conclusion.

**Verdict.** Faster is true and harder does not follow. The claim describes a change in the tempo at which the same problems are solved, not a change in the problems.

### "Statistics from earlier eras are not comparable"

The fair reading. This one is largely correct and it is the strongest of the four. Fixture counts, competition structures, penalty-taking conventions, squad sizes, and the tactical specialization of the opposition all differ, and totals generated under those conditions cannot be lined up against modern totals as though they measured the same thing.

The overstatement. Not comparable is not the same as not informative. Relative dominance within a period is a meaningful and available measure. How far ahead of his contemporaries a forward was, sustained across how many years, against which defensive systems, is a comparison that survives the objection because it normalizes against the same conditions the forward faced.

**Verdict.** Raw totals are not comparable and relative dominance is. Anybody who wants to make a cross-era case with numbers should be making it in the second currency.

### "Cross-era comparison is meaningless"

The fair reading. As a demand for a definitive ranking, it is close to meaningless, and the counterfactual that transplants a person from one period into another without his development environment is not a serious form of argument.

The overstatement. The comparison is meaningless only if the question is who was better. If the question is what changed, what it did to the duel, and what survives, then cross-era work is not merely meaningful, it is one of the most useful things a coach can do, because it isolates the parts of the craft that are conditional and the parts that are permanent. Refusing the exercise entirely throws away the constant duel along with the ranking.

**Verdict.** Meaningless as a ranking exercise, valuable as a mechanism exercise. This article is the second kind, and the distinction is the reason it can reach conclusions the argument normally cannot.
## What Transfers Into a Session This Week

The whole point of isolating the variables is to find out what is still usable. Here is the honest sorting, and it divides into three piles.

The first pile is what is illegal now and must never be taught. The recovery challenge from behind on a forward running with the ball is the obvious one, and it should not appear in any session under any framing, including as a demonstration of what defenders used to do. Persistent holding and shirt-pulling in the penalty area belong in the same pile. The reason to be firm about this is not squeamishness. It is that a young defender who learns a habit that gets punished at the level above has been taught a defect, and unlearning a defensive reflex is much harder than learning one.

The second pile is what is legal but obsolete, meaning it will not get a player penalized and will not help him either. Following a forward across the full width of the pitch without a hand-off boundary is the clearest example. It was correct inside a system that had a sweeper behind it and a screening midfielder in front of it, and it is simply a way of disorganizing your own defense inside a system that has neither. The sweeper's covering depth belongs in the same pile for the same reason, which is that the role only makes sense when the line behind it is not the offside line.

The third pile is what is legal, useful, and under-coached, and it is larger than most coaches expect.

Goal-side reference with an open body angle is the first item and the most valuable. The old marking systems taught it explicitly, because a marker who lost his goal-side position had failed at his single job. Modern zonal coaching often teaches line discipline and forgets to teach the individual body angle that lets a defender see both the ball and his immediate opponent. That habit is fully legal, entirely transferable, and it is the single highest-return coaching point in defending.

The pre-agreed hand-off boundary is the second item. It came from man-marking systems, where a defender needed to know at exactly which line he passed his opponent to a teammate, and it is the precise thing modern zonal defenses need at the seams. A hand-off that is agreed in the week and called out loud in the match is a rehearsed behavior. A hand-off that is improvised is a goal against.

Screening the entry pass is the third item. The old systems understood that a marker without a screen is isolated, and the modern equivalent is that a centre-back defending a forward who receives between the lines without a midfielder in front of him is being asked to solve a problem that has no individual solution. This is a midfield coaching point that lives inside a defending session, and it is routinely skipped.

Legal contact as continuous disruption is the fourth item, and it needs care. There is a legitimate, legal, and largely untaught skill in using arm and shoulder contact early and continuously to disrupt a forward's timing and balance, in a way that never becomes the single decisive push that a referee penalizes. It is taught properly in very few environments and it is one of the clearest things a coach can take from studying older defending. It should be introduced late in a player's development rather than early, and it should be framed as timing disruption rather than as physicality, because the framing determines what a young player actually practices.

Near-post priority is the fifth item and the easiest to install. The first defender attacks the near post as though the delivery is coming there. This was true when the ball was heavy and it is true now.

Turning these into an actual session, with constraints, progressions, and small-squad versions, is a separate craft and this article does not attempt it. The series owns that ground in [the session design for rehearsing a containment plan](/2020/10/15/training-to-contain-a-superstar/), which is the right next stop for a coach who wants the drills rather than the principles.

## How to Read Historical Footage Without Fooling Yourself

Analysts and coaching students get one specific benefit from this framework, which is a method for watching old matches without importing conclusions that no longer hold.

Watch for the law before you judge the action. When a defender does something that looks wrong, ask first whether it was legal and correct at the time. A defender committing early into a challenge from a poor position was frequently exercising an option that has since been removed. A defender standing off on a heavy surface was frequently respecting a real risk. Neither is evidence of poor defending.

Watch the system before you judge the shape. A back line that looks disorganized may be a marking system doing exactly what it was designed to do, with individual defenders positioned by their opponents rather than by the line. Judging a marking system by the standards of line discipline is a category error and it is the most common mistake in this kind of viewing.

Watch the surface before you judge the technique. A forward taking a heavy touch on a rutted pitch is not necessarily a forward with a poor first touch. A defender who does not play out from the back on that surface is not necessarily a defender who could not.

Watch the competition structure before you judge the intensity. Squad sizes, substitution allowances, and fixture density all shape how hard a side can play for how long, and a match that looks slow may be a match being managed rather than a match being played badly.

And separate what the forward did from what the forward was allowed to do. This is the same discipline in reverse. A forward who repeatedly received unopposed between the lines was not necessarily finding space through genius; he may have been operating in a period before the screening obligation was widely coached. The genius, where it exists, shows up in what he did after receiving.

## Turning the Four Constants Into Things You Can Actually See

A framework that cannot be observed is a framework that cannot be coached. Each of the four constants has visible markers, and an analyst or a coach watching a single match can pick all four out without any equipment beyond attention. This is the practical bridge between the argument above and a Tuesday session, and it works equally well on old footage and on a live match at any level.

For separation, watch the forward's feet in the two seconds before the ball arrives, not the moment of the pass. The marker is a change of direction that is preceded by a weight transfer in the opposite direction, and the observable evidence is the defender's hips turning first. If you see the defender's hips commit and the forward move against that commitment, you have watched separation being created and you have identified the exact deception the forward uses. Most forwards have one or two, repeated constantly, and they are visible within twenty minutes.

The defensive marker for the same constant is the body angle. Watch whether the defender can see the ball and his opponent without turning his head fully in either direction. A defender who has to choose which one to look at has already lost the information contest, and the failure will show up two or three times per half in the same way. This is the most reliably identifiable defensive fault in football and it is fixable in one session.

For timing, take your eyes off the forward entirely and watch the supplier. The observable marker is the relationship between the passer's body opening and the forward's first step. When they are simultaneous, the forward is reading the passer. When the forward moves first and then waits, he is guessing, and a defensive line will eventually catch him. This one observation tells you more about a forward's intelligence than any highlight compilation, and it costs nothing to make.

For first contact, watch the shoulder and the leading foot in the moment before the ball arrives. The forward who wins first contact establishes his frame with a step across the defender rather than a lean into him, because a step is legal and durable and a lean can be walked around. The defender who wins it is the one whose front foot is already in the space the forward intends to occupy. Whoever gets there first usually wins the sequence regardless of who is stronger, and this is the observation that most changes how a coach talks to a physically outmatched defender.

For near-post geometry, watch the first defender on any cross. The question is simply whether he attacks the near-post space as a default or waits to read the delivery. The waiting version looks more thoughtful and concedes more goals, because the near post is the delivery a defense has least time to react to and therefore the one that has to be covered on assumption rather than on evidence. Count the crosses in a half where the near-post space was unoccupied at the moment of delivery, and you will have a number that predicts trouble.

Four observations, none of which requires data, all of which apply to every match ever played. That is the practical content of the constant duel, and it is the reason a coach can study a forward from a period he never saw live and come away with something to use on Tuesday.
## If You Coach at Amateur or Youth Level

Most of what has been said so far describes the professional game, and the reader most likely to act on it is coaching a squad of fourteen on a shared pitch with two sessions a week. The translation is worth doing explicitly, because the naive version of it does damage.

The first translation is about the individual duel. At the professional level the individual duel has been de-emphasized relative to structure, and that ordering is correct there. It is not correct for a side where a single quick forward can beat two defenders and score without any tactical assistance whatsoever, which describes most amateur and youth football. Teach the structure, but do not let the fashion for structure convince you that a defender who cannot defend a one against one has been coached. Approach angle, distance management, and the decision to delay rather than commit are the highest-value items in an amateur defending session, and they are exactly the items the loss of the tackle from behind made compulsory.

The second translation is about which historical habits to keep away from young players. Do not teach anything that involves contact from behind, in any framing. Do not teach a young defender to follow his opponent across the pitch without a boundary, because he will disorganize a defense that has no sweeper to cover him. Do not teach the sweeper's covering depth as a general principle, because your line is the offside line and a player standing behind it is giving the opposition forwards permission to stand in your penalty area.

The third translation is about contact work, and it needs care. The legal, continuous use of arm and shoulder contact to disrupt a forward's timing is a genuine skill and it is worth coaching, but it should be introduced late rather than early. Young players who are taught to use contact before they have learned position and timing simply use contact instead of position and timing, and they arrive at the next level with a reflex that concedes free kicks. Teach the position first, the timing second, and the contact last, and frame it as timing disruption rather than as strength.

The fourth translation is about what to do when the opposition forward is simply better than everybody you have, which is the situation that actually arrives. No amount of archetype analysis solves it and no individual defender solves it. What solves it, partially, is deciding in advance what you are conceding. Double the duel and concede the switch. Deny the supply and concede possession in areas that do not hurt you. Drop the line and concede the shot from distance. The decision is yours and it should be made in the week rather than in the eightieth minute, and it should be communicated to the players as a plan rather than discovered by them as a collapse.

The fifth translation is about the ball and the pitch, since the fourth variable applies to your Sunday morning surface far more than it applies to a professional stadium. If your pitch is heavy, the forward's touch will run and the defender's recovery challenge will work, which means your defending can be slightly more aggressive than the modern coaching orthodoxy suggests. If your pitch is good, it cannot. Coaching the same defensive distances regardless of surface is a small error that produces a large number of conceded goals.
## What This Framework Cannot Tell You

Honesty about the limits is part of the method, and there are three things the four-variable account genuinely cannot settle.

It cannot tell you how any individual would have performed in a period he did not play in, and no amount of framework refinement will change that. The variables explain what changed in the contest. They do not license a simulation of a person, because a person is the product of a development environment, a coaching culture, and a set of incentives that travel with him and cannot be separated from him. Anybody who tells you the framework proves a specific forward would have scored a specific amount in a different era has stopped doing analysis.

It cannot weight the four variables against each other with any precision. The account above argues that the removal of the tackle from behind is the largest single change, and that argument rests on the fact that it altered the defender's primary tool rather than the conditions around it. That is a reasoned position rather than a measured one, and somebody could argue for the marking-system change instead with respectable reasoning. The ranking is contestable; the list is not.

It cannot account for competitive standard, which is the variable nobody can isolate. How strong the opposition was, week to week, in a given league in a given period is not knowable in a way that supports comparison, and it interacts with everything else. This is the largest genuine hole in every cross-era argument ever made, including this one, and the correct response is to state it rather than to pretend the other variables cover it.

What the framework does do is remove the arguments that are simply wrong: that rule changes were neutral, that goal totals are a common currency, that athleticism is the only axis, and that permissiveness is evidence of defensive quality. Clearing those away leaves a much smaller and much more interesting disagreement.

## The Verdict

The greatest strikers and how defenders coped with them is not a question about who was better. It is a question about which problem each generation of defenders was handed, and the four variables in the table above are the honest account of how the problem changed.

Three of the four changes moved value toward the forward. The backpass rule made pressing profitable and therefore made the working forward a weapon. The removal of the tackle from behind took away the defender's recovery tool and released shielding, back to goal receiving, and running at defenders as safe primary skills. Better surfaces and balls raised the return on acceleration and fine technique. The fourth change, the move to coordinated zonal lines, moved value toward the defense, but toward the defensive unit rather than the individual defender, which is why individual defending looks less dominant now than it did and why it is nonetheless better.

The era war itself resolves into three questions with three different answers, and anyone who insists it resolves cleanly in one direction is arguing from loyalty rather than from evidence. Athletically the transplant argument fails in both directions. Tactically the modern game is better organized, which is a fact about coaching. On the laws, earlier eras were physically harder in a specific and real way, and that is the strongest card the older side holds.

Underneath all of it sits the constant duel, and it is the reason this article ends with a coaching instruction rather than a ranking. Separation, timing, first contact, and near-post geometry have not moved in any era, under any set of laws, on any surface. A defender who is excellent at those four things would have had a career in every period of the sport's history, and a forward who is excellent at them would have scored in every period. Everything else in this article is context that changes how those four are contested. The four themselves are the job.

There is a final implication for anyone who coaches. If the constants are the job and everything else is context, then the most valuable defensive session a coach can run is one that works on the constants directly rather than one that rehearses a shape. Shape is necessary and it is also the thing most sides already spend their time on. The body angle that keeps the ball and the opponent in view, the habit of reading the passer rather than the runner, the step into contested ground before contact, and the automatic near-post priority are four coaching points that improve a defense against every forward it will ever face, in every system it will ever play, under any set of laws the sport adopts next. That is an unusually durable return on a Tuesday evening.

Which is why the correct use of a cluster like this one is not to settle an argument at a bar. It is to watch a forward from a period you never saw live, identify which of the four constants he was exceptional at, and steal the mechanism. The forwards in this cluster were exceptional at different ones, and that is precisely what makes them worth studying together rather than ranking.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Were strikers of past eras harder to defend than today's?

Not harder in general, harder in different dimensions. Forwards of earlier eras operated against defenses with fewer collective tools, which made individual quality more decisive, and they did so on surfaces and against contact permissions that punished technical ambition. Forwards today operate against far better organized defenses but with the laws protecting their primary skills and with surfaces that reward acceleration and first touch. The dimension that has genuinely got harder for a defense is the coordination requirement, because forwards now attack the seams between responsibilities rather than beating an assigned opponent. The dimension that has genuinely got easier for a defense is the individual duel, because a defender no longer stands alone in it.

### Q: How did rule changes make defending a forward harder?

Two changes did most of the work. The backpass rule removed the goalkeeper's hands as an escape, which meant a defense under pressure could no longer terminate a pressing sequence and had to solve pressure with its feet. The progressive removal of the tackle from behind took away the recovery tool that capped the cost of being beaten, so a defender who is beaten now stays beaten. Together they raised the price of every positional error and forced the defensive craft to move earlier, into approach angle, distance management, and denial of the reception, rather than sitting in the challenge itself.

### Q: Did the backpass rule change how forwards were defended?

Substantially, and indirectly. It did not change the box duel, but it changed the value of a forward's defensive work, because pressure applied to a defense could now produce a turnover instead of ending in a goalkeeper's hands. That made pressing forwards genuinely dangerous and made the technical standard of centre-backs a live opposition target. For a defense, it meant the build-up phase became part of the plan against a forward rather than a separate concern, and it meant a forward who worked was creating chances even when he never touched the ball in the attacking phase.

### Q: How did the ban on tackling from behind affect forwards?

It released a set of skills that were previously too expensive to use as primary weapons. Shielding, receiving with the back to goal, and running directly at a defender all became far safer once a defender could no longer legally take the ball through the forward from behind. Within a generation, forwards who specialized in holding and inviting contact became a standard and highly valued profile. The wider effect is what this article calls tool substitution: remove a defensive tool and the attacking quality it suppressed reappears, which is why law changes reshape attacking technique more than defensive technique.

### Q: What did old fashioned man marking actually involve?

Three parts, all of which had to be present. The marker took a specified starting distance from his forward, kept a goal-side reference, and had an explicit instruction on whether to follow across the pitch or hand off at an agreed boundary. Behind him a sweeper played at a covering depth chosen so a beaten marker was not the last obstacle. In front of him a midfielder screened the entry pass so the marker was not defending a pass and a man at once. When the system failed it usually failed at the third part, because a marker without a screen is isolated by definition.

### Q: Would a classic poacher still function in modern football?

Yes, though in a narrower job. The specialist's core qualities, which are the short first step inside the box, the willingness to strike from awkward body positions, and the instinct to occupy where the ball will go rather than contest where it is, are all fully effective against a modern defense and are arguably more effective against a compact low block than a mobile forward's qualities are. What has changed is selection economics. A side that gives one attacking place to a player who contributes little to pressing and build-up has to be confident the finishing return covers the cost, and most are not.

### Q: How has offside interpretation changed the striker duel?

It has moved the contest earlier and made it a matter of centimetres and timing rather than of judgment. A forward's run against a stepping line was once partly a negotiation with an official's eye; it is now a precise technical skill with a narrow tolerance, which rewards forwards who can accelerate late from a held position and punishes those who drift early. For defenders, the line step became a rehearsable collective action with a genuine payoff, which is one reason high lines became viable and one reason the defensive craft moved from the individual to the unit.

### Q: Were defenders more physical in earlier eras?

They were permitted more physical contact, which is a different claim and the distinction matters. Defenders in earlier periods had a legal toolkit that included actions no longer available, and they used those tools because using them was correct under the laws of the time. Modern defenders are, on average, stronger and better conditioned athletes who are allowed to do less with it. Reading old footage as evidence that defenders were tougher confuses permission with quality, and it usually overlooks that the same permissive environment left positional defending less developed because it did not have to be.

### Q: Which defensive skills from past eras are still worth teaching?

Four, and all four are legal and under-coached. Goal-side reference with an open body angle, so a defender sees the ball and his opponent at once. The pre-agreed hand-off boundary, which came from marking systems and is exactly what zonal defenses need at their seams. Screening the entry pass, since a defender left alone against a receiver between the lines has no individual solution. And the continuous, legal use of arm and shoulder contact to disrupt a forward's timing, taught late in development and framed as timing rather than as physicality.

### Q: How did pitch and ball quality affect forward play?

Heavy surfaces compressed acceleration, made first touch unreliable, and rewarded strength and balance over fine technique, while heavier balls in wet conditions made long delivery and shooting less predictable. Forwards adapted by playing with larger margins for error, attacking contested balls decisively rather than waiting, and building their games around physical qualities that the surface did not take away. Defenders gained from the same conditions, because sliding recovery challenges worked better and a forward's loose touch was a plannable event. Judging technique from that period by modern standards marks a forward down for adapting correctly.

### Q: Did older forwards have less space or more?

More space between the lines and behind the defense, and less space at the moment of receiving. Defenses sat deeper, pressed less as a coordinated unit, and left larger vertical distances, so a forward could find the ball in areas a modern block would not concede. Once he had it, though, he was usually met by a dedicated marker with permission to use contact that no longer exists, so the space around the reception was smaller and more hostile. Modern forwards have the reverse arrangement, which is why the two eras are not comparable on a single space measurement.

### Q: Which era produced the hardest forwards to defend?

The transitional period produced the most awkward problems, because defenses were operating hybrid systems while the laws were changing underneath them. A defense carrying man-marking habits into a zonal structure, without the permissive contact that made the old habits work, is a defense with the weaknesses of both approaches. Forwards who could beat an individual and read a structure were, for that window, facing defenses that were fully equipped for neither. That is a statement about defensive organization rather than about the forwards themselves, which is the correct way to answer any question phrased this way.

### Q: What can a modern coach borrow from old defensive methods?

Chiefly the individual clarity. Marking systems forced every defender to know precisely what his job was, at what distance, with which reference point, and to which boundary, and that clarity is often missing in zonal coaching that teaches the shape without teaching the individual behavior inside it. Borrow the goal-side body angle, the agreed hand-off line, and the explicit screening obligation. Do not borrow the sweeper's covering depth, the habit of following a forward across the full width, or anything involving contact from behind, all of which are obsolete or illegal.

### Q: How do you compare forwards across different eras fairly?

Compare relative dominance rather than raw output, and state the variables you are controlling for. Ask how far ahead of his contemporaries a forward was, in which qualities, and against which defensive systems and law permissions. Then ask which of his core qualities depend on conditions that have changed and which do not. Timing, separation, first contact, and near-post attacking transfer completely. Beating a dedicated individual marker transfers poorly, because the assignment no longer exists. Anyone comparing goal totals without naming the fixture counts, competition structures, and marking systems is not making an argument.

### Q: Did sweepers make defending a great forward easier?

In one dimension, yes. The sweeper gave a marker permission to engage aggressively, because a second defender covered the space behind, which is a real advantage against a forward who would otherwise be through on goal the moment he turned. The cost was that two defenders were committed to one forward, leaving the rest of the defense numerically thin and giving a forward with intelligent movement the ability to create space for teammates without touching the ball. The system also prevented vertical compression, which handed the opposition midfield room to play.

### Q: What stays constant in defending a great forward?

Four things, and they define the job in every era. Separation, meaning the half yard the forward creates and the defender denies at the moment the ball is struck. Timing, meaning that both men read the passer's body rather than the ball. First contact, meaning who establishes their frame in the contested space and on what terms. And the geometry of the near post, which is fixed by the dimensions of the goal and rewards the forward who attacks it and the defender who assumes the delivery is going there.
