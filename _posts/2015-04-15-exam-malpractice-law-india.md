Almost everyone who argues about examination malpractice law in India argues about a statute that does not exist. One side of the argument assumes that the Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024 sends cheating candidates to prison for years, and denounces it for cruelty. The other side assumes the same thing, and praises it for toughness. Both are describing a law Parliament did not pass. The statute enacted in February 2024 aims its prison terms at the people who leak, sell, solve, impersonate, and organise, and it deliberately leaves the candidate outside its own punishments. The disagreement worth having is not about that imaginary law. It is about the real one, and the real disagreement can be stated in terms both camps accept: whether a criminal statute aimed at the supply side of cheating can protect examinations whose failures are, on the record of 2024 itself, substantially failures of administration.

![Exam malpractice law in India, showing what the Public Examinations Act 2024 punishes and whom it leaves out - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-95.webp)

Stated fairly, the case for the Act is that organised paper leaking had become an industry, prosecuted before 2024 under general offences of cheating, forgery, and criminal conspiracy that were never designed for it, and that a dedicated statute with heavy fines, property forfeiture, and debarment of complicit firms was the missing instrument. Stated fairly, the case against is that leaks are produced by a system: a single agency conducting high-stakes examinations for lakhs of candidates, logistics and technology outsourced to private firms, question papers moving through long physical chains, and no statutory standard of examination security imposed on anyone. On that view the Act punishes the symptom with great severity and the cause not at all. Both statements are made by serious people, and this article gives each its strongest form before reaching a verdict.

What no reader needs is another retelling of the anger. What the public debate has lacked is the statutory detail: which examinations the Act covers, which acts it criminalises, what each actor in the chain actually faces, and the single fact that governed everything that happened in the summer of 2024, which is that the Act was brought into force on 21 June 2024, four months after it received assent, and a criminal statute cannot reach backwards. The examinations whose compromise dominated that summer were held before the commencement date. The law that the controversy produced was therefore, as a matter of constitutional arithmetic, the one law that could never apply to the events that produced it. That sequence, and what follows from it, is the spine of this article.

## What the Act actually says, stripped of characterisation

Public argument about this statute consistently misdescribes its text, so the starting point is the text. The Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024 is Act 1 of 2024. It was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 5 February 2024 by Dr Jitendra Singh, the Minister of State for Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions, passed by the Lok Sabha on 6 February and by the Rajya Sabha on 9 February, and received the assent of the President on 12 February 2024. It contains eighteen sections and a Schedule. Its long title states its purpose without ornament: an Act to prevent unfair means in the public examinations and to provide for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto.

The Act does not apply to examinations in general. It applies to a "public examination", which Section 2 defines as an examination conducted by a public examination authority specified in the Schedule, or by any other authority the Central Government notifies. The Schedule names five authorities: the Union Public Service Commission, the Staff Selection Commission, the Railway Recruitment Boards, the Institute of Banking Personnel Selection, and the National Testing Agency, together with the ministries and departments of the Central Government and their attached and subordinate offices, for the recruitment of staff. Everything else is outside. State public service commissions, state staff selection boards, school boards, and universities conducting their own examinations are not covered, a boundary examined in the state law section of this article. The National Testing Agency's presence in the Schedule is what brings the great entrance examinations within the Act: it is the agency conducting university entrance tests such as the Common University Entrance Test and the national eligibility and entrance tests for medicine and engineering, and its inclusion connects this statute to the wider architecture of [the University Grants Commission Act 1956](/2015/02/15/ugc-act-1956-explained/), under which the university system it feeds is organised.

Section 3 lists the acts that constitute unfair means, and the list is the working core of the statute. It covers the leakage of a question paper or answer key or any part of either; participating in collusion with others to effect such leakage; unauthorised access to, or possession of, a question paper or an optical mark reader response sheet; providing a solution to one or more questions by an unauthorised person during a public examination; directly or indirectly assisting a candidate in any manner in the examination; tampering with an answer sheet, including an optical mark reader response sheet; tampering with any document for the purposes of shortlisting candidates or finalising a merit list or rank; deliberate violation of the norms, standards, or rules governing the conduct of examinations; tampering with a computer network, a computer resource, or a computer system; manipulation in the conduct of an examination, including manipulation of seating arrangements and of the allocation of dates and shifts; harming or threatening the life or liberty of, or wrongfully restraining, persons associated with the public examination authority or a service provider; obstructing the conduct of a public examination; the creation of a fake website to cheat or for monetary gain; the conduct of a fake examination, and the issuance of fake admit cards or fake offer letters for the same purposes; and the resolution or writing down of answers by an unauthorised person in place of the candidate. The list is deliberately weighted toward the infrastructure of cheating rather than the act of copying in the hall. A candidate who glances at a neighbour's sheet commits none of these acts in the ordinary case; the person who sold the paper the night before commits several.

The sections that follow build the perimeter. Section 4 makes conspiracy to commit unfair means an offence in itself. Section 5 covers the disruption of the conduct of a public examination by a person, a group, or an institution. Section 6 sweeps up related offences, including offences by persons entrusted with the work of the examination. Section 7 provides that no premises other than an examination centre shall be used for holding a public examination, a provision aimed at the private venues at which solved papers were rehearsed. Section 8 addresses service providers and other persons directly: a service provider must report an incident of unfair means to the police and to the examination authority, failure to report is itself an offence, and a service provider may not shift an examination centre from the allotted centre without the authority's permission. The architecture is worth pausing on. The statute treats the private firm that prints, transports, hosts, or invigilates the examination not as a contractor governed by its contract but as a regulated participant inside the criminal scheme, with duties of its own and liability of its own.

Section 9 fixes the procedural character of every offence under the Act: all offences are cognizable, non-bailable, and non-compoundable. Cognizable means the police may arrest without a warrant. Non-bailable means release on bail is a matter for a court's discretion rather than the accused person's entitlement. Non-compoundable means the offence cannot be settled away by compromise between the parties. Section 12 fixes the rank of the investigator: an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police or Assistant Commissioner of Police must investigate, and the Central Government may refer the investigation to a central investigating agency. Section 13 deems the members, officers, and employees of a public examination authority to be public servants, and Section 14 protects action taken in good faith. Section 15 provides that the Act operates in addition to, and not in derogation of, other laws, which is the provision that keeps the general penal code in play for everything the Act does not reach. Sections 16 to 18 give the Centre rule-making power, require the rules to be laid before Parliament, and confer a power to remove difficulties. Rules under the Act were notified alongside its commencement.

The punishment scheme sits in Sections 10 and 11, and it is graduated by actor rather than by act, which is the design decision on which the whole controversy turns. Its detail is set out in the actor table later in this article. The other design decision is the one that does not appear as a section at all. The Statement of Objects and Reasons accompanying the Bill recorded that a candidate, as defined in the Act, shall not be liable for action within the purview of the Act and shall continue to be covered under the administrative provisions of the concerned public examination authority, and the minister piloting the Bill stated the same position in the House: candidates are kept outside the Act and remain governed by the rules of the body conducting their examination, which may cancel a candidature, debar a candidate, or annul a result, but may not send the candidate to prison under this statute. The exclusion is examined from both sides below. What matters at the level of text is that the harshest penalties in the Act fall on the firm, on the firm's directors where consent or connivance is proved, and above all on the organised group, and that the person the public imagines in a cell under this Act, the individual examinee, is the one person the Act was designed not to put there.

Two features of the punishment text deserve a closer reading than they usually receive, because both will matter in the Act's first prosecutions. The first is a piece of drafting archaeology. Section 10 provides that where a fine is not paid, additional imprisonment shall be imposed as per the provisions of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, and then adds a proviso that until that Sanhita is brought into force, the Indian Penal Code shall apply in its place. The Act was passed in February 2024, in the interval between the enactment of the new criminal codes and their commencement on 1 July 2024, and the proviso is a bridge built for a five-month gap: a reminder that this statute was drafted in the middle of the largest transition in Indian criminal law since the codes of the nineteenth century, and that its references had to be written to survive the crossing. The second feature is a genuine tension in the text, and candour requires it to be stated in a section devoted to the text. Section 10(1) punishes "any person or persons resorting to unfair means and offences under this Act". The candidate exclusion, by contrast, appears not in Section 10 but in the Statement of Objects and Reasons and in the minister's statement in the House. The government's position is clear and was stated repeatedly: candidates are outside the Act. But the operative words are wide, the Section 3 list includes directly or indirectly assisting a candidate and the unauthorised writing down of answers, and the boundary between a candidate who merely benefits, whom the design protects, and a candidate who participates, whom the words arguably reach, has not been drawn by any court. Commentators noted the gap between the assurance and the text at the time of passage. Until a prosecution tests it, the honest statement of the law is the one this article has given: the Act's purpose and its pilot's assurance exclude the candidate, its literal punishment clause does not say so, and the prudent reader will not treat the assurance as if it were a section.

The definitions repay the same care. A service provider, for the Act's purposes, is the person or organisation engaged by a public examination authority to perform services in connection with the conduct of an examination, a description wide enough to take in the technology company running a computer-based test, the printer, the logistics firm, and the agency supplying centre staff, which is why the Section 8 duties and the Section 10 penalties have such reach. An institution, in the organised crime provision, is the vehicle through which forfeiture operates. The breadth is deliberate, and it is also the source of a fair drafting criticism: a scheme that sweeps the photocopier's employer and the national testing platform into one defined term must rely on prosecutorial discretion and on the consent-or-connivance threshold to sort the culpable from the merely present, and discretion of that width, in a non-bailable statute, is a power that will need watching in the Act's early cases.

## A bill passed in four days

The manner of the Act's passage is part of its record, because the speed of February 2024 explains both the statute's strengths and its silences. The Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 5 February 2024, in the budget session, and was passed by that House the next day. The Rajya Sabha passed it on 9 February. It was not referred to a parliamentary standing committee, and the interval between introduction and enactment was shorter than the interval most legislatures allow for the printing of amendments. Presidential assent followed on 12 February. Whatever else is said about the scrutiny the Bill received, it cannot be said that its scheme was unconsidered in one respect: the Statement of Objects and Reasons is unusually candid about the problem and the design. It records that malpractices in public examinations lead to delay and cancellation of examinations and adversely affect the prospects of millions of youth; that there was no specific substantive law to deal with unfair means in public examinations; that elements exploiting the vulnerabilities of the examination system needed to be identified and dealt with through comprehensive central legislation; and that the Act would serve as a model for the states to adopt for their own examinations. The debate in both Houses followed the contours the rest of this article examines. The minister's case rested on the gap in the general law and on the protection of honest candidates. Members across the opposition benches supported punishment for leak networks while pressing the questions the text does not answer: what the Bill did to prevent leaks rather than punish them, what accountability attached to the authorities conducting the examinations, and why the system that produced the failures was outside the Bill's field. No amendment on those points was carried.

Speed of passage cuts both ways, and a fair account records both edges. In the government's favour, the mischief was not novel and the drafting draws visibly on the state statutes that preceded it; a legislature is not obliged to deliberate at length over a template that several assemblies had already tested. Against the government, the questions the opposition pressed were not drafting details but structural choices, the exclusion of any duty on the examination authority chief among them, and four days of debate in a budget session is a thin record on which to settle choices of that weight. The commencement history then compounded the thinness: a Parliament that had legislated in four days watched its statute wait four months for a notification, so that the urgency claimed for the passage and the leisure taken with the commencement sit in the same record and must be explained together. No explanation that reconciles them has been offered, and the discrepancy is itself evidence in the controversy, because it suggests that the Act's primary function in February was declaratory, a statement that the state regarded leaking as organised crime, and that its operational life was an afterthought until June made it one.

## The four months: assent in February, force in June

The commencement clause is where this statute's history is decided. Section 1 provides that the Act comes into force on such date as the Central Government may appoint by notification. Parliament therefore enacted a complete criminal code for examinations in February 2024 and left the switch in the executive's hand, unflipped. For four months the Act existed as law on the statute book and as a dead letter in the world. Nothing in the record suggests urgency in the interval; the Bill itself had moved through both Houses in four days. Then, in the third week of June 2024, in the middle of the gravest examination controversy in the country's experience, the Ministry of Personnel, Public Grievances and Pensions issued the notification appointing 21 June 2024 as the date on which the provisions of the Act would come into force.

The sequence of that summer must be stated precisely, because the article's central claim depends on it and because motive must not be smuggled into chronology. The National Eligibility cum Entrance Test for undergraduate medical admissions was held on 5 May 2024. Allegations of a leaked paper, an unusual concentration of perfect scores, and a controversy over compensatory marks followed the declaration of results on 4 June 2024, and petitions reached the Supreme Court. The University Grants Commission National Eligibility Test was held on 18 June 2024 and was cancelled by the Ministry of Education the following day, after inputs were received that the integrity of the examination may have been compromised, with the matter handed to the Central Bureau of Investigation. The commencement notification for the Public Examinations Act was issued in the same period, appointing 21 June 2024. Whether the notification was a response to the controversy is a question about intention on which this article takes no position. What is not a matter of interpretation is the legal consequence of the dates.

That consequence flows from the commencement date and from the Constitution. Article 20(1) of the Constitution provides that no person shall be convicted of an offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act charged. A criminal statute that commences on 21 June cannot punish conduct of May or early June, however exactly that conduct matches its definitions. The NEET-UG paper was sat on 5 May. The UGC-NET paper was sat on 18 June, three days before commencement. Every act of leakage, assistance, impersonation, or tampering connected with those examinations therefore fell outside the new Act entirely, and the investigations proceeded, as they had to, under the general law: the cheating, forgery, impersonation, and criminal conspiracy provisions whose long comparison with their successors is traced in [the comparison of the Indian Penal Code and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/), together with state law where it applied. The gap is a specimen of a wider Indian legislative habit examined elsewhere in this series, the statute that is enacted with ceremony and commenced at leisure, but with a cruel refinement: the delay here was four months, not four decades, and it was long enough to exclude precisely the events that made the country demand the law. The general principle that penal law does not operate backwards, the same principle that anchors the objection to [retrospective taxation in the Vodafone dispute](/2012/10/15/retrospective-taxation-vodafone-india/), is not in question and was affirmed by no one more clearly than by the government itself, which never suggested the Act could be applied to the 2024 examinations. The claim that defines this statute is therefore a chronological one: notified too late for the leak that notified it.

This series has traced commencement gaps before, statutes that waited years or decades for the notification that would give them life, and the gap is usually a story of legislative indifference, a reform enacted for the record and shelved by the machinery. The four months of 2024 do not fit that pattern, and the difference is what makes them instructive. The delay was short enough to exclude any theory of forgetfulness and long enough to span exactly one examination season: the medical entrance test in May, the eligibility test in June, and the commencement three days after the second of them. In the longer gaps this series has examined, the cost of delay was diffuse, a reform postponed across a generation of cases. Here the cost was specific and enumerable: the two examinations whose failure defined the problem nationally are the two examinations the statute cannot touch, and every later examination it governs will be one whose compromise has not happened. Measured in days, it is among the shortest commencement gaps in the series. Measured by the share of the statute's intended work that the gap removed, it is arguably the most consequential, because the work it removed was the whole of the work that existed when the notification was signed. A commencement clause is usually read as boilerplate. In this statute it is the operative fact, and any evaluation of the Act that begins with its penalties and not with its dates has started in the wrong place.

## Who the law reaches and who it does not

The public debate about this Act has been conducted, on both sides, without an answer to its most elementary question: as against each person in the examination chain, what does the statute actually do? The table below answers it actor by actor, from the text of Sections 10 and 11 and the classification in Section 9. It is the findable artifact of this article, and it is the quickest route past the two standard errors, which are that the Act imprisons examinees and that it leaves firms untouched. It does neither. It imprisons organisers and consenting managers, fines firms into debarment, reaches officials and impersonators through the general offence, and sends the candidate back to the examination authority's own rulebook.

| Actor | Does the Act apply? | Provision | Punishment or consequence | Other law that applies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organised gang leaking or selling papers | Yes, in full | Sections 3, 4 and 11 | Imprisonment of five to ten years and a fine of not less than Rs 1 crore; attachment and forfeiture of property where an institution is involved, with recovery of a proportionate cost of the examination | General penal provisions on cheating, forgery and criminal conspiracy; state anti-cheating Acts where the examination is a state one |
| Service provider firm engaged by the authority | Yes | Sections 8 and 10(2) | Fine of up to Rs 1 crore, recovery of a proportionate cost of the examination, and debarment from conducting public examinations for four years; failure to report malpractice is itself an offence | Contract liability to the authority; general penal law for its employees' acts |
| Director or senior manager of that firm | Yes, where consent or connivance is established in the investigation | Section 10(3), with the due diligence defence in Section 10(4) | Imprisonment of three to ten years and a fine of Rs 1 crore; no liability where the offence occurred without the person's knowledge and all due diligence was exercised | General penal law; company law duties |
| Examination authority official | Yes, for acts within Section 3, including deliberate violation of norms and tampering with documents or merit lists | Sections 3, 6 and 10(1); the official is a public servant under Section 13 | Imprisonment of three to five years and a fine of up to Rs 10 lakh for resorting to unfair means | Anti-corruption law for bribery; departmental proceedings; general penal law |
| Invigilator or other person assisting a candidate | Yes | Sections 3 and 10(1) | Imprisonment of three to five years and a fine of up to Rs 10 lakh | General penal law; the authority's disciplinary rules where the person is its employee |
| Impersonator sitting the examination for another | Yes | Sections 3 and 10(1) | Imprisonment of three to five years and a fine of up to Rs 10 lakh | General penal provisions on impersonation and forgery; state Acts, several of which punish impersonation expressly |
| Candidate who benefits from a leak | No, by the Act's stated design | Outside the Act; the minister's statement and the Statement of Objects and Reasons reserve candidates to the conducting authority's administrative provisions | No punishment under this Act; the authority may cancel the candidature or result and debar the candidate under its own rules | State anti-cheating Acts, several of which do imprison candidates; general penal law where the candidate's own conduct amounts to cheating, forgery or conspiracy |

Three features of the table deserve emphasis. The first is the steepness of the organised crime provision. Section 11 defines organised crime as an unlawful activity by a person or a group of persons indulging in unfair means in collusion and conspiracy, to pursue or promote a shared interest for wrongful gain in respect of a public examination, and it punishes that offence with imprisonment of not less than five years extending to ten, and a fine that shall not be less than Rs 1 crore, adding attachment and forfeiture of an institution's property and recovery of the examination's proportionate cost where an institution is the offender. The second feature is the shape of the service provider's exposure: the firm itself faces money and exclusion, not imprisonment, since a firm cannot be imprisoned, while the human beings who directed it face prison where their consent or connivance is proved, subject to a genuine due diligence defence. The third feature is the asymmetry the whole controversy is about: the one row in which the Act does nothing is the candidate's, and the consequences that do reach the candidate arrive from the authority's administrative powers and from other statutes, including state statutes considerably harsher than this one.

## Prosecuting under the Act: what the state must prove

A punishment section is a promise; a prosecution is the machinery that has to keep it, and the distance between the two is where special statutes most often fail. The Act's procedural design repays attention because it reveals what its drafters expected the typical case to look like. The classification in Section 9, cognizable, non-bailable, and non-compoundable, is the standard Indian legislative signal for organised offending, borrowed from the anti-terrorism and organised crime statutes rather than from education law. It buys the police immediate arrest and buys the prosecution protection against the quiet settlement, and it costs the accused the ordinary presumption in favour of bail. The investigation rank fixed by Section 12, not below a Deputy Superintendent of Police or an Assistant Commissioner of Police, with a power in the Centre to transfer a case to a central investigating agency, assumes cases that cross districts and institutions. Nothing in the procedural scheme contemplates the lone invigilator or the single copying candidate; the machinery is built for the network case, which is coherent with the Act's theory of the crime.

The evidential burden the scheme imposes is the part of the design least discussed. The base offence requires proof that the accused resorted to one of the acts in Section 3, which in a leak case means proving a chain: that this paper, or this file, passed through this person's hands for an unlawful purpose. The organised crime offence requires a further layer, proof of collusion and conspiracy directed at a shared interest for wrongful gain, which is a fact about the relationships among the accused and not only about their individual acts, and which will ordinarily have to be built from communications records, money trails, and the testimony of participants turned approvers. The service provider offences require the prosecution to establish the firm's engagement and the act or omission, and the personal liability of directors requires the further fact of consent or connivance, established during the investigation, against which the accused may plead absence of knowledge and the exercise of all due diligence. Each layer is a point at which a prosecution can fail honestly, and the experience of comparable statutes suggests that the layers, rather than the penalty ceilings, will determine the Act's conviction record. A mandatory minimum of five years for organised crime also has a predictable judicial consequence that drafters of Indian special statutes keep rediscovering: where the sentence cannot be adjusted to the strength of the evidence or the marginality of the accused's role, courts scrutinise the evidence harder, bail jurisprudence hardens in compensation, and marginal participants are acquitted or not charged, so that severity at the top of the ladder can coexist with impunity at its edges. None of this is an argument against the Act's existence. It is an argument that its effectiveness will be decided in chargesheets and courtrooms over years, by the quality of investigations conducted at the rank Section 12 prescribes, and that the public scoreboard for the statute should be convictions sustained on appeal rather than arrests announced. The 2024 prosecutions offer a preview of the difficulty, conducted as they were under the general law: a multi-state investigation, arrests across the leak chain, and an evidentiary record that the Supreme Court itself had to test against performance data before it could characterise the breach. That is the terrain on which Section 11 will have to operate, and it is harder terrain than the punishment section suggests.

Detection is the terrain's hardest feature, and the Act's treatment of it is revealing. A question paper leak is a crime with no complaining victim at the moment it occurs: the seller and the buyer are both satisfied, the honest candidates do not know, and the authority learns of the compromise, in the typical case, from a paper circulating on a messaging platform or from a journalist or a rival candidate after the event. Every successful prosecution therefore begins with an informant, a defector from the network, a digital trail carelessly left, or a candidate who refuses an offer and reports it. The Act builds one detection channel of its own, the service provider's duty in Section 8 to report incidents of unfair means, backed by an offence for failure to report; it is a sensible provision aimed at the actor best placed to see the compromise early. Beyond that channel, the statute is silent in a way its state counterparts are not always silent: it contains no protection for an informant, no reward, no witness protection scheme of its own, and no duty on the examination authority to act on a report within any period or to tell candidates that a compromise has been reported. The candidate who is offered a paper and reports the offer, the single most valuable actor in the entire enforcement scheme, receives from this statute neither an assurance of anonymity nor an acknowledgement that her report matters. A legislature designing for detection rather than for punishment would have started with her. The omission does not make the Act's offences unenforceable, since the ordinary machinery of informants and approvers remains available, but it marks the limit of the statute's imagination: it is a law about what happens to the network after it is found, written by a system that has historically found networks late, by accident, or not at all.

## The firms in the middle: outsourcing and the debarment sanction

Between the authority and the candidate stands an industry that the debate rarely names and the Act places at the centre of its scheme: the private examination services business. A national entrance examination is not conducted by the authority whose name is on the admit card in the way a school examination is conducted by a school. The application portal, the question paper's digital custody, the centre network, the invigilation logistics, the scanning of answer sheets, and the processing of results are performed, in whole or in part, by companies engaged for the purpose, which in turn subcontract and staff at a scale that mirrors the examination itself. The National Testing Agency's model made this structure famous, since the agency's own permanent establishment is small in relation to the millions of candidates it examines, and its operations run through vendors; but the pattern is general across the Schedule's authorities and across the state commissions, because no public body maintains a standing army of printers, drivers, centre staff, and software engineers for peaks that arrive a few times a year. Outsourcing is therefore not a scandal in itself; it is the operating model of mass examination in India, and any serious law of examination malpractice has to decide what the model means for liability.

The Act's decision is the boldest in its text. The service provider is brought inside the criminal scheme as a principal actor: it carries a duty to report malpractice, a prohibition on shifting centres, a fine of up to one crore rupees, recovery of the examination's proportionate cost, and debarment from conducting public examinations for four years, while its directors and senior managers carry personal imprisonment where consent or connivance is established. Of these sanctions, the one least discussed is the one a firm will fear most. A fine of one crore rupees is a cost that a large vendor can price; debarment for four years is exclusion from the market that constitutes the firm's business, in a sector where public examinations are the dominant buyer and where a debarred firm's centres, staff, and contracts migrate to competitors it cannot recover them from. Debarment, unlike the fine, cannot be insured against or absorbed, and unlike imprisonment it requires no individual's guilt to destroy the enterprise. The four-year term is also the provision through which the Act will speak to firms that are never prosecuted: a rational vendor reading Section 10(2) prices the duty to report, the custody protocols, and the vetting of its own subcontractors against the loss of the business, and the compliance departments that Indian examination vendors have built since 2024 are the statute's earliest measurable effect.

The design has two exposed edges that the first prosecutions will test. The first is reach: debarment and fine attach to the service provider, the legal person engaged, and the examination industry is adept at corporate reincarnation, through affiliates, renamed entities, and fresh incorporations staffed by the same people; whether a debarment follows the humans or dies with the company is a question the text does not answer and the rules will have to. The second edge is dependence: an authority that debars its principal technology vendor in the middle of an examination calendar may find that the sanction punishes the calendar, and therefore the candidates, as much as the firm, which gives large vendors a measure of practical immunity that the text does not intend. These are the ordinary difficulties of regulating an indispensable intermediary, and they do not diminish the design's intelligence. They mark the point at which the Act's scheme passes from the legislature's hands to the procurement system's, and they explain why the statute's treatment of firms, for all its severity on paper, will be judged by a small number of early decisions: which provider is first reported, first fined, first debarred, and whether the market believes the sanction will be used again.

## The organised crime label, and what it imports

The Act's gravest offence is not defined by an act but by a form of association, and the choice of that device deserves scrutiny because it imports into education law the conceptual machinery of organised crime legislation. Section 11 requires an unlawful activity, committed by a person or group indulging in unfair means, in collusion and conspiracy, to pursue or promote a shared interest for wrongful gain. Strip the adverbs and the elements are three: plurality or collusion, a shared interest, and wrongful gain. The definition is aimed at the leak network's business form, and it is well aimed: a solver gang is precisely a conspiracy with a shared commercial interest, and a provision that punished only the individual acts of leaking and assisting would leave the prosecution to reconstruct the enterprise out of the general conspiracy offence in every case. By naming the enterprise itself, the Act lets the prosecution charge the business as the offence, and the forfeiture and cost recovery provisions then operate on the enterprise's assets. This is the technique of the state organised crime statutes, adapted to a narrower field, and within its field it is the Act's most original move.

The breadth of the device is its corresponding risk. A shared interest for wrongful gain is a description that fits the professional gang exactly and the amateur arrangement uncomfortably well: a family that pools money to buy a paper for one candidate, a coaching teacher who leaks to his own class for reputation and fees, a group of candidates who commission a solver jointly. Each involves collusion and gain of a kind, and each therefore presents a prosecutor with the option of charging the five-year-minimum offence and a court with the task of reading it down to its intended target. Indian courts construing special statutes have historically managed this problem by insisting on the organised and continuing character of the enterprise, and the phrase "organised crime" will invite the same discipline; but the discipline will be the courts' work, not the text's, and it will take years of litigation to settle where the line lies. In the interval, the breadth has a bargaining function that should be recognised for what it is: the threat of the organised charge is leverage to turn participants into witnesses against the network, which is a legitimate investigative technique and also the standard mechanism by which special statutes produce their conviction figures against the small and their acquittals of the large. Whether Section 11 becomes the provision that dismantles leak enterprises or the provision under which the enterprises' least members are imprisoned will depend on charging practice, and charging practice is the one part of this statute that no reading of its text can predict.

## The problem the statute was answering

No honest assessment of this Act can begin with the Act. It has to begin with the scale of the system being protected and with the evidence that the protection was failing. The public examinations within the Schedule are among the largest standardised assessments conducted anywhere. The NEET-UG examination whose compromise produced the 2024 crisis was taken by more than twenty-three lakh candidates, on the figures placed before the Supreme Court, and state recruitment examinations operate at comparable scale: the Uttar Pradesh police constable recruitment examination whose leak forced its cancellation in February 2024 drew more than forty-eight lakh candidates. An examination at that scale is not an event; it is a logistical industry, with papers set, printed, stored, transported, distributed, sat, collected, scanned, and evaluated through chains of custody that pass through the hands of thousands of people and dozens of organisations, many of them private. Every link in such a chain is a point at which a paper can be photographed, a file copied, a seal broken, a solver inserted.

### How many candidates sit the examinations this law protects?

The NEET-UG alone was taken by more than twenty-three lakh candidates in 2024, and a single state police recruitment examination in Uttar Pradesh in the same season drew more than forty-eight lakh. The Schedule's examinations collectively decide the futures of a population the size of a large city every year, which is why a compromised paper is a mass event.

### What did the Indian Express investigation find about paper leaks?

An investigation by the Indian Express, discussed on the floor of the Lok Sabha during the debate on the Bill, counted forty-one instances of question paper leaks across fifteen states over five years, affecting more than 1.4 crore candidates, most of them in state recruitment examinations outside this Act.

The second element of the problem is the economics. A leaked paper for a high-stakes examination commands a price because the examination allocates scarce goods of enormous lifetime value: a government post with security and pension, a medical seat that converts into a profession. Where a single success is worth that much, the rational criminal investment in defeating the examination's security is correspondingly large, and the leak market professionalised accordingly, with brokers, solvers, safe premises, and impersonators operating as a supply chain. The Rajasthan Eligibility Examination for Teachers leak of 2021, the Vyapam admissions scandal in Madhya Pradesh that unfolded over the preceding decade, and the repeated compromise of state subordinate services examinations all displayed the same structure: not a candidate cheating, but an organisation selling certainty. General penal law could prosecute the participants after the fact, under cheating, forgery, impersonation, and conspiracy, but it offered no offence fitted to the conduct, no debarment of the complicit firm, no forfeiture aimed at the institution, and no duty on the service provider to report what it saw. That mismatch between the shape of the crime and the shape of the available law is the gap the Act was drafted to close, and any critique that ignores it is not engaging with the statute's reason for existing.

The third element is the one the critics emphasise, and it belongs in this section because it is part of the problem rather than part of the rhetoric: the examination system itself generates the risk it then asks the criminal law to suppress. Entrance testing in India has been progressively centralised, with a single agency conducting the medical entrance examination for the entire country, so that one compromised paper contaminates one national merit list rather than one state's. The agency at the centre of the 2024 controversy, the National Testing Agency, is a registered society rather than a statutory body, conducting examinations of national consequence through extensive outsourcing to private service providers for technology, logistics, and centre management. High stakes, single points of failure, long physical chains of custody, and outsourced operations: the structural description is not in dispute between the two sides of this controversy. What is in dispute is the remedy, and it is to the two remedies on offer, punishment and redesign, that the argument turns.

## How a question paper travels, and where the chain breaks

To understand why the two sides of this controversy talk past each other, it helps to follow a question paper on its journey, because the criminal and the administrative theories of the leak are descriptions of the same journey read at different points. A paper begins with setters, who draft questions under conditions of secrecy and surrender them to the authority. It is then composed, printed, packed, and sealed, frequently by a private printer engaged for the purpose. The sealed packets travel, by road and air, to storage points across the country, treasuries, bank vaults, and strongrooms, where they may rest for days. They move again to examination centres on the morning of the test, are opened in the presence of officials, are distributed to halls, and after the examination the answer sheets begin the return journey for scanning and evaluation. In a computer-based test the physical chain shrinks and a digital one replaces it: the paper exists as an encrypted file, decrypted at centres shortly before the test, and the points of vulnerability become the file's custodians, the centre's systems, and the people with credentials.

Every stage of either journey is staffed, and the staffing is the point. The setters, the printer's employees, the transport crew, the strongroom guards, the centre superintendents, the invigilators, and the technology provider's engineers are a population of thousands for a national examination, drawn substantially from outside the authority itself. A leak requires exactly one of them. The Supreme Court's hearings in the NEET-UG case were, in large part, an inquiry into this chain: the Court pressed on when the compromise occurred relative to the papers being lodged in bank vaults, on the security of the strongroom at the Hazaribagh centre from which the leak was traced, and on the conduct of the logistics by which papers reached centres. The criminal theory of the leak reads the chain as a series of opportunities for a seller, and answers with Section 3: the setter who discloses, the custodian who photographs, the broker who distributes, each commits a named offence, and the chain's every link is a potential accused. The administrative theory reads the same chain as a design problem, and answers with the number of links, the quality of custody at each, the audit trail, and the speed with which a compromise can be detected and contained. Both readings are accurate. They diverge only on the remedy, and the divergence is sharpened by a detection asymmetry that runs through the whole subject: a leak that succeeds is usually discovered, if it is discovered at all, after the examination, when the remedy of cancellation is at its most destructive, whereas a leak prevented by custody design leaves no trace and earns no one any credit. Institutions are therefore systematically rewarded for the visible instrument, prosecution after failure, over the invisible one, prevention before it, and a legislature composed of the same institutions should be expected to reach for the criminal statute first. The Act is best understood as the product of that asymmetry no less than of any theory of deterrence, which is why its defenders and its critics can both be describing it correctly when one calls it a serious law against an organised trade and the other calls it a statute that arrives after the examination is over.

## Impersonation: the fraud that needs no leak

Leaking dominates the debate because it dominates the headlines, but the examination fraud economy has a second mode that the Act must also carry, and it operates without any paper leaving custody. Impersonation substitutes a person rather than a document: a stronger candidate, a professional proxy, or in the Rajasthan recruitment scandals' notorious variant, a dummy candidate enrolled to sit in place of the real applicant, whose photograph, signature, and identity documents are manipulated across the application chain. The mode defeats a different control from the leak. It is indifferent to strongrooms and encryption, and it is defeated, where it is defeated, by identity verification: at application, at the centre gate, at the desk, and increasingly by biometric comparison across stages, so that the face that applied is the face that sat is the face that joins. The Radhakrishnan committee's emphasis on multi-stage digital authentication is directed at exactly this mode, and the Act's Section 3 covers it by name through the resolution of answers by an unauthorised person and the assistance offences, with the impersonator himself exposed to the general offence and to the penal code's personation provisions in addition.

The two modes illuminate each other. The leak attacks the paper's secrecy and scales effortlessly, one compromised file serving a market; impersonation attacks the candidate's identity and scales laboriously, one proxy serving one buyer, which is why it flourishes in recruitment examinations with localised centres and weaker verification rather than in national tests with centralised processing. A statute that treated examination fraud as synonymous with leaking would have missed half the trade; the Act's Section 3, to its credit, does not, and its inclusion of manipulation of seating, dates, and shifts recognises the third and quietest mode, the administrative fix, in which no paper leaks and no proxy sits, but the conditions are arranged, a compliant centre, a chosen shift, a doctored merit document, by someone inside the process. The taxonomy matters for the verdict on the Act: as a catalogue of the fraud economy's methods, the statute is observant and complete. The controversies that survive this section are not about what it criminalises. They are about whom it declines to, and what it declines to require, and those are the controversies to which the argument returns.

## The case for the Act, in its strongest form

The strongest case for the Act is the one its pilot made, and it deserves to be stated as Dr Jitendra Singh stated it in the Lok Sabha: there was, before this statute, no substantive central law dealing with unfair means in public examinations at all. Malpractice on an industrial scale was met with provisions written for the individual cheat and the individual forger. The Bill, he argued, was aimed at the organised groups and institutions that exploit the vulnerabilities of the examination system for monetary and wrongful gain, and its Statement of Objects and Reasons framed the objective as bringing transparency, fairness, and credibility to public examination systems and reassuring the country's youth that sincere effort would be rewarded. That framing matters to the statute's defence, because it identifies the intended beneficiary precisely: not the examination authority, whose reputation is a secondary matter, but the honest candidate, whose one asset in the competition is the integrity of the paper, and who has no remedy at all when that integrity is sold before she enters the hall.

The deterrent logic, stated at full strength, runs as follows. Leak networks are businesses. They respond to expected returns, and expected returns are a function of price, probability of detection, and penalty on conviction. The general law kept the third term low and the second term diffuse, because no offence described the business and no agency owned the investigation. The Act moves all three terms that law can move. It defines the business as a set of offences, including conspiracy at Section 4 and the use of unauthorised premises at Section 7, so that the network can be prosecuted at its joints rather than only at its retail end. It raises the penalty for the organised form to a minimum of five years and a minimum fine of Rs 1 crore, with forfeiture of institutional property, which is calibrated to the scale of the profits rather than to the scale of an individual's cheating. It makes the offences cognizable and non-bailable, so that arrest does not wait on a warrant and release does not follow as a matter of course. It fixes investigation at a senior rank and lets the Centre hand a case to a central agency. And it attacks the network's infrastructure by converting the service provider from a bystander into a regulated participant: a firm that profits from conducting examinations must report malpractice on pain of committing an offence, may not quietly relocate a centre, faces a fine of up to Rs 1 crore and recovery of the examination's cost if it offends, and is barred from the business for four years, while its directors and senior managers face prison where the offence was theirs in consent or connivance. A defender of the Act is entitled to say that this is not a statute that merely raises numbers. It reallocates risk onto every actor who was previously positioned to profit from a leak while bearing none of its legal cost.

The candidate exclusion, on the strongest version of the defence, is not a loophole but the statute's most considered choice. The candidate who buys a leaked paper is culpable, the argument runs, but she is also the least powerful participant in the transaction, frequently young, frequently acting under family and coaching pressure, and always replaceable from the network's point of view: imprisoning buyers does not dismantle a supply chain that can find other buyers, and the threat of prosecution deters precisely the reporting on which detection depends, since the first person to know of a leak is often a candidate who was offered it. Administrative sanctions, cancellation of candidature, debarment by the conducting authority, and annulment of results are severe in career terms and can be imposed without a criminal trial's delays. Several state legislatures have chosen otherwise, as the state section shows, and the contrast is a genuine one; but the central choice is a defensible theory of criminal law's proper target, not an oversight. The minister said in terms that candidates were kept outside the purview of the Act, and a Parliament that passed the Bill within days of introduction, with that statement on the record, legislated the exclusion with its eyes open.

Finally, the defence can point to the federal design. Because the Act reaches only central examinations, the Centre offered it as a model for the states, whose recruitment examinations are where the documented leak epidemic has been concentrated. A state that adopts the template gains the offence definitions, the service provider regime, and the organised crime provision without drafting from nothing. On this account the Act is a floor and a template rather than a complete answer, and it should be judged as one: the question is not whether it solves examination security, which no criminal statute could, but whether the conduct it criminalises was previously under-punished and whether its penalties are fitted to the profits of the trade. To both parts of that question, the defence answers yes, and the answer is not frivolous.

## The case against the Act, in its strongest form

The strongest case against the Act begins where the defence ends: with the admission that no criminal statute solves examination security, and with the observation that this statute does not seriously attempt the part of the problem that is solvable by law. A legislature that wished to reduce leaks had two instruments available. It could raise the price of leaking, which it did. Or it could impose duties on the institutions whose failures make leaking possible: statutory standards for the setting, printing, storage, transport, and digital handling of question papers; audit obligations; a duty to report compromises to candidates within a fixed time; consequences, personal or institutional, for an authority whose examination is compromised through its own negligence. The Act contains none of this. Its Section 8 duties fall on service providers. Its public servant provision, Section 13, classifies the authority's own people without burdening the authority. There is no security standard anywhere in its eighteen sections, no obligation on the National Testing Agency or any other authority to conduct an examination to any standard whatsoever, and no consequence in the Act for an authority under whose watch a paper leaks. The institution whose conduct is the largest single variable in whether a leak happens is the one institution the statute leaves entirely alone. Opposition members made the general form of this point during the passage debates, arguing that the Bill punished the offence after the event while leaving unreformed the system that produced it; the text bears the point out, and it is the heaviest charge the Act has to carry.

The second charge concerns the concentration that the statute leaves untouched and arguably entrenches. The risk profile of Indian entrance testing is a product of design decisions: one national agency, one paper, one day, one merit list, for examinations that allocate the most coveted opportunities in the society. A leak in such a system is not a local failure but a national one, and the remedy of multiplying penalties does nothing to reduce the blast radius. The demand that follows from this analysis is not softer punishment but different architecture: multiple sessions, larger question banks, computer-based testing with distributed papers, district-level secure centres, and the rebuilding of the testing agency as a properly staffed and accountable institution. That this is not a partisan fantasy is demonstrated by the Centre's own response to the 2024 crisis, which ran on both tracks at once: the criminal statute was commenced, and a high-level committee of experts under Dr K. Radhakrishnan, the former chairman of the Indian Space Research Organisation, was constituted by the Ministry of Education in June 2024 to recommend reforms to the examination process, to data security, and to the structure and functioning of the National Testing Agency itself. The committee reported in October 2024 with one hundred and one recommendations, covering matters such as the restructuring of the agency, secure testing infrastructure, multi-stage testing, and digital authentication of candidates. A critic is entitled to read the two responses side by side and ask why the institutional half of the answer was left to a committee's recommendations, carrying no legal force, while the punitive half was enacted as law within four days. The asymmetry is the critique in its most concrete form: punishment was made statutory and permanent; reform was made advisory and optional.

The third charge is about severity and process. The offences are non-bailable and non-compoundable across the board, including the base offence of resorting to unfair means, which carries three to five years, and the perimeter offences in Sections 4 to 8, some of which, such as a service provider's failure to report, are offences of omission by corporate employees. Mandatory minimum sentences of three and five years remove the sentencing court's ability to fit punishment to the least culpable participant in a chain, and the history of Indian special statutes teaches that severe minima combined with non-bailable classification produce long undertrial incarceration and low conviction rates rather than deterrence, because courts grow reluctant to convict on weak evidence where the sentence is inflexible, and because the investigative burden of proving an organised conspiracy to the criminal standard is heavy. The state experience, examined below, in which legislatures have competed to raise maxima to life imprisonment, suggests a ratchet in which each leak produces a harsher statute and no leak produces an audit. A legislature confident in deterrence theory should be troubled by the direction of that evidence.

The fourth charge is federal and goes to the examinations the Act cannot reach. Education sits in the Concurrent List, and recruitment to state services is the states' own affair; the documented epidemic of leaks, on the Indian Express count, ran through state examinations. The central Act therefore governs the smaller and better-resourced part of the problem and offers the larger part a model. Whether the model is the right template is itself contestable: states that legislated before the Centre, and states that legislated after it, mostly chose harsher and more candidate-inclusive designs, which suggests that the political economy of leaks pushes every legislature toward severity on examinees, and that the Centre's restraint on that single point may be the part of the model least likely to be copied. The critique, assembled, is not that the Act is wicked or useless. It is that the Act is the easiest third of a response, enacted with the greatest ceremony: it criminalises the trade, exhorts the firm, spares the candidate, and says nothing at all to the institution whose system the trade exploits.

## The economics that punishment must defeat

Deterrence is an economic claim before it is a legal one, and it should be tested as such. The leak trade sells a change in life outcomes. A government post obtained through a leaked recruitment paper carries a salary, security of tenure, a pension, and social standing across a working life; a medical seat obtained the same way carries entry to a profession whose lifetime earnings are a multiple of the median. Against a prize of that order, the price a network can charge per candidate runs, on the accounts given in successive investigations, into lakhs of rupees, and a single paper serving even a small cohort of buyers yields a sum against which the costs of the operation, a bribed custodian, a rented premises, a solver's fee, are minor. The Act's financial architecture shows that its drafters understood this arithmetic: the fine for organised crime has a floor of one crore rupees rather than a ceiling, the institution's property is forfeitable, the examination's cost is recoverable, and the firm is excluded from the market for four years. These are provisions aimed at the balance sheet, and they are the statute's most sophisticated feature.

The weakness of the arithmetic lies in its other term. Expected punishment is the penalty multiplied by the probability of suffering it, and the probability is a product of detection, prosecution, and conviction, each of which the record shows to be low and slow. Leaks are detected late or by accident; prosecutions of the pre-2024 episodes ran for years under the general law; and the conviction of a network requires proof of conspiracy among persons whose trade is concealment. A penalty of ten years discounted by a small probability yields an expected cost that a profitable network can treat as a business expense, and the rational operator's response to a higher penalty is not necessarily exit; it may be a higher price, which the value of the prize can bear, and greater investment in the custody points where detection begins. None of this refutes deterrence as a component of policy. It establishes the ordering that the critics insist on: until the probability term moves, through custody design, audit, reporting duties with teeth, and investigative capacity, increases in the penalty term purchase less than their announcement suggests. The states supply the longitudinal evidence, since Andhra Pradesh has carried three-to-seven-year mandatory terms since 1997 and its successor states' examinations were still leaking in 2023. Severity is the variable legislatures control completely and announce easily. Probability is the variable that decides outcomes and belongs to institutions. The Act of 2024 moves the first variable to its practical maximum and the second only at the margins, and that asymmetry, more than any single provision, is the ground on which its record will be judged.

## What is disputed on facts, and what the record supports

A controversy conducted at this temperature generates factual claims that must be separated from the values underneath them, because several of the loudest disputes of 2024 were, in principle, answerable. Three factual questions dominated, and the record answers each of them with reasonable clarity.

The first is whether paper leaking is a large problem or a small one inflated by coverage. The record supports large. The Indian Express investigation counted forty-one leak instances in fifteen states over five years affecting more than 1.4 crore candidates, and that count covered documented instances rather than allegations. Individual episodes corroborate the pattern at scale: a state police recruitment examination cancelled with more than forty-eight lakh candidates enrolled in Uttar Pradesh in February 2024; the Rajasthan teachers' eligibility examination of 2021 cancelled after its paper leaked; subordinate services examinations in Uttarakhand compromised in successive years. These are the figures as reported by the named sources for the periods they covered, and they are stated separately here because they count different things; merged into a single national total they would mislead. What they establish, without merger, is that leakage is recurrent, geographically dispersed, and concentrated in recruitment examinations. No serious participant in the debate denies this, and it is the factual foundation on which the Act's defenders are entitled to stand.

The second factual question is whether the NEET-UG examination of 2024 was systemically compromised. Here the record supports a narrower answer than either camp's rhetoric. That a leak occurred was established: the Central Bureau of Investigation's investigation identified beneficiaries at Patna in Bihar and Hazaribagh in Jharkhand, put before the Supreme Court as one hundred and fifty-five students. That the compromise extended across the examination was not established on the material before the Court, which examined the data, including the distribution of high scores, and concluded that there was no material to show a systemic breach of the sanctity of the examination. The episode of the compensatory marks, under which one thousand five hundred and sixty-three candidates had been awarded grace marks for lost time and the results were thrown into confusion, was an administrative failure of a different kind, and it is a factual error, common in the debate, to treat it as part of the leak. The leak was real and localised; the grading controversy was real and national; the claim that the entire examination was vitiated was the proposition the evidence did not sustain. Each side of the public argument tended to keep only the half of that sentence that suited it.

The third factual question is the one this article opened with: whether the new Act governed the 2024 examinations. It did not, and the point is not contestable. The examinations were held on 5 May and 18 June 2024; the Act commenced on 21 June 2024; Article 20(1) of the Constitution forbids conviction except under a law in force at the time of the act. Reporting and commentary that described the NEET accused as facing the new Act's penalties were simply wrong, and the prosecutions proceeded under the general penal law and, where applicable, state law. On the factual questions, then, the record supports the defenders on the scale of the disease, supports neither camp's full account of the 2024 examination, and supports the critics on the point of timing, which was a fact about the government's own notification and not about the statute's drafting.

The executive's own conduct in those weeks is also part of the factual record, and it supports the institutional reading of the crisis over the purely criminal one. Alongside commencing the statute, the government handed the NEET-UG investigation to the Central Bureau of Investigation, replaced the Director General of the National Testing Agency in June 2024, withdrew the compensatory marks and put the affected candidates to a re-test on the agency's revised position, and constituted the Radhakrishnan committee to redesign the examination system. Each of those responses is an administrative act directed at the institution or the investigation, and none of them is an exercise of the new Act, which could not be exercised. A government that believed the crisis was principally a deficit of punishment had the statute ready from February; its actual response, when the crisis came, was to change the management, commission the redesign, and call in the investigators, which is as close as governments come to conceding where they think the failure lay.

## Two cancellations, two logics

The summer of 2024 supplied a natural experiment in how compromised examinations are handled, because its two crises were resolved by opposite routes, and the contrast exposes what the law does and does not govern. The UGC-NET was cancelled by the executive within a day of being held, on inputs received about a possible compromise, before results, before litigation, and before the extent of any leak was publicly established. The NEET-UG was not cancelled by anyone: the executive defended it, the candidates litigated, and the Supreme Court preserved it after examining the evidence, on the ground that the breach was localised and its beneficiaries identifiable. In the first case the decision was administrative, precautionary, and taken at the price of the entire cohort's examination on the strength of an input the public has never fully seen tested. In the second the decision was judicial, evidence-based, and taken at the price of leaving a compromised cohort's results standing, with the beneficiaries to be separated by the investigation. Neither route is governed by the Public Examinations Act. The Act says nothing about when an examination should be cancelled, what threshold of compromise requires it, who decides, or what is owed to the cohort when it happens; those questions remain where they have always been, in the discretion of the conducting authority disciplined after the fact by judicial review under the framework the courts have built. The point is worth dwelling on because cancellation, not prosecution, is the consequence of a leak that candidates actually experience, and it is the consequence about which the statute is wholly silent. A candidate whose examination is cancelled on an untested input and a candidate whose leaked examination is preserved on tested evidence have both been governed, in the decisive respect, by a body of law this Act does not contain. The statute criminalises the cause of the crisis and leaves the crisis itself to improvisation, which is a defensible allocation only if the improvisation is principled, and the two logics of June and July 2024, precaution by the ministry and proof by the court, point in opposite directions on the question that matters most to the cohort: how certain must the state be, before it takes the year away?

## The judicial position: cancellation, and the limits of the remedy

Courts enter this controversy at two points: as the forum in which every major leak dispute is finally resolved, and as the authors of the framework within which those disputes are decided. That framework, built across decades of examination litigation and restated in the NEET-UG case, distinguishes sharply between two situations. Where malpractice is localised and the beneficiaries are identifiable, the proportionate remedy is to proceed against the beneficiaries and preserve the examination for the honest majority. Where the compromise is systemic, so that the honest and the dishonest can no longer be separated, the examination itself is vitiated and cancellation with a fresh test is the only remedy that does not reward the fraud. Courts have been consistently reluctant to order the second remedy, and the reluctance is principled rather than timid: cancellation visits the consequences of the crime on its victims, destroys a year of preparation for lakhs of candidates who did nothing wrong, disrupts the academic and admission calendar with cascading effects, and is justified only where the evidence shows the taint cannot be isolated. The burden, in practice, lies on those who seek cancellation to demonstrate systemic breach, and it is a burden about evidence and data, not about the gravity of allegations.

The NEET-UG litigation of 2024 applied that framework under maximum pressure. Petitions sought cancellation of the examination held on 5 May 2024 and a re-examination. A bench of the Supreme Court comprising the Chief Justice, Dr D. Y. Chandrachud, Justice J. B. Pardiwala, and Justice Manoj Misra heard the matter over several days, took the investigation's status reports and the performance data onto the record, and on 23 July 2024 declined to cancel the examination or to order a re-test, holding that there was an absence of material to show that the result was vitiated or that there had been a systemic breach, that the leak was confined to Patna and Hazaribagh, and that a fresh examination would carry serious consequences for more than twenty-three lakh students. Students with individual grievances were left free to pursue their own remedies. In its reasoned judgment delivered on 2 August 2024, the Court widened the lens from the dispute to the system: it recorded the errors in the agency's handling of the examination, and it enlarged the remit of the committee of experts the government had constituted under Dr K. Radhakrishnan, directing attention to the evaluation process, standard operating procedures, the allotment of examination centres, identity checks, closed-circuit monitoring, the security of the logistics chain, and a proper grievance redressal mechanism. The litigation therefore ended with the examination preserved and the institution instructed, which is the judicial position in this controversy in miniature: the Court would not destroy the examination on the evidence, and it would not pretend that the evidence disclosed a healthy system.

### What did the Supreme Court criticise about the National Testing Agency itself?

In its reasoned judgment the Court said the agency must avoid the flip-flops that had marked its handling of the examination, pointing to the security of strongrooms where question papers were stored and to the grant of compensatory marks that had distorted the results, and it directed that the failings be identified and rectified rather than repeated.

For the purposes of this article, the judgment's significance is its allocation of responsibility. The Court treated the leak as a crime committed by identifiable persons against the examination, which is the criminal law's frame and the new Act's frame. But it treated the conditions that made the crime consequential as failures of the examination authority, to be answered by procedure, audit, and institutional reform, which is the critics' frame. The judiciary, in other words, declined to choose between the two theories of the problem and instead assigned each to its proper instrument: prosecution for the network, redesign for the system. A statute that performs only the first of those assignments is, on the Court's own analysis, half a response, and the half that was already available in substance under the general law, albeit in less tailored form.

## The other response: the committee and the redesign agenda

Set against the statute, and inseparable from any verdict on it, is the reform programme that the same government commissioned from the Radhakrishnan committee, because the committee's report is the authoritative statement of what the administrative theory of the problem requires. Its recommendations, one hundred and one in number and submitted in October 2024, begin from the structure of the testing agency rather than from the criminal law. The National Testing Agency, the committee proposed, should be restructured under an empowered governing body with dedicated sub-committees for test audit, ethics and transparency, and for stakeholder relations; its leadership should consist of domain experts; and its work should be organised through specialised verticals rather than through generalist deputation. On the conduct of examinations, the recommendations reach every point at which the 2024 examination failed or was seen to fail: secure protocols for the setting, printing, and transportation of question papers; a testing centre allocation policy that gives candidates centres within their district of residence; secure testing infrastructure in every district, with mobile units for remote areas; coordination committees linking the agency with state and district authorities; multi-session and multi-stage testing, including for the medical entrance examination, so that no single paper carries an entire national cohort; and a digital authentication architecture, described as a Digi-Exam system on the analogy of the Digi-Yatra identity framework, verifying the candidate at application, at the test, and at admission. On the handling of failure, the committee and the Supreme Court's expanded remit converge: documented and transparent normalisation where multiple sessions are used, a grievance redressal mechanism worthy of the name, and data security treated as a first-order design requirement rather than an afterthought.

The significance of the report for this controversy is not any single recommendation. It is the demonstration, by the government's own chosen experts, of the distance between a punishment statute and a secure examination system. Nothing in the committee's programme is achievable by a court imposing a sentence; all of it is achievable, if at all, by budgets, staffing, procurement, and sustained administrative attention over years, and none of it is guaranteed by law, since the report is advisory and its implementation rests with the executive that commissioned it. The two responses of 2024 thus embody the two theories in their purest institutional forms: the statute, permanent, justiciable, and aimed at the network; the report, hortatory, unenforceable, and aimed at the system. A reader who wants to predict the integrity of the examinations of the coming decade should watch the second document's implementation record at least as closely as the first document's prosecution record, and a Parliament that wished to bind the future rather than exhort it could have enacted the committee's core institutional duties, on governance, audit, disclosure, and grievance redressal, as statutory obligations on every authority in the Schedule. That it did not is the precise point at which the controversy over this Act will still be alive when the first prosecutions under it are forgotten.

## The comparative check: aiming at the supplier

One comparison disciplines the debate about the candidate exclusion, because India is not the only system to have faced commercialised cheating, and the most instructive foreign response chose the same target as the Indian Act while rejecting its severity. Australia's contract cheating problem, in which commercial services sold completed assignments and examination assistance to university students, was met by federal legislation in 2020 that created offences of providing and advertising academic cheating services, punishable by imprisonment and heavy fines, and empowered the regulator to have cheating websites blocked. The philosophy was stated plainly by its proponents: the student who buys the service is dealt with by the institution's academic discipline, and the criminal law is reserved for the business that sells the cheating, because the business is the scalable harm and the student is its customer rather than its proprietor. The Indian Act of 2024 is built on the identical allocation, arrived at independently: administrative sanctions for the candidate, criminal sanctions for the supplier, the organiser, and the complicit firm. The comparison does not settle the Indian argument, since a school-leaving or recruitment examination allocates public goods in a way a university assignment does not, and the Indian state legislatures that punish candidates are answering a different scale of fraud. What the comparison does establish is that the Centre's allocation is a recognised legislative position with a reasoned basis, not a drafting accident or a concession extracted in debate, and that the worldwide argument in this field is genuinely between two theories of the buyer: as the demand that creates the market, to be deterred like any purchaser of contraband, or as the weakest party to the transaction, to be disciplined by the institution and left out of the criminal code. India's federal structure has, characteristically, enacted both theories at once, in different jurisdictions, for examinations taken by the same generation of candidates.

## Before the central Act: cheating under the general law

The claim that there was no law against examination fraud before 2024 is true only in the specific sense the Statement of Objects intended: there was no dedicated central statute. There was a great deal of law, and its inadequacy is a matter of record rather than rhetoric. The general penal code supplied the offences of cheating, cheating by personation, forgery, forgery for the purpose of cheating, using a forged document as genuine, destruction of evidence, and criminal conspiracy, and it was under these provisions that the largest examination scandal of the preceding decade was prosecuted. The Vyapam scandal in Madhya Pradesh, centred on the state's professional examination board, involved impersonation networks, manipulated answer sheets, and the sale of admissions and recruitments over years; it produced arrests of officials, middlemen, and beneficiaries on a large scale, and in 2015 the Supreme Court transferred the investigation to the Central Bureau of Investigation. The episode established two things that frame the 2024 statute. It demonstrated that organised examination fraud could operate as an industry inside a state apparatus under the general law's nose, and it demonstrated the general law's limits as a response: the prosecutions were constructed offence by offence and accused by accused out of provisions written for individual dishonesty, with no mechanism to debar a complicit institution, forfeit the enterprise's assets, or impose duties on the organisations through which the fraud had flowed.

The pattern repeated at the Centre. In 2018, allegations that the Staff Selection Commission's combined graduate level examination paper had leaked produced sustained protests by aspirants in Delhi and a Central Bureau of Investigation inquiry, conducted again under the general penal provisions, into a recruitment examination run by one of the very authorities the 2024 Schedule would later name. Between 2021 and 2023 the state recruitment examinations supplied a drumbeat: the Rajasthan teachers' eligibility examination of 2021 cancelled after its leak and investigated by the state's special operations group; the Uttarakhand subordinate services examinations compromised in successive cycles; the Telangana State Public Service Commission's 2023 examination cancelled after a question paper leak traced to persons with access to the commission's own systems. Each episode was investigated, where it was investigated, under cheating, forgery, conspiracy, and information technology offences, supplemented in the states that had them by their dedicated Acts. The pre-2024 legal landscape was therefore not an absence but a patchwork: severe state statutes in a minority of states, general offences everywhere, and no instrument anywhere that treated the examination supply chain, the firm, the custodian, the solver, the broker, and the institution, as the unit of liability. That patchwork is the factual predicate of the Act's defence, and it also supplies the critics' rejoinder, because the patchwork era's failures were failures of custody and investigation in cases where the substantive offences already existed and carried years of imprisonment. A new offence definition does not repair a strongroom.

## State exam malpractice laws: an older, harsher map

The central Act of 2024 was not the beginning of examination malpractice legislation in India, and in several respects it was not even the frontier. Because state examinations sit outside the central Schedule, a candidate's legal exposure depends entirely on which examination the candidate sat, a consequence of the federal distribution of education and public employment examined in [the working of the Concurrent List in education](/2015/05/01/education-concurrent-list-federalism/). States have legislated on this subject for more than four decades, and the arc of their statutes tells the deterrent story in its purest form: each generation of law is harsher than the last, and the leaks continued through all of them.

The oldest stratum is modest to the point of quaintness. The Maharashtra Prevention of Malpractices at University, Board and Other Specified Examinations Act, 1982, among the earliest of these statutes, is aimed at university and school board examinations. A paper setter who supplies the paper he has set faces imprisonment of up to one year or a fine of up to one thousand rupees; a person entrusted with printing who does the same faces the same; supplying a question paper before the examination carries up to one year; and the candidate-facing offence, copying or impersonating at an examination, carries imprisonment of up to six months or a fine of up to five hundred rupees. The fines were small even in 1982 and the statute reads as a disciplinary code with criminal edges. The Andhra Pradesh Public Examinations (Prevention of Malpractices and Unfair Means) Act, 1997 marks the escalation: contravention of its prohibitions on leakage, unauthorised disclosure, and malpractice carries imprisonment of not less than three years and up to seven, with a fine, rising to between five and ten years where the offence is committed with preparation to cause hurt, and the Act adds punishment for an official's wilful neglect of examination duties and forfeiture of property used in or acquired through the offence. By 1997, in other words, a state statute already carried mandatory minimum imprisonment in the same range the Centre would adopt in 2024, which complicates any claim that the central Act's severity was unprecedented. What was new in 2024 was the organised crime architecture and the service provider regime, not the discovery of prison as an instrument.

### What is a solver gang?

A solver gang is the organised form of examination fraud: a network that obtains the question paper in advance and supplies answers to paying candidates during the examination, using impersonators, dictated solutions, or devices, and it is the actor the statutes are written against. Uttar Pradesh's Act names the solver gang and punishes its repeat offences with life imprisonment.

The recruitment statutes of the last several years then compete in severity. The Rajasthan Public Examination (Measures for Prevention of Unfair Means in Recruitment) Act, 2022, passed after the leak that destroyed the state's teachers' eligibility examination of 2021, divides its penalties in a way the Centre declined to copy: an examinee who indulges in unfair means faces imprisonment of up to three years and a fine of not less than one lakh rupees, while any person who leaks, conspires to leak, or abets the offence faces imprisonment of five to ten years and a fine of between ten lakh and ten crore rupees, with attachment and confiscation of property and a two-year debarment of a convicted examinee. In 2023 the state's Chief Minister, Ashok Gehlot, announced that the government would bring a further Bill raising the maximum for paper leaks to life imprisonment; the announcement is recorded here as an announcement, and it captures the direction of political travel even where the traveller changes. Uttarakhand, after successive compromises of its subordinate services examinations and street protests in Dehradun in February 2023, legislated by ordinance in that month and then by Act: the Uttarakhand Competitive Examination (Measures for Control and Prevention of Unfair Means in Recruitment) Act, 2023 provides life imprisonment and a fine of up to ten crore rupees for those involved in leaking and organised unfair means, including printing presses, service providers, management bodies, and coaching institutions; a candidate who cheats faces three years and a fine of not less than five lakh rupees, rising on a second conviction to ten years and a fine of not less than ten lakh rupees, with debarment from competitive examinations for ten years on conviction; the offences are cognizable, non-bailable, and non-compoundable, and property acquired through unfair means is liable to confiscation. Gujarat's Public Examination (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2023, passed unanimously after the leak of a panchayat junior clerk examination paper, grades its offences in the same escalating pattern: an examinee who indulges in unfair means faces imprisonment of up to three years and a fine of not less than one lakh rupees; the general offence carries five to ten years and a fine of ten lakh to one crore rupees; organised crime in conspiracy carries seven to ten years and a fine of not less than one crore rupees, with attachment of assets; a convicted examinee is debarred for two years; and an institution found guilty must bear the whole cost and expenditure of the examination and stands banned. Jharkhand enacted its own competitive examinations statute in 2023 on a similar pattern. Uttar Pradesh, after the cancellation of its police constable recruitment examination in February 2024, legislated first by ordinance and then through the Uttar Pradesh Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024, whose scheme names the solver gang as a distinct offender: a solver gang faces seven to fourteen years and a fine of ten to twenty-five lakh rupees, a repeat offence by a solver gang carries life imprisonment and a fine of fifty lakh to one crore rupees, threatening or obstructing those conducting an examination for gain carries life imprisonment, and an official who wilfully neglects examination duties so that a paper leaks faces two to seven years.

Read together, the state map carries three lessons the central debate tends to miss. The first is that severity has been tried, at scale and for decades, in the very examinations where leaks are most frequent, and the correlation the deterrence theory predicts, harsher statute followed by quieter examinations, is not visible in the record: Maharashtra's gentle fines and Uttarakhand's life imprisonment bracket forty years of continuous leakage. That is not proof that penalties do nothing; it is proof that penalties alone do not do the thing asked of them, since the binding constraint appears to be detection and institutional integrity rather than the number printed in the punishment section. The second lesson is the candidate question. The Centre's exclusion of candidates is a minority position among Indian legislatures: Rajasthan, Uttarakhand, and the older Andhra Pradesh scheme all punish the examinee's own cheating criminally, and a candidate in a state recruitment examination therefore faces a materially different law from a candidate in a central one, arising from the same conduct in the same country. The third lesson is federal in the plain sense: the epidemic, on the best count available, is a state epidemic, and the central statute, however well drafted, is a response addressed to the smaller share of it, offered to the states as a model at the moment the states were outbidding it in severity.

## The gap at the centre of the design

There remains the criticism that this article regards as the strongest available against the Act, and it is the one the passage debate discussed least: the statute imposes no obligation of any kind on the examination authority whose examination is compromised. Trace the duties in the text. The service provider must report malpractice, must not shift centres, must pay, must submit to debarment. The director must exercise diligence or answer for connivance. The gang must fear forfeiture. The official who personally tampers or deliberately violates norms commits the general offence, as any person does. But the authority as an institution, the body that chose the printer, designed the chain of custody, appointed the centre, set the timetable, and announced the results, owes the statute nothing. No standard of examination security is prescribed for it to meet. No audit of its processes is required. No consequence follows in the Act when its examination leaks, not even a mandatory inquiry whose report must be published. The Radhakrishnan committee's recommendations, which addressed precisely this territory, were addressed to executive discretion rather than enacted as obligations, and the Supreme Court's directions in the NEET-UG judgment were directions in a case, binding for that examination's reform rather than a general statutory code for every authority in the Schedule.

The defenders of the design have an answer, and it should be recorded fairly: examination authorities are public bodies answerable through administrative law, departmental accountability, judicial review, and ultimately to Parliament and the legislatures, and a criminal statute is a strange vehicle for setting process standards, which belong in rules, manuals, and contracts. The answer is respectable and incomplete. It is respectable because the criminal law genuinely is a poor instrument for specifying how question papers should be printed. It is incomplete because the statute was not confined to the criminal law's natural territory: it legislated in detail for the private firm's conduct, including an omission offence for failing to report, and it could as easily have imposed on authorities the two or three institutional duties that are legal in form rather than technical in content, such as a duty to notify candidates of a confirmed compromise within a fixed period, a duty to commission and publish an independent inquiry after any cancellation for leakage, and a duty to lay an annual examination integrity report before Parliament. The absence of any such provision is a choice, not a necessity of genre, and it produces the statute's defining asymmetry: every private participant in a compromised examination faces the Act, and the public participant whose system was compromised faces only criticism. For the candidate deciding what this law means in practice, the general position set out in this article is legal information drawn from the statute and the public record, and no substitute for advice on an individual case, where the applicable law will turn on which examination was sat and when the conduct occurred. Candidates preparing for the central examinations are better served by the cluster's study treatment in [the education laws examination guide](/2015/05/15/education-laws-exam-guide/), and those who need to keep the actor table and the penalty scheme straight while revising from a deck of slides can open the file in the browser through [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) rather than hunting for the right software.

## What the law means for the candidate in the hall

The reader with the most at stake in this statute is the one least addressed by it, and a controversy article owes that reader a plain account of her position. A candidate in a central examination who is caught copying, carrying a device, or using an unauthorised aid is dealt with by the conducting authority under its own rules: the candidature may be cancelled, the result withheld or annulled, and the candidate debarred from the authority's examinations for a period that the rules prescribe, consequences that can end a career plan as surely as a conviction. What the candidate does not face, by the central Act's design, is a prosecution under that Act for the act of cheating as a candidate. Two boundaries of that protection deserve to be understood rather than assumed. The first is that the protection describes the candidate as candidate. The offence definitions in Section 3 describe conduct, leaking, impersonating, tampering, selling, without naming the status of the person who does it, and a candidate who steps out of the candidate's role and into the network's, by impersonating another, by passing a paper onward for gain, or by serving as the gang's instrument inside the hall, presents a harder question than the minister's assurance answers; that boundary has not been tested in a reported prosecution and should not be treated as settled in the candidate's favour. The second boundary is jurisdictional and is tested every season: the same young person who sits a central entrance test in one month may sit a state recruitment examination in another, and in the state examination the protection vanishes. Under the Rajasthan and Uttarakhand statutes the examinee's own unfair means is a criminal offence carrying imprisonment, and under the older Maharashtra statute even hall copying is punishable. The candidate's true legal position is therefore a function of the examination calendar, and the single most practical sentence in this article is the least dramatic: before assuming that cheating is an administrative matter, a candidate should know which legislature's law governs the examination in front of her, because the answer changes at the state line.

The candidate's other exposure under this body of law is not as an offender at all but as a victim whose remedies are thin. When an examination is cancelled for leakage, the honest candidate's loss, a year of preparation, fees, travel, and a place in an age-limited queue, is compensated by nothing in any of these statutes; the re-examination is treated as the remedy, and its costs in time are borne by the candidates. When an examination is not cancelled, the honest candidate competes against the beneficiaries of the leak unless the investigation identifies them, which is why the Supreme Court's insistence in the NEET-UG case on the separability of beneficiaries mattered so much to the lakhs who had no part in the fraud. The statutes in this field distribute their solicitude unevenly: elaborate protection of the examination as an institution, severe attention to the network that attacks it, administrative discipline for the candidate who cheats, and near silence on the candidate who is cheated. A reform programme that took the honest candidate as its centre would begin from that silence, with enforceable duties of timely disclosure when a compromise is detected, compensation or fee protection on cancellation, and a statutory grievance mechanism of the kind the Supreme Court directed the expert committee to design. That none of this is in the Act of 2024 is the final measure of the distance between the statute's promise to reassure the youth of the country and the instrument it actually built.

## What is settled, what is contested on facts, what is contested on values

Template honesty requires the closing separation. Settled: the Act is Act 1 of 2024, assented to on 12 February 2024 and in force from 21 June 2024; it covers the five scheduled authorities and central departments; it criminalises the supply side of cheating with penalties rising to ten years and a minimum fine of one crore rupees for organised crime; it fines and debars service providers and imprisons their consenting managers; it does not punish candidates, who remain under the conducting authority's administrative regime; it did not and could not apply to the examinations of May and June 2024; and it imposes no security duty on the examination authority itself. Contested on facts: the true incidence of leaks beyond the documented counts, the degree to which deterrence of this form reduces offending where detection is the weak link, and whether the 2024 NEET compromise reached beyond the two centres the investigation identified, a question the Supreme Court answered on the material before it while leaving individual grievances open. Contested on values: whether examinees should ever face prison for cheating, on which Indian legislatures are genuinely divided; whether the concentration of national testing in a single agency is a price worth paying for uniformity, the question that underlies the long-standing demand for the abolition of the national medical entrance test itself; and whether, when an examination fails, the moral claim of the honest candidate is better honoured by punishing the network that sold the paper or by rebuilding the institution that lost it. On the evidence assembled here, this article's verdict is that the Act was a necessary statute and an insufficient response: necessary because the organised leak trade had outgrown the general law and its profits demanded forfeiture-scale penalties, insufficient because its own commencement history demonstrated the truth its critics press, that the fate of an examination is decided by the competence and accountability of the institution conducting it, a subject on which the statute is silent. A law that cannot reach the leak that produced it, and does not regulate the authority that will conduct the next examination, has answered the easiest of the three questions the summer of 2024 asked. The other two remain open, and they are institutional, not criminal.

A statute of this kind also deserves a stated test by which its defenders and critics can both agree to judge it, since the alternative is an argument renewed at every leak with the same speeches. Four measures would settle most of the disagreement. The first is the prosecution record under Section 11: whether organised crime charges are brought against networks rather than only against the individuals caught holding a paper, and whether they end in convictions that survive appeal. The second is the debarment record under Section 10(2): whether the market exclusion is used against a significant provider even once, since a sanction never used deters only the cautious. The third is the implementation record of the committee's programme: whether the testing agency acquires the governing structure, the secure district infrastructure, the multi-stage design, and the grievance machinery its own experts prescribed, or whether the report joins the archive of unimplemented reform. The fourth is the incidence record in the Schedule's own examinations across successive cycles, read with the honesty this article has tried to practise about attribution, since a quiet season proves little and a compromised one, under a commenced statute with every sanction available, would prove a great deal. If the first two measures move and the third is implemented, the defenders' theory will have earned its confidence. If the penalties stand unused while the institution is left as it was, the critics' description of the Act, a severe answer to the easiest question, will have been borne out by the only evidence that finally counts.

One further measure belongs to the federal design with which the Act began. The Centre offered the statute as a model for the states, and the states' response will reveal what Indian legislatures actually believe about this subject when the examination is their own. A state that adopts the template wholesale takes the organised crime architecture, the service provider regime, and the candidate exclusion together. A state that adopts the architecture and rejects the exclusion, as the legislatures of 2022, 2023, and 2024 had already done before the model existed, declares that the Centre's most considered mercy is the one feature it does not want. The pattern of adoption is therefore a verdict in itself, delivered by the only electorate whose members also conduct examinations, and it will show whether the restraint at the heart of the central design was a principle ahead of its time or an exception that survived only because the Centre does not conduct the examinations where the leaking is worst.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Public Examinations Act 2024 make an offence?

Section 3 of the Public Examinations (Prevention of Unfair Means) Act, 2024 lists the acts that amount to unfair means, and the list is aimed at the machinery of cheating rather than copying in the hall. It covers leaking a question paper or answer key, colluding with others to leak it, unauthorised access to or possession of a question paper or optical mark reader response sheet, supplying answers or assisting a candidate during the examination, tampering with answer sheets or with documents used for shortlisting and merit lists, violating examination norms deliberately, tampering with computer networks or systems, manipulating seating, dates, or shifts, threatening or restraining persons connected with the examination, obstructing its conduct, creating fake websites, and conducting fake examinations or issuing fake admit cards and offer letters.

### Q: Which examinations does the Public Examinations Act cover?

The Act covers a public examination, meaning an examination conducted by an authority named in its Schedule or notified by the Central Government. The Schedule names the Union Public Service Commission, the Staff Selection Commission, the Railway Recruitment Boards, the Institute of Banking Personnel Selection, and the National Testing Agency, together with ministries and departments of the Central Government and their attached and subordinate offices recruiting staff. Through the National Testing Agency it reaches examinations such as the national medical and engineering entrance tests and the common university entrance test. It does not cover examinations conducted by state governments, state public service commissions, school boards, or universities acting on their own, which are governed by state law or by those bodies' own rules.

### Q: Are candidates punished under the Public Examinations Act?

No. The exclusion of candidates is a deliberate feature of the design. The Statement of Objects and Reasons recorded that a candidate shall not be liable for action within the purview of the Act and shall continue to be covered under the administrative provisions of the concerned public examination authority, and the minister piloting the Bill stated the same position in the House. A candidate implicated in malpractice therefore faces the authority's own sanctions, such as cancellation of the candidature or the result and debarment from future examinations, but not imprisonment or a fine under this statute. That position is not universal in India: several state anti-cheating Acts do punish candidates criminally, so the answer depends on which examination was sat.

### Q: Why did the Public Examinations Act take four months to come into force?

Because Parliament left the commencement date to the executive. Section 1 of the Act provides that it comes into force on the date the Central Government appoints by notification, and no notification issued between the assent on 12 February 2024 and the third week of June 2024. The notification appointing 21 June 2024 was issued during the national controversy over the NEET-UG examination and the cancellation of the UGC-NET, roughly four months after assent. The Act itself fixes no deadline for commencement, so the delay was lawful. Its consequence was severe, however: because a criminal statute cannot operate retrospectively, the four-month gap placed the examinations whose compromise had created the demand for the law beyond the law's reach.

### Q: Did the Act apply to the 2024 paper leaks?

No. The NEET-UG examination was held on 5 May 2024 and the UGC-NET on 18 June 2024, and the Act came into force on 21 June 2024. Article 20(1) of the Constitution bars conviction for an offence except under a law in force when the act was committed, so conduct preceding the commencement date falls outside the Act however exactly it matches the Act's definitions. The investigations and prosecutions arising from the 2024 examinations therefore proceeded under the general penal law, including the provisions on cheating, forgery, impersonation, and criminal conspiracy, and under any applicable state law. The Act governs only conduct on or after 21 June 2024, which is why its practical operation belongs to examinations held after that date.

### Q: What punishment does an examination service provider face?

A service provider engaged by a public examination authority that commits an offence under the Act faces a fine of up to one crore rupees under Section 10(2), recovery from it of a proportionate cost of the examination, and debarment from being assigned any responsibility for the conduct of any public examination for a period of four years. The firm itself is punished in money and exclusion because a firm cannot be imprisoned. Separate consequences fall on the human beings who run it: where an offence is established to have been committed with the consent or connivance of a director, senior management, or the persons in charge of the firm, those individuals face imprisonment of three to ten years and a fine of one crore rupees.

### Q: What counts as organised crime under the Public Examinations Act?

Section 11 of the Act treats as organised crime an unlawful activity committed by a person or a group of persons indulging in unfair means in collusion and conspiracy, to pursue or promote a shared interest for wrongful gain in respect of a public examination. The definition therefore requires more than a single act of cheating: it requires collusion, a shared interest, and wrongful gain, which is the statutory description of the leak network or solver gang as a business. The punishment is imprisonment of not less than five years and up to ten years, with a fine that shall not be less than one crore rupees. Where an institution commits the offence, its property is liable to attachment and forfeiture, and a proportionate cost of the examination is recovered from it.

### Q: Which states have their own examination malpractice laws?

Several states legislated long before the Centre. Maharashtra's Prevention of Malpractices at University, Board and Other Specified Examinations Act dates from 1982 and carries modest penalties, including imprisonment of up to six months or a fine of up to five hundred rupees for copying or impersonation. The Andhra Pradesh Public Examinations (Prevention of Malpractices and Unfair Means) Act, 1997 prescribes imprisonment of three to seven years for its principal offences. Rajasthan enacted its recruitment examinations Act in 2022, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand legislated in 2023, and Uttar Pradesh enacted its Act in 2024 after the cancellation of a police recruitment examination. The state statutes generally cover state recruitment and board examinations, and several are harsher than the central Act, including on candidates.

### Q: What is the punishment for leaking a question paper under the Public Examinations Act?

The punishment depends on who leaks and in what form. An individual who resorts to unfair means, which includes leaking a question paper or answer key and colluding in a leak, faces imprisonment of not less than three years and up to five years, with a fine of up to ten lakh rupees, under Section 10(1). Where the leak is the work of an organised group acting in collusion for wrongful gain, Section 11 raises the punishment to imprisonment of five to ten years and a fine of not less than one crore rupees, with attachment and forfeiture of property where an institution is involved. All offences under the Act are cognizable, non-bailable, and non-compoundable, so arrest may be made without a warrant and the charge cannot be settled by compromise.

### Q: Can a director of an examination service provider be jailed under the Act?

Yes, in defined circumstances. Section 10(3) provides that where it is established during the investigation that an offence under the Act was committed with the consent or connivance of a director, senior management, or the persons in charge of the service provider firm, that individual is liable to imprisonment of not less than three years and up to ten years, together with a fine of one crore rupees. The liability is personal and fault-based rather than automatic: Section 10(4) protects a person who proves that the offence was committed without his knowledge and that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it. The firm's own liability is separate and financial, a fine of up to one crore rupees, recovery of examination costs, and four years of debarment.

### Q: Who investigates offences under the Public Examinations Act?

Section 12 of the Act reserves investigation to senior officers: an officer not below the rank of Deputy Superintendent of Police or Assistant Commissioner of Police must investigate any offence under the Act. The rank requirement reflects the statute's target, since leak networks operate across districts and involve firms, officials, and organised groups rather than a single examination hall. The same section empowers the Central Government to refer an investigation to a central investigating agency, which is the route by which a case of national dimension can be moved out of a single state's police machinery. The pattern was visible in 2024, when the Central Bureau of Investigation investigated the NEET-UG compromise under the general penal law, because the new Act did not reach that conduct.

### Q: Are offences under the Public Examinations Act bailable?

No. Section 9 of the Act declares that all offences under it shall be cognizable, non-bailable, and non-compoundable. Cognizable means the police may arrest without first obtaining a warrant. Non-bailable means an accused person has no entitlement to release on bail; bail is a matter for a court's discretion, exercised after weighing the gravity of the charge and the risk of interference with the investigation. Non-compoundable means the offence cannot be closed by a compromise between the complainant and the accused, so a settlement with the examination authority or a candidate cannot extinguish the prosecution. The classification applies to every offence in the Act, including the base offence of resorting to unfair means, which is one of the design choices its critics question on proportionality grounds.

### Q: What happens to an institution convicted of organised crime under the Act?

Section 11 of the Act attaches institutional consequences to the organised crime offence. Where an institution is found to have committed organised crime in relation to a public examination, its property is liable to attachment and forfeiture to the government, and a proportionate cost of the examination is recovered from it, in addition to the imprisonment of five to ten years and the fine of not less than one crore rupees that the offence carries for the persons responsible. The forfeiture limb is aimed at the economics of the leak trade: premises, equipment, and assets built up through the business of selling papers can be taken away, so that conviction destroys the enterprise and not merely the liberty of the individuals caught running it.

### Q: Why are candidates treated differently from leak gangs under the Act?

The difference rests on a theory about where criminal law does its work. The government argued, through the minister piloting the Bill and the Statement of Objects and Reasons, that the disease is a supply chain of leakers, brokers, solvers, and complicit firms, that candidates are the least powerful participants in that chain and are readily replaced by it, and that threatening them with prison would deter the reporting of leaks by the very people first offered them. Candidates were therefore left to the conducting authority's administrative sanctions, which can end a career without a criminal trial. The choice is contested: Rajasthan and Uttarakhand punish the cheating examinee with imprisonment, reasoning that demand sustains the market, and that a buyer who pays for a leaked paper is a participant in the fraud rather than a bystander to it.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide about cancelling the NEET-UG 2024 examination?

The Supreme Court declined to cancel the examination or to order a re-test. Petitions had challenged the NEET-UG held on 5 May 2024 after a paper leak. A bench headed by the Chief Justice, Dr D. Y. Chandrachud, with Justice J. B. Pardiwala and Justice Manoj Misra, held on 23 July 2024 that there was an absence of material to show that the result was vitiated or that there had been a systemic breach of the examination's sanctity, finding the leak confined to Patna and Hazaribagh, where the investigation identified one hundred and fifty-five beneficiaries. The Court reasoned that a fresh examination would carry serious consequences for more than twenty-three lakh students, and it left candidates with individual grievances free to pursue their own remedies. In its reasoned judgment it also directed attention to reform of the testing agency.

### Q: What was the Radhakrishnan committee on examination reform asked to do?

The Ministry of Education constituted a high-level committee of experts in June 2024, chaired by Dr K. Radhakrishnan, the former chairman of the Indian Space Research Organisation, in the aftermath of the NEET-UG controversy. Its remit was administrative rather than criminal: to recommend reforms in the mechanism of the examination process, to improve data security protocols, and to examine the structure and functioning of the National Testing Agency. The Supreme Court's reasoned judgment in the NEET-UG case enlarged that remit, pointing the committee toward evaluation processes, standard operating procedures, centre allotment, identity checks, closed-circuit monitoring, secure logistics, and grievance redressal. The committee submitted its report in October 2024 with one hundred and one recommendations, including restructuring of the agency. Its recommendations are advisory; unlike the criminal statute, they bind no one until the executive acts on them.

### Q: How do state anti-cheating laws treat candidates differently from the central Act?

The central Act excludes candidates from its punishments and reserves them to the conducting authority's administrative sanctions. The leading state statutes take the opposite position. Under the Rajasthan Act of 2022, an examinee who indulges in unfair means faces imprisonment of up to three years and a fine of not less than one lakh rupees, and a convicted examinee is debarred for two years. Under the Uttarakhand Act of 2023, a candidate who cheats faces three years of imprisonment and a fine of not less than five lakh rupees, rising on a second conviction to a minimum of ten years, with debarment from competitive examinations for ten years on conviction. Maharashtra's Act of 1982 punishes copying or impersonation by a candidate with up to six months of imprisonment. The result is that identical conduct by an examinee is an administrative matter in a central examination and a crime in a state one.

### Q: Which other laws apply to a paper leak that the Public Examinations Act does not cover?

Section 15 of the Act provides that its provisions operate in addition to other laws, so the general penal law remains available for conduct inside and outside the Act's field. A leak falling outside the Act, because the examination is a state examination or the conduct predates the commencement of 21 June 2024, is prosecuted under the provisions on cheating, cheating by personation, forgery and use of forged documents, destruction of evidence, and criminal conspiracy, in the Indian Penal Code for conduct before its replacement and in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita thereafter. Bribery of an official engages anti-corruption law, computer-based offences engage information technology law, and state anti-cheating Acts apply to state examinations. The 2024 investigations proceeded on exactly this basis, since the new Act could not reach them.

### Q: What must a service provider do when it discovers malpractice during an examination?

Section 8 of the Act converts the service provider from a contractor into a participant with legal duties. A service provider engaged for the conduct of a public examination must report an incident of unfair means or the commission of an offence to the police and to the public examination authority, and failure to report is itself an offence under the Act; where the offence is committed by the service provider, the duty to report falls on the examination authority. The same section forbids a service provider from shifting an examination centre from the centre allotted to it without the permission of the examination authority, a provision aimed at the use of unauthorised venues in organised cheating. These duties sit alongside the provider's exposure to a fine of up to one crore rupees and four years of debarment when it offends.

### Q: Can an examination authority be punished under the Act when its own examination is leaked?

Not as an institution. The Act imposes duties on service providers, penalties on offenders, and forfeiture on institutions that commit organised crime, but it prescribes no security standard that an examination authority must meet, no audit of its processes, and no consequence for the authority when an examination conducted by it is compromised. An individual official who personally leaks a paper, tampers with documents, or deliberately violates the norms falls under the general offence in Section 3 like any other person, and the authority's staff are deemed public servants by Section 13, which exposes them to anti-corruption and disciplinary law. The institutional accountability that critics demand, consequences for the body whose system failed, is the element the statute omits, and it is the largest gap in its design.
