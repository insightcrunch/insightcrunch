The choice framed as hybrid war vs open invasion risk is not an academic exercise for the people who have to sign budgets, allocate staff officers, and decide which ministry owns which problem. It is a live allocation fight, and it is genuinely hard, because the two threats sit at opposite corners of the risk grid. One arrives continuously, cheaply, and below the level that triggers any formal response. The other has not arrived at all, would cost the aggressor enormously, and would remake Europe if it did. A planner who treats these as the same problem with different intensities will misallocate. A planner who treats them as unrelated problems will be surprised twice.

![Hybrid war vs open invasion risk to Poland, a comparative threat analysis - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-18.webp)

The disagreement is real and it runs through serious institutions. One camp holds that the conventional case deserves the overwhelming share of attention, because it is the only path that produces territorial loss, mass casualties, and the collapse of the European security order, and because the sub-threshold campaign, whatever its irritations, has not cost Poland a single kilometer of ground. The other camp holds that the deniable campaign deserves the overwhelming share, because it is happening, it is producing measurable damage to infrastructure and public confidence, and no plan that waits for tanks will ever engage it. Both camps are describing something true. Neither has produced a rule for weighting the two that survives contact with a finite budget.

This article supplies that rule. It compares the two threats across four axes that actually determine planning weight, presents the comparison as a scored matrix, names the deciding factor, and then gives the judgment by case, because the correct weighting for a border guard commander is not the correct weighting for a defense minister. The claim advanced here is deliberately uncomfortable: the more likely threat is the less catastrophic one, and that inversion, not any dispute about Russian intent, is what makes the allocation problem hard.

## The Real Decision Behind Hybrid War vs Open Invasion Risk

Strip away the vocabulary and the decision is a resource-weighting question with a probability term and a consequence term that point in opposite directions. Poland has finite money, finite trained personnel, finite legislative attention, and finite alliance bandwidth. Every zloty spent hardening a substation against sabotage is a zloty not spent on a howitzer, and every hour a general staff spends on counter-subversion coordination is an hour not spent on reinforcement timelines. The allocation is real even where the two efforts overlap, because overlap is partial, not total.

The naive version of the question is which threat is bigger. That version cannot be answered, because bigness conflates two independent quantities. The sub-threshold campaign is bigger in frequency and smaller in per-event consequence. The conventional case is smaller in frequency, arguably approaching zero in any given period, and larger in consequence by orders of magnitude that no honest analyst can bound. Multiplying the two into a single expected-loss number is the standard move, and it fails here for a specific reason worth naming early: the consequence tail of an open attack is so long and so poorly bounded that the expected-value calculation becomes a function of whatever tail estimate the analyst plugs in. Expected value is a fine tool when both distributions are well behaved. It is a bad tool when one of them is a tail risk that the analyst is guessing at.

The useful version of the question is therefore not which threat is bigger but which threat should receive the marginal unit of effort, given what is already allocated. That reframing does most of the analytical work. It converts an unanswerable comparison of totals into an answerable comparison of margins, and margins are what planners actually control. A defense establishment that has already invested heavily in armored formations and air defense faces a different marginal question than one that has not. A society that has already built redundancy into its power grid faces a different marginal question than one whose grid has single points of failure.

Second, the reframing exposes the sequencing question hiding inside the allocation question. Some capabilities take a decade to build and cannot be surged. Others can be stood up in months. If the slow-building capability addresses the low-probability threat and the fast-building capability addresses the high-probability one, then the correct answer is not a static split but an ordering, and an ordering is a very different policy object than a percentage.

### Which threat is Poland actually facing right now?

Poland is facing sustained sub-threshold pressure continuously and an open conventional attack not at all. That asymmetry in occurrence is the single most reliable fact in the comparison. Everything else, including the probability of a future armed attack, is assessment rather than observation, and should be labeled as such in any honest planning document.

That distinction between observation and assessment is not pedantry. It determines how much confidence a planner is entitled to place in each side of the comparison. The sub-threshold campaign is documented in the open record: infrastructure incidents, navigation interference across the Baltic region, coordinated information operations, and engineered pressure on the eastern border are all things that have been observed, investigated, and in many cases publicly attributed by governments. The conventional case is a projection built from capability, intent inference, and historical pattern. Both are legitimate inputs to planning. They are not epistemically equivalent, and a comparison that treats them as equivalent has smuggled in a conclusion.

## Stating the Two Options Precisely

Comparisons fail most often at definition rather than at analysis. If the two options are described loosely, the verdict is decided by whichever description was drawn more generously. So both sides need pinning down before any scoring begins.

The first option, the deniable campaign, means a sustained program of actions calibrated to stay beneath the level that would trigger a collective-defense response: interference with critical infrastructure, arson and sabotage against logistics and industrial targets, navigation and communications disruption, cyber intrusion and disruption short of destructive effect, information operations aimed at fracturing public consensus and delegitimizing the government, engineered migration pressure at the frontier, recruitment of low-level proxies for deniable tasks, and the occasional airspace or maritime probe that can be explained as error. The defining property is not the tactic. It is the calibration. Each act is chosen because it imposes cost while remaining explicable as accident, criminality, or the work of actors the sponsoring state can disown. The parent treatment of this campaign as a coherent program rather than a series of incidents is developed in the cluster's assessment of [Russia's gray-zone war on Poland](/2026/12/11/russias-gray-zone-war-on-poland/), and this article does not restate it.

The second option, open attack, means the deliberate use of organized military force against Polish territory or forces at a scale and in a manner that no state could plausibly deny and that would be understood by the alliance as an armed attack. That includes a limited land grab intended to present a fait accompli as much as it includes a general offensive, because both are unambiguous and both would trigger the same legal and political machinery. The initiation logic, the phases, and the branch points belong to the scenario treatment of [how a Russia-Poland war would begin](/2025/02/21/how-a-russia-poland-war-would-begin/), which is the canonical owner of that question; the concern here is only what the option costs and produces, not how it would unfold.

Two definitional cautions matter. First, the deniable campaign is not defined by low damage. A well-executed infrastructure attack can produce economic loss exceeding that of a border skirmish. It is defined by ambiguity of authorship, which is a legal and political property rather than a physical one. Second, open attack is not defined by scale. A brigade-sized seizure of a small piece of territory is an open attack. What separates the categories is whether the responsible party can be denied, not how many units are involved.

Between the two sits a contested middle that neither definition cleanly captures: acts that are unambiguous in authorship but small in effect, or ambiguous in authorship but large in effect. That middle is where escalation actually happens, and it is treated as its own subject in the analysis of [when gray-zone becomes open war](/2027/02/11/when-gray-zone-becomes-open-war/). For scoring purposes this article treats the two options as the poles of a spectrum and acknowledges the middle rather than pretending it away.
## The Four Axes That Decide the Comparison

Any comparison needs axes chosen before the scoring, or the axes get selected to produce the answer the analyst already holds. Four are used here, and the case for each is that it independently changes what a planner should do.

Likelihood is the first axis, and it answers how often the threat arrives in a given period. It is the axis that most public discussion collapses the whole question into, and it is necessary but nowhere near sufficient, because a threat that arrives weekly and costs little is not automatically the one that deserves the budget.

Cost to the aggressor is the second axis, and it answers what the initiating state pays to use the option. This axis is doing more work than it appears to. Cost to the aggressor is the mechanism that generates the likelihood difference in the first place, which means it is the axis that tells a planner whether the likelihood picture is stable or fragile. A likelihood estimate that rests on high aggressor cost will move if that cost falls, and knowing that is worth more than the estimate itself.

Difficulty for the defender is the third axis, and it answers how hard the threat is to counter with the tools a state can realistically field. This is distinct from consequence. A threat can be devastating and yet straightforward to defend against in principle, and a threat can be minor per event and yet close to impossible to prevent.

Consequence is the fourth axis, and it answers what happens if the threat succeeds. It has to be scored in a form that captures both the magnitude and the reversibility of the outcome, because a large but recoverable loss and a large but permanent one demand different responses.

A fifth candidate axis, warning time, is deliberately excluded from the scoring, not because it is unimportant but because it is owned elsewhere in the series and because it is largely derivative of the other four for this particular comparison. Readers who want the warning picture in its own right should go to the indicator treatment rather than expecting it here.

### Which is more likely, hybrid pressure or an open attack?

Sub-threshold pressure is far more likely, and the gap is not close. The deniable campaign is ongoing and repeating, while an open conventional attack on Polish territory remains a low-probability contingency gated by alliance response. Any planning document that scores these as comparable in frequency has misread the evidence available in the open record.

## Axis One: Likelihood, and Why the Gap Is So Wide

The likelihood asymmetry is the least contested finding in this comparison, and it is worth understanding why rather than simply asserting it.

Deniable coercion is likely because it is cheap, repeatable, reversible, and unpunished in any way that meaningfully deters. An operation that fails costs the sponsor little beyond the expended asset. An operation that succeeds imposes cost on the target and generates useful information about the target's detection thresholds, attribution speed, and political tolerance. An operation that is detected and attributed produces, at worst, diplomatic expulsions and sanctions that the sponsoring state has already priced in. The payoff structure rewards volume. When a tool is cheap, deniable, and lightly punished, the rational expectation is that it gets used continuously rather than saved for a moment of crisis, and that expectation matches what the open record shows.

An open attack sits under an entirely different payoff structure. It is expensive in force, in materiel, and in the political capital required to justify it. It is irreversible in the sense that once organized military force crosses a frontier and is attributed, the initiating state cannot walk it back to the prior status quo. And it invites a response from an alliance whose combined resources dwarf the aggressor's, which is the central deterrent fact of the whole eastern-flank problem. The overall probability assessment for that contingency, with the reasoning laid out and the main dissenting views represented, belongs to the series pillar on [whether Russia will attack Poland](/2025/01/01/will-russia-attack-poland-risk-assessment/), and the figure used here is simply the qualitative judgment that it is low relative to the alternative.

There is a subtlety that the raw likelihood comparison hides, and it matters for anyone using this comparison to argue for a budget line. The likelihood of the deniable campaign is close to one for the campaign as a whole but much lower for any specific act against any specific target. A given substation, a given rail junction, a given cable, a given regional government office is unlikely to be the object of an operation in any given period, even though the probability that some target somewhere is hit approaches certainty. This is the aggregate-versus-specific distinction, and it is where a great deal of muddled resource allocation originates. Arguing that the campaign is near-certain is correct. Arguing from that to hardening every candidate target is not, because the per-target probability is low and the hardening budget is finite. Correct sub-threshold planning is portfolio planning across a target set, not point defense of each item in it.

The reverse subtlety applies to the conventional case. The probability of an armed attack is low in aggregate, but conditional on certain observable developments it rises sharply and non-linearly. A planner who carries only the unconditional number is carrying the wrong number, because the decision that matters is not what to do at baseline but what to do when the conditional probability moves. This is why the likelihood axis alone cannot settle the allocation: it scores a static picture, and the conventional threat's defining feature is that its likelihood is dynamic and its preparation lead times are long.

## Axis Two: What Each Option Costs the Aggressor

The cost-to-aggressor axis is where the comparison stops being a description and starts being an explanation.

Deniable operations are inexpensive by design. They use small numbers of people, often recruited transactionally rather than trained at length, and they exploit access that already exists rather than requiring access to be forced. They require no mobilization, no logistics tail, no reserve call-up, and no legal or constitutional preparation inside the sponsoring state. They can be conducted at a tempo that does not register as a change in the sponsor's posture, which means they generate no warning signature of the kind conventional preparation generates. And crucially, they can be abandoned without cost. If a line of effort stops producing returns, it simply stops, and nothing about the sponsor's position has been committed.

The financial dimension is almost trivial in comparison to defense budgets. The dominant cost of a sub-threshold campaign is not money but organizational attention and the slow attrition of networks as they are rolled up. That is a real constraint, and it explains why such campaigns tend to shift methods over time rather than escalate linearly, but it is nothing like the constraint that gates a conventional offensive.

Open attack costs on a different order entirely. It requires the assembly and sustainment of formations, the accumulation of munitions and fuel at scale, the movement of that mass into position where it can be observed, and the acceptance that the force committed is the force at risk. It requires political preparation of a domestic population for casualties. It requires accepting economic isolation of a severity that the sanctions imposed after 2014 and 2022 only partially previewed, since an attack on alliance territory would trigger responses that an attack on a non-member did not. And it requires accepting the possibility of direct engagement with the combined conventional forces of the alliance, which is the outcome the initiating state's own planning would be built around avoiding.

The critical analytical move is to notice the direction of causation between axes one and two. The likelihood gap is not a fact about Russian preferences that happens to hold. It is a consequence of the cost gap. Deniable action is used constantly because it is nearly free; open attack is withheld because it is ruinously expensive. That means the likelihood finding is conditional on the cost structure holding, and a planner should treat the cost structure as the thing to monitor rather than the likelihood estimate itself. What deters the expensive option, and under what conditions that deterrent weakens, is the subject of the deterrence treatment of [what deters Russia from attacking Poland](/2025/02/11/what-deters-russia-attacking-poland/), which owns that question in full.

Two things could compress the cost gap, and both are worth stating plainly because they are the conditions under which this entire comparison would need re-scoring. The first is a degradation in the credibility or capacity of the alliance response, which would lower the expected cost of open attack without changing anything about the cheap option. The second is a hardening of the target that raises the cost of deniable operations by making them harder to execute and faster to attribute. The first compresses the gap from above and is dangerous. The second compresses it from below and is desirable, since a campaign that becomes expensive and traceable loses the properties that made it attractive.
## Axis Three: How Hard Each Threat Is to Defend Against

This axis produces the counterintuitive result in the comparison, and it is the one most often scored backwards in public discussion.

Intuition says the larger threat must be the harder one. Intuition is wrong here, and the reason is structural. Defending against organized military force is a problem that states have spent centuries building institutions to solve. It is legible. It has a defined adversary, a defined geography, a defined body of doctrine, a defined organization that owns it, and a defined set of capabilities that address it. Poland knows exactly which ministry is responsible, exactly which formations would fight, exactly which allies are obligated, and exactly what would be procured with an additional appropriation. The problem is enormously expensive and genuinely difficult, but it is well posed.

Defending against a calibrated deniable campaign is not well posed. The target set is effectively the entire national infrastructure, most of which is privately owned and none of which was designed with sabotage as a governing constraint. Responsibility is distributed across police, internal security, border services, energy regulators, telecommunications operators, port authorities, municipal governments, and the armed forces, none of whom own the whole problem and several of whom have no legal mandate to act preventively. Detection depends on distinguishing a deliberate act from an accident or an ordinary crime, which is often impossible at the moment of occurrence and sometimes impossible afterwards. Attribution to a sponsoring state requires evidence that may exist only in intelligence channels that cannot be used publicly without cost. And the response, once attribution is established, has to be calibrated against an escalation risk that the responder cannot fully model.

### Which threat is harder for Poland to defend against?

The deniable campaign is harder in practice. Conventional defense is a well-posed problem with clear ownership, established doctrine, and known capabilities, however costly. Sub-threshold defense spans a vast civil target set, fragmented institutional responsibility, ambiguous detection, and contested attribution, with no single authority owning the whole problem.

That verdict comes with an important qualification, because it is easy to overread. Harder does not mean more damaging, and it does not mean more urgent. It means that the marginal unit of effort is less certain to produce a defensive result, and that the barrier to progress is organizational and legal rather than financial. This has a direct planning implication that is frequently missed: money is not the binding constraint on sub-threshold defense in the way it is on conventional defense. Legal authority, information sharing between civil and security institutions, and the willingness of private operators to accept security requirements they did not ask for are the binding constraints. A government that responds to the deniable campaign by appropriating money and declining to touch the authority and coordination problems will have spent without buying much.

There is a second asymmetry inside this axis worth separating. Conventional defense is subject to a clear threshold effect: at some level of capability, a defense becomes credible enough to deter, and below that level it does not. Sub-threshold defense has no comparable threshold. It is continuous, and improvements produce proportional rather than step-change returns. Every increment of redundancy in a power network reduces the leverage a single act of sabotage produces. Every improvement in forensic capacity shortens attribution time. Every point of public awareness reduces the yield of an information operation. This continuity is genuinely good news, because it means that partial investment in the harder problem still returns value, whereas partial investment in conventional deterrence can fail to buy deterrence at all. The mechanics of that continuous defense, and the framework for building it, belong to the resilience treatment of [building resilience to sabotage](/2027/03/11/building-resilience-to-sabotage/), which owns the policy layer this article only scores.

A third point belongs here because it is where analysts most often go wrong. The difficulty of sub-threshold defense is frequently described as a detection problem, and detection is genuinely hard, but the harder problem is response. A state that detects and attributes an act of sabotage still faces a decision with no good options: a public response invites escalation and may reveal collection methods, a private response teaches the sponsor nothing about the cost of continuing, and no response confirms that the tactic works. The response side of that problem, including the argument that exposure and attribution are themselves the sharpest available tools, is developed in the treatment of [fighting below the threshold](/2027/02/21/fighting-below-the-threshold/), and this article defers to it.

## Axis Four: Consequence, and the Problem of the Long Tail

Consequence is where the comparison reverses, and the reversal is total rather than marginal.

A successful sub-threshold campaign, sustained over a long period and executed competently, produces cumulative damage that is real and should not be minimized. It degrades infrastructure reliability and raises the cost of operating it. It consumes security and investigative resources that would otherwise be doing other work. It erodes public confidence in the state's ability to protect ordinary services. It can, in its information dimension, weaken the political consensus behind defense spending and alliance commitment, which is the effect the sponsor most wants and the hardest one to measure. It imposes friction on the movement of assistance and materiel. Over years, that accumulation matters, and a government that dismisses it as nuisance is making a real error.

But it is recoverable. Damaged infrastructure is repaired. Investigated networks are rolled up. Information operations are exposed and their yield falls. Public confidence, once shaken, can be rebuilt by visible competence. Nothing in the campaign changes borders, removes population, destroys the armed forces, or ends the state's independent existence. The damage function is cumulative and largely reversible, and that reversibility is the defining property.

A successful open attack is not recoverable in the same sense. It produces military and civilian casualties on a scale that no European state has experienced since the middle of the last century. It produces destruction of infrastructure not as a nuisance but as a campaign objective. It produces displacement of population. It puts territory at risk of loss, and territory once lost is recovered only by counteroffensive, which is itself a catastrophic undertaking. And it puts the European security order into a condition from which no return to the prior state is available regardless of the military outcome, because the demonstration that a member state's territory can be attacked changes the alliance permanently whether the attack succeeds or fails.

The consequence gap is therefore not a matter of degree. It is a matter of category. One outcome is damage to a functioning state. The other is a question about whether the state continues to function in its present form and within its present borders.

This is where the honest analyst has to acknowledge a limit. The consequence of an open attack has a tail that cannot be bounded with any confidence, because it includes escalation paths whose endpoints are not calculable. Any attempt to score consequence as a number therefore embeds an arbitrary decision about where to truncate the tail. The scoring in the matrix below handles this by scoring consequence on an ordinal scale and stating explicitly that the top of the scale is open-ended rather than pretending to a bounded estimate. That is less satisfying than a number and considerably more honest.

There is one further asymmetry in consequence that deserves its own treatment, because it cuts against the neat picture just drawn. The sub-threshold campaign's most serious potential consequence is not the direct damage it causes but its capacity to degrade the conditions that make the conventional case unlikely. An information campaign that succeeds in fracturing alliance consensus lowers the expected cost of open attack. Sabotage that succeeds in demonstrating that reinforcement routes can be disrupted lowers the expected cost of open attack. Persistent probing that establishes where response thresholds actually sit lowers the expected cost of open attack. Scored on its own terms, the deniable campaign's consequence is moderate and recoverable. Scored for its effect on the other threat's likelihood, it is more serious than its direct damage suggests. Both scores are correct, and holding them simultaneously is the analytical discipline this comparison demands.

The last row is the point of the exercise, and it is worth stating outside the table because it is the finding most likely to be lost. The correct answer to the allocation question is not a percentage. It is a division by resource type. Operational attention, institutional reform, legal authority, and coordination bandwidth should flow predominantly toward the deniable campaign, because that is where those resources produce returns and because that is the threat that is actually consuming them. Capital investment, force structure, munitions stocks, and alliance-facing diplomatic effort should flow predominantly toward the conventional case, because those resources take a decade to convert into capability and cannot be generated inside a crisis.

A planner who accepts that framing stops arguing about a ratio and starts arguing about which category a given proposal falls into, which is a far more productive argument. ## The Inverse-Severity Problem

The rule this article advances is that the more likely threat is the less catastrophic one, and that this inversion is the source of the planning difficulty rather than a curiosity alongside it. Call it the inverse-severity problem.

In most risk domains, likelihood and severity are positively correlated or at least uncorrelated, and prioritization is straightforward: address the things that are both likely and damaging first. When likelihood and severity are inversely correlated, every prioritization heuristic that works elsewhere breaks. Ranking by likelihood produces a plan that ignores the outcome that would end the state's independent existence. Ranking by severity produces a plan that ignores the pressure the state is under continuously and that will therefore be quietly abandoned by the people who have to execute it. Ranking by expected value produces whatever answer the analyst's tail estimate implies, which means the answer is determined by an assumption rather than by evidence.

The inverse-severity problem also explains why the institutional politics of this question are so bad. The two threats are naturally championed by different constituencies with different professional formations, different vocabularies, and different budget lines. The conventional constituency, built around armed forces and procurement, has the institutional weight, the established doctrine, and the capital budget. The sub-threshold constituency, built around internal security, infrastructure regulators, and civil agencies, has the operational tempo and the daily evidence but rarely the capital budget or the seat at defense planning. Because their arguments run on different axes, they do not actually engage each other. The conventional advocate says the other threat cannot take territory. The sub-threshold advocate says the other threat is not happening. Both statements are true and neither answers the other.

A further consequence deserves naming. Under inverse severity, the two errors available to a planner are not symmetric. Underinvesting in the likely threat produces continuous, visible, moderate damage that generates political pressure to correct it, which means the error is self-correcting on a timescale of a few years. Underinvesting in the rare threat produces nothing at all for years and then, if the contingency arrives, produces a failure that cannot be corrected, because the capability that was not built cannot be built inside the warning window. The likely threat punishes neglect immediately and forgivingly. The rare one punishes neglect never and then absolutely. That asymmetry in error structure, rather than any judgment about which threat is bigger, is the strongest argument for protecting the slow-building conventional capabilities from being raided to fund the urgent problem.

The mirror argument is equally valid and should be stated with the same force. A conventional force structure defends a country that has already had its infrastructure degraded, its political consensus fractured, and its logistics network mapped by an adversary who spent a decade probing it unopposed. Deterrence of the rare contingency depends in part on the state being visibly hard to coerce, and a state that absorbs sub-threshold pressure without response is demonstrating exactly the opposite. Neglecting the likely threat does not merely produce moderate damage; it degrades the conditions on which the deterrence of the severe threat rests.
## The Two Schools, Stated at Their Strongest

Serious people disagree about this weighting, and the disagreement deserves to be represented in its strongest form rather than in the caricature each side holds of the other.

The conventional-primacy school argues as follows. Security policy exists to prevent the outcomes that cannot be recovered from, and only one of these two threats qualifies. Every other consideration is secondary to that. The deniable campaign, whatever its cumulative cost, has not removed a single Polish citizen from Polish jurisdiction, has not destroyed a division, and has not altered the map. Treating it as comparable to armed attack is a category error driven by recency and by the fact that it is the threat currently generating headlines. Furthermore, the capabilities that address armed attack take a decade or more to build, which means the decision to build them has to be made long before the contingency looks likely, and any diversion of capital toward the immediate problem is a decision to arrive at the severe contingency underprepared. The school's sharpest point is that the sub-threshold campaign is partly designed to produce exactly this diversion, so responding to it by reallocating from conventional capability is doing the sponsor's work.

The gray-zone-primacy school argues as follows. Planning has to engage the world as it is, and the world as it is contains a continuous campaign against Polish infrastructure, information space, and border integrity, and does not contain armored formations crossing the frontier. A plan oriented entirely toward a contingency that may never arrive, while the actual pressure goes underaddressed, is not prudence but displacement activity. The school notes that the conventional case is already heavily addressed by alliance posture, by Polish modernization, and by the deterrent weight of the alliance itself, which means the marginal return on further conventional investment is lower than the marginal return on the sub-threshold problem, where the baseline is much thinner. Its sharpest point is that the deniable campaign is not a lesser version of armed attack but a different mechanism for achieving similar political outcomes, and that a state coerced into strategic passivity by sustained sub-threshold pressure has lost something real without a shot being fired.

Both schools are correct in their premises and both overreach in their conclusions. The conventional school is right that the consequence gap is a category difference and right that slow capabilities must be built ahead of need. It overreaches in treating sub-threshold pressure as strategically inert, which it demonstrably is not, since its effects on consensus, on infrastructure reliability, and on the credibility of response feed directly into the deterrence of the severe contingency. The gray-zone school is right that the immediate pressure is real, right that the marginal return on the underaddressed problem is higher, and right that the sub-threshold campaign has its own theory of victory. It overreaches in implying that the conventional case can be deprioritized, since the entire reason the deniable campaign stays deniable is that the alternative is deterred, and that deterrence has to be paid for continuously.

The synthesis is not a compromise between the two. It is the recognition that they are arguing about different resource types and that the matrix above resolves the argument by separating those types rather than by splitting a percentage.

## How the Two Threats Connect

The single most consequential error in this comparison is treating the two threats as separate problems that happen to involve the same adversary. They are connected in at least four specific ways, and each connection has a planning implication.

The first connection is preparatory. Sub-threshold activity generates information that would be valuable in a conventional contingency: which infrastructure nodes are fragile, how fast the state detects intrusion, which institutions coordinate poorly under pressure, where public opinion is soft, and how long attribution takes. None of that reconnaissance value requires an intention to attack, which is why it is a poor warning indicator in isolation, but it accumulates regardless of intent and would be available if intent ever formed. The planning implication is that resilience investment made for its own sake also degrades the preparatory value of the campaign, which is a return that resilience advocates undersell.

The second connection is enabling. If a conventional contingency ever arrived, it would almost certainly open with intensified sub-threshold action rather than replace it: disruption of mobilization, interference with reinforcement movement, degradation of command and communications, and information operations aimed at delaying decision. The two are not sequential phases where one ends and the other begins. The deniable layer runs throughout and intensifies. The planning implication is that counter-sabotage and infrastructure protection are not alternatives to conventional preparation but components of it, which is the argument that most cleanly dissolves the false choice.

The third connection is threshold-testing. Persistent probing establishes empirically where the alliance's response line actually sits, as opposed to where it is declared to sit. Every act that produces no response moves the perceived line, and the perceived line is what the sponsor plans against. This is the mechanism by which sustained sub-threshold activity can lower the expected cost of the severe option, and it is the strongest available argument against absorbing pressure without response. Where exactly that line sits and what would move it across into open conflict is the subject of the threshold analysis elsewhere in this cluster, and it is not re-litigated here.

The fourth connection is substitutive. Deniable coercion is in part a substitute for open attack: it is what a state does when it wants to impose cost and shape outcomes but has judged the conventional option too expensive. This has a genuinely uncomfortable implication. Successful deterrence of the severe threat does not eliminate the pressure; it displaces the pressure into the channel that remains available. A state that hardens itself perfectly against armed attack and not at all against deniable coercion has not reduced the total pressure it faces. It has channeled all of it into the undefended lane. The corollary is equally important and less often stated: raising the cost of the deniable lane does not simply reduce total pressure either, since it removes the cheap substitute and marginally raises the relative attractiveness of the expensive one. That effect is small, because the cost gap is very large, but it is not zero, and it is the reason that resilience work should be paired with visible conventional deterrence rather than substituted for it.

Taken together, these four connections support a claim stronger than the usual observation that the threats exist on a spectrum. They exist in a feedback relationship. The deniable campaign shapes the conditions that determine the likelihood of the severe contingency, and the credibility of defense against the severe contingency shapes how much the sponsor relies on the deniable channel. Neither can be planned in isolation without producing a systematically wrong answer.
## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The verdict is that neither threat wins the allocation outright, and that the question of which is more important is malformed. What decides the allocation is not relative importance but a property neither axis in the conventional framing captures: the speed at which the relevant capability can be built.

That is the deciding factor. Capabilities that can be generated quickly should be funded against the threat that is arriving now. Capabilities that take a decade to generate should be funded against the threat that is not arriving now, because that is the only window in which they can be funded at all. Everything else in the comparison, including the likelihood gap and the consequence gap, is subordinate to this, because build time is what converts a planning judgment into either a capability or a regret.

The reasoning runs as follows. Suppose a planner is wrong about the likelihood picture and the conventional contingency is more probable than assessed. If capital investment has continued in force structure, munitions stocks, air and missile defense, and the enablers that make reinforcement work, the error is survivable, because the capability exists even though the judgment was wrong. If capital was diverted to the immediate problem, the error is not survivable, because a decade of armored and enabling capability cannot be reconstituted in a warning window measured in weeks or months.

Now suppose the planner is wrong in the other direction and the deniable campaign is more damaging than assessed. If operational attention, legal authority, coordination structures, and resilience investment have flowed toward that problem, the state absorbs the campaign with degraded but functioning services. If they have not, the state suffers cumulative damage, and then corrects, because the corrective measures are fast to build. The error is painful and it is recoverable.

The asymmetry in the recoverability of errors, and not the asymmetry in the threats themselves, is what settles the allocation. It is also what makes the resource-type split in the matrix the right structure rather than an evasion of the percentage question. A percentage split treats all resources as fungible. They are not. A euro of capital equipment and an hour of interagency coordination are not exchangeable inputs, and pretending they are is what produces the sterile argument between the two schools.

### Where should Poland concentrate its effort?

Poland should concentrate operational attention, legal reform, and interagency coordination on sub-threshold pressure, and concentrate capital investment, force structure, and alliance-facing effort on the conventional contingency. The split is by resource type rather than percentage, because build time, not relative importance, determines which resource must be committed early.

## The Judgment by Case: Different Readers, Different Weights

A single recommended weighting is useless to most readers because most readers control only one category of resource. The judgment therefore has to be given by case.

For a defense minister or a general staff, the weighting should lean toward the conventional contingency, because that is the resource category they control and because it is the category with the long build time. The specific discipline required is protecting the slow-building lines from being raided to fund visible responses to recent incidents. That temptation is real, since incidents generate political demand for immediate action and force structure generates no headlines. The minister's contribution to the sub-threshold problem is mostly not money but advocacy for the legal and coordination reforms that sit outside the defense budget entirely.

For an interior ministry, internal security service, or border authority, the weighting should lean heavily toward the deniable campaign, because that is both the threat they face daily and the one their tools address. The discipline required here is resisting the framing that their work is a lesser adjunct to real defense. It is not, and the argument that it is not runs through the connection points above rather than through any claim about relative severity.

For an infrastructure operator, whether in energy, telecommunications, rail, or ports, the weighting should be almost entirely toward the deniable campaign, with one qualification. Hardening measures should be specified with wartime survivability in mind rather than only sabotage resistance, because the same node that a saboteur might target is a node that would matter in a conventional contingency, and redundancy designed for one serves the other at low marginal cost. This is the single highest-return insight available to that reader.

For a legislator or a candidate, the weighting question is mostly a framing question. The useful contribution is refusing the false choice in public debate, because the false choice is what produces oscillating policy: a surge of attention to sabotage after an incident, followed by a swing back to conventional procurement after a summit, with neither line sustained. Consistency of funding across both categories is worth more than the precise ratio, and the political system is the only actor that can supply it.

For an analyst or researcher, the weighting is a research-agenda question, and the underserved area is the interaction between the two rather than either in isolation. The single-threat literatures are mature. The literature on how sub-threshold activity moves the conditional probability of the severe contingency, and on how conventional posture changes the sponsor's reliance on the deniable channel, is thin, and that is where marginal work has the most value.

For an informed citizen, the useful takeaway is a reading discipline rather than a policy position. When a public argument asserts that one of these threats is the real one and the other a distraction, that argument has almost certainly scored a single axis and generalized from it. The question worth asking of any such claim is which axis it is reasoning from and whether it has considered the others.

## The Overlap Dividend: What Buys Against Both

Because the two threats are connected rather than separate, a set of investments returns value against both, and identifying that set is the most practical output of the comparison. This is the closest thing available to a free lunch in the allocation, and it should be exhausted before any contested trade-off is made.

Infrastructure redundancy is the clearest case. A power network, a rail corridor, or a communications backbone with genuine alternative paths is harder to disrupt with a single deniable act and harder to paralyze with a conventional strike campaign. The redundancy is not threat-specific. Neither is the hardening of the nodes that carry the most load, nor the maintenance of manual and degraded-mode operating procedures for systems that normally run on automated control.

Rapid attribution capacity is the second case. Forensic capability, cross-agency information sharing, and the legal machinery to act on findings shorten the time between an act and a response. Against the deniable campaign, that directly attacks the deniability the campaign depends on. In a conventional contingency, the same capability supports the speed of decision that reinforcement timelines require, since the political decision to move forces depends on establishing what is happening and who is doing it.

Societal cohesion and information resilience is the third case. A population that can distinguish an engineered narrative from an organic one is a harder target for an information operation and a more reliable base for the mobilization and endurance that a conventional contingency would demand. Investment here returns against both threats and is among the cheapest lines available, though it is also the slowest to show effect and the hardest to measure, which is why it is chronically underfunded.

Interagency command and coordination is the fourth case, and it may be the highest-return item on the list. The structures that let civil, security, and military institutions share information and act together are exercised by the sub-threshold campaign continuously, and they are precisely the structures that would be under maximum stress in a conventional crisis. The deniable campaign therefore functions, from a defender's perspective, as a continuous live exercise of exactly the coordination machinery that a severe contingency would require. Treating it that way, as a training opportunity rather than only as a burden, converts the most irritating feature of the threat picture into a genuine asset.

The overlap dividend has a limit worth stating so the point is not overread. Heavy armored formations, long-range fires, integrated air and missile defense, and munitions stockpiles do very little against a saboteur, and counter-disinformation capacity does very little against an armored breakthrough. The overlap is real, it is larger than either school usually concedes, and it does not extend to the core of either capability set. Once the dividend is exhausted, the trade-off is genuine and the matrix above is the tool for making it.
## Why Expected-Value Arithmetic Fails on This Comparison

The standard tool for weighing a frequent minor risk against a rare severe one is expected value: multiply probability by consequence and compare the products. It is the right tool in insurance, in engineering reliability, and in most of the domains where risk management as a discipline was developed. It is the wrong tool here, and understanding why is worth the detour, because a great many planning documents reach for it reflexively and inherit its failure without noticing.

The first failure is the unbounded tail already noted. Expected value requires a consequence estimate, and the consequence of open attack includes escalation paths whose endpoints are not calculable. Any number an analyst assigns is a truncation decision dressed as a measurement, and because the probability term is small, the product is extraordinarily sensitive to where the truncation falls. Two analysts who agree on every observable fact and differ only in where they cut the tail will produce recommendations that differ by an order of magnitude. When a method's output is dominated by an unconstrained assumption, the method is not adding information; it is laundering the assumption into apparent rigor.

The second failure is that expected value assumes the outcomes are commensurable, meaning that enough small losses add up to one large one. That assumption holds for money and fails for the things at stake here. A decade of degraded infrastructure reliability and the loss of national territorial integrity are not the same kind of thing measured at different magnitudes. They are different kinds of thing, and a state's obligations toward them differ in kind as well. A finance ministry can reasonably treat a small certain loss and a large improbable one as exchangeable. A state cannot treat a repairable inconvenience and an existential outcome as exchangeable, and no amount of arithmetic makes them so.

The third failure is that expected value treats the probability term as exogenous, when in this comparison it is endogenous. The likelihood of open attack is not a fact about the world that planning responds to; it is partly a product of what the planning produces. A state that builds credible conventional defense lowers the probability it is calculating with. A state that lets the deniable campaign run unanswered raises it. Feeding an endogenous probability into a formula that assumes exogeneity produces a number that is stale the moment the resulting decisions are implemented.

The fourth failure is that expected value optimizes the average outcome, which is the correct objective when a decision-maker faces the same decision many times and can absorb variance. It is the wrong objective for a decision faced once, where the relevant question is not the average outcome but the distribution of survivable ones. A gambler who can play a thousand hands should maximize expected value. A gambler who can play once and cannot afford ruin should not. States are in the second position with respect to the severe contingency.

The alternative used in this article is error-recoverability logic, which asks not what the expected loss is but which errors can be corrected after they are discovered. That question can be answered with observable facts about build times and institutional capacity rather than with guesses about unbounded tails. It produces a recommendation that is robust to wide disagreement about probability, which is exactly the property a framework needs in a domain where the probability estimate is the most contested input. It is a weaker tool in the sense that it produces an ordering rather than an optimum. It is a stronger tool in the sense that its answer does not change when an unmeasurable assumption is adjusted.

There is one place where expected value remains useful, and it should not be discarded entirely. Within the sub-threshold problem, where events are frequent, consequences are bounded, and the decision recurs many times, the arithmetic works as designed. Deciding how much to spend hardening a class of infrastructure node against a class of deniable act is a well-behaved problem with a computable answer. The tool fails at the comparison between the two threats, not inside either of them.

## The Comparison From the Other Side of the Table

A comparison written entirely from the defender's chair misses half the structure, because the choice between the two options is made by the sponsor, not by Poland. Reconstructing that choice, carefully and without pretending to knowledge of any particular leadership's reasoning, sharpens the assessment considerably.

The sponsoring state faces its own version of the allocation problem, and its version is easier. Deniable coercion offers a favorable return: low cost, low risk of catastrophic response, useful intelligence yield, and cumulative political effect. Open attack offers a much higher potential return, since it can achieve outcomes deniable pressure cannot, at a cost that is not merely higher but potentially regime-threatening. Given that structure, the rational default is to run the cheap option continuously and hold the expensive one in reserve against a contingency in which the cost calculation changes. That is what the observable pattern shows, and it is the pattern that should be expected on the basis of the incentives rather than merely inferred from behavior.

Two implications follow, and both are uncomfortable for the defender.

The first is that the deniable campaign is not evidence of restraint. It is easy to read continuous sub-threshold activity as a substitute for attack and therefore as reassuring, on the theory that a state doing this is a state that has chosen not to do the other thing. That reading confuses the current cost calculation with a settled preference. The campaign runs because it is cheap and the alternative is expensive. Nothing in it commits the sponsor to continuing to find the alternative expensive.

The second implication is sharper. From the sponsor's side, the most valuable thing the cheap option produces may not be its direct effects at all but the information it generates about the expensive option's cost. Every probe that establishes a response threshold, every act that goes unattributed, every incident that produces consultation but no consequence is a data point refining the estimate of what an unambiguous action would cost. The defender experiences these as separate incidents to be investigated and closed. The sponsor may be experiencing them as a single continuous measurement campaign. That difference in framing is the strongest argument in the entire comparison for treating sub-threshold response as a deterrence problem rather than a policing problem.

There is a mirror-imaging caution attached to all of this, and it is a serious one. The reconstruction above assumes a rational cost-benefit calculation of the kind an analyst would perform, and states do not always decide that way. Decisions get made under distorted information, on the basis of ideological commitments, in response to internal political pressure, or by leaderships that have talked themselves into believing the opposition will fold. The historical record of great-power miscalculation is largely a record of decisions that made no sense on the cost-benefit arithmetic available to outside observers at the time. The cost gap analyzed here is a strong structural reason to expect restraint on the expensive option, and it is not a guarantee, because it holds only for a decision-maker who is calculating and who has accurate inputs. Building a defense posture on the assumption that the adversary will always price correctly is the error that the entire conventional line of investment exists to insure against.
## The Contested Middle, and Why Judgment Lives There

Polar comparisons clarify by simplifying, and the simplification has a cost that has to be paid back explicitly. Neither of the two options described here is where the difficult cases actually sit. The difficult cases sit in the band between them, and a planner who has internalized the poles without mapping the band will be prepared for the two situations least likely to occur.

The band contains several distinguishable kinds of case. There are unattributed uses of lethal force, where the act is unambiguous but the actor is not, which occupies the awkward position of being too serious to absorb and too uncertain to answer formally. There are attributed but sub-lethal military acts, such as airspace violations that are clearly deliberate and clearly state-conducted but cause no damage, which are unambiguous in authorship and negligible in effect. There are incidents at sea affecting cables, pipelines, and shipping, where the physical facts are often recoverable but the intent behind them frequently is not. There are actions conducted through a third state, where authorship is one step removed and the responsible party can point to an intermediary with its own agency. And there is open non-military coercion, including economic and diplomatic pressure that is entirely deniable-free but also entirely outside the military response frame.

Each of these breaks one of the two definitions used in the scoring. The band is not a small residual category. It is arguably where most of the real cases live, and it is certainly where the decisions that matter get made, because the poles are easy: an unambiguous armed attack triggers a machinery that already exists, and a low-level deniable act is handled as a criminal or security matter without strategic decision. The band is where a government has to decide, without a template, whether it is facing a policing problem, a deterrence problem, or the opening of something larger.

The framework in this article still helps in the band, but it has to be applied rather than looked up. The four axes remain the right questions to ask of any specific incident: how often does this kind of thing happen, what did it cost the actor, how hard is it to defend against, and what would follow if this became routine. The build-time logic remains the right resource test. What the framework cannot supply is the classification, and classification is where analytical judgment is irreducibly required. The threshold question itself, meaning where the band ends and open conflict begins, is treated as its own subject in this cluster and is not resolved here.

One practical discipline helps in the band more than any framework. Classify by the actor's calibration rather than by the act's severity. If the act appears chosen for its ambiguity, it belongs to the deniable category and should be answered with attribution and resilience. If the act appears chosen for its unambiguity, meaning the actor wanted it understood as deliberate state action, it belongs to the other category regardless of how little damage it did, because the actor is communicating rather than merely imposing cost. That single test resolves a surprising number of band cases and it does so on evidence a defender can usually assemble, which is more than most classification schemes offer.

## How the Comparison Gets Mis-Scored

Certain errors recur often enough in public and institutional argument about this question that naming them serves as a practical checklist for anyone evaluating a claim, including the claims made here.

The most common error is scoring a single axis and generalizing from it. An argument that the deniable campaign is the real threat because it is happening has scored likelihood and stopped. An argument that only open attack matters because it is the only outcome that ends the state has scored consequence and stopped. Both are valid observations presented as complete conclusions, and both are recognizable by the absence of any mention of the other three axes.

The second error is treating the likelihood finding as durable. The likelihood gap is a consequence of the cost gap, which means it is a derived quantity rather than a primitive one. Citing the low probability of open attack without noting what that probability rests on produces a false sense of stability, and it is the specific error that leaves a planner unprepared for the case where the underlying cost structure shifts.

The third error is the aggregate-to-specific slide. The observation that the sub-threshold campaign is near-certain in aggregate is correct. Using it to justify point defense of every candidate target is not, because per-target probability remains low and the hardening budget is finite. Correct planning against a distributed threat is portfolio work, concentrating protection where a successful act would produce disproportionate systemic effect rather than spreading it evenly across a target set that cannot all be covered.

The fourth error is treating the threats as sequential. The picture in which deniable pressure runs until it stops and open conflict then begins is intuitive and wrong, and it produces plans in which counter-sabotage is a peacetime activity that gets deprioritized precisely when it would matter most. The two run together, and the deniable layer becomes more valuable to the sponsor once open conflict starts rather than less.

The fifth error is confusing difficulty with importance. The finding that sub-threshold defense is harder is often deployed as an argument that it deserves more resources. That inference does not follow. Difficulty tells a planner how much effort is required per unit of result and where the binding constraint sits. It says nothing about whether the result is worth having, which is a consequence question answered on a different axis.

The sixth error is the one this article is most at risk of committing itself, and it deserves flagging in the same terms. A framework that resolves a contested allocation into a clean resource-type split is suspiciously tidy, and tidiness in this domain usually indicates that something contested has been assumed away. What has been assumed away here is the possibility that the two resource categories are not as separable in practice as they are in principle. Political attention is not infinite, legislative bandwidth is a genuinely shared resource, and a defense establishment that becomes preoccupied with infrastructure protection may lose the institutional focus that force development requires. The split is cleaner on paper than in any ministry. A reader should hold the framework as a structure for argument rather than as a procedure that removes the need for judgment.
## The Time Dimension: Why the Correct Weighting Is Not Static

Every allocation argument in this domain is conducted as though the answer is a fixed setting to be found once and then held. It is not. The correct weighting moves across a planning cycle, and treating it as static is what produces the oscillation that afflicts real policy: a surge toward infrastructure protection after a visible incident, a swing back toward procurement after an alliance summit, and neither line sustained long enough to produce a capability.

Three time-dependent factors govern the movement.

The first is the state of the existing baseline. Marginal return is a function of what has already been built. A state that has spent a decade rebuilding armored formations, air and missile defense, and munitions stocks has a thinner marginal return on the next increment of the same than a state that has not. A state that has never conducted a serious review of infrastructure single points of failure has an enormous marginal return on the first such review and a much smaller one on the fifth. Neither of these positions is permanent, which means the correct answer to the allocation question shifts as each baseline fills. A recommendation that ignores the baseline is a recommendation for a country that does not exist.

The second factor is the phase of the adversary's own reconstitution. A period in which the sponsoring state's ground forces are heavily committed elsewhere is a period in which the conventional contingency is structurally constrained regardless of intent, and it is therefore the period in which the marginal urgency of conventional capability is at its lowest even though the marginal importance of building it is at its highest. That sounds contradictory and is not. Urgency and importance separate here precisely because build times are long. The right moment to build slow capability is the moment when it is least needed, because that is the only moment when there is time to build it. A planner who lets urgency drive the capital budget will always build the slow capability too late, since by the time it feels urgent the window has closed.

The third factor is the tempo and character of the deniable campaign itself, which is not constant. Such campaigns shift methods as networks are rolled up and as defenses adapt, and they intensify or slacken in response to political conditions the defender does not control. A period of elevated tempo justifies a temporary reallocation of operational attention, and the discipline required is that it be a reallocation of attention rather than of capital, because attention can be moved back and capital cannot.

Putting these together produces a rule that is more useful than any fixed ratio. Capital allocation should be smoothed across the cycle and protected from incident-driven variance, because the capabilities it buys take longer to produce than the cycle over which conditions fluctuate. Operational allocation should be responsive and allowed to vary substantially, because the capabilities it buys can be surged and stood down. The failure mode in real institutions is the exact inverse: capital budgets that swing with recent events and operational postures that ossify around whatever structure was set up after the last reorganization.

There is a further temporal asymmetry worth drawing out, because it is the strongest practical reason to distrust one's own instincts on this question. Recency shapes the perception of both threats in opposite directions. Frequent sub-threshold incidents keep that threat continuously salient, which means its perceived importance tracks its actual frequency reasonably well. The severe contingency has no such reinforcement. In the absence of an event, its perceived probability decays toward zero regardless of whether its actual probability has changed at all. That decay is a psychological fact about how organizations weight risk, not an analytical finding about the world, and its effect is to bias every allocation cycle slightly further toward the immediate problem than the previous one. The bias is small in any single cycle and compounds across many. Institutional mechanisms that force periodic explicit reconsideration of the rare contingency exist precisely to counteract it, and their unpopularity is a predictable consequence of the fact that they are correcting for a bias the people being corrected do not experience as one.

The counterpart bias also exists and is less discussed. Defense establishments built around conventional capability have a professional and institutional interest in the severe contingency remaining the organizing threat, and that interest can outlive the analytical case for it. An establishment that has spent decades organized around one problem will tend to interpret ambiguous evidence as confirming that problem's centrality. Both biases are real, they point in opposite directions, and neither cancels the other, since they operate on different institutions with different budgets. The practical defense against both is the same: make the axes and the scoring explicit, write down what would change the judgment, and revisit on a schedule rather than in response to events.
## What Would Change This Judgment

A comparison that cannot say what would overturn it is an opinion rather than an assessment. Several developments would require re-scoring, and naming them in advance is what makes the judgment falsifiable and what gives a planner something to monitor.

A visible weakening of alliance cohesion or of the credibility of the collective response would compress the cost gap from above and raise the likelihood score for the conventional case without changing anything about the deniable one. This is the most consequential single variable in the entire comparison, because the low likelihood score rests almost entirely on the expected cost of the alliance response. A planner monitoring one thing should monitor this.

A demonstrated reconstitution of ground and enabling forces at a scale exceeding what the war in Ukraine has consumed, combined with a force posture oriented westward rather than southward, would raise the conventional likelihood score on capability grounds. Capability alone does not establish intent and should never be scored as though it does, but capability is a necessary condition, and its absence is currently doing real work in keeping the likelihood score low.

A shift in the character of sub-threshold activity from cost-imposition toward preparation would matter more than any increase in its volume. Volume is the wrong metric. What would change the assessment is a change in target selection toward nodes with military rather than economic significance, or a change in the pattern from opportunistic to systematic mapping of specific corridors. That is a warning-indicator question rather than a comparison question, and it belongs to the indicator articles in this series rather than to this one.

A sustained failure of the sub-threshold response, in which attribution stays slow and response stays absent across repeated significant incidents, would raise both the deniable campaign's consequence score and, through the threshold-testing connection, the conventional case's likelihood score. This is the compounding failure mode, and it is the one most within Polish and allied control.

Conversely, a demonstrated capacity to attribute quickly and respond in ways that impose real cost would lower the deniable campaign's effectiveness and, through the same connection, reinforce rather than undermine the deterrence of the severe contingency. That is the strongest available argument that the two efforts are complements rather than competitors, and it is worth carrying out of this article even by a reader who rejects everything else in it.

## The Honest Limits of This Comparison

Several limits apply, and stating them is part of the assessment rather than a hedge appended to it.

The scoring is ordinal and judgmental. The numbers in the matrix are a structure for argument, and a reader who disagrees with a score should substitute their own and see whether the resource-type conclusion survives. It generally does, because that conclusion rests on the build-time asymmetry rather than on the specific scores, which is a feature of the framework rather than a defense of it.

The consequence tail of the conventional case is genuinely unbounded, and every scoring system that includes it is making an arbitrary truncation. This is not a solvable problem. It is a reason to treat expected-value arithmetic in this domain with suspicion and to prefer the error-recoverability logic used here, which does not require the tail to be estimated.

The comparison is conceptual and does not attempt to score specific capabilities, specific budget lines, or specific incidents. That is deliberate, both because operational specificity in this domain provides uplift to the wrong readers and because specifics change while the structure does not. A reader who wants the specifics should take the structure and apply it to the current picture rather than expecting the structure to carry them.

The two-option framing is a simplification. Real risk lives on a continuum that includes limited unattributed uses of force, incidents at sea and in the air that are neither clearly deniable nor clearly deliberate, and coercion that is open but non-military. Treating the poles as the analytical anchors is defensible and clarifying, but a planner working the contested middle should treat this comparison as a frame rather than a complete taxonomy.

Finally, the assessment of intent underlying both likelihood scores is inference rather than observation. Intent is the least accessible variable in the entire problem, and analysts of good faith disagree about it substantially. The framework here is designed to be robust to that disagreement, which is why it rests on cost structures and build times rather than on any claim about what a particular leadership intends. A reader who holds a much more pessimistic view of intent than the one implied here can raise the conventional likelihood score and will find that the resource-type recommendation shifts in emphasis but not in structure.

## Closing Verdict

The comparison between hybrid war vs open invasion risk resolves not into a ranking but into a rule about which resources go where. Sub-threshold pressure is far more likely, considerably harder to defend against in practice, and moderate and largely recoverable in consequence. Open attack is far less likely, better understood as a defensive problem, and categorically worse in consequence with a tail that cannot be bounded. The inversion between likelihood and severity is what makes the allocation hard, and no heuristic that ranks by one of them alone survives it.

What settles the question is build time and the asymmetric recoverability of the two possible errors. Fast-building capability goes to the threat arriving now, because it can be generated when needed. Slow-building capability goes to the threat that is not arriving now, because that is the only window in which it can be generated at all. Underinvestment in the frequent threat produces visible, moderate, correctable damage. Underinvestment in the rare threat produces nothing for years and then a failure that cannot be corrected.

The two threats are connected by preparation, by enablement, by threshold-testing, and by substitution, which means the common framing of a choice between them is not merely unhelpful but analytically wrong. A serious plan exhausts the overlap dividend first, protects the slow-building lines from being raided to fund urgent responses, and treats the daily friction of the deniable campaign as a continuous exercise of the coordination machinery that a severe contingency would demand. Neglecting either threat for the other remains the classic error, and the reason it is classic is that each neglect looks locally rational and only reveals itself as a mistake from a vantage point the planner does not have at the time of the decision.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is a hybrid campaign or an open invasion more likely against Poland?

The deniable sub-threshold campaign is far more likely, and the gap is wide enough that it is not a close analytical call. Sub-threshold pressure is observed rather than inferred: infrastructure incidents, navigation interference across the Baltic region, engineered border pressure, and coordinated information operations are documented in the open record and have in many cases been publicly attributed by governments. An open conventional attack on Polish territory has not occurred and remains a low-probability contingency, gated principally by the expected cost of the collective response it would trigger. The important qualification is that the likelihood gap is a consequence of the cost gap rather than an independent fact, which means the estimate is only as stable as the cost structure producing it.

### Q: Which is harder for Poland to defend, hybrid pressure or invasion?

Sub-threshold pressure is harder in practice, which is counterintuitive and frequently scored backwards. Conventional defense is enormously expensive and genuinely demanding, but it is a well-posed problem: a defined adversary, established doctrine, clear institutional ownership, and known capabilities that an appropriation can buy. Defense against a calibrated deniable campaign spans a national infrastructure that is mostly privately owned, responsibility fragmented across police, internal security, regulators, operators, and the armed forces, detection that often cannot distinguish sabotage from accident at the moment of occurrence, and attribution that may rest on evidence too sensitive to use publicly. Harder does not mean more damaging. It means the marginal unit of effort is less certain to produce a defensive result, and that the binding constraint is legal authority and coordination rather than money.

### Q: Where should Poland concentrate effort between hybrid and invasion?

The answer is a split by resource type rather than by percentage. Operational attention, legal and institutional reform, interagency coordination, and infrastructure resilience should flow predominantly toward the sub-threshold problem, because those resources produce returns there and because that is the threat consuming them daily. Capital investment, force structure, munitions stocks, integrated air and missile defense, and alliance-facing effort should flow predominantly toward the conventional contingency, because those capabilities take a decade to build and cannot be generated inside a warning window. Framing the question as a ratio produces a sterile argument, since a euro of capital equipment and an hour of interagency coordination are not exchangeable inputs. Framing it as a resource-type division turns the debate into a productive one about which category a given proposal falls into.

### Q: How do hybrid pressure and open invasion connect on a spectrum?

They connect in four specific ways rather than merely sitting at two ends of a line. Sub-threshold activity is preparatory, generating knowledge of fragile nodes, detection thresholds, and coordination weaknesses that would be available if intent ever formed. It is enabling, since a conventional contingency would open with intensified deniable action rather than replace it. It is threshold-testing, because each unanswered act moves the perceived response line the sponsor plans against. And it is substitutive, since deniable coercion is partly what a state does when it has judged the conventional option too expensive. Those four connections make the relationship a feedback loop rather than a spectrum, which is why planning either threat in isolation produces a systematically wrong answer.

### Q: Why is the more likely gray-zone threat to Poland the less catastrophic one?

Because the property that makes an option cheap and repeatable is the same property that caps its consequence. Deniable coercion works by staying explicable as accident, criminality, or the act of disownable proxies, and an action large enough to be unambiguous stops being deniable. That calibration ceiling keeps the damage cumulative, corrosive, and largely reversible: infrastructure is repaired, networks are rolled up, exposed information operations lose yield. Open attack has no such ceiling because it has abandoned deniability entirely, which is what makes it expensive and rare and also what makes its consequence categorical rather than incremental. The inversion is structural rather than coincidental, and it is the reason ordinary risk-prioritization heuristics break on this problem.

### Q: What planning tension divides hybrid and invasion risk for Poland?

The tension is that likelihood and severity run in opposite directions, which breaks every prioritization heuristic that works elsewhere. Ranking by likelihood produces a plan that ignores the outcome capable of ending the state's independent existence. Ranking by severity produces a plan that ignores the pressure the state is under continuously, and such plans are quietly abandoned by the people who have to execute them. Ranking by expected value produces whatever the analyst's estimate of an unbounded consequence tail implies, which means the answer is set by an assumption rather than by evidence. The tension is compounded institutionally, because the two threats are championed by different constituencies with different budget lines who argue on different axes and therefore never actually engage each other's case.

### Q: Does only invasion matter, or does the gray zone deserve weight?

The gray zone deserves substantial weight, and the argument for it does not depend on claiming comparable severity. Sustained deniable pressure degrades infrastructure reliability, consumes security resources, erodes public confidence, and can weaken the political consensus behind defense spending and alliance commitment. More consequentially, it feeds directly into the likelihood of the severe contingency: an information campaign that fractures alliance consensus, sabotage that demonstrates reinforcement routes can be disrupted, and probing that maps where response thresholds actually sit all lower the expected cost of open attack. A state that absorbs sub-threshold pressure without response is demonstrating that it is coercible, which degrades the conditions on which deterrence of the severe threat rests.

### Q: How do aggressor costs compare for hybrid versus invasion?

The cost gap is the widest asymmetry in the comparison and the engine driving everything else. Deniable operations use small numbers of transactionally recruited people, exploit existing access, require no mobilization or logistics tail, generate no posture change that would register as warning, and can be abandoned at any point without having committed the sponsor to anything. Their dominant cost is organizational attention and the slow attrition of networks as they are rolled up, not money. Open attack requires assembling and sustaining formations, accumulating munitions and fuel at scale, moving that mass where it can be observed, preparing a domestic population for casualties, accepting economic isolation more severe than any previously imposed, and accepting possible direct engagement with the alliance's combined conventional forces.

### Q: What consequence gap separates hybrid pressure from invasion?

The gap is one of category rather than degree. A sustained deniable campaign produces cumulative damage that is real and should not be dismissed, but it is recoverable: infrastructure is repaired, networks are dismantled, exposed operations lose their yield, and public confidence can be rebuilt through visible competence. Nothing in it changes borders, removes population, or destroys the armed forces. An open attack produces casualties on a scale Europe has not seen since the middle of the last century, destruction as a campaign objective rather than a side effect, population displacement, territorial risk, and a permanent change to the European security order regardless of the military outcome. Its consequence tail also cannot be bounded, which is why honest scoring treats the top of that scale as open-ended rather than assigning it a number.

### Q: Why is neglecting either hybrid or invasion the classic error?

Because each neglect looks locally rational and reveals itself as a mistake only from a vantage point the planner lacks at the moment of decision. Neglecting the frequent threat produces continuous moderate damage while also degrading the conditions that keep the severe contingency unlikely, since a visibly coercible state is a cheaper target. Neglecting the rare threat produces nothing at all for years and then, if the contingency arrives, produces a failure that cannot be corrected, because a decade of force structure cannot be reconstituted inside a warning window. The two errors are asymmetric in their recoverability, and that asymmetry, rather than any judgment about which threat is bigger, is what the allocation has to be built around.

### Q: Can a hybrid campaign and an open invasion run at the same time?

They would almost certainly run simultaneously rather than sequentially. The common mental model treats sub-threshold activity as a phase that ends when open conflict begins, and that model is wrong. A conventional contingency would open with intensified deniable action and sustain it throughout: disruption of mobilization, interference with reinforcement movement, degradation of command and communications, and information operations aimed at delaying allied decision. The deniable layer becomes more valuable rather than less once open conflict starts, because delaying a decision by days is worth more then than at any other time. The planning implication is direct: counter-sabotage and infrastructure protection are components of conventional preparation rather than alternatives to it.

### Q: What would shift the balance of risk from gray-zone pressure toward invasion?

Two developments would compress the cost gap and therefore shift the balance. The first and most consequential is a visible weakening of alliance cohesion or of the credibility of the collective response, which would lower the expected cost of open attack without changing anything about the cheap option. Because the low likelihood score rests almost entirely on that expected cost, this is the single variable most worth monitoring. The second is a demonstrated reconstitution of ground and enabling forces at scale, combined with a posture oriented westward. Capability never establishes intent and should not be scored as though it does, but it is a necessary condition, and its current absence is doing real work in keeping the likelihood assessment low.

### Q: Do defenses built against hybrid pressure also help against an invasion?

Substantially, though not completely, and identifying the overlap is the most practical output of the comparison. Infrastructure redundancy makes a network harder to disrupt with a single deniable act and harder to paralyze with a strike campaign. Rapid attribution capacity attacks deniability directly and also supports the speed of political decision that reinforcement timelines depend on. Societal cohesion and information resilience harden the population against influence operations and provide the base for endurance in a conventional contingency. Interagency coordination structures are exercised continuously by the deniable campaign and are exactly what would be under maximum stress in a crisis. The limit is real: armored formations and long-range fires do little against a saboteur, and counter-disinformation capacity does little against a breakthrough.

### Q: Which threat does the alliance handle better, hybrid pressure or invasion?

The alliance handles open attack considerably better, and the reason is institutional design rather than effort. Collective defense machinery was built for unambiguous armed attack: the obligation, the consultation mechanisms, the command structures, and the reinforcement plans all assume an event that is visible and attributable. Deniable coercion is designed precisely to avoid triggering that machinery, and it succeeds, because consensus among many members is slow to form around ambiguous facts and because the political cost of acting on contested attribution falls unevenly across members. The result is that the threat the alliance handles worst is the one arriving most often, which is an uncomfortable finding and a strong argument for building national and bilateral response capacity rather than waiting for consensus.
