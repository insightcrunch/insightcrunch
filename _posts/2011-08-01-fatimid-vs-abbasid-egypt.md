In the year 1000 there were three men calling themselves caliph, each claiming to be the rightful leader of all Muslims, sitting in Baghdad, Cairo and Córdoba. The one in Baghdad had the recognition of most of the Islamic world and could not leave his palace without permission from the Iranian dynasty controlling him. The one in Cairo had an army, a fleet, a treasury, the richest province in the region and almost no recognition outside it.

That split is the subject of this article, and the claim it defends is the two caliphates verdict: that in this period power and prestige came apart, that the Fatimids held the first and the Abbasids the second, and that neither could convert what it had into what it lacked.

![Fatimid Cairo compared with Abbasid Baghdad, showing how power and prestige split between two rival caliphates - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-75.webp)

The assumption this corrects is the tidiest one in the whole subject, which is that the Islamic world had a caliph in the way Christendom had a pope. It did not, and for a substantial period it had three, which is a fact most summaries omit because it complicates every sentence that follows.

The comparison is worth making carefully because it is usually made badly. Accounts written from the Baghdad side treat the Fatimids as a heretical breakaway; accounts written from the Cairo side treat the Abbasids as a spent force; and both are describing something real while missing what makes the period interesting, which is that each side had exactly what the other needed.

This article owns the comparison and the verdict. The Fatimid dynasty belongs to the article on [Fatimid Egypt](/2010/11/01/fatimid-egypt-complete-guide/), the doctrinal division to the article on [Shia Islam and the Fatimid caliphate](/2011/03/01/shia-islam-and-fatimid-caliphate/), the earlier Abbasid position in Egypt to the article on [the Arab conquest of Egypt](/2010/01/01/arab-conquest-of-egypt/) and its cluster, and the commercial position to the article on [Fatimid trade](/2011/04/01/fatimid-trade-and-wealth-of-egypt/).

## Three Caliphates, Not One

The starting correction is the most important thing in this article, because every other comparison depends on it.

The Abbasid caliphate had been the single acknowledged caliphate of the Sunni world since 750, and the tenth century ended that condition permanently.

The Fatimid claim came first, proclaimed in North Africa in 909, and the article on Fatimid Egypt sets out why it was not merely a rival dynasty: the caliph was also an imam, which is a claim of a different order from anything the Abbasids asserted.

### How many caliphates existed at the same time?

Three, for roughly a century. The Abbasids in Baghdad had held the title since 750, the Fatimids proclaimed theirs in North Africa in 909 and moved it to Egypt in 973, and the Umayyad ruler of Córdoba declared himself caliph in 929. All three claims ran simultaneously until the Spanish caliphate collapsed in 1031.

The Spanish claim followed in 929, when the Umayyad ruler of Córdoba, whose family the Abbasids had displaced two centuries earlier, took the title himself, partly in response to the Fatimid proclamation in North Africa which threatened his own position there.

The situation from 929 to 1031 was therefore three simultaneous caliphates, and the Spanish one collapsed in 1031 leaving the Cairo and Baghdad rivalry as the main event.

The theoretical problem this created was severe. Islamic political thought had generally assumed one caliph, and the existence of three required either denying two of them or accepting a plurality that the theory did not accommodate.

The practical resolution was that everyone denied the others and life continued, since the caliphate's practical significance for most people was the name in the Friday sermon, and that could be settled locally.

The lesson for a reader is that the medieval Islamic world was politically plural in a way that later summaries flatten, and the assumption of a single caliphate is the first thing to discard.

## The Two Claims

The legitimacy each caliphate asserted differed in kind and not merely in the person claimed, and understanding the difference explains why the rivalry could not be settled.

The Abbasid claim rested on descent from the Prophet's uncle, on the acclamation of the community, and on the endorsement of the scholarly establishment, which is a claim about legitimate succession to a political and religious office.

### How did each caliphate claim legitimacy?

The Abbasids claimed descent from the Prophet's uncle, acclamation by the community and endorsement by the jurists, which makes the caliph a legitimate successor holding an office. The Fatimids claimed descent from the Prophet's daughter through a designated line of imams carrying infallible guidance, which makes the caliph a different kind of being.

The Fatimid claim rested on descent from the Prophet's daughter through a line of designated imams, and the article on Shia Islam and the Fatimid caliphate sets out its three properties: designation by the predecessor, inner knowledge of the revelation, and protection from error.

The asymmetry is decisive. An Abbasid caliph is a man holding an office and can be a poor one without the office being invalidated. A Fatimid caliph is a man who holds a status no election could confer and no failure could remove.

The consequence for the rivalry is that no compromise existed. Two claims to the same office can in principle be adjudicated; a claim to an office and a claim to a divinely guided status are not the same kind of claim, and there is no procedure that resolves them.

The Abbasid response was to attack the genealogy rather than the doctrine, which is the more practical line of attack, and a public declaration issued from Baghdad in 1011 gathered jurists and notables to deny that the Fatimids descended from the Prophet's family at all.

### Why did the Fatimids break from the Abbasids?

They did not break away, because they never accepted Abbasid authority in the first place. The movement held that the imamate had passed through a designated line from the Prophet's family and that the Abbasids were usurpers, so the Fatimid claim was a rival from the beginning rather than a secession from an accepted order.

The Fatimid response was to ignore the attack rather than answer it, which is what an unanswerable genealogical dispute permits, and the article on Fatimid Egypt treats the descent question as genuinely unresolvable in either direction.

## Who Actually Governed

The most striking difference between the two caliphates in this period is not doctrinal but practical, and it concerns whether the caliph ran anything.

The Abbasid caliph after 945 did not. An Iranian dynasty from the Caspian region took Baghdad in that year, installed itself as the real government, and kept the caliph as a figurehead whose functions were ceremonial and whose deposition was at their discretion.

### Who controlled the Abbasid caliph during this period?

An Iranian dynasty from the Caspian region took Baghdad in 945 and controlled the caliphate for over a century, followed by the Seljuk Turks from 1055. The caliph remained the source of legitimacy for both, was named in the sermon and on the coinage, and exercised no independent political or military power.

The paradox in that arrangement is worth pausing on. The dynasty controlling the Sunni caliph was itself Shia, of a different branch from the Fatimids, and it neither abolished the Abbasid caliphate nor transferred recognition to Cairo.

The reason is instructive. A Sunni caliph under Shia control is a useful instrument, since he legitimates the controlling power in the eyes of a Sunni population, and abolishing him would destroy the asset. Recognizing the Fatimids would have meant submitting to a caliph who actually claimed authority.

The Seljuk Turks replaced that arrangement in 1055, taking Baghdad as Sunni restorers, and the caliph's position improved in dignity without improving much in power.

The Fatimid caliph by contrast governed. The article on the Fatimid caliphs establishes that the early rulers exercised real authority, and it also establishes that the office was hollowed out after the crisis of the 1060s, when military viziers reduced the caliphs to figureheads in their turn.

The convergence is the striking part. By the twelfth century both caliphs were ceremonial figures controlled by military men, having arrived at the same condition from opposite directions and about a century apart.

## Two Endings Compared

The manner in which each caliphate came to an end repays a direct comparison, since the two endings test, better than any other evidence, everything this article has argued about what each of them actually possessed.

The Fatimid ending required no battle at all. A Kurdish commander who was already governing Egypt as vizier simply had the Abbasid caliph's name read in the Friday sermon in 1171, and a caliphate more than two and a half centuries old, counting from its proclamation in North Africa, ceased to exist between one week and the next.

The absence of resistance is the decisive fact, and the article on the Fatimid caliphs uses it as the strongest available evidence that the dynasty's doctrine had never acquired a constituency among the population it ruled.

The Abbasid ending required an army and a sack. The Mongols took Baghdad in 1258, destroyed much of the city and killed the caliph, and the event registered across the Sunni world as a rupture in a way the Egyptian change of name had not.

The difference measures exactly what this article has been arguing. A caliphate with capacity and no constituency ended when one man gave an instruction; a caliphate with a constituency and no capacity required a foreign conquest to remove, and its removal was felt everywhere.

The revival confirms it. No attempt was made to restore the Fatimid caliphate, while the Abbasid title was resurrected in Cairo under Mamluk protection because a power that wanted legitimating value found the name still worth something.

The final irony sits in that arrangement. The city built to house the caliphate that opposed the Abbasids ended by housing an Abbasid caliph kept as an instrument, which is as complete a reversal as the period offers.

The general observation is that how a state ends reveals what it actually had. Institutions with real constituencies are hard to remove and are missed; institutions resting on the loyalty of soldiers and officials disappear the moment those people are indifferent.

## Why the Comparison Is Worth Making

A closing argument for the exercise itself, since a reader might reasonably ask why two medieval caliphates deserve a side by side treatment.

The first reason is that it corrects the largest misconception about the period, which is the assumption of a single Islamic authority, and no amount of describing either caliphate separately achieves that correction as efficiently as putting them beside each other.

The second is that it isolates variables. Two states with the same administrative inheritance, the same legal framework, the same military system and the same religious vocabulary, differing in doctrine, geography and economic base, come close to a natural experiment on what those differences produce.

The third is that it demonstrates the separation of power from prestige more cleanly than most historical cases, because here the two were held by different parties at the same moment and each was visible on its own.

The fourth is that it explains Egypt's position. The article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt argues that the country spent most of its history as somebody's province, and the Fatimid period is when it stopped, which is only legible against what it stopped being part of.

The fifth is that it prepares the ground for what follows. The article on Ayyubid Egypt takes up a regime that ended one caliphate and served the other nominally, and the arrangement makes no sense without this rivalry behind it.

The sixth is methodological, since the six requirements for a fair comparison set out earlier transfer to any pair of rival states in any period, and this case exercises all of them.

The general recommendation is that comparisons are most valuable where the answer refuses to resolve, because a comparison with a clean winner teaches you about the winner while one that splits teaches you about the criteria.

## Reading the Friday Sermon

The single mechanism through which this entire rivalry operated deserves its own section, because it is unfamiliar and it does a remarkable amount of work.

The practice is that the weekly congregational sermon names the ruling authority, which the article on the Tulunid dynasty describes as the point at which sovereignty is publicly acknowledged.

The properties that make it useful are worth listing. It is public, so everyone present hears it. It is weekly, so a change is registered immediately. It is universal, since every congregational mosque does it. And it costs nothing to change, requiring only an instruction.

The consequence is that allegiance in this world is cheap to declare and cheap to reverse, which is exactly why the contested regions of Syria changed hands rhetorically so often.

The evidentiary value for historians is high, since chroniclers record whose name was read where and when, and a map of sermon allegiance is a map of claimed sovereignty that no other source supplies.

The dramatic instances in this series are all sermon events. The Fatimid caliphate was abolished in 1171 by a change of name, as the article on the Fatimid caliphs sets out. The Fatimids nearly won at Baghdad in 1058 by the same instrument. The Tulunids maintained formal deference by keeping the Abbasid name.

The contrast with a European equivalent is instructive. Nothing in medieval Christendom performed quite this function with quite this frequency, and the weekly renewal of a political statement in every place of worship is a distinctive feature of the Islamic political system.

The general observation is that identifying the mechanism through which legitimacy is actually expressed in a given system explains more about its politics than any amount of theory, and in this one the mechanism is a sentence spoken once a week.

## The Value of a Free Endorsement

The Abbasid asset deserves closer analysis than the article has yet given it, because a power that governs nothing and is nonetheless wanted by everyone is a genuinely odd object.

The product was recognition. A ruler anywhere in the Sunni world could seek a diploma of investiture confirming him in his position, and receiving one converted a man who had taken power into a man exercising delegated authority.

The buyer's motive was domestic. A ruler's own Sunni population and its scholars cared whether he was legitimate, and the caliph's endorsement supplied an answer to that question in a form the religious establishment accepted.

The seller's cost was nothing. Granting a diploma required no troops, no money and no risk, and the caliph could issue one to a ruler he had never met governing a territory he had never seen.

The price paid was correspondingly low. Recognition of the caliph in the sermon and on coinage, occasional gifts, and formal deference cost the recipient very little, which the article on the Tulunid dynasty describes as the standard arrangement for autonomous regimes.

The equilibrium was therefore stable and useless to the caliph. Everyone wanted the product, nobody paid much for it, and the caliphate's income from an asset the whole Sunni world valued was negligible.

The Fatimid problem is visible against this. They could not offer the same product, because their recognition was worth nothing to a Sunni ruler's Sunni subjects, and their own doctrine's requirements meant they could not manufacture a constituency that would value it.

The general observation is that an asset everyone wants and nobody will pay for is not a source of power, and the Abbasid caliphate's peculiar condition, universally sought and materially destitute, is one of the clearer illustrations of that distinction in any period.

## The Rivalry in Later Memory

How the contest has been remembered differs from what happened, and the divergence is instructive.

The Sunni tradition remembered it as an episode of heresy suppressed, which is the framing the article on al-Azhar shows operating on that institution after 1171, and which treats the Fatimid centuries as an aberration rather than as a period of Egyptian history.

The Egyptian record complicated that framing without overturning it, since the city, the mosque and the commercial position were Fatimid achievements that no successor regime could disown while disowning the dynasty.

The modern nationalist reading has had its own difficulties with the period, since a foreign dynasty of contested descent ruling Egypt from a closed enclosure does not fit a story about Egyptian self assertion, whatever its achievements.

The Ismaili tradition preserves its own memory, in which the Fatimid caliphate is the one period when the imamate held temporal power, and the article on Shia Islam notes that the movement survived the state's fall in Yemen and western India.

The western reading, when it exists, has tended to reach the period through the crusades, arriving at Fatimid Egypt as the power the crusaders encountered and the Abbasid caliphate as a background institution.

The scholarly reassessment of the last century has recovered a great deal, particularly through the manuscript materials the article on Shia Islam mentions and through the Egyptian documentary archives.

The general observation is that a defeated claimant's history is written by the side that won and then partially recovered when its own materials resurface, and the Fatimid case is a good illustration of both stages.

## What Would Change This Picture

Naming the evidence that would revise the account is the fairest test of a comparison built largely on structural reasoning.

Fiscal series from either state would transform everything. The relative wealth argument rests on mechanism and visible expenditure, and even a partial revenue series from one century would convert an argument into a measurement.

A surviving Fatimid historical tradition would rebalance the narrative evidence, since the comparison rests on the Sunni tradition supplying most of the story and the article on al-Hakim sets out how severe that imbalance is.

Documentary material from Iraq comparable to the Egyptian archives would remove the asymmetry described in the section on sources, and it would probably complicate the picture of Baghdad's decline considerably.

Systematic coin assay across both series would firm up the currency comparison, which rests on samples rather than on a comprehensive study.

Agricultural evidence from the Iraqi alluvium, in the form of survey data on settlement and canal use across the relevant centuries, would establish the pace of the decline that this article treats as a given.

Records of the missionary organization's actual reach would settle how far the Fatimid claim penetrated Abbasid territory, which is inferred from episodes rather than measured.

None of these is likely soon, and setting them out marks the boundary. The political chronology, the control of each caliph, the doctrinal difference, the Syrian contest and the endings are secure. The relative wealth, the cultural balance and the extent of recognition are arguments, and this article has tried to present them as such.

## Egypt Before and After the Rivalry

Placing these two centuries in Egypt's longer story shows what the rivalry meant for the country rather than for the dynasty.

Egypt before 969 had been a province, as the article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt establishes, ruled from elsewhere for more than a thousand years with the Ptolemaic period and the brief autonomous episodes as exceptions.

The Tulunid and second autonomous regimes described in the article on the Tulunid dynasty had established that independent rule was possible, but neither claimed anything beyond a governorship in form.

The Fatimid arrival changed the category. Egypt became the seat of a caliphate claiming universal authority, which meant the country was the centre of something for the first time since the Ptolemies rather than the wealthy edge of somebody else's empire.

The consequences were concrete. A resident court, a resident army, a purpose built capital, retained revenue and a foreign policy conducted from Egypt for Egyptian interests are all things the country had not had for a very long time.

Egypt after 1171 did not revert. The Ayyubid and Mamluk regimes that followed ruled from Cairo, kept the revenue, ran their own foreign policy and made Egypt the leading power in the region for centuries, which the article on Fatimid trade describes on the commercial side.

The symbolic irony completes the picture, since the Mamluks eventually housed a puppet Abbasid caliphate in the city the Fatimids had built to oppose the Abbasids.

The general observation is that the Fatimid period's most durable effect on Egypt was not doctrinal, since the article on Shia Islam establishes that Egypt stayed Sunni, but positional: the country stopped being a province and never became one again in quite the same way.

## The Scholars in Between

The learned class occupied an awkward and interesting position in this rivalry and its behaviour is worth examining.

The Sunni establishment was the Abbasid caliphate's principal asset, since it produced the arguments, issued the opinions, trained the judges and supplied the endorsement that made the caliph's recognition worth having.

Its independence was real nonetheless, and the article on al-Azhar sets out the mechanism: endowments protected in law from seizure gave scholarly institutions a revenue base independent of any ruler, so an establishment that endorsed a caliph was not simply an arm of his government.

The Fatimid position was structurally weaker, and the article on Shia Islam identifies why. The regime's own tradition transmitted doctrine through restricted initiation, so it could not build a broad scholarly constituency, and it therefore employed and tolerated Sunni jurists who taught Sunni law throughout its territory.

The consequence is remarkable when stated plainly. The Fatimid caliphate funded the teaching of the tradition its rivals used against it, because it had no alternative that could serve a population.

The migration of individual scholars between the two, described in the section on culture, shows that the doctrinal frontier was porous for practical purposes, and a mathematician who moved from Basra to Cairo was following patronage rather than making a confessional statement.

The Seljuk madrasa programme changed the balance by giving the Sunni side an institution designed for mass instruction, and the article on al-Azhar contrasts the madrasa's defined curriculum and appointed teachers with the older mosque based teaching.

The general observation is that a state's relationship with its learned class determines what kind of legitimacy it can generate, and the Fatimid inability to build one is the single best explanation for why an enormously wealthy and militarily capable regime could not make its claim general.

## What Neither Could Do

Setting out the shared limits is more informative than listing the differences, because the constraints both faced explain why the rivalry ended the way it did.

Neither could reach the other. The distance between the Nile and the Tigris, across desert and through Syria's contested regions, exceeded what either could sustain a campaign across, which is why two centuries of hostility produced no invasion of either heartland.

Neither could control its own army. The article's section on the armies sets out the shared failure, arriving at Baghdad in 945 and at Cairo in the 1070s, and both caliphs ended as figureheads.

Neither could convert its principal asset into the other's. The Fatimids spent enormously on a missionary apparatus that could not produce mass recognition because its transmission model excluded it, and the Abbasids could not turn recognition into revenue because the whole value of their endorsement to those who sought it lay in its being free.

Neither could stabilize its succession. The article on the Fatimid caliphs describes six child accessions in eleven reigns and two permanent schisms, and the Abbasid line was made and unmade by its controllers for three centuries.

Neither could reverse the economic trend it sat on. Egypt's advantages and Iraq's degradation were both structural, driven by hydrology and by centuries of accumulated maintenance or its absence, and no policy available to either state could have changed them quickly.

Neither survived a determined third party. A Kurdish commander ended one and the Mongols ended the other, and in both cases the caliphate proved to have far less independent resilience than its claims implied.

The pattern in these shared limits is that both caliphates were considerably more fragile than their rhetoric, and the rivalry between them was less consequential than the rise of the military powers that eventually disposed of them both.

## Comparing Two States Fairly

The methodological problem this article has been solving is worth stating explicitly, since comparisons between polities are among the most commonly botched exercises in popular history.

The first requirement is specifying the period. Two states that existed for different spans cannot be compared as wholes, and the honest procedure names a window and compares within it, which is why this article works chiefly with the tenth and eleventh centuries.

The second requirement is specifying the criterion. Greater is not a property; wealth, military capacity, territorial extent, recognition, durability, institutional legacy and cultural production are separate properties that frequently point different ways, and a comparison that does not name its criterion is unanswerable.

The third requirement is matching the units. Comparing a caliph with a caliphate, or a court with a civilization, produces confusion, and this article's section on power has to distinguish the Abbasid caliph's impotence from the Abbasid caliphate's continuing symbolic force precisely because the two are different objects.

The fourth requirement is checking the evidence symmetry. If one side is documented far better than the other, the better documented side will appear more complex, more active and more interesting for reasons that have nothing to do with what happened.

The fifth requirement is naming the counterfactual. Saying one state was stronger implies something about what would have happened in a contest, and where no contest occurred the claim needs the mechanism spelled out.

The sixth requirement is resisting the single verdict, which is the same discipline the article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt applies to transformation and the article on al-Hakim applies to reputation.

Applied here, the six requirements produce the split verdict this article defends, and applied to any other pair of rival states they will usually produce something equally untidy, which is generally a sign that the comparison has been done properly.

## What the Sources Allow

The evidence for this comparison is uneven in specific ways, and stating them is necessary before any verdict is trusted.

The narrative sources are partisan on both sides and the imbalance is severe. The Sunni historical tradition is enormous and hostile to the Fatimids as a matter of doctrine; the Fatimid tradition survives far less well, as the article on al-Hakim sets out, so the comparison is made largely with one side's material.

The Egyptian documentary evidence is exceptional and one sided. The merchant archive described in the article on Fatimid trade, and the papyri and administrative material from earlier periods, give Egypt a documentary depth that Iraq in this period does not match.

The consequence is a distortion running against intuition. The side better documented by hostile narrative is the Fatimid one, and the side better documented by its own paperwork is also the Fatimid one, so Baghdad in these centuries is known chiefly through chronicles and through the writing of scholars.

The archaeological evidence favours neither clearly, since both capitals have been continuously occupied and both present the difficulties the article on the founding of Cairo describes for the Fatimid palaces.

The coinage is the most evenly available body of evidence, as the earlier section sets out, and it is one of the few places where the two can be compared on identical terms.

The economic evidence is absent on both sides in the form that would settle anything, and the article on Fatimid trade explains at length why medieval revenue figures cannot be used.

The general observation is that a comparison is only as good as the weaker side's evidence, and every conclusion in this article about relative wealth, power or cultural production should be read as an argument from structure and from visible expenditure rather than as a measurement.

## The Spanish Caliphate

The third claimant is usually omitted from accounts of this rivalry and including it clarifies the whole situation.

The claim was proclaimed in 929 by the Umayyad ruler of Córdoba, whose family had held the caliphate before 750 and had been displaced by the Abbasids, so his assertion was a restoration claim rather than a novel one.

The timing is the informative part. The proclamation came two decades after the Fatimid one and was substantially a response to it, since Fatimid expansion in North Africa threatened Umayyad interests there and a ruler competing for allegiance in the region needed a title equal to his rival's.

The character of the Spanish claim was Sunni, which means the Islamic world briefly contained two Sunni caliphs as well as a Shia one, and the theoretical difficulty this creates for the assumption of a single caliphate is even sharper than the Cairo Baghdad rivalry alone.

The North African theatre is where all three claims touched, since Fatimid, Umayyad and Abbasid aligned powers all pursued influence among the Berber confederations of the region, and the article on Fatimid Egypt notes that the dynasty's western base was entrusted to a governing family when the caliph moved east.

The collapse came in 1031, when the Spanish caliphate fragmented into competing successor states, ending the three way situation after roughly a century.

The consequence for the main rivalry was to simplify it, leaving Cairo and Baghdad as the two claimants until 1171.

The general observation is that the proliferation of caliphates in the tenth century reflects a general fact about the period rather than a peculiarity of the Fatimid case: once the Abbasid monopoly on the title had visibly failed, anyone with sufficient standing and a plausible genealogy could assert it, and several did.

## Minorities Under Both

The treatment of Christians and Jews under each caliphate deserves fuller treatment than a single verdict allows, since the question is asked constantly and answered badly.

The shared framework is the starting point. Both states operated the same legal arrangement, described in the article on Copts under early Islamic rule as protected but pressured: a guarantee of worship, property and communal jurisdiction alongside fiscal disadvantage, sumptuary rules and building restrictions, enforced episodically rather than continuously.

The employment pattern was similar in both. The article on Copts establishes that Christians dominated the Egyptian fiscal bureaucracy for centuries despite recurring decrees dismissing them, and comparable arrangements existed in Iraq, where Christian and Jewish physicians, translators and administrators served at the highest levels.

The commercial dimension was substantial in both, and the article on Fatimid trade notes that the merchant letters documenting medieval Mediterranean and Indian Ocean commerce most fully were written by Jewish traders operating across both territories without difficulty.

The variation is by ruler and decade rather than by dynasty. Both records contain periods of severe enforcement and long periods of practical openness, and the article on al-Hakim describes the sharpest single departure in the Fatimid record along with its equally sharp reversal.

The structural argument against sustained persecution applies to both. The article on conquest and conversion sets out the fiscal contradiction: the poll tax fell only on non Muslims, so forced conversion destroyed revenue, which is why campaigns were rare and episodic enforcement was the norm.

The comparison therefore does not produce a ranking, and attempts to produce one usually work by selecting a bad decade from one side and a good one from the other.

The general observation is that the question is better replaced than answered. Asking which regime was more tolerant invites cherry picking; asking what the framework was, how often it was enforced, who was employed and what the incentives were produces something checkable.

## The Declaration of 1011

The Abbasid counterattack on Fatimid legitimacy deserves its own treatment, because it shows what a symbolic power does when it has no other weapons.

The instrument was a public document issued from Baghdad, to which jurists, judges, notables and members of the Prophet's descendants were summoned to subscribe, denying that the Fatimid line descended from the Prophet's family at all.

The target was the genealogy rather than the doctrine, which is the shrewder choice. Arguing doctrine engages an opponent on his own ground and can be answered; asserting that he is not who he says he is attacks the foundation of everything he claims.

The mechanism of assembling signatures matters. A declaration subscribed by the recognized religious authorities of the capital converts an assertion into something with the appearance of a finding, which is the same technique the article on the Tulunid dynasty describes when jurists were convened at Damascus in 882 to pronounce against a regent.

The Fatimid response was to ignore it, which was probably correct, since answering would have conceded that the question was open and the article on Fatimid Egypt treats the descent question as genuinely unresolvable in either direction.

The evidentiary value of the declaration is close to nil for the historical question and considerable for a different one. It tells us nothing about the genealogy and a great deal about what the Abbasid establishment feared and what tools it had.

The recurrence of the technique across the period is worth noting, since assembling jurists to certify a political conclusion appears repeatedly in this series and is one of the standard instruments of medieval Islamic politics.

The general observation is that when a power cannot reach its rival physically, it attacks the rival's identity, and genealogical denial is the medieval form of an argument that recurs in every period in some vocabulary.

## The 1058 Episode

The year in which the Fatimid caliph's name was read in the Friday sermon at Baghdad deserves examination, because it is the single moment when the rivalry might have been decided and it went nowhere.

The circumstances were a Turkish commander in Abbasid service who fell out with the establishment at Baghdad, sought Fatimid support, received some, and took the city.

The act was the one that mattered. He had the sermon read in the Fatimid caliph's name, which is the formal transfer of sovereignty that the article on Fatimid Egypt identifies as the mechanism by which caliphates were both asserted and abolished.

The duration was about a year, and the arrangement collapsed when Seljuk forces arrived and restored the Abbasid position.

The Fatimid failure to support him is the interesting part and it has several possible explanations. The regime was entering the crisis of the 1060s described in the article on Fatimid Egypt, with successive low floods and army factionalism; the distance was very great; and the vizier managing Fatimid policy may not have believed the position could be held.

The counterfactual is worth stating because it clarifies what was at stake. A Fatimid caliph recognized at Baghdad would have converted prestige into the one currency the dynasty lacked, and the article's central argument is that this was exactly what they could never buy.

The verdict is that the opportunity was real and the capacity to exploit it was not, which is a different thing from the opportunity never existing.

The general observation is that decisive moments in long rivalries are often visible only afterward, and that the side which fails to exploit one is usually failing for structural reasons rather than through an identifiable mistake.

## The Instruments Each Side Had

Listing what each caliphate could actually deploy against the other clarifies why the contest took the shape it did.

The Fatimids had an army, and it could reach Syria but not Iraq, since the distance, the desert and the intervening powers made a campaign against Baghdad impracticable.

They had a fleet, which gave them the Mediterranean coast and the Red Sea and was useless against a landlocked rival.

They had money, which bought allies, subsidized local rulers and funded the missionary apparatus, and which the article on Fatimid trade explains was the dynasty's most reliable advantage.

They had the missionary organization, described in the article on Shia Islam as a second administration operating in territory the dynasty did not govern, which is a genuine instrument of subversion and one with a structural ceiling, since graded initiation cannot produce mass conversion.

The Abbasids had recognition, which cost nothing to grant and was wanted by rulers everywhere, and which produced allies without expenditure.

They had the scholarly establishment, which produced the arguments, the legal opinions and eventually the declaration of 1011, and which the Fatimids could not match in reach because their own doctrine restricted who could be taught.

They had, from 1055, the Seljuks, which is the decisive item and was not an Abbasid instrument in any meaningful sense but a third party whose interests happened to align.

The asymmetry in the lists is the article's whole argument in miniature. One side's instruments were material and limited in reach; the other's were symbolic and unlimited in reach but incapable of acting on their own, and neither set could produce what the other had.

## Syria, the Contested Ground

The region between the two caliphates was where the rivalry was actually conducted, and it deserves description because its character explains why neither could win.

The geography made it the natural theatre. Syria lies between Egypt and Iraq, connects to both, and is the land approach to Egypt, which the article on the Tulunid dynasty identifies as the reason every independent Egyptian power sought to hold it.

The political character made it contestable. Syria was not a single unit but a set of regions with their own centres, their own elites and their own local dynasties, none strong enough to hold the whole and all capable of choosing a patron.

The mechanism of contest was allegiance rather than conquest. A local ruler declared for whichever power offered better protection, better terms or more immediate threat, expressed the choice through the name in the Friday sermon, and reversed it when conditions changed.

The cost to Egypt was continuous, and the article on the Tulunid dynasty makes the general argument that Syria was on balance a liability for any Egyptian power: expensive to hold, impossible to abandon, and demanding attention far from the revenue base.

The cost to Baghdad was different, since the Abbasid caliph had no forces of his own and the campaigns were conducted by whichever power controlled him, so the region was contested by the caliph's controllers rather than by the caliph.

The other actors complicate any simple picture. Byzantium was active in northern Syria through this period, local dynasties pursued their own interests, and after 1098 the crusader states added a further set of participants who had no interest in either caliphate's claims.

The general observation is that a contested march between two powers with no capacity to reach each other's heartland becomes a permanent arena rather than a route, and Syria functioned as exactly that for two centuries.

## The Armies

Both caliphates depended on imported professional soldiers and the consequences were similar enough to constitute a shared structural problem.

The Abbasid transformation came first and is described in the article on the Tulunid dynasty: caliphal armies were rebuilt around slave soldiers recruited from Central Asia, professional, loyal to their commanders personally, and vastly more effective than the tribal levies they replaced.

The political consequence at Baghdad arrived within a generation. Such troops became the decisive power at the centre, made and unmade caliphs, and the article on the Tulunid dynasty describes the hollowing out of central authority that followed.

The Fatimid army was assembled on the same principle from different sources, and the article on Fatimid Egypt names four imported groups: Berber troops from the dynasty's North African base, Turkic soldiers, African infantry and later Armenian contingents.

The political consequence at Cairo arrived later and was worse in one respect. Four groups organized by origin and quartered separately, as the article on the founding of Cairo describes, produced a factional geography that erupted in the crisis of the 1060s with the army fighting itself in the streets of the capital.

The identical outcome from different starting points is the finding. Both caliphates solved the problem of military effectiveness by importing professionals, both discovered that professionals paid in cash become the decisive political power, and both ended with caliphs controlled by soldiers.

The Tulunid case supplies the third instance, since the article on that dynasty identifies an army of imported groups held together only by pay and personal authority as the structural flaw that destroyed the regime after its founder's death.

The general observation is that a solution which works is not the same as a solution that is safe, and the medieval Islamic world's answer to military effectiveness reliably produced a specific political failure across three separate states within two centuries.

## Two Capitals

The cities themselves invite comparison and the comparison is informative, since each was purpose built by a caliphate to house a claim.

Baghdad was founded in 762 as a caliphal capital, planned on a scale and to a geometry that expressed the Abbasid conception of universal authority, and it became within a century among the largest cities in the world.

Cairo was founded in 969, and the article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo argues that it began as a closed royal and military enclosure rather than as a city, shut to the general population for roughly a century.

The difference in conception is real. Baghdad was built as a capital for an empire that already existed; Cairo was built as a seat for a caliphate that intended to acquire one, and the difference shows in the scale of each relative to the population it served.

The trajectories diverged. Baghdad contracted across the tenth and eleventh centuries as the caliphate's revenue and territory shrank, suffered repeatedly from factional violence, and never recovered its earlier scale. Cairo grew, absorbed the older commercial city beside it after 1168, and became the metropolis it remains.

The endings differ correspondingly. Baghdad was sacked in 1258 with enormous destruction, while Cairo passed through the change of 1171 without disruption and continued growing under every subsequent regime.

The lesson about purpose built capitals is the one the article on the founding of Cairo draws. Such foundations survive when they acquire functions beyond the ones they were built for, and Cairo acquired commerce, population and a religious institution of the first rank, none of which was in the plan.

The general observation is that a capital's fate tracks the fortunes of the state that built it with a lag, and comparing two capitals across the same centuries is a compact way of comparing the states.

## The Coinage War

Currency is where the rivalry becomes physically visible, and reading the coins is the most direct evidence available for how each side pressed its claim.

The function of coinage in this world is set out in the article on Fatimid Egypt: a coin carries the issuer's name and religious formulae, so it is a statement of sovereignty that circulates, and a coin found in someone else's territory is a claim travelling in a merchant's purse.

The Fatimid issues named the caliph, used formulae expressing the dynasty's doctrinal position, and were maintained at a high and consistent standard of gold fineness, which the article on Fatimid trade identifies as commercial infrastructure and which also made them widely acceptable.

The combination is the interesting part. A coin that merchants want because it is reliable will circulate widely, and a widely circulating coin carries its issuer's claim into places his armies cannot reach, so monetary quality and propaganda reinforced each other.

The Abbasid issues in this period were struck under the control of whichever power held Baghdad, naming the caliph and frequently the controlling ruler alongside, which is itself a record of the real distribution of power.

The quality comparison favours Cairo. Fatimid gold held its standard across long periods while the coinage of a contracting Iraqi economy under changing military masters did not maintain equivalent consistency.

The evidence value for historians is considerable, since coins are dated, named, provenanced when found in context, and assayable, which makes them a harder body of evidence than any chronicle.

The general observation is that a state's currency is simultaneously an economic instrument and a political statement, and the Fatimid case shows the two working together: a coin good enough to be wanted is a message nobody can refuse to carry.

## Wealth

The economic comparison is the clearest of all the criteria and the one where the verdict is least contested.

The Egyptian base was renewing itself. The article on taxation in early Islamic Egypt describes an agricultural system in which the flood deposits fresh silt annually and the basin system distributes it, which means the soil does not degrade under cultivation the way irrigated soils elsewhere do.

The Iraqi base was degrading. The irrigated agriculture of the southern alluvium depended on an enormous canal network requiring continuous maintenance, and salinization, canal neglect and the disruption of a major slave revolt in the late ninth century had reduced the cultivated area substantially before the period this article covers.

### Was Fatimid Egypt richer than Abbasid Iraq?

Almost certainly, and the reasons are structural. Egypt's flood based agriculture renewed its own soil annually while Iraq's irrigated system was degrading through salinization and canal neglect, and the eastern trade shifted from the Gulf route to the Red Sea across the same period. No figures exist for either, so the comparison rests on mechanism rather than measurement.

The trade shift compounded the agricultural difference. The article on Fatimid trade sets out how the eastern traffic moved from the Gulf route serving Iraq to the Red Sea route serving Egypt, for political rather than geographical reasons, and the transfer took revenue from one and gave it to the other.

The visible expenditure supports the conclusion. The Fatimids built a walled capital with two palaces, a great mosque, a house of learning and a fleet, and paid four imported military groups in cash, while Baghdad in the same period was contracting.

The figures cannot be quoted, following this series' practice throughout, since medieval revenue totals are rhetorical and no usable series exists for either state.

The qualification is that Iraq remained a substantial economy and Baghdad a great city, and decline relative to a rising Egypt is not collapse.

## Culture

The cultural comparison is where the verdict is most period dependent and where careless accounts go furthest wrong.

The Abbasid inheritance was enormous and it was earlier. The translation movement that rendered Greek philosophy, medicine, mathematics and astronomy into Arabic, the development of Arabic literary prose, the consolidation of legal and traditional scholarship, and the great names of Islamic science mostly belong to the eighth to tenth centuries, which is before or at the start of the Fatimid period in Egypt.

### How did Fatimid and Abbasid culture differ?

The Abbasid inheritance was larger and earlier, centred on the translation movement, literary prose and the consolidation of the religious sciences, with its great period already past by 969. Fatimid production was smaller, later and different in emphasis, strongest in luxury craft, architecture, mathematics and optics, and organized around a doctrinally committed court.

The Fatimid production was later, smaller and differently shaped. The article on al-Azhar describes a house of learning supplying books, materials and teaching across many subjects, and the article on Fatimid art and architecture treats the luxury craft that the dynasty patronized at the highest level anywhere.

The migration of talent is the most telling single piece of evidence. Scholars moved from Iraq to Egypt in this period, and the most famous case is the mathematician and optician who came from Basra to Cairo in the early eleventh century and did his major work there.

That direction of movement is exactly what one would expect if the money and the patronage had shifted, and it is more informative than any comparison of outputs, since scholars go where they are paid.

The comparison is nonetheless unfair to Baghdad in an obvious way. Comparing a century of Fatimid Cairo with three centuries of Abbasid Baghdad flatters Cairo, and comparing them at the same moment, say the year 1000, produces a much closer result.

The general observation is that cultural comparisons between polities are usually comparisons between periods in disguise, and specifying the period is the first thing an honest comparison does.

## Did They Fight

The rivalry was conducted mostly by other means, and the record of direct confrontation is thinner than the rhetoric suggests.

The theatre was Syria. Both powers claimed it, both intervened there repeatedly, and the campaigns of the tenth and eleventh centuries in that region are the closest thing to a Cairo Baghdad war.

### Did the two caliphates ever fight each other directly?

Rarely in a direct sense. The rivalry ran through Syria, where both intervened repeatedly and where local dynasties switched allegiance between them, and through propaganda, subsidy and the missionary network. Neither ever mounted an invasion aimed at the other's capital, and the Fatimids came closest to victory through an ally rather than an army.

The mechanism was proxy and allegiance rather than invasion. Local rulers in Syria and northern Mesopotamia switched the name in the Friday sermon according to which side was stronger or paying better, and the article on Fatimid Egypt notes that the sermon is where sovereignty was declared.

The nearest thing to a Fatimid victory came in 1058, when a Turkish commander who had turned against the Baghdad establishment took the city and had the sermon read in the Fatimid caliph's name for about a year.

That episode is worth dwelling on because of how it ended. The Fatimids did not exploit it, sent no adequate support, and the arrangement collapsed when the Seljuks arrived, which suggests the Cairo regime either could not or would not convert an extraordinary opportunity into a permanent gain.

The propaganda war was continuous and better documented. The Baghdad declaration of 1011 attacking Fatimid descent is one instrument, and the Fatimid missionary network described in the article on Shia Islam operated inside Abbasid territory throughout, which is the other.

### Did the caliphates use propaganda against each other?

Extensively, and it was their principal weapon. The Abbasids issued a public declaration in 1011 gathering jurists to deny Fatimid descent from the Prophet's family. The Fatimids ran a missionary organization inside Abbasid territory, and both used coinage, the Friday sermon and the patronage of scholars to press their claims.

The Seljuk response added a further instrument, since the network of endowed colleges established under Seljuk patronage from the mid eleventh century was among other things an apparatus for training Sunni scholars to answer Ismaili arguments.

The general observation is that a rivalry between two powers with no shared border and no capacity to reach each other's centre becomes a competition for allegiance in the space between them, which is what Syria was for two centuries.

## Which Was More Powerful

The direct question deserves a direct answer and it requires specifying a period.

In the tenth and early eleventh centuries the answer is the Fatimids, and it is not close. They governed their territory, commanded their own army, controlled their own revenue, projected force into Syria and across the Red Sea, and their caliph made his own decisions.

### Were the Fatimids more powerful than the Abbasids?

In the tenth and early eleventh centuries, decisively yes. The Fatimid caliph governed his territory, commanded his own army and controlled his own revenue, while the Abbasid caliph was a figurehead controlled by an Iranian dynasty. The Abbasids retained something the Fatimids never obtained, which was recognition across most of the Islamic world.

The Abbasid caliph in the same period governed nothing, commanded nothing and controlled nothing, and the article's earlier section on who actually governed sets out the arrangement.

The qualification is that the comparison misidentifies the units. The Abbasid caliph was powerless and the Abbasid caliphate as a symbolic system was extremely powerful, since rulers from Spain to Central Asia sought its recognition and used its name.

The recognition was worth something concrete. A ruler who received a diploma of investiture from Baghdad gained legitimacy with his own Sunni population, which is a real political asset that cost the caliph nothing to grant and that the Fatimids could not supply to anyone outside their own tradition.

The later period reverses part of the answer. After the crisis of the 1060s the Fatimid caliph became a figurehead too, and the Seljuk restoration gave the Abbasid caliph a somewhat better position, so by the late eleventh century the two were closer than they had been.

The verdict this article defends is that power and prestige separated. The Fatimids had capacity without recognition, the Abbasids had recognition without capacity, and neither found a way to exchange one for the other.

## Shia Rule and Sunni Rule

The doctrinal difference between the two regimes had less effect on how they governed than a reader might expect, and setting out why is one of this article's more useful corrections.

The article on Shia Islam and the Fatimid caliphate establishes the central fact for the Egyptian side: the Fatimids ruled a Sunni majority by accommodation, Sunni law was taught and applied throughout, and what changed publicly was a short list of items including the call to prayer formula, the name in the sermon, some added festivals and the calendar.

### How did Fatimid Shia rule compare to Abbasid Sunni rule?

Less differently than expected. The Fatimids governed a Sunni majority with Sunni law taught and applied, changing only a short list of public items. Both states used the same inherited machinery, the same legal framework for minorities and the same fiscal categories. The difference was sharp at the ruler's claim and shallow at the village.

The Abbasid side had its own complications, since the dynasty had at various points patronized theological positions that later Sunni orthodoxy rejected, and the relationship between caliphs and the scholarly establishment was frequently tense.

The administrative machinery was essentially the same in both. Both inherited the fiscal and bureaucratic apparatus of earlier empires, both used the same categories of taxation, and the article on taxation in early Islamic Egypt describes a system that operated identically under every regime that held the country.

The legal framework for non Muslims was the same framework in both, and the article on Copts under early Islamic rule describes its normal operation as protected but pressured with episodic enforcement.

The difference that mattered was at the top. A Fatimid caliph claimed an authority no Abbasid claimed, which shaped the court, the ceremonial and the missionary apparatus, and it barely reached the village.

### Were the Abbasids or Fatimids more tolerant?

The question is badly posed and neither ranks clearly. Both operated the same legal framework for non Muslims, both employed Christians and Jews at high levels, and both had episodes of severe enforcement. The Fatimid record includes one exceptionally harsh reign and long periods of unusual openness, and the Abbasid record varies similarly by ruler and decade.

The vocabulary problem the article on Copts identifies applies here too, since tolerance and persecution both presuppose answers, and the useful comparison is between specific practices in specific decades rather than between reputations.

## Why Baghdad Declined

The Abbasid trajectory needs explaining on its own terms, since it is often treated as a background condition rather than as a process with causes.

The military transformation came first. The article on the Tulunid dynasty describes the rebuilding of caliphal armies around slave soldiers from Central Asia, which produced effective troops who became the decisive political power at the centre within a generation.

The fiscal consequence followed. Paying professional armies in cash required reliable revenue, provinces were granted to commanders in lieu of pay, and a province assigned rather than administered stops sending money to the centre.

### Why did Baghdad decline while Cairo rose?

Baghdad's decline was structural and predated the Fatimids: an army that became the political power at the centre, provinces assigned to commanders rather than administered, an irrigated agricultural base degrading through salinization and neglect, and repeated internal conflict. Cairo rose on renewing agriculture, transit trade and a regime that governed its own territory directly.

The provincial losses accumulated. The article on the Tulunid dynasty describes Egypt's own separation in the 870s as one instance of a general pattern, and by the tenth century most of the caliphate's territory was held by dynasties that acknowledged the caliph and remitted nothing.

The agricultural decline compounded everything, as the section on wealth sets out.

The internal conflicts, including a great civil war early in the ninth century and a major slave revolt later, damaged both the capital and the productive base.

The arrival of the Iranian dynasty in 945 formalized a condition that had developed over the preceding century rather than creating it.

The general observation is that the Fatimid rise and the Abbasid decline were largely independent processes that happened to coincide, and treating either as the cause of the other misreads both.

## What Ordinary Muslims Did

The question of how the rivalry affected people who were neither rulers nor scholars deserves an answer, and the answer is deflating in a useful way.

The practical content of allegiance was the name in the Friday sermon, which is a matter for whoever governed the locality rather than for individuals, and a person praying in a mosque heard whichever name the local ruler had chosen.

### Did ordinary Muslims have to choose between the caliphates?

No, in any practical sense. Allegiance was expressed through the name in the Friday sermon, which was determined by whoever governed the locality, so a person heard whichever name the local ruler had chosen. Nobody was asked to profess anything, and in territories that changed hands the name changed without the population being consulted.

The consequence in contested territory was that the name changed when the ruler changed, sometimes repeatedly, without anyone being asked to profess anything, which the article on Shia Islam identifies as the general pattern of Fatimid religious governance.

The doctrinal question was separate and reached far fewer people. Becoming an adherent of the Fatimid tradition required entering the graded instruction the article on Shia Islam describes, which was restricted by design, so the vast majority of people under Fatimid rule were Sunnis who remained Sunnis.

The scholarly class experienced the rivalry more directly, since the arguments were theirs to make and the patronage on both sides went to people who could make them.

The merchant class was affected commercially rather than doctrinally, and the article on Fatimid trade describes networks that crossed the political division without difficulty.

### How did the rivalry affect trade routes?

Substantially, though the causation ran mostly the other way. The eastern trade shifted from the Gulf route serving Iraq to the Red Sea route serving Egypt because Egypt offered better security, a trusted currency and moderate customs while Iraq's centre weakened. The shift strengthened the Fatimids rather than being caused by them.

The pilgrimage was the one point where the rivalry touched ordinary religious life directly, since control of the holy cities and the safety of the routes to them mattered to everyone, and both powers sought influence there.

The general observation is that great political rivalries usually matter less to ordinary people than the accounts written by their participants suggest, and the test is always what a person would have been asked to do differently.

## The Seljuk Intervention

The arrival of the Seljuk Turks in the mid eleventh century changed the balance decisively and it deserves its own treatment.

The event was the Seljuk capture of Baghdad in 1055, replacing the Iranian dynasty that had controlled the caliphate for over a century with a Turkish power that presented itself as a Sunni restoration.

### How did the Seljuks change the balance between them?

Decisively. Arriving in Baghdad in 1055 as Sunni restorers, they gave the Abbasid caliphate military backing it had lacked for a century, pushed the Fatimids out of Syria, took Jerusalem in 1073, and funded a network of endowed colleges that trained scholars to answer Ismaili arguments. The Fatimid position never recovered.

The military consequence was immediate. Seljuk forces pushed into Syria, reversed Fatimid gains, and took Jerusalem in 1073, which ended the Fatimid position in the region that had been the theatre of the rivalry.

The doctrinal consequence was equally significant. Seljuk patronage funded the network of endowed colleges that produced a generation of Sunni scholars, and the article on al-Azhar describes the madrasa as a purpose built institution with appointed teachers and a defined curriculum, which is a far more effective instrument for mass instruction than the Fatimid graded initiation could ever be.

The asymmetry is the point. The article on Shia Islam argues that the Fatimid transmission model made mass conversion impossible by design, and the Seljuks funded an institution designed for exactly the mass instruction the Fatimids could not attempt.

The timing was disastrous for Cairo, since the Seljuk pressure arrived at the same moment as the internal crisis of the 1060s that the article on Fatimid Egypt describes, so the regime faced an external challenge while its own army was fighting itself.

The general observation is that the rivalry was never settled between the two caliphates. A third power arrived, took the Sunni side, and altered the balance permanently, which is how most long standing rivalries actually end.

## How It Ended

The rivalry's conclusion is instructive because neither side won it.

The Fatimid end came in 1171, when a Kurdish commander serving a Syrian ruler who himself served the Seljuk order took control of Egypt and abolished the caliphate by having the Abbasid caliph's name read in the Friday sermon.

### What happened to the rivalry in the end?

Neither side won. The Fatimid caliphate was abolished in 1171 by a Kurdish commander who simply had the Abbasid name read in the Friday sermon, and it met no resistance. The Abbasid caliphate survived another 87 years as a symbolic institution before Baghdad was sacked by the Mongols in 1258. Both were ended by third parties.

The manner of the ending is the striking part and the article on Fatimid Egypt makes it the decisive evidence for a wider argument: a caliphate that had lasted two centuries was abolished by a change of name and met no resistance, which shows how little its doctrine had penetrated the population it governed.

The Abbasid end came in 1258, when the Mongols took Baghdad and killed the caliph, ending an institution that had existed since 750.

The comparison of the two endings is worth drawing. The Fatimid caliphate ended because it had no constituency; the Abbasid caliphate ended because a military catastrophe destroyed the city it sat in, and it had constituency enough that its abolition was felt across the Sunni world as a rupture.

The survival of the idea is the last element. A branch of the Abbasid family was maintained in Cairo under Mamluk protection afterward as a symbolic caliphate, which is the Abbasid title being kept alive as an instrument by a power that wanted its legitimating value, and the article on al-Azhar describes the same regime's investment in Sunni religious institutions.

The irony in that arrangement is considerable, since the city built to house a rival caliphate ended by housing a puppet version of the one it had opposed.

### Which caliphate produced more lasting institutions?

The Abbasids, on any reckoning, since the caliphal ideal itself, the consolidation of Sunni law and the translated Greek inheritance all descend from their period. The Fatimid legacy is narrower and remarkably durable: the city of Cairo, al-Azhar, and a religious tradition that survives in several countries.

## Studying the Comparison

For a student, this topic organizes around one correction and a short list of contrasts.

The correction is that there were three caliphates and not one, from 929 to 1031, and two afterward until 1171.

The contrasts to hold are that the Fatimids governed and the Abbasids were governed; that the Fatimid revenue base was renewing and the Abbasid one degrading; that the Abbasids held recognition across the Sunni world and the Fatimids held almost none outside their territory; and that both caliphs ended the period as figureheads controlled by military men.

The dates to hold are 909 for the Fatimid proclamation, 929 for the Spanish one, 945 for the Iranian takeover of Baghdad, 969 for the Fatimid arrival in Egypt, 1031 for the Spanish collapse, 1055 for the Seljuk arrival, 1171 for the Fatimid end and 1258 for the Abbasid one.

## Myths Worth Correcting

Six claims about this rivalry circulate and each misleads.

The first is that the Islamic world had one caliph. It had three from 929 to 1031 and two afterward until 1171, and the assumption of a single caliphate is the largest single error in popular accounts of the period.

The second is that the Fatimids seceded from the Abbasids. They never accepted Abbasid authority, holding that the imamate had passed through a different line from the beginning, so the claim was a rival rather than a breakaway.

The third is that the Abbasid caliph in this period was a powerful ruler. He was a figurehead controlled first by an Iranian dynasty and then by the Seljuks, and had been since 945.

The fourth is that the two caliphates fought major wars. They competed through Syria, propaganda and allegiance, never invaded each other's heartland, and the Fatimids' nearest approach to victory came through an ally who was not supported.

The fifth is that Shia rule meant a Shia Egypt. The article on Shia Islam establishes that Egypt remained Sunni throughout and that the Fatimids governed by accommodation, which is why the restoration in 1171 cost nothing.

The sixth is that the rivalry was resolved. Neither side defeated the other. A Kurdish commander ended one and the Mongols ended the other, both of them third parties.

A seventh belief is a framing error rather than a myth, which is treating the rivalry as fundamentally religious. The doctrinal difference was real and the competition was for territory, revenue, allegiance and the trade routes, in which doctrine functioned as the vocabulary rather than the object.

## The Verdict

The argument this article has defended is that power and prestige separated in this period and that the separation is the whole story.

The Fatimids had capacity. They governed their territory, commanded their army, controlled their revenue, built a capital, funded a fleet and made their own decisions for a century, which is more than the Abbasid caliph did at any point after 945.

The Abbasids had recognition. Rulers who owed them nothing and sent them nothing still wanted their diplomas, still named them in the sermon, and still treated their endorsement as worth having, which is a real asset that the Fatimids never obtained anywhere outside their own tradition.

Neither could convert one into the other. The Fatimids spent two centuries and an enormous missionary apparatus attempting to buy recognition and never achieved it, for the structural reason the article on Shia Islam identifies: a doctrine transmitted by graded initiation cannot become general. The Abbasids could not convert prestige into troops or revenue, because the people who valued their endorsement valued it precisely because it cost them nothing.

The verdict on greatness therefore depends entirely on which currency is being counted, and this article declines to pick one. In wealth, military capacity, administrative reach and the vigour of its court, the Fatimid caliphate of the tenth and early eleventh centuries was the stronger state. In recognition, durability, institutional legacy and consequence for the subsequent shape of Islamic civilization, the Abbasid caliphate was the more significant institution.

### Which was greater, the Fatimid or Abbasid Caliphate?

It depends which currency is counted, and the honest answer refuses to pick. The Fatimids were the stronger state in wealth, military capacity and administrative reach during the tenth and early eleventh centuries. The Abbasids were the more significant institution in recognition, duration and legacy, and the caliphal ideal itself descends from them.

The qualifications are those stated throughout. No revenue figures are quotable for either. The cultural comparison depends heavily on which period is chosen. The extent of Fatimid recognition outside Egypt is documented unevenly. And both states' internal conditions varied enormously across the centuries being compared.

What remains is the observation this article opened with. Three men called themselves caliph in the year 1000. The one who could raise an army had no authority beyond his borders, the one whose authority reached across a continent could not leave his palace, and neither of them ended the century that followed in possession of anything he had wanted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Fatimid Egypt differ from Abbasid rule?

Most fundamentally in whether the caliph actually governed. The Fatimid caliph ran his state for most of the tenth and eleventh centuries, commanding an army paid from Egyptian revenue and making his own decisions, while the Abbasid caliph after 945 was a figurehead controlled by whichever military power held Baghdad. The Fatimids also claimed a different kind of authority, since their caliph was also an imam with infallible guidance, and they built a purpose made capital, a missionary organization and a commercial policy that the Abbasid centre in this period could not match.

### Q: Were the Fatimids more powerful than the Abbasids?

In the tenth and early eleventh centuries, decisively. The Fatimid caliph governed his territory, commanded his own army and fleet, controlled his own revenue and projected force into Syria and across the Red Sea. The Abbasid caliph in the same decades governed nothing, having been reduced to a figurehead by an Iranian dynasty in 945. The qualification is that the comparison misidentifies the units: the Abbasid caliph was powerless while the Abbasid caliphate as a symbolic system remained extremely valuable, since rulers across the Sunni world sought its recognition.

### Q: How did Fatimid Shia rule compare to Abbasid Sunni rule?

Less differently than expected at the level of daily governance. The Fatimids ruled a Sunni majority by accommodation, with Sunni law taught and applied throughout and only a short list of public changes including the call to prayer formula, the name in the sermon, some added festivals and the calendar. Both states used essentially the same inherited administrative machinery, the same fiscal categories and the same legal framework for non Muslims. The doctrinal difference was sharp at the level of the ruler's claim and shallow at the level of the village.

### Q: Was Fatimid Egypt richer than Abbasid Iraq?

Almost certainly during this period, and the reasons are structural rather than a matter of policy alone. Egypt's flood based agriculture renewed its own soil annually through fresh silt, while Iraq's irrigated system was degrading through salinization, canal neglect and the disruption of a major slave revolt. The eastern trade shifted from the Gulf route serving Iraq to the Red Sea route serving Egypt across the same centuries. No usable revenue figures exist for either state, so the comparison rests on mechanism and on visible expenditure rather than on measurement.

### Q: Why did the Fatimids break from the Abbasids?

They never broke away, because they never accepted Abbasid authority in the first place. The movement held that the imamate had passed from the Prophet through his daughter and a designated line of imams, and that the Abbasids, descended from the Prophet's uncle, were usurpers of an office they had no right to. The Fatimid claim was therefore a rival from its proclamation in 909 rather than a secession from an accepted order, which is why no compromise between the two was ever conceivable.

### Q: How did Fatimid and Abbasid culture differ?

The Abbasid inheritance was larger and earlier, built on the translation movement that rendered Greek philosophy, medicine and mathematics into Arabic, on the development of Arabic literary prose, and on the consolidation of the religious sciences, most of which belongs to the eighth to tenth centuries. Fatimid production was later, smaller and differently shaped, strongest in luxury craft, architecture, optics and mathematics, and organized around a doctrinally committed court with its own house of learning. The migration of scholars from Iraq to Egypt in this period is the most telling evidence of where the patronage had moved.

### Q: Were the Abbasids or Fatimids more tolerant?

The question is badly posed and neither ranks clearly. Both operated the same legal framework for non Muslims, described elsewhere in this series as protected but pressured with episodic enforcement. Both employed Christians and Jews at high levels in their administrations. Both had periods of severe enforcement and long periods of practical openness. The Fatimid record includes one exceptionally harsh reign and considerable openness before and after it, and the Abbasid record varies similarly by ruler and decade. Useful comparison is between specific practices in specific decades, not between reputations.

### Q: Which was greater, the Fatimid or Abbasid Caliphate?

It depends entirely on which currency is counted, and the honest answer declines to pick one. In wealth, military capacity, administrative reach and the vigour of its court, the Fatimid caliphate of the tenth and early eleventh centuries was the stronger state by a clear margin. In recognition across the Islamic world, in duration, in institutional legacy and in consequence for the subsequent shape of Islamic civilization, the Abbasid caliphate was the more significant institution. The caliphal ideal itself descends from the Abbasids, and Cairo and al-Azhar descend from the Fatimids.

### Q: Did the two caliphates ever fight each other directly?

Rarely in any direct sense, and neither ever mounted an invasion aimed at the other's capital. The rivalry ran through Syria, where both intervened repeatedly and where local dynasties switched the name in the Friday sermon according to which side was stronger or paying better. It also ran through propaganda, subsidy and the Fatimid missionary network operating inside Abbasid territory. The nearest approach to a Fatimid victory came in 1058 through an allied Turkish commander in Baghdad, and Cairo failed to support him.

### Q: Who controlled the Abbasid caliph during this period?

An Iranian dynasty from the Caspian region took Baghdad in 945 and controlled the caliphate for over a century, followed by the Seljuk Turks from 1055. The first arrangement contained a striking paradox: the dynasty controlling the Sunni caliph was itself Shia of a different branch from the Fatimids, and it neither abolished the caliphate nor transferred recognition to Cairo, because a Sunni caliph under its control was a useful instrument that legitimated its rule over a Sunni population. The caliph remained the source of legitimacy for both and exercised no independent power.

### Q: How many caliphates existed at the same time?

Three, for roughly a century. The Abbasids in Baghdad had held the title since 750. The Fatimids proclaimed theirs in North Africa in 909 and transferred it to Egypt when their caliph arrived in 973. The Umayyad ruler of Córdoba declared himself caliph in 929, partly in response to the Fatimid proclamation which threatened his position in North Africa. All three ran simultaneously until the Spanish caliphate collapsed in 1031, leaving Cairo and Baghdad as the main rivalry until 1171.

### Q: Did ordinary Muslims have to choose between the caliphates?

Not in any practical sense. Allegiance was expressed through the name recited in the Friday sermon, which was determined by whoever governed the locality, so a person simply heard whichever name the local ruler had chosen. In contested territory such as Syria, the name changed when the ruler changed, sometimes repeatedly, and nobody was asked to profess anything. Becoming an actual adherent of the Fatimid tradition required entering a graded programme of instruction that was restricted by design, so the overwhelming majority under Fatimid rule remained Sunnis.

### Q: How did each caliphate claim legitimacy?

The Abbasids claimed descent from the Prophet's uncle, acclamation by the community and the endorsement of the scholarly establishment, which makes the caliph the legitimate holder of an office. The Fatimids claimed descent from the Prophet's daughter through a line of designated imams, each possessing inner knowledge of the revelation and protection from error, which makes the caliph a different kind of being rather than a better officeholder. The asymmetry is why no adjudication was possible: the two were not claims to the same thing.

### Q: Why did Baghdad decline while Cairo rose?

The two processes were largely independent and happened to coincide. Baghdad's decline was structural and predated the Fatimids: an army rebuilt around slave soldiers became the decisive political power at the centre, provinces were assigned to commanders rather than administered, an irrigated agricultural base degraded through salinization and neglect, and a great civil war and a major slave revolt damaged both the capital and the productive base. Cairo rose on renewing flood based agriculture, a transit trade that was shifting toward the Red Sea, and a regime that governed directly.

### Q: How did the rivalry affect trade routes?

Substantially, though the causation ran mostly the other way. The eastern trade shifted from the Gulf route serving Iraq to the Red Sea route serving Egypt across the tenth and eleventh centuries, because Egypt under a stable regime offered better security, a gold coin merchants trusted without assay, moderate customs and direct access to Mediterranean buyers, while the Iraqi centre weakened. The shift strengthened the Fatimids considerably rather than being caused by them, and it is a clean demonstration that geography sets possibilities while politics decides which are used.

### Q: What happened to the rivalry in the end?

Neither side won it. The Fatimid caliphate was abolished in 1171 by a Kurdish commander who simply had the Abbasid caliph's name read in the Friday sermon, and the change met no resistance at all, which is the strongest evidence that Fatimid doctrine had never taken hold among the population. The Abbasid caliphate survived another 87 years as a symbolic institution before the Mongols sacked Baghdad and killed the caliph in 1258. Both were ended by third parties rather than by each other.

### Q: Did the caliphates use propaganda against each other?

Extensively, and it was their principal weapon given that neither could reach the other militarily. The Abbasids issued a public declaration from Baghdad in 1011, gathering jurists and notables to deny that the Fatimids descended from the Prophet's family at all, which attacked the genealogy rather than the doctrine because the genealogy was the more practical target. The Fatimids ran a missionary organization inside Abbasid territory throughout. Both used coinage, the Friday sermon and the patronage of scholars to press their claims continuously.

### Q: Which caliphate produced more lasting institutions?

The Abbasids, on any reasonable reckoning. The caliphal ideal itself, the consolidation of Sunni legal scholarship, the great translated inheritance of Greek learning and much of the administrative vocabulary of later Islamic states all descend from their period. The Fatimid institutional legacy is narrower and remarkably durable nonetheless: the city of Cairo, which grew from their walled palace enclosure into one of the world's great cities, the mosque of al-Azhar, which became the foremost school of the Sunni world, and a religious tradition that survives in several countries.

### Q: How did the Seljuks change the balance between them?

Decisively and permanently. Arriving in Baghdad in 1055 as Sunni restorers, they gave the Abbasid caliphate the military backing it had lacked for over a century, pushed Fatimid forces out of Syria, and took Jerusalem in 1073. Equally important, Seljuk patronage funded a network of endowed colleges producing Sunni scholars trained to answer Ismaili arguments, which was an instrument for mass instruction that the Fatimid model of graded initiation could not match by design. The timing coincided with the Fatimid internal crisis of the 1060s.

### Q: Were there other rival caliphates besides these two?

Yes. The Umayyad ruler of Córdoba took the title in 929 and his line held it until the Spanish caliphate fragmented in 1031, so three caliphates ran simultaneously for a century. Various other claimants appeared at other times and places with less durability. After 1258 a branch of the Abbasid family was maintained in Cairo under Mamluk protection as a symbolic caliphate, which is the title being preserved as an instrument by a power that valued its legitimating function, and the irony of Cairo housing an Abbasid caliph is considerable.
