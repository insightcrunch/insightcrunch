Strong writing is shown, not just described. That is the claim this article tests, and it states it at the start so every paragraph that follows can be measured against it: a set of model paragraphs for Gatsby essays, each sentence annotated to name the move it makes, teaches more about analytical writing than any catalogue of rules. Rules name the parts. Models show the parts working. When a reader watches a point get made, then sees evidence folded into the sentence that carries it, then watches that evidence get read closely for what its language does, the abstract advice about analysis stops being a slogan and becomes a visible sequence. That sequence is what the annotated examples below make concrete, sentence by sentence, so that the components the stage articles describe can be seen combining on the page.

The stage articles exist to teach the components separately, and this article is built to keep a clean boundary with them. One teaches how to frame a thesis, another how to order an argument, another how to choose and place quotations, others how to open and close an essay. This article owns the model-paragraphs resource alone. It does not re-teach the thesis, because the thesis article owns that ground. It does not rehearse the essay skeleton, because the structure article owns that ground. What it owns is the finished paragraph as a unit: the moment where claim, quotation, and commentary stop being three items on a checklist and become one continuous piece of thinking. That ownership matters, because it is the difference between knowing the vocabulary of analysis and being able to hear the music of it in a paragraph that holds together.

The complication arrives early and honestly. Hand a reader a set of excellent paragraphs and some readers will copy them. They will lift a sentence about the green light into an essay about the green light, or borrow a formulation about Daisy's voice and wear it as if it were their own thinking. Copying is the failure mode of every model, and this article treats it as a live risk rather than a footnote. The answer is not to withhold the models. The answer is to make the transferable moves explicit, to name what each sentence is doing in language that travels, so that a reader learns a move rather than memorizing a paragraph. A move survives transplantation. A memorized paragraph dies the moment the prompt changes. Everything in the annotated set is arranged to teach the first and starve the second.

## Why Seeing Beats Describing

There is a simple reason examples out-teach rules in writing instruction, and it has nothing to do with laziness and everything to do with how craft knowledge transfers. A rule is general. It says that analysis should connect technique to meaning, or that a paragraph should develop its point rather than restate it. Generality is the rule's strength and its limit: it applies everywhere, so it shows nothing in particular. A model paragraph is particular. It shows one writer handling one quotation about one idea in one sequence of sentences, and in that particularity the reader can watch decisions being made. The decision to open with a tension rather than a summary. The decision to let the quotation arrive inside a clause instead of after a colon. The decision to spend two sentences on a single adjective. These are the decisions rules cannot fully specify, and they are exactly the decisions that separate competent writing from strong writing.

The annotation layer exists to make those decisions legible. A model without annotation is a beautiful object with no instructions; a reader admires it and learns little. Annotation turns the object into a lesson by naming the move each sentence makes, in terms that describe function rather than content. It does not say "this sentence is about the green light," which would teach nothing portable. It says "this sentence embeds the quotation inside the claim," which names a maneuver any writer can attempt with any quotation on any topic. The pairing of model and annotation is the whole method: the sentence shows, the annotation tells what was shown, and the reader's job is to carry the telling into new territory. That is how a resource stays distinct from copying, and it is why the annotated set below is the center of this article rather than its decoration.

## The Anatomy of a Working Paragraph

A working paragraph has three visible parts, but the parts are not the point. The point is the joints between them. Point, evidence, and analysis are the standard vocabulary, and the standard vocabulary is accurate as far as it goes, but it misleads when it suggests that a paragraph is assembled in three clean stages: first the claim, then the quote, then the comment. Strong paragraphs do not stage their parts. They interleave them. The claim sharpens as the evidence arrives. The evidence means more because the claim has prepared a place for it. The analysis begins before the quotation ends, because the way the quotation is introduced already starts to read it. Watch for that interleaving in every model below, and the anatomy becomes less like a sandwich and more like a braid: three strands that stay distinct and keep crossing.

The point strand is the argumentative spine. It is not a topic label, and the models make that distinction sharp. "This paragraph is about the green light" is a label; it tells the reader where to look and nothing about what to think. "The green light matters because it makes desire permanent and satisfaction impossible" is a point; it takes a position that a reader could dispute, which is the minimum requirement for analysis to have anything to do. Every model below opens with a sentence that could be argued with, and the annotation names that opening move so it can be recognized and repeated. A paragraph that opens with a label spends its remaining sentences decorating a fact. A paragraph that opens with a point spends them proving a case. The difference is audible within one sentence, and it is the first thing the annotated set teaches the ear to hear.

The evidence strand is the quotation, and in the models it never appears as a guest dropped into the paragraph by a colon and left to speak for itself. Evidence in these paragraphs is embedded, which means the writer's sentence continues through the quotation, supplying the grammar that makes the quoted words mean something in context. "Fitzgerald describes the light as the thing Gatsby believed in, 'the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us,' and the grammar of that clause does the thinking" is a sentence in which the quotation is load-bearing: remove it and the sentence collapses, because the claim about the clause depends on the words the clause contains. That is what embedded means in practice, not a formatting trick but a structural dependency. The quotation article in this series teaches the mechanics of embedding; the models show the mechanics paying off, the sentence standing or falling on the words it has folded in.

The analysis strand is where the paragraph earns its mark, and it is the strand most often faked. Analysis is not a reaction to the quotation, not a restatement of it in the writer's own words, and not a label for the technique it displays. It is an account of how the quotation's language produces its effect, followed by an account of why that effect matters to the argument. When a model sentence says that "orgastic" turns longing into something bodily while "recedes before us" makes retreat the light's permanent condition, it is doing two jobs at once: it is reading the words, and it is connecting the reading to the paragraph's claim about desire that defeats itself. That double job is the signature of real analysis. It cannot be done by a sentence that merely notices a metaphor. It requires the writer to stay with the language long enough to say what the language does, and then to lift that doing back up to the point. The models below perform that lifting repeatedly, and the annotations mark each lift, so the move can be isolated and practiced.

The braid image also explains why the models resist being reduced to formulas. A formula assigns each sentence a fixed job: sentence one makes the point, sentence two gives the quotation, sentence three analyzes. The models show sentences doing double duty constantly: the tension sentence also introduces the evidence, the embedding sentence also begins the analysis, the development sentence also returns to the claim. That doubling is what makes the paragraphs feel like thinking rather than assembly, and it is why the annotation sometimes names more than one effect for a single sentence. Writers learning from the set should aim for that doubling deliberately. A sentence that only does one job is not wrong, but a sentence that does two is stronger, and the strongest paragraphs in the set are the ones where every sentence carries more than its label. Unity, in the end, is not the absence of parts but the density of their overlap.

### What makes a point arguable rather than factual?

A point becomes arguable the moment it takes a position a reasonable reader could dispute. "Gatsby loves Daisy" is plot fact; "Gatsby loves the idea of Daisy more than Daisy herself" is arguable, forcing the paragraph to marshal evidence. If a sentence cannot be contradicted without contradicting the novel, it is a fact wearing a point's clothes.

The models below apply this test in their first sentences, and the application is worth watching closely because the arguable point is doing structural work, not just setting a topic. Consider the difference between opening a paragraph with "This paragraph will discuss the valley of ashes" and opening it with "The valley of ashes is where the novel's glitter turns into geography." The first sentence promises a tour. The second makes a claim about conversion, about one register of the novel being transformed into another, and that claim immediately creates obligations: the paragraph must now show the glitter, show the geography, and show the transformation. Those obligations are what give the subsequent sentences their direction. Without them, evidence drifts; with them, every quotation arrives with a job already assigned. Arguability is therefore not a stylistic preference but a planning device. It tells the writer what the paragraph must accomplish before the paragraph begins.

There is a subtler version of the same lesson inside the models, which is that the strongest points contain their own tension. "Gatsby's greatness and his ruin come from the same act of will" does not just argue; it sets two outcomes against one cause, and the friction between them pulls the reader forward. A point without tension can still be arguable, but a point with tension is self-propelling, because the reader wants to see how one cause can produce opposite effects. The annotation calls this move "adds the tension" when it appears as a second sentence, but many of the models build the tension into the opening claim itself, which is the more economical version. Writers learning from the set should practice both: the two-sentence version, where the claim lands and then a complication sharpens it, and the one-sentence version, where the claim arrives already carrying its own counterweight. Either way, the goal is the same, which is a paragraph that has something to resolve rather than something to report.

### How does embedded quotation change what evidence does?

Embedded quotation turns evidence from illustration into argument. A dropped-in quotation shows the text contains the claim; an embedded one sits inside the writer's grammar, so claim and evidence share one syntactic life. Examiners reward that integration because it proves the writer thinks with evidence, not just finds it.

The change is structural rather than cosmetic. "Fitzgerald describes the light as the thing Gatsby believed in, 'the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us,' and the grammar of that clause does the thinking" cannot be split into claim plus quote without losing its force, because the sentence's argument is about the quoted clause's grammar, and the clause has to be present for the argument to exist. The evidence is not supporting the sentence from outside; it is inside the sentence, doing the work the sentence describes.

The models demonstrate several embedding shapes worth stealing as moves: the quotation as the object of a verb of description, the quotation as an appositive renaming what came before it, the short quoted phrase tucked into a longer analytical sentence where it functions as the sentence's key term. Each shape keeps the writer's voice in control of the sentence while letting Fitzgerald's language do the persuading. The annotation marks these embeddings explicitly, because the difference between a quotation that is visited and a quotation that is inhabited is one of the clearest dividers between mid-band and top-band writing, and it is a divider that can be practiced sentence by sentence.

There is also a discipline hidden inside the embedding move, which is selectivity. The models quote briefly, often three to eight words at a time, and the brevity is not timidity but control. A long quotation hands the paragraph over to Fitzgerald; the writer becomes a tour guide pointing at someone else's prose. A short quotation keeps the writer in charge, because a short phrase must be placed, introduced, and read, all of which are analytical acts. The models' longest quotations are complete clauses, never full sentences, and even those arrive inside sentences that are already analyzing them. The rule of thumb the set implies is that the writer's words should always outnumber the quoted words in any paragraph, and usually by a wide margin. Evidence should season the paragraph, not constitute it. A paragraph that is mostly quotation is a paragraph that has outsourced its thinking, and no annotation can rescue thinking that was never done.

### What separates technique named from method understood?

Naming a technique labels it; understanding a method explains its cause and effect. "Fitzgerald uses a metaphor" could be written by anyone who noticed it. "The metaphor holds two truths together: the future is desired and unreachable" could only be written by someone who asked what the device achieves. The second sentence makes the quotation mean more.

The difference shows up in a single sentence, and examiners hear it instantly, because the first kind adds nothing to the quotation it follows. The models are built to demonstrate the second kind repeatedly, and the demonstration follows a pattern worth internalizing. First the model identifies the device in the act of reading it, not as a separate labeling step but folded into the analysis: the sentence about "orgastic" does not announce "this is diction" before discussing the word, it simply discusses the word with enough precision that the attention to language is visible. Then the model connects the device to an effect: the word turns longing bodily, the image makes retreat permanent. Then, and this is the step most paragraphs skip, the model connects the effect to the argument: the bodily longing and the permanent retreat are exactly what the paragraph's claim about self-defeating desire requires. Three moves, often in two sentences, and the paragraph has traveled from technique to meaning to thesis without ever pausing to label. The annotation names these as "reads the language," "names the method," and "connects method to meaning," and the naming is meant to make the pattern portable: any device, any effect, any argument, the same three-step climb.

There is a reason the models avoid the vocabulary of technique-spotting even while performing technique analysis, and the reason is that the vocabulary can become a substitute for the thinking. A paragraph that says "the writer uses juxtaposition to show contrast" has said almost nothing, because juxtaposition means contrast and the sentence has traveled in a circle. The models instead describe what is being placed next to what and what the placement produces: enchantment set against repulsion in a single balanced clause, so the sentence performs the narrator's divided stance. That is juxtaposition understood as method rather than named as technique, and it teaches the deeper lesson that the device words are optional. A writer who can describe the effect precisely does not need the term; a writer who has only the term cannot describe the effect. The models keep the terms light and the descriptions heavy, which is the correct ratio for top-band work.

### How does a paragraph's final sentence move forward rather than backward?

A final sentence moves forward when it says something the paragraph has earned but not yet said. Restating the opening is the paragraph tapping its own shoulder. The models instead spend their earned knowledge: redefining a term, drawing a consequence, or widening the scope the analysis now permits.

The backward version is the most common weak ending in student writing: the paragraph proves its point and then, nervous that the examiner missed it, restates the point as if repetition were emphasis. "The green light therefore shows Gatsby's dream is unreachable" adds nothing to a paragraph that has just shown exactly that. The models' final sentences work differently, taking the reading the paragraph has built and extending it one step further, into a consequence, a redefinition, or a widening of scope that the earlier sentences made possible but did not themselves state.

Watch how the endings operate across the set. The American Dream paragraph does not end by repeating that the dream recedes; it ends by redefining the prompt's central term, arguing that "the American Dream" is accurate only if it is allowed to mean a structure of desire built to defeat itself. That is a new claim, but it is a claim the paragraph has earned, because every sentence has been building toward exactly that redefinition. The Nick paragraph ends by widening from the narrator to the reader's method: the gap between Nick's stated principle and his actual practice is "where the reader learns to think," which turns a character observation into a statement about how the novel teaches. The ending paragraph about the novel's final page ends with a lesson for essay writing itself, that the strongest closings do not tie the knot but widen the water. Each of these final sentences could stand as the start of a new paragraph, and that is the test: a forward-moving ending opens a door, while a backward-moving ending closes one that was already shut.

The practical version of this move is to ask, at the paragraph's end, what the analysis now permits the writer to claim that could not have been claimed at the start. At the start, the writer had a point and a plan. At the end, the writer has evidence that has been read closely and connected to the argument, which means the writer now knows more than the opening sentence knew. The final sentence should spend that new knowledge. It can draw the consequence the evidence implies, name the larger pattern the instance belongs to, or turn the reading back on the question with a sharper formulation. What it must not do is summarize, because summary treats the paragraph as a closed loop, while analysis is an open one. The annotation marks these endings as "closes by extending" or "returns to the claim with new force," and the two labels describe the same forward motion from different angles: extension outward to consequence, return inward to a claim now carrying the weight of everything proved.

### What makes analysis specific to Gatsby rather than portable to any novel?

Analysis is Gatsby-specific when it dies upon transplantation: replace this novel's particulars with another book's and the sentences stop working. "The author uses vivid imagery" survives anywhere, so it says nothing here. "Ashes growing like wheat parodies agriculture" belongs to Fitzgerald's valley alone, which proves the writer read the passage.

Specificity is not decoration; it is the proof that the writer has actually read the passage rather than applying a template to it. Examiners reward it because it cannot be faked by a writer who has only memorized moves, and the transplant test catches the failure every time: "The author uses vivid imagery to create atmosphere" survives transplantation onto any novel ever written, which is exactly why it says nothing about this one, while "Ashes growing like wheat parodies agriculture, suggesting that what this economy harvests is ruin dressed in the language of fertility" dies the moment it leaves the valley of ashes, because its every term belongs to Fitzgerald's image and the world the image organizes.

The models achieve specificity by staying inside the novel's language and its world at every step, and the annotation shows how. When the Daisy paragraph reads "full of money," it does not drift into a general essay about wealth and speech; it stays with the purse that jingles, the cold currency, the cage, all terms drawn from the quotation's own implications and the novel's class geography. When the Eckleburg paragraph discusses the billboard, it does not generalize about religion in literature; it stays with Wilson's misreading, the retinas a yard high, the sight without judgment that belongs to this valley and no other. The discipline is to let the novel's particulars do the analytical work instead of importing generalities to do it for them. A writer who has learned the moves in the abstract must still do the reading in the particular, and the models show what that reading looks like when it refuses to leave the text.

This is also where the copying danger meets its natural limit, and the limit is worth stating plainly. A paragraph about the green light cannot be copied into an essay about the valley of ashes, because its specificity is its substance; lift the sentences and they stop making sense. That is the paradox the whole article is built on: the more specific a model is, the less copyable it becomes, and the more it teaches. General models teach templates, and templates get copied. Specific models teach moves, because the only way to use a specific model on a new topic is to perform the move yourself with new material. The annotated set is therefore deliberately concrete, anchored in particular quotations and particular readings, precisely so that imitation is useless and transfer is the only option. Specificity is the copy protection, and it is also the pedagogy.

The American Dream paragraph and the ending paragraph take the novel's largest ideas. The Gatsby and Daisy paragraphs take its central figures. The valley of ashes and Nick paragraphs take its most analyzed image and its narration. Together they demonstrate that the same moves work across every kind of material, which is the transferability claim in action. | "Beat on" is stubborn and percussive, effort as pulse, while "borne back" surrenders to the water in the same breath, so the sentence performs the defeat it names. | Reads the language: sets the two verbs against each other and hears the sentence enact its meaning. |
| The shift from "I" to "we" is the move that matters most: the narrator drafts every reader into the boat, making aspiration's futility a shared condition rather than one man's tragedy. | Names the method: identifies the pronoun shift as the paragraph's key technical observation. |
| This is the same future that earlier "eluded us" even as we promised that "tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms farther," the green light's recession now written as physics. | Adds a second piece of evidence: the earlier passage as the same motion in a different register. |
| Fitzgerald's wager is that a sentence can be both elegy and indictment, mourning the dream while exposing its machinery. | Connects method to meaning: names what the double register achieves. |
| The ending earns its fame not by resolving the plot but by refusing to, letting the current keep its argument. | Develops the consequence: the refusal as the source of the ending's power. |
| That refusal is the paragraph's lesson for essay writing itself: the strongest closings do not tie the knot, they widen the water. | Closes by extending: turns the reading into a principle the reader can carry away. |

## The Transferable Moves, Named

The annotation column above names nearly a dozen moves, but they reduce to a smaller set of transferable maneuvers, and naming that smaller set is where the copying danger gets defused. A move is transferable when it can be described without mentioning the green light, Daisy, or any other particular of the novel, and then applied to new particulars by a writer who has never seen the model. "Open with a claim that contains its own tension" is a move. "The green light shows desire defeating itself" is material. The first travels. The second stays where it is. The paragraphs above are built so that every sentence performs a move of the first kind while handling material of the second kind, and the annotations are written to keep the two layers separate. Learn the layer that travels.

The claim-and-tension move is the most portable of the set, because every essay question can be answered with a sentence that sets two things against each other. The pattern is simple: name the topic, then split it. Gatsby's will creates and destroys. The valley accuses and excuses no one. Nick judges and withholds judgment. Daisy speaks and is silenced by her own fluency. The ending mourns and indicts. In each case the tension is not imported from outside; it is found inside the topic by asking what pulls against what. A writer practicing this move does not need the models' topics. Any prompt, on any character or symbol, yields to the same question: where is the friction inside this thing? Answer that in the opening sentence and the paragraph has its engine. The move also protects against the most common weak opening, the label, because a label cannot contain tension. "This paragraph is about Tom" splits into nothing. "Tom's brutality is inseparable from his insecurity" splits into a paragraph.

The embedded-evidence move is the most technical of the set, and the most worth practicing as a drill. Take any quotation and write three sentences that use it differently: one where it arrives as the object of a verb, one where it renames what came before it, one where a short phrase from it becomes the key term of an analytical sentence. The models show all three shapes. "Fitzgerald describes the light as the thing Gatsby believed in" makes the quotation the object of description. "A beacon that is both a real dock lamp and a symbol" uses apposition to rename. "The stammer of the question" takes a quality of the quoted speech and makes it the sentence's subject. None of these shapes requires a colon, a line break, or a framing clause that stops the paragraph to announce evidence. The paragraph never pauses to say "for example" or "this is shown when," because the evidence is already inside the thinking. Practiced across a dozen quotations, the shapes become reflexes, and the reflex is what examiners reward under the name of fluency.

The read-the-language move is the heart of analysis, and it is the move most writers perform too briefly. The models spend whole sentences on single words: "orgastic," "incredulously," "inexhaustible," "suddenly." The discipline is to treat every word in a short quotation as a candidate for attention and to ask of each one what it contributes that a synonym would not. "Incredulously" rather than "loudly" tells the reader the outburst belongs to feeling. "Suddenly" rather than "quietly" tells the reader the observation escaped. These are not decorative observations; they are the mechanism by which the paragraph proves its point at the level of the text rather than above it. A writer who learns to spend a sentence on a word has learned the difference between mentioning language and analyzing it, and that difference is most of what the top band pays for.

The develop-through-consequence move is what gives paragraphs their forward motion and their length without padding. After the evidence has been read, the models ask what follows: what does this reading imply for the character, the symbol, the novel's larger argument? The valley paragraph moves from the simile to the class geography of the whole book. The Nick paragraph moves from the broken promise to the reader's education. The Gatsby paragraph moves from the insistence to the tragic mechanism. In each case the development is not a new topic but the same topic seen from one step back, the instance becoming a pattern. Writers who stall at mid band usually stall here: they have made their point and read their evidence, and then they stop, unsure what a paragraph is supposed to do with its remaining sentences. The answer the models give is to widen the lens by one click, to ask what the reading means for the next largest unit, whether that is the character's arc, the chapter's design, or the novel's thesis. One click is enough. Two clicks is an essay. The paragraph needs one.

The close-by-extension move is the last of the portable set, and it follows naturally from the development move. A paragraph that has widened its lens can end by naming what the widened view reveals: a redefinition, a principle, a verdict. The models' final sentences all spend the knowledge the paragraph has earned, and none of them repeats the opening. The practical drill is to write the opening sentence and the final sentence as a pair and check that the final one could not have been written before the paragraph existed. If it could have, it is a restatement, and the paragraph has moved backward. If it could not have, because it depends on the evidence read and the consequences drawn, then the paragraph has traveled, and the examiner can feel the distance. That felt distance is what "development" means in a mark scheme, and the models make it visible as a property of the last sentence.

## Copying Versus Learning

The risk was stated at the start and it deserves a full treatment, because the line between learning from a model and copying it is the line this whole article either holds or fails to hold. Copying is easy to define: it is the transfer of material without the transfer of understanding. A writer who lifts "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us" into an essay and then writes around it with borrowed phrasing has transferred the material. A writer who studies how the model paragraph opens with tension, embeds the quotation, reads two words, and closes by redefining the term, and then performs those moves on a different quotation about a different topic, has transferred the understanding. The first writer's essay will sound like the model and say less. The second writer's essay will sound like themselves and say more. Examiners, who read hundreds of responses, can tell the difference within a paragraph, because borrowed phrasing sits on the page like rented furniture: it fits the room it was made for and no other.

There is a practical test for which side of the line a writer is on, and it uses the annotation column as the instrument. Cover the model sentences and read only the annotations down the column: "makes the claim," "embeds the evidence," "reads the language," "develops the consequence," "closes by extending." Then take a new quotation, one the models never touch, and write a paragraph that performs exactly that sequence. If the resulting paragraph works, the moves have transferred. If it collapses, the writer was depending on the models' material rather than their method, and the honest response is to repeat the drill with the annotations visible until the sequence holds on unfamiliar ground. The test is diagnostic rather than moral. It does not accuse; it reveals. And it reveals the precise point of failure, because a paragraph that collapses at "reads the language" needs different practice than one that collapses at "develops the consequence."

The deeper defense against copying is built into the models' specificity, as the earlier section argued, and it is worth restating as a principle: the more particular the model, the less portable its sentences and the more portable its moves. A generic model, full of phrases like "this effectively conveys" and "the writer successfully portrays," can be copied onto any essay because it says nothing in particular; it is a template with slots, and templates are made to be filled. The models above say a great deal in particular, about specific words in specific sentences, which means their sentences cannot survive transplantation. The writer who wants to use them must do the work of reading anew, and that work is the learning. This is why the article refuses to offer sentence starters or fill-in frames, which belong to a different article in the series. Frames teach the shape of a paragraph. Models teach the thinking inside the shape. The shape without the thinking is what gets copied, and the thinking is what cannot be.

There is also a legitimate middle ground that should be named rather than shamed, which is deliberate imitation as a stage of practice. A writer who rewrites a model paragraph in their own words, keeping the moves but changing the sentences, is not copying; they are doing the oldest exercise in rhetoric, and it works. A writer who writes a new paragraph that follows a model's annotation sequence step by step is not copying; they are using the model as scaffolding, and scaffolding is meant to be removed. The line is crossed only when the model's material appears in the final essay as if it were the writer's own reading. Everything short of that line is study, and the article is built to reward study: the annotations exist precisely so that a writer can imitate the moves deliberately, in practice, until the moves become their own. The goal is not to keep writers away from the models but to bring them close enough to see the machinery, because machinery can be rebuilt and surfaces can only be traced.

## Failure Patterns the Models Expose

Models are diagnostic instruments as well as exemplars, and the annotated set is useful partly for what it reveals about the paragraphs that fail. Read the models against the common failure patterns and each failure becomes visible as a missing move, which is more actionable than a vague judgment like "needs more analysis." The summary paragraph, which retells plot instead of arguing, is a paragraph with no claim move: its opening sentence is a fact, so nothing downstream can be analysis, because analysis needs a position to develop. The quotation-dump paragraph, which stacks three long extracts with a sentence of comment each, is a paragraph with no embedding move: the evidence stands outside the thinking, so the thinking has nothing to work on. The label paragraph, which names techniques without reading them, is a paragraph with no read-the-language move: it has the vocabulary of analysis and none of the activity. Each failure is the absence of something the models perform, and the annotation names the absences by naming the presences.

The most instructive failure is the competent paragraph that does everything except develop, because it is the failure ambitious writers actually produce. It opens with a real point, embeds a real quotation, reads the language with genuine attention, and then ends, having proved its point and spent its sentences. It feels finished and it is unfinished, because it never takes the one step back that turns an instance into a pattern. Against the models, the missing move is visible: there is no "develops the consequence" sentence, no widening of the lens, no spending of the earned knowledge. The fix is mechanical, which is good news. Add the sentence that asks what follows. The paragraph about Daisy's voice, without its final two sentences, would still be competent; with them, it becomes an argument about the novel's thesis. The distance between competent and strong is often exactly one sentence, the sentence that develops, and the models are built to make that sentence impossible to miss.

A second instructive failure is the paragraph that analyzes the wrong thing, usually the plot rather than the language. It reads the quotation for what happens next instead of for how it is written, so its "analysis" is really narration with analytical vocabulary sprinkled over it. The models expose this by contrast: their analytical sentences are always about words, images, syntax, and structure, never about events. "The parallelism performs the balance" is about syntax. "The simile parodies agriculture" is about the image's vehicle. "The pronoun shift drafts the reader" is about grammar. A writer checking a draft against the models can ask of each analytical sentence whether it mentions something on the page that a reader could point to, a word, a phrase, a structural choice. If the sentence is about what happens, it is summary. If it is about how the telling works, it is analysis. The test is simple and it catches the failure every time, which is why the models keep their analytical sentences so close to the quoted words.

A third failure the models expose by contrast is the breadth paragraph, which tries to cover three points in the space of one. It opens with a claim, then marches through three quotations with a sentence of comment each, and ends having said something small about three things instead of something substantial about one. The models' discipline of two quotations with differentiated jobs is the direct correction: depth beats coverage, because analysis is what happens when a writer stays with evidence long enough for it to yield. A paragraph that moves on after one sentence of comment has not analyzed; it has toured. The examination rewards the paragraph that proves one thing thoroughly over the paragraph that gestures at three, and the models show what thorough looks like: the same quotation revisited, the same word turned over, the same image seen from a second angle. Writers who feel their paragraphs are thin usually do not need more material; they need to stay longer with the material they have.

## A Worked Rewrite: From Mid-Band to Model

The rewrite drill is easier to trust when it is seen performed, so this section takes one weak paragraph through the full transformation, showing the diagnosis and the rebuild in the open. The weak paragraph below is invented to be typical: it is the kind of paragraph a capable writer produces when the moves have not yet become reflexes. Read it as a specimen, not as an object of scorn, because its failures are instructive and its material is sound.

"The valley of ashes is an important setting in the novel. It is described as 'a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens.' This shows that the area is poor and ugly, in contrast to the wealthy areas like East Egg and West Egg. Fitzgerald uses imagery here to create a depressing atmosphere. The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg look down on the valley, which suggests that God is watching the characters. This is effective because it adds a moral dimension to the setting."

The diagnosis, run through the four lifts, finds three missing moves. The opening is a label wearing a point's clothes: "an important setting" cannot be disputed, so nothing downstream has anything to prove. The quotation is dropped in beside the sentence rather than inside it, which makes the evidence illustration rather than argument. The analytical sentences name imagery and atmosphere without reading a single word: "poor and ugly" restates the quotation's content instead of analyzing its language, and "this is effective because" is the hollowest sentence in student writing, asserting an effect without showing its mechanism. The Eckleburg reading, that the eyes suggest God watching, is the paragraph's one arguable claim, but it arrives unexamined and unsupported, a thesis without a paragraph. Only the contrast between the valley and the Eggs gestures toward real thinking, and even that is left as a list of areas rather than developed.

The rebuild follows the five steps in order, and each step can be checked against the annotation vocabulary. First, the opening becomes a claim with tension: "The valley of ashes is where the novel's glitter turns into geography, a place that converts industrial waste into moral atmosphere." The same topic, now with something to prove: a conversion must be shown. Second, the quotation gets embedded: "Fitzgerald renders it as 'a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens,' and the simile does the valley's whole job." The evidence now sits inside a sentence that is already reading it. Third, the language gets its sentence: "Ashes growing like wheat parodies agriculture, suggesting that what this economy harvests is not produce but ruin, dressed in the language of fertility." The vague "imagery" and "depressing atmosphere" are gone, replaced by an account of what the image does. Fourth, the Eckleburg material becomes the development move rather than a dangling thesis: "Above it all preside the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, 'blue and gigantic,' a billboard that has outgrown advertising into a weather system, offering sight without judgment, presence without intervention." The God-reading is corrected inside the analysis, because the paragraph now has the space and the method to correct it. Fifth, the close moves forward: "Symbol here is not decoration laid over meaning but meaning's most honest container." Nothing is repeated. The paragraph has traveled from a claim about conversion to a principle about symbol, and the distance is the development the original lacked.

Compare the two versions side by side and the lesson is quantitative as well as qualitative. The weak paragraph is about one hundred and ten words. The rebuilt paragraph is about two hundred and thirty. The added one hundred and twenty words are not padding; they are the language-reading sentence, the development sentence, and the forward close, which is to say they are analysis. This is the most encouraging fact in the whole article for a writer trying to improve: the distance between mid band and top band is not talent but sentences, specific sentences performing named moves, and sentences can be added. The rewrite drill makes the addition mechanical. Diagnose the missing move, write the sentence that performs it, and check the result against the models. Repeated across a dozen paragraphs, the drill stops being a procedure and becomes a habit, which is the point at which the writer no longer needs the models at all.

## From One Paragraph to a Whole Essay

A paragraph is not an essay, and the models do not pretend otherwise, but the step from one to the other is smaller than it looks once the moves are internalized. An essay is a sequence of paragraphs, each performing the same basic sequence of claim, evidence, and analysis, with the claims arranged so that each one advances the thesis. The structure article in this series teaches that arrangement; what the models add is the confidence that each unit in the arrangement can carry its weight. A writer who can produce one paragraph that opens with tension, embeds its evidence, reads its language, and closes by extending has the only skill an essay requires, repeated four or five times with different material and linked by the thesis. The essay is not a different kind of writing. It is the paragraph's moves, scaled.

The [companion on a complete high-scoring essay](/2030/11/25/gatsby-high-scoring-essay/) shows that scaling in full, taking the paragraph-level craft the models teach and displaying it across an entire response, from introduction to conclusion. Read the two articles as a pair: this one for the unit, that one for the assembly. The unit must come first in practice even though the assembly comes first in planning, because a writer who plans a beautiful structure but cannot write a strong paragraph has built a frame with no canvas. The drills suggested throughout this article, covering the annotations, rewriting weak paragraphs to model level, practicing each move on unfamiliar quotations, are the way the unit gets built. Once it is built, the essay is an exercise in arrangement rather than invention, and arrangement is the easier skill. That is the promise the models make and the reason the article treats the paragraph as the decisive unit of essay writing.

## Fitting the Moves to Real Prompts

Moves prove their transferability on unfamiliar prompts, so this section sketches how the same sequence adapts to three prompt shapes a Gatsby writer actually meets: the character question, the theme question, and the extract-based question. The sketches are deliberately brief, showing the adaptation logic rather than writing full paragraphs, because the logic is what travels.

A character prompt such as "How does Fitzgerald present Gatsby's dream?" invites the summary trap, since the material is biographical and the temptation is to narrate the dream's history. The claim-and-tension opening redirects it: "Gatsby's dream is presented less as a goal than as a mechanism, something that manufactures distance as fast as he closes it." That sentence cannot be answered with plot; it demands evidence about mechanism. The embedded evidence follows from the novel's own formulations, the green light's recession or the lawn scene's near-grasp, and the language reading has rich material in verbs of pursuit. The development move then widens from Gatsby to the novel's treatment of desire itself, and the close redefines the prompt's term, "dream," as the paragraph has earned the right to redefine it. The same five moves, none of them specific to the green light, all of them applicable to any character the prompt names.

A theme prompt such as "Explore the significance of wealth in the novel" invites the survey trap, the paragraph that lists rich people and poor people and concludes that wealth matters. The tension opening refuses the survey: "Wealth in the novel is significant less for what it buys than for what it deafens, dulling the moral hearing of everyone who holds enough of it." Now the paragraph needs evidence of deafness rather than evidence of riches, which changes which quotations get chosen: Daisy's voice full of money rather than Gatsby's shirts, the careless people passage rather than the mansion's description. The language reading then has a precise job, showing how Fitzgerald makes affluence audible or visible as a kind of numbness, and the development move connects the instance to the novel's larger argument about class. The prompt said "significance"; the paragraph decides what kind of significance, and that decision is the analysis.

An extract-based prompt, where a passage is supplied and the writer must respond to it, changes the evidence move but nothing else. The quotations are given, so selection disappears and embedding becomes even more important: the writer must still fold the supplied lines into their own sentences rather than pointing at them from outside. The claim-and-tension opening is built from the extract's own friction, found by asking what pulls against what inside the passage. The language reading goes deeper than usual, because the extract is the whole field and there is nowhere else to look. The development move connects the passage outward to the novel, which is the step extract responses most often miss: they stay inside the lines given and never show what the passage means for the book. The models' habit of widening the lens by one click is exactly the correction, applied here as the move from passage to novel. Three prompt shapes, one move sequence, and the sequence bends without breaking, which is what transferable means.

## What the Models Leave Out, and Why

Honesty about a resource's limits is part of teaching its use, and the annotated set has limits worth naming plainly. The six paragraphs do not show introductions or conclusions, because those belong to the stage articles that own them, and a model paragraph is a middle unit by design. They do not show the linking between paragraphs, the sentences that carry an essay's argument from one unit to the next, which is a separate skill with its own moves. They do not show a writer disagreeing with the prompt, taking issue with the question's terms rather than accepting them, which is sometimes the strongest response available. They do not show what to do when evidence is thin, when a topic offers one usable quotation instead of two and the paragraph must be built on a single passage read twice as hard. Naming these omissions does not weaken the models; it locates them. They teach the paragraph as a unit. Everything around the unit must be learned elsewhere, and the series provides the elsewhere.

The most important omission is also the most instructive: the models never show a paragraph performing only some of the moves. Every annotated paragraph runs the full sequence, claim through extension, because the set is meant to display the complete machinery. Real paragraphs are often shorter and more selective. A paragraph early in an essay might open with the claim and spend its sentences on evidence, leaving development for a later paragraph that picks up the thread. A paragraph answering a narrow extract question might compress the tension and the close into single sentences. The full sequence is the repertoire, not the requirement. A writer who has internalized all eight moves must still judge which ones each paragraph needs, and that judgment is itself a skill the models cannot perform for the reader. The annotation column shows what each sentence does; deciding which sentences a paragraph needs is the writer's own work, and it is work that begins where the models end.

There is a second omission that follows from the first, which is that the models are all successful. They show no false starts, no abandoned readings, no quotations that resisted analysis. Real drafting includes all of those, and a writer meeting resistance in practice should not conclude that the moves have failed. A quotation that will not yield to word-level reading may be the wrong quotation, and the correct move is to choose another rather than to force the analysis. A claim that will not generate tension may be a fact rather than a point, and the correct move is to re-argue it rather than to decorate it. The models, being finished products, hide the selection and rejection that produced them. The article can state the principle explicitly even though the paragraphs cannot show it: strong writing is mostly deletion, and the paragraph the examiner sees is the survivor of several paragraphs the examiner never will. Practice should therefore include the wastebasket. Write the paragraph that does not work, diagnose the missing move, and write it again. The models show the destination. The drafting shows the road.

A final omission concerns the examination itself. The models assume a writer with time to choose words carefully, and the examination does not always grant it. The section on the clock addresses the compression, but there is a deeper point: under pressure, writers regress to their oldest habits, not their newest learning. A writer who has performed the moves fifty times in practice will perform them in the examination; a writer who has read about them once will not. This is why the article keeps returning to drills rather than to admiration. The models are not there to be appreciated. They are there to be taken apart, rebuilt, and rebuilt again, until the sequence of moves is closer to reflex than to recollection. At that point the writer has outgrown the resource, which is the only successful outcome a teaching resource can have. The models leave out the practice. The practice is the point.

## Writing at Speed: the Examination Clock

The models were written without a clock, and honesty requires saying so, because examination paragraphs are written against one. The question is which moves survive compression, and the article's answer is that all of them do, provided the writer knows what to protect and what to cut. A standard examination essay allows roughly forty-five minutes: five for planning, thirty-five for writing, five for checking. Four or five paragraphs in thirty-five minutes means seven to eight minutes per paragraph, which is enough for the full sequence if the sequence is practiced, and not enough if every sentence has to be invented from nothing. The drill work the article recommends is what buys the time: a writer who has embedded a hundred quotations in practice does not spend examination minutes deciding how to embed this one.

Under pressure, protect the moves in this order: the arguable claim first, because a paragraph without one cannot be rescued by anything downstream; the embedded evidence second, because the quotation must be inside the thinking; one language-reading sentence third, spending the available time on the single most important word rather than spreading it thin; and the forward close fourth, because the last sentence is what the examiner carries into the next paragraph. What gets cut first is the second quotation. The models use two, but a single quotation deeply read outperforms two shallowly cited, and the time saved goes to the development sentence, which is the move most worth protecting after the claim. A compressed model paragraph is therefore six sentences rather than eight: claim, tension, embedded evidence, language reading, development, forward close. It is shorter than the annotated set and it still performs every lift, which proves the lifts are structural rather than decorative.

The planning minutes matter more than writers expect, because the moves can be sketched before the writing starts. For each paragraph, the plan needs only three notes: the claim in one sentence, the quotation in three to eight words, and the word to be read closely. That is enough. The tension, the embedding shape, the development direction, and the close can be trusted to emerge in the writing if the claim and the quotation are well chosen, because the moves are habits by then rather than decisions. Writers who plan in full sentences waste the writing minutes; writers who plan in moves spend them. The five-minute check at the end has one job: read each paragraph's first and last sentences as a pair and confirm the last could not have been written before the paragraph existed. Any paragraph that fails that test gets its final sentence rewritten on the spot, since the forward close is the cheapest high-value repair in the whole repertoire.

## Verdict

The verdict this article takes is direct: for a writer preparing Gatsby essays, studying annotated model paragraphs move by move is the highest-yield practice available, higher than reading more criticism, higher than memorizing more quotations, higher than collecting techniques. The reason is that the examination rewards the performance of analysis, not the possession of knowledge, and models are the only study material that shows the performance itself. A quotation memorized is potential. A paragraph that embeds and reads that quotation is the potential realized, and the realization is what gets marked. The annotated set above is built to be studied in that spirit: not admired, not borrowed, but taken apart and rebuilt, until the moves belong to the writer rather than the page.

That verdict comes with the condition the whole article has enforced, which is that the models must be used as moves rather than material. Used that way, they are inexhaustible, because every new quotation is a new occasion for the same maneuvers, and every new prompt is a new field for the same claim-and-tension opening. Used the other way, as phrases to lift, they are a trap, and the trap is set by their very quality: the better the model, the more tempting the theft, and the more obvious the theft to an examiner who has seen the model's thinking before. The article's position is therefore not that models are safe but that they are powerful, and that power is worth the risk when the annotation layer does its job of naming what travels. Strong writing shown, not just described: the claim holds, and the six paragraphs are the evidence.

## Study: Putting the Models to Work

Study turns the models from examples into skills, and the work divides naturally into finding evidence and practicing the moves. For evidence, the [annotated text at VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html) gathers the novel's key passages with the kind of line-level attention the models assume, which makes it the right place to find the quotations a new paragraph needs; a writer building a paragraph on the valley of ashes or the final page can ground every claim in a passage that has already been closely read, and the models show what to do with such a passage once it is found. The sequence the article recommends is to choose a quotation from the annotated text, cover the model sentences, read only the annotation moves, and write a fresh paragraph performing that sequence on the new material. That is the transfer drill, and it is the single exercise that converts the models from something read into something owned.

For practice with feedback, the [essay practice tool at ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/great-gatsby-essay-practice.html) is where model-level paragraphs get written under conditions that resemble the examination, with responses returned on the writing rather than just the ideas; the value is in the loop, writing a paragraph, seeing where the claim softens or the evidence detaches, and rewriting with the moves named explicitly. The models above set the standard the loop aims at, and the annotation vocabulary gives the writer and the feedback a shared language: a response can say that the embedding slipped or the development stopped one sentence early, and the writer knows exactly which move to repair. Used together, the annotated text as the evidence store and the practice tool as the training ground, the study routine is complete: find the material, perform the moves, get the reading, repeat. The paragraph is the unit, the moves are the method, and the repetition is what makes them permanent.

A concrete weekly routine makes the study specific. In one session, choose three quotations from the annotated text and write one paragraph on each, performing the full annotation sequence with the models visible beside the draft. In the next session, rewrite the weakest of the three from memory, with the models covered, and diagnose which move collapsed. In a third session, take the rewritten paragraph into the practice tool and work the feedback back into a final version. Three sessions, three paragraphs, one move sequence repeated until it holds without support. The routine is deliberately narrow: it practices one unit, the paragraph, until the unit is reliable, and only then scales to the essay. Writers who follow it for a month arrive at the examination with the moves closer to reflex than to recollection, which is the only preparation that survives the clock.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What do model Gatsby essay paragraphs look like?

They look like thinking made visible: a paragraph that opens with an arguable point, folds its quotations into its own sentences, spends time on the language of the evidence, and ends by extending the reading rather than repeating it. The six models in the annotated set average eight or nine sentences, and every sentence has a job the annotation names. What they do not look like is a template filled in. There are no dropped-in block quotations, no technique labels standing in for analysis, no plot summary wearing analytical vocabulary. The surface feature a reader notices first is density: the models say a lot per sentence, because each sentence is doing argumentative work rather than marking time. The second feature is control: the writer's voice never surrenders the paragraph to Fitzgerald, because the quotations are short and embedded, serving the sentences rather than interrupting them. A reader who wants the quick visual test can check any paragraph against three questions: does the first sentence argue, does the evidence sit inside the writer's grammar, and does the last sentence say something new. The models answer yes three times, and that triple yes is what the look amounts to.

### Q: How do the models show point, evidence, and analysis?

They show the three components working as a braid rather than a stack, which is the article's central demonstration. The point appears first as an arguable claim, often carrying its own tension, and it creates obligations the rest of the paragraph must meet. The evidence arrives embedded inside the writer's sentences, so the claim and the quotation share one syntactic life and the reader can see the dependency. The analysis grows out of the quotation's language: the model reads specific words, names what they do, connects the doing to an effect, and lifts the effect back to the claim. Crucially, the components interleave. The second sentence often sharpens the point while introducing the evidence. The embedding sentence already begins the analysis by the way it frames the quotation. The final sentence returns to the point with the force the analysis has earned. A reader watching for the three labels will find them, but a reader watching for the joints between them will learn more, because the joints are where the paragraph's real craft lives. The annotation column exists to make those joints explicit, naming the move each sentence makes so the interleaving can be followed deliberately.

### Q: What does each annotated sentence do?

Each annotation names the sentence's function in the paragraph's argument, using a vocabulary of moves rather than a description of content. The recurring moves are making the claim, adding tension, embedding the evidence, reading the language, naming what the method achieves, connecting the parts to the claim, developing the consequence, and closing by extending. The annotation never says what the sentence is about; it says what the sentence does. "Reads the language" tells the writer that the sentence's job is close attention to words, regardless of which words. "Develops the consequence" tells the writer that the sentence's job is to widen the lens by one click, regardless of the topic. This functional naming is what makes the annotations portable: a writer can take the sequence of moves from the Daisy paragraph and perform it on a paragraph about Tom, because the moves describe operations, not material. Reading the annotation column straight down, with the model sentences covered, gives the paragraph's skeleton as a pure sequence of operations, and that skeleton is the most transferable thing the article offers.

### Q: What lifts a paragraph to top band?

Four things, all visible in the models and all named by the annotations. First, an arguable point that contains tension, so the paragraph has something to resolve rather than something to report. Second, embedded evidence, so the quotations argue from inside the writer's sentences instead of illustrating from outside them. Third, language-level analysis that stays with specific words and says what they do, connecting the device to an effect and the effect to the claim, rather than labeling techniques. Fourth, development and a forward-moving close: the paragraph widens its lens by at least one click and ends by spending the knowledge it has earned, saying something the opening could not have said. Paragraphs missing any one of these stall below the top band in a characteristic way: no tension gives summary, no embedding gives quotation-dumping, no language reading gives technique-spotting, no development gives the competent-but-static paragraph. The models perform all four, and the article's drills isolate each one, because top-band writing is not a mystery but a set of moves performed together, and moves can be practiced.

### Q: Should you copy the models?

No, and the article treats this as a live risk rather than a formality. Copying means transferring the material without the understanding: lifting the models' sentences or close phrasing into an essay as if the reading were your own. It fails on two levels. Practically, examiners recognize borrowed phrasing, and a paragraph built from someone else's reading cannot adapt when the prompt shifts, because the writer does not own the thinking underneath it. Pedagogically, copying bypasses the exact work the models are meant to cause, which is the work of reading closely and arguing precisely. What the article recommends instead is deliberate imitation as practice: rewrite a model paragraph in your own words keeping the moves, or write a fresh paragraph following a model's annotation sequence on a new quotation. That is study, not theft, and it is the oldest exercise in rhetoric. The models are built to reward it and punish copying, because their specificity makes their sentences untransplantable while their moves transfer freely. Learn the moves. Leave the sentences.

### Q: How do the models teach transferable moves?

They teach them by separating the move from the material in every annotation, so the reader always sees two layers: what the sentence says about Gatsby, and what the sentence does as an operation. "Makes the claim" is an operation performable on any topic. "Embeds the evidence" is an operation performable with any quotation. "Reads the language" is an operation performable on any passage. The article then names the smaller portable set explicitly: the claim-and-tension opening, the embedded-evidence shapes, the word-level reading, the develop-through-consequence widening, and the close-by-extension ending. Each is described without reference to the models' particulars and each comes with a drill for practicing it on unfamiliar material. The transfer test the article proposes makes the teaching verifiable: cover the model sentences, read only the annotations, and write a new paragraph performing that move sequence on a quotation the models never touch. If the paragraph works, the moves have transferred. The models thus function as a gymnasium rather than a gallery: the equipment is fixed, but the strength built on it goes wherever the writer goes.

### Q: How long should a model Gatsby paragraph be?

Long enough to complete the full move sequence and short enough that every sentence works, which in the annotated set means eight or nine sentences and roughly two hundred to two hundred and sixty words. That length is not a rule but a consequence: a paragraph needs an opening claim, a tension or narrowing, embedded evidence, language reading, method-to-meaning connection, development, and a forward close, and those jobs take about eight sentences when none of them is padded. Shorter paragraphs usually drop the development move, which is why five-sentence paragraphs so often feel competent but static. Longer paragraphs risk drifting into a second point, which belongs in a second paragraph. The practical guidance is to count moves rather than words: if every sentence performs one of the annotated operations and the sequence runs from claim to extension, the paragraph is the right length whatever the word count. Writers working under time pressure should protect the development and closing moves first, since those are the sentences most often sacrificed and the ones examiners most reward.

### Q: How many quotations fit inside one model paragraph?

Two, in the annotated set's consistent practice: a primary quotation that carries the main analysis and a secondary one that deepens or extends the point. The models never build a paragraph on a single quotation, because one piece of evidence can be read brilliantly yet still leave the paragraph feeling narrow, and they never use three, because a third quotation starts to crowd out the analysis the paragraph exists to perform. The two-quotation structure also creates the paragraph's rhythm: the first quotation gets the full treatment of embedding and language reading, while the second arrives later and more swiftly, as confirmation or complication. Notice how the secondary quotations in the models are shorter and handled in a single sentence, while the primary ones get two or three sentences of attention. That asymmetry is deliberate. It tells the examiner which evidence matters most and keeps the paragraph's analytical weight on the writer's reading rather than on the accumulation of proof. One strong quotation deeply read beats three shallowly cited, and two well-differentiated beats one.

### Q: Can one paragraph analyze more than one quotation at once?

Yes, and the models do it by giving the two quotations different jobs rather than equal treatment. The primary quotation is embedded early and read at length: its words are unpacked, its method named, its effect connected to the claim. The secondary quotation arrives after the reading is established and functions as extension, showing the same mechanism at a new site or adding a complication the first quotation could not supply. The Daisy paragraph reads "full of money" across three sentences, then brings in the "beautiful little fool" verdict in one, as the same cold currency in another key. The ending paragraph gives the final line the full treatment, then uses the "tomorrow we will run faster" passage as the same motion in a different register. The discipline is that the second quotation must earn its place by doing something the first cannot: deepening, complicating, or widening. A second quotation that merely repeats the first's work is padding, and the models never permit it. Two quotations, two jobs, one argument.

### Q: How do the models handle counter-readings inside a paragraph?

They absorb the counter-reading into the claim's tension rather than staging a separate objection-and-reply. The models rarely pause to say "some might argue," because the arguable point already contains its own counterweight: Gatsby's will creates and destroys, the valley accuses and excuses no one, Nick judges while promising not to. That built-in tension does the work a counter-argument paragraph would do, but inside a single line of thought, which keeps the paragraph unified. Where a genuine alternative reading exists, the models acknowledge it inside the development move, as the valley paragraph does with Wilson's misreading of the eyes: the wrong reading is named, shown to be wrong, and the correction becomes part of the analysis rather than a detour from it. This is more economical than the bolted-on counter-paragraph and more honest, because it treats the alternative as material for the argument rather than an obstacle to it. The transferable lesson is to let the opening claim carry the friction, so the paragraph argues with itself productively from the first sentence.

### Q: How do you diagnose a paragraph that stays at mid band?

Check it against the four lifts and find which one is missing, because mid-band paragraphs almost always perform three of the four and drop one. Read the opening sentence and ask whether a reasonable reader could dispute it; if not, the claim move is missing and the paragraph is summary. Find the quotations and ask whether they sit inside the writer's sentences or beside them; if beside, the embedding move is missing and the paragraph is illustration. Read the analytical sentences and ask whether they mention specific words doing specific work; if they name techniques without reading language, the analysis move is missing. Read the final sentence and ask whether it could have been written before the paragraph existed; if yes, the development move is missing and the paragraph is static. The models make this diagnosis mechanical by giving each move a visible form, so a writer can hold a draft next to the annotated set and spot the absence the way a mechanic spots a missing part. Most mid-band writers are surprised by which move is missing, which is why the check has to be explicit rather than intuitive.

### Q: How do you turn plot summary into a model paragraph?

Start by replacing the paragraph's factual opening with an arguable claim about the same material, because summary is what happens when a paragraph has nothing to prove. A paragraph that begins "Gatsby throws parties to attract Daisy" can only narrate, since the sentence is true and complete. The same material becomes analytical when the opening claims something disputable: "Gatsby's parties are advertisements for a product, himself, that Daisy never ordered." Now the paragraph must show the advertising, show the product, and show the mismatch, and every sentence has work. Next, convert the plot details into evidence by embedding short quotations instead of retelling events; the parties become "men and girls came and went like moths," a phrase to read rather than a scene to summarize. Then read the language of that phrase, develop the consequence for the character's strategy, and close by extending. The transformation is mechanical: claim replaces fact, quotation replaces retelling, reading replaces narration. The models demonstrate each substitution, and a writer can perform the same surgery on any summary paragraph by working through the annotation sequence.

### Q: How much context do the model paragraphs use?

Very little, and what they use stays inside the analysis rather than standing apart from it. The models contain no biographical background, no historical scene-setting, no separate context sentences, because the paragraphs are built to demonstrate the point-evidence-analysis braid at full density, and context would loosen it. Where the wider world enters, it enters through the reading: the valley paragraph's "what this economy harvests" carries the economic context inside an analytical sentence, and the Daisy paragraph's "a world that punishes women for seeing clearly" carries the gendered context inside the consequence. This is the embedded-context discipline the series teaches elsewhere: context should serve a textual point, not preface it. A writer adapting the models for an essay that requires contextual discussion should follow the same pattern, letting context arrive as part of a sentence that is primarily about the novel's language. The models' spareness on context is therefore not a prohibition but a demonstration of proportion: the paragraph belongs to the text, and everything else enters only as it illuminates the text.

### Q: What transitional moves link sentences inside the models?

The models transition by logic rather than by signposting, which is why they contain almost no transitional phrases of the "furthermore" and "in addition" kind. The links are carried by ideas: a pronoun that reaches back, a term repeated with a shift, a consequence drawn explicitly. "That doubleness" reaches back to the two truths the previous sentence named. "That same insistence" carries the insistence from speech to smile. "This is why" draws the consequence the evidence requires. "The two utterances belong together" announces the unification the paragraph has been building toward. Each of these is a logical joint, not a decorative hinge: remove the sentence before it and the transition collapses, which is the test of a real link. The transferable lesson is that strong paragraphs cohere through argument, not through vocabulary. A writer who finds themselves stacking "moreover" and "additionally" is usually stacking points that do not connect, and the fix is not better transitions but a better through-line. The models show the through-line doing the transitional work, sentence by sentence.

### Q: How do the models track a symbol that changes meaning?

They track it by staging the change as the paragraph's development, so the shifting meaning becomes the argument rather than a complication of it. The green-light paragraph does not treat the light as a fixed emblem; it follows the light from dock lamp to "orgastic future" to lawn to universal recession, and each stage re-reads the symbol at a new distance. The valley paragraph moves from simile to billboard to class geography, letting the symbol accumulate meanings without ever fixing one. The method is to attach each meaning to a specific textual site and to make the movement between sites the paragraph's forward motion: the symbol means one thing here and another thing there, and the paragraph's claim is about the pattern of the change. This avoids the two standard failures, which are fixing the symbol's meaning once and then repeating it, and listing meanings without connecting them. A symbol that changes is best handled by a paragraph that changes with it, and the models show the paragraph's development move doing exactly that work, widening the lens as the symbol widens its significance.

### Q: Do the model moves transfer to any Gatsby prompt?

Yes, because the moves are operations on argument and evidence, not content about particular topics, and any prompt supplies argument and evidence to operate on. The claim-and-tension opening works whether the question asks about a character, a theme, a symbol, or the narration, because every such topic contains internal friction waiting to be named. The embedded-evidence shapes work with any quotation, since they are grammatical maneuvers rather than interpretive ones. The word-level reading works on any passage, because every passage has words doing specific work. The develop-through-consequence move works under any rubric, because every reading implies something for the next largest unit. The article's six models deliberately span theme, character, symbol, narration, and the ending to demonstrate this range, but the demonstration is illustrative rather than exhaustive: the moves would work identically on Tom, on Jordan, on the Plaza scene, on any material the examination can name. The only thing that does not transfer is the models' content, which is the point. Moves travel. Material stays.

### Q: How do you practice rewriting a weak paragraph to model level?

Take a weak paragraph, diagnose the missing move using the four-lift check, and rebuild it move by move against the annotation sequence. The procedure is mechanical. First, replace the opening with an arguable claim that contains tension; if the original opening was a fact or a label, this single change re-aims every sentence that follows. Second, embed the quotations inside the writer's sentences, cutting any block quotation down to its load-bearing phrase and supplying the grammar that makes it argue. Third, add the language-reading sentences, spending at least one full sentence on the most important word in each quotation and saying what it does. Fourth, add the development sentence that widens the lens one click, asking what the reading implies for the character, the symbol, or the novel. Fifth, replace the closing restatement with a forward-moving final sentence that spends the paragraph's earned knowledge. Work through the five steps in order, checking each against the corresponding annotation in the models. The rewritten paragraph will be longer than the original, and the added length will be analysis rather than padding, which is the definition of the improvement.

### Q: What does the annotation reveal about paragraph rhythm?

It reveals that the models alternate between short, punchy sentences and longer, unfolding ones, and that the alternation is functional rather than decorative. The claim sentences are short and declarative: they land. The embedding and reading sentences are longer and more complex: they unfold. The development sentences stretch further, carrying the paragraph outward. The closing sentences tighten again, delivering the verdict. Read the Daisy paragraph aloud and the rhythm is audible: claim, evidence, a quick jab on "suddenly," a longer unpacking of "full of money," the second quotation arriving swiftly, the consequence widening, the close landing. The annotation makes this visible by showing that different moves naturally take different shapes: making a claim wants brevity, reading language wants space, developing wants reach. Writers who produce monotonous paragraphs usually write every sentence at the same length because they are performing the same operation repeatedly, often summary. Varying the moves varies the rhythm automatically, which is why the article teaches rhythm through function rather than through style advice. Get the moves right and the music follows.

### Q: Where should the topic sentence sit in the model paragraph?

First, without exception in the annotated set, and the consistency is a deliberate teaching choice rather than a universal law. The models put the claim first because the claim assigns every later sentence its job: the reader knows what the evidence must prove and what the analysis must develop before either arrives. A delayed claim can work in skilled hands, building suspense toward a revelation, but it risks the paragraph reading as a tour until the point lands, and it asks the examiner to hold unassigned evidence in memory. For examination writing, where clarity under time pressure is rewarded, the first-sentence claim is the stronger default. The models also show that the first sentence can do double duty, carrying the tension that a weaker paragraph would need a second sentence to add. That economy is worth practicing: a first sentence that both claims and complicates gives the paragraph its engine immediately. Once the move is mastered, a writer can experiment with delayed openings from strength rather than from habit, but the models teach the strong default first.

### Q: How do the models use contrast inside a paragraph?

They use it as an engine rather than an ornament, setting two things against each other so the friction generates the analysis. The Gatsby paragraph sets creation against destruction in a single act of will. The Nick paragraph sets enchantment against repulsion in a single balanced clause. The valley paragraph sets the pastoral promise against its violation in a single phrase. The ending paragraph sets "beat on" against "borne back" in a single sentence. In each case the contrast is not between two topics but inside one: the paragraph's subject contains both poles, and the analysis lives in the space between them. This is why contrast is so productive as a paragraph device: it gives the writer something to resolve, and the resolution is the analysis. The transferable form is the "not X but Y" and "both X and Y" constructions the models favor, which force precision by requiring the writer to name both sides. A paragraph without contrast often drifts because it has nothing to push against; a paragraph built on an internal contrast has its direction from the first sentence, and the annotation marks these moments so the device can be recognized and reused.
